The Honest Page

By Cory Brent

This page exists so you can trust the books without taking our word for it. Every Bible you have held came through human hands, and hands make mistakes, and pages do not last forever. We would rather show you where this edition is strong, where it is uncertain, and where the parchment itself gives out, than dress it up. Honesty is not a footnote to this work. It is the work.

How an ancient book reaches you

No ancient book arrives directly. What survives is a witness — a manuscript copied from an earlier copy, which was itself copied from an earlier copy still. Along that chain, ink fades, parchment tears, a word is smudged or worn at the foot of a page, and a later copyist does his best with what he can still see.

The Ethiopian books here are no exception. Some of the 81 books came through sound public-domain English editions, printed as received. Where no usable English existed, a book was translated anew, directly from the Ge'ez, the ancient liturgical language of Ethiopia. Either way, every text was checked against its source — a printed edition page by page, or a manuscript read from photographs, folio by folio.

The two kinds of marks

In the workshop files — the working texts from which the books were set — uncertain readings are marked, in two kinds, because there are two kinds of uncertainty.

A reading marked [?] is a word whose reading is uncertain. A reading marked (?) is a probable reading — the best reading the evidence supports, held a little less firmly. Across the whole corpus there are 32,883 such marks: 23,327 marked [?] and 9,556 marked (?).

An uncertain mark is not a hole. In every one of those 32,883 places, the text exists. The word is there on the page; it is simply hard to read — faded, damaged, or written in a hand that leaves room for honest doubt. We marked it rather than quietly choosing a reading and letting you believe it was sure.

The only true losses

The one real loss in the whole edition is in Josippon, the history that stands in this canon. Josippon was translated for this edition from a single base witness, British Library EAP704/2/3, a Ge'ez codex copied around 1600 — and that witness is itself damaged. In three places it has physically lost the text: chapter 7, verses 12–14; chapter 84, verses 21–35; and chapter 104, verses 14–19. Together that is 24 verse units — the only physical losses in the entire corpus.

Those passages were hunted — through the witness at Strasbourg, through other copies at the British Library, and through a catalogue of some 5,000 Ge'ez manuscripts — and they were never found. The other witnesses that could be reached do not carry them at all. So in the working text they stand as honest blanks.

They were not reconstructed, not bridged with plausible words, and not invented. What the parchment gives, we give. What it does not, we do not invent. We would rather hand you a gap we can explain than a smooth paragraph we cannot.

Why the book you read is clean

In the reader editions you will not see any of those marks. The printed and reading editions strip them all, so the text reads clean, as a Bible should when you sit down to read and study. A page crowded with brackets would get between you and the words.

But the marks were not destroyed. They live in the workshop files — the record anyone can consult to see where a reading is sure, where it is probable, and where it is uncertain. Cleaning the reader's page and keeping the workshop record are the same honesty, seen from two sides: we do not clutter your reading, and we do not hide our work.

To see how the canon is counted, and why Ethiopia kept books other traditions set aside, start with Why 81 books?

Two eyes on every word

Every passage passed under two independent sets of eyes: the translator, and then a separate auditor who compared the English against the source and reported every divergence, so the reports could be worked through one by one. On top of that, fabrication screens were run — checks designed to catch invented content, numbers never in the source, and matter that belonged to a scribe or an editor rather than to the text itself. That chain did catch things, and catching them is what it is for.

Now the honest ceiling, stated plainly. Verse-level fidelity across some 1.37 million words rests on that audit chain. It does not rest on a literal 100% word-by-word re-read of every word by a second human from first page to last. And the fresh translations made directly from the Ge'ez are strong working translations — a specialist could argue with them in places, as specialists honestly do. That is a fair account of good, careful work. We will not claim more for it, because claiming more would break the rule the work was built on.

That rule can be said in the words of the translator's note printed in the books themselves: "We did our best with every word."

Stay as true to the manuscript word as possible. Mark what you cannot read with confidence. Record what is lost instead of bridging it. And never invent.
