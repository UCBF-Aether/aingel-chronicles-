Study Notes — Mark

Chapter 7

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Then came together to him the Pharisees and certain scribes from Jerusalem. Jerusalem's learned men travelled to judge a Galilean teacher, and their coming shows the opposition was now organised. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: When they saw some of his disciples eat bread with defiled, that is, unwashen, hands, they found fault. Their fault was not with dirt but with ceremony: hands unwashed by ritual were called defiled, though they were clean. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: For the Pharisees and all the Jews, except they wash their hands oft, eat not, holding the tradition of the elders. Hand-washing before meals was an elders' tradition, not Moses' law, yet it bound conscience as if it were God's command. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: When they come from the market, except they wash, they eat not, and many other things they hold, as washing cups, pots, brazen vessels, and tables. Tradition multiplied washings until vessels and furniture bore the same yoke as hands, and religion became a matter of pots. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Then the Pharisees and scribes asked him, Why do your disciples not walk according to the tradition of the elders, but eat with unwashen hands? They charged the disciples, aiming through them at Christ, and made custom the test of godliness. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: He answered, Well has Isaiah prophesied of you hypocrites, as it is written, This people honours me with their lips, but their heart is far from me. Isaiah's charge fit them exactly: lip-service with a distant heart is the definition of their religion. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: However, in vain do they worship me, teaching for doctrines the commandments of men. Worship built on men's commands is vain, however earnest, because God will not accept his own worship on man's terms. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: For laying aside the commandment of God, you hold the tradition of men, as washing pots and cups, and many such like things you do. Tradition did not merely add to God's law; it displaced it, and pots were honoured while precepts were set aside. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: He said, Full well you reject the commandment of God, that you may keep your own tradition. Their rejection was deliberate and clever: tradition was kept precisely by breaking the commandment. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: For Moses said, Honour your father and mother, and, Whoever curses father or mother, let him die the death. The fifth commandment demands both honour in deed and reverence in speech, with a severe sanction. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: But you say, If a man says to his father or mother, It is Corban, a gift, by whatever you might be profited by me, he shall be free. By vowing his goods to the temple as Corban, a son could claim release from supporting his parents, and the scribes allowed it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: And you suffer him no more to do anything for his father or mother. The tradition did not merely excuse neglect; it forbade the very help the commandment required. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Making the word of God of no effect through your tradition, which you have delivered, and many such like things you do. One nullified commandment proves the system: tradition that voids Scripture is itself condemned, however many cases follow it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: When he had called all the people to him, he said, Hearken to me, every one of you, and understand. Christ turns from the scribes to the people, for the truth about defilement concerns everyone, and all must understand it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: There is nothing from without a man, that entering into him can defile him, but the things which come out of him, those defile the man. Food and touch cannot stain the soul; defilement is moral, not ceremonial, and comes from within. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: If any man have ears to hear, let him hear. The saying is a call to attention, for a truth that overturns tradition must be weighed, not merely heard. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: When he entered the house from the people, his disciples asked him concerning the parable. Private questions again follow public teaching, and disciples are not blamed for asking what the crowd could not follow. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: He said, Are you so without understanding also? Do you not perceive that whatever from without enters the man cannot defile him? Their slowness grieved him, for the principle was plain: what is eaten does not reach the conscience. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Because it enters not into his heart, but into the belly, and goes out into the draught, purging all meats. Food passes through the body without touching the heart, and in saying so Christ declared all meats clean in principle. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: He said, That which comes out of the man, that defiles the man. The mouth and the life reveal the heart, and it is the heart's outflow that stains. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: For from within, out of the heart of men, proceed evil thoughts, adulteries, fornications, murders. The catalogue begins with thoughts, for sin is conceived within before it is committed without. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Thefts, covetousness, wickedness, deceit, lasciviousness, an evil eye, blasphemy, pride, foolishness. The list moves from deeds to desires to dispositions, showing the heart's full corruption in every direction. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: All these evil things come from within, and defile the man. Every one of them is born inside, and together they prove that washing hands can never wash a heart. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: From there he arose and went into the borders of Tyre and Sidon, and entered a house, and would have no man know it, but he could not be hid. Even in Gentile borders, seeking privacy, his fame found him, for a light cannot be hidden. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: For a certain woman, whose young daughter had an unclean spirit, heard of him, and came and fell at his feet. A mother's hearing brought her to his feet, and her daughter's demon became her own petition. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: The woman was a Greek, a Syrophenician by nation, and she besought him to cast forth the devil out of her daughter. A Gentile outsider sought a Jewish Messiah, and her earnest prayer crossed every boundary of nation and custom. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: But Jesus said to her, Let the children first be filled, for it is not meet to take the children's bread and cast it to the dogs. The children are Israel, first served by the covenant, and the dogs are Gentiles; the saying tests whether she will accept the order of grace. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: She answered, Yes, Lord: yet the dogs under the table eat of the children's crumbs. She accepts the name of dog and turns it into a plea: even crumbs from the children's table are enough for her need. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: He said to her, For this saying go your way; the devil is gone out of your daughter. Her wise and humble answer won the cure, and faith expressed in a single sentence prevailed. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: When she came to her house, she found the devil gone out, and her daughter laid upon the bed. The cure was wrought at a distance by his word, and the quiet daughter on the bed proved the demon's departure. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: Again, departing from the coasts of Tyre and Sidon, he came to the Sea of Galilee, through the midst of the coasts of Decapolis. His route through Gentile Decapolis foreshadowed a gospel that would travel those roads. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: They brought to him one that was deaf and had an impediment in his speech, and they besought him to put his hand upon him. Friends brought the deaf man, as friends had brought the palsied man, and their prayer sought Christ's touch. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: He took him aside from the multitude, and put his fingers into his ears, and he spit, and touched his tongue. Private, tangible signs suited a man who could not hear explanation, and each sense was touched in turn. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: Looking up to heaven, he sighed, and said to him, Ephphatha, that is, Be opened. The upward look owned heaven as the source, the sigh showed compassion for human misery, and the word commanded what nature could not do. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: Straightway his ears were opened, and the string of his tongue was loosed, and he spoke plain. Hearing and speech were healed together, for the same power opens both ear and tongue. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: He charged them that they should tell no man, but the more he charged them, the more a great deal they published it. Disobedience to his charge, though from wonder, still hindered the quiet he sought, and their publishing could not be contained. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

37 COMMENTARY: They were beyond measure astonished, saying, He has done all things well: he makes both the deaf to hear and the dumb to speak. Their verdict is the gospel's summary: all Christ does is well done, restoring creation's broken senses. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
