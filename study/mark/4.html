Study Notes — Mark

Chapter 4

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: He began again to teach by the sea side, and a great multitude gathered, so he entered a ship and sat in the sea while the multitude stood on the land. The boat became a pulpit, that all might hear, and Christ adapted his place to his hearers. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: He taught them many things by parables, and said to them in his doctrine. Parables put heavenly truth in earthly dress, that it might be remembered and searched out. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Hearken; behold, there went out a sower to sow. The call to hearken demands attention, for the parable judges every hearer by the ground of his heart. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: As he sowed, some seed fell by the way side, and the fowls came and devoured it. Seed on a beaten path never enters the soil, and is easily snatched away. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Some fell on stony ground, where it had not much earth, and immediately sprang up because it had no depth. Quick growth without depth promises much and proves little. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: When the sun was up, it was scorched, and because it had no root, it withered away. Trial is the sun that reveals rootless religion, which withers under the heat it once welcomed. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Some fell among thorns, and the thorns grew up and choked it, and it yielded no fruit. Good seed can be starved by stronger growth beside it, until nothing ripens. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Other seed fell on good ground and yielded fruit that sprang up and increased, some thirty, some sixty, some a hundred. Good ground differs not in kind of fruit but in measure, and all true fruit increases. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: He said, He that has ears to hear, let him hear. The parable is a test: those with ears must use them, and attention is itself a duty. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: When he was alone, those about him with the twelve asked him concerning the parable. Private inquiry after public preaching is the mark of disciples who will not let a hard saying pass unasked. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: He said, to you it is given to know the mystery of the kingdom of God, but to those without, all things are done in parables. Disciples are entrusted with the key, while wilful outsiders hear only the veil. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: That seeing they may see and not perceive, and hearing they may hear and not understand, lest they be converted and their sins forgiven. Judicial blindness follows chosen blindness: those who shut their eyes are left in the dark they chose. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: He said, Know you not this parable, and how then will you know all parables? The sower is the key parable; to miss it is to miss the pattern of all gospel preaching. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: The sower sows the word. The seed is not human wisdom but God's word, and its power lies in itself, not in the sower's skill. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: These by the way side are those where the word is sown, but when they have heard, Satan comes immediately and takes away the word sown in their hearts. Satan's first work is theft: he steals the word before it can take root. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: These on stony ground, when they have heard the word, immediately receive it with gladness. Joy at hearing is no proof of root; feeling can be warm while the heart is shallow. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: They have no root in themselves, and endure but for a time; afterward, when affliction or persecution arises for the word's sake, immediately they are offended. The same word that gladdened them offends them when it costs them, and they fall away. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: These sown among thorns are such as hear the word. Hearing alone does not save, if what is heard is left to compete with stronger loves. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: The cares of this world, the deceitfulness of riches, and the lusts of other things entering in, choke the word, and it becomes unfruitful. Cares distract, riches deceive, and lusts crowd out, until the word bears no fruit at all. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: These on good ground are such as hear the word, receive it, and bring forth fruit, some thirtyfold, some sixty, some a hundred. Receiving is more than hearing: it keeps the word, and kept word always bears fruit in some measure. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: He said, Is a candle brought to be put under a bushel or under a bed, and not set on a candlestick? Truth given to disciples is meant to shine, not to be hidden under business or ease. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: For there is nothing hid which shall not be manifested, nor anything kept secret but that it should come abroad. Present obscurity is temporary; the kingdom's secrets will be proclaimed in due time. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: If any man have ears to hear, let him hear. The repeated call warns that hearing is a trust, and the use we make of light decides whether more light follows. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Take heed what you hear: with what measure you mete, it shall be measured to you, and to you that hear shall more be given. Diligent, generous attention is rewarded with increase, and careless hearing is its own loss. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: For he that has, to him shall be given, and he that has not, from him shall be taken even that which he has. Grace used grows, and grace unused withers; gifts neglected are at last withdrawn. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: He said, so is the kingdom of God, as if a man should cast seed into the ground. The kingdom grows by the word sown, not by the sower's watching or management. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: And should sleep and rise night and day, and the seed should spring and grow up, he knows not how. The sower cannot explain or force the growth; God's secret power works while man rests. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: For the earth brings forth fruit of herself: first the blade, then the ear, after that the full corn in the ear. Grace grows gradually and in order, from first stirrings to full ripeness. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: But when the fruit is brought forth, immediately he puts in the sickle, because the harvest is come. Growth has an end in view: harvest, when God gathers what his word has ripened. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: He said, to what shall we liken the kingdom of God, or with what comparison shall we compare it? No single likeness suffices, so Christ multiplies comparisons to show its nature. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: It is like a grain of mustard seed, which, when sown, is less than all seeds in the earth. The kingdom's beginnings are the smallest and most despised, which is no argument against its future. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: But when it is sown, it grows up and becomes greater than all herbs, and shoots out great branches, so that the fowls of the air may lodge under its shadow. From a tiny seed comes shelter for many, and the church that began in obscurity will give rest to nations. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: With many such parables he spoke the word to them, as they were able to hear it. Christ fitted his teaching to his hearers' capacity, giving milk before meat. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: But without a parable he did not speak to them, and when they were alone he expounded all things to his disciples. The crowd heard the veil, the disciples heard the opening, and private exposition belongs to those who follow him home. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: The same day, when evening was come, he said, Let us pass over to the other side. After a day of teaching, Christ orders a crossing, and his word is the disciples' warrant for the voyage. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: When they had sent away the multitude, they took him even as he was in the ship, and other little ships were with him. They took him without preparation, as he was, and lesser boats followed the greater. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

37 COMMENTARY: There arose a great storm of wind, and the waves beat into the ship, so that it was now full. Obedience to Christ's command did not exempt them from storms; the sea rose even on his errand. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

38 COMMENTARY: He was in the hinder part of the ship, asleep on a pillow, and they woke him, saying, Master, do you not care that we perish? His sleep showed his true humanity and his perfect trust, while their cry showed fear that mistook his rest for indifference. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

39 COMMENTARY: He arose, rebuked the wind, and said to the sea, Peace, be still, and the wind ceased, and there was a great calm. The same authority that commanded devils commanded wind and sea, and the calm was as great as the storm. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

40 COMMENTARY: He said to them, Why are you so fearful? How is it that you have no faith? Fear rebukes faith, and faith in a present Christ should have quieted them even before the wind fell. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

41 COMMENTARY: They feared exceedingly, and said to one another, What manner of man is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him? A new, reverent fear replaced their fear of drowning, for they saw in their ship a power greater than nature. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
