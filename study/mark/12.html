Study Notes — Mark

Chapter 12

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: He began to speak to them by parables: A certain man planted a vineyard, set a hedge about it, dug a place for the winefat, built a tower, let it out to husbandmen, and went into a far country. The vineyard is Israel, hedged, equipped, and entrusted to husbandmen, and the owner's departure tests their stewardship. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: At the season he sent to the husbandmen a servant, that he might receive from the husbandmen of the fruit of the vineyard. The season's servant asks only the agreed fruit, for the vineyard's produce belongs to its owner, and prophets come to collect God's due. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: They caught him, beat him, and sent him away empty. The first servant's beating and empty return is the pattern of Israel's treatment of prophets, whose message was answered with violence. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Again he sent another servant, and at him they cast stones, wounded him in the head, and sent him away shamefully handled. A second servant met stoning and shame, for rejection grew bolder with repetition. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Again he sent another, and him they killed, and many others, beating some and killing some. The catalogue rises from beating to killing, and many prophets share the same fate, until patience seems exhausted. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Having yet therefore one son, his wellbeloved, he sent him also last to them, saying, They will reverence my son. The son, last and beloved, is sent in hope that reverence for the heir will succeed where servants failed, and the owner's love is the measure of the mission. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: But those husbandmen said among themselves, This is the heir; come, let us kill him, and the inheritance shall be ours. The heir's coming became the motive for murder, for the husbandmen reasoned that killing the son would make the vineyard theirs. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: They took him, killed him, and cast him out of the vineyard. Killing and casting out repeat the son's fate outside the vineyard, as Christ would be cast out of Jerusalem and killed beyond its walls. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: What shall therefore the lord of the vineyard do? He will come and destroy the husbandmen, and will give the vineyard to others. The question answers itself: destruction for the murderers and transfer to others, for God's vineyard is not left to faithless keepers. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Have you not read this scripture, The stone which the builders rejected is become the head of the corner? The rejected stone becomes the corner, for the builders' judgment is reversed by the Lord, and rejection is the prelude to exaltation. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: This was the Lord's doing, and it is marvellous in our eyes? The exaltation is the Lord's work, not man's, and its marvel lies in reversal: the stone men cast aside holds the building together. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: They sought to lay hold on him, but feared the people, for they knew he had spoken the parable against them, and they left him and went their way. They knew the parable was against them, yet fear of the people, not repentance, stayed their hands, and they left the judge they could not answer. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: They sent to him certain Pharisees and Herodians, to catch him in his words. Pharisees and Herodians, enemies in politics, united to trap him in speech, for a word against Caesar or against God would serve either party. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: When they came, they said, Master, we know you are true, and care for no man, for you regard not the person of men, but teach the way of God in truth: Is it lawful to give tribute to Caesar, or not? Flattery prefaced the trap, praising his truthfulness to bind him to an answer that must offend one side. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Shall we give, or shall we not give? But he, knowing their hypocrisy, said, Why tempt you me? Bring me a penny, that I may see it. He names their hypocrisy and asks for a coin, turning the trap into a lesson the coin itself will teach. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: They brought it, and he said, Whose is this image and superscription? And they said, Caesar's. The image and superscription are Caesar's, and their own coin convicts them of using Caesar's money while disputing Caesar's due. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Jesus answering said, Render to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's, and they marvelled at him. The answer divides the duties without confusing them: Caesar's coin to Caesar, and God's image, man himself, to God, and marvel silenced the trappers. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Then came to him the Sadducees, who say there is no resurrection, and they asked him, saying. Sadducees, denying resurrection, brought a case meant to make resurrection absurd, for their question assumed what it sought to prove. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Master, Moses wrote to us, If a man's brother dies and leaves his wife behind, and leaves no children, his brother should take his wife and raise up seed to his brother. The levirate law is cited correctly, for Moses did command a brother to raise seed, and the Sadducees build their case on it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Now there were seven brothers, and the first took a wife, and dying left no seed. The case is stacked: seven brothers in succession marry one woman, each dying childless, to force an impossible choice. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: The second took her and died, neither left seed, and the third likewise. Each brother in turn repeats the pattern, and the accumulation is meant to bury resurrection under absurdity. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: The seven had her and left no seed; last of all the woman died also. All seven and the woman die, and the Sadducees' question waits on the resurrection they deny. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: In the resurrection therefore, when they shall rise, whose wife shall she be of them? For the seven had her to wife. The question assumes resurrection life repeats earthly marriage, and on that false assumption it seems unanswerable. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Jesus answering said, Do you not therefore err, because you know not the scriptures, neither the power of God? Their error has two roots: ignorance of Scripture and ignorance of God's power, and both together produce the false dilemma. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: For when they shall rise from the dead, they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are as the angels in heaven. Resurrection life is angelic, not earthly: without death there is no need for marriage's succession, and the Sadducees' case dissolves. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: As touching the dead, that they rise: have you not read in the book of Moses, how in the bush God spoke to him, saying, I am the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob? Moses' own book, which Sadducees honoured, calls God the God of patriarchs long dead, and the present tense proves they live. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: He is not the God of the dead, but the God of the living: you therefore do greatly err. A God of the living cannot be God of the extinct, and the Sadducees' error is great because it denies both Scripture and the living God. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: One of the scribes came, and having heard them reasoning together, and perceiving he had answered well, asked him, Which is the first commandment of all? A scribe, pleased with the answer, asks sincerely, seeking the chief commandment among many. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Jesus answered, The first of all the commandments is, Hear, O Israel: The Lord our God is one Lord. The Shema opens the answer: one Lord is the foundation, and all obedience rests on God's unity. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, soul, mind, and strength: this is the first commandment. Love with heart, soul, mind, and strength is total, leaving no faculty unclaimed by God. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: The second is like, namely this, You shall love your neighbour as yourself: there is none other commandment greater than these. The second is like the first, for love to God shows itself in love to neighbours, and no commandment outranks these two. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: The scribe said, Well, Master, you have said the truth, for there is one God, and there is none other but he. The scribe repeats the answer with approval, owning one God and none other, a confession that answers the Shema he quoted. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: To love him with all the heart, understanding, soul, and strength, and to love his neighbour as himself, is more than all whole burnt offerings and sacrifices. The scribe adds the practical conclusion: love outweighs all sacrifice, for offerings without love are empty. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: When Jesus saw that he answered discreetly, he said, You are not far from the kingdom of God, and no man after that dared ask him any question. Discreet answering brought him near the kingdom, though not yet in it, and his question silenced all further traps. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: Jesus answered and said, while he taught in the temple, How say the scribes that Christ is the son of David? Christ now questions the scribes, turning from defence to teaching, and David's sonship is the puzzle they cannot solve. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: For David himself said by the Holy Ghost, The LORD said to my Lord, Sit on my right hand, till I make your enemies your footstool. David, speaking by the Spirit, calls the Messiah Lord, and seats him at God's right hand above his enemies. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

37 COMMENTARY: David therefore himself calls him Lord, and from where is he then his son? And the common people heard him gladly. If David calls him Lord, he is more than David's son, and the common people, unlike the scribes, heard this gladly. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

38 COMMENTARY: He said to them in his doctrine, Beware of the scribes, who love to go in long clothing, and love salutations in the marketplaces. Long clothing and market salutations are the scribes' love of display, and Christ warns his hearers against admiring what he condemns. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

39 COMMENTARY: And the chief seats in the synagogues, and the uppermost rooms at feasts. Chief seats and upper rooms are the same ambition in worship and in society, seeking honour wherever men gather. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

40 COMMENTARY: Who devour widows' houses, and for a pretence make long prayers: these shall receive greater damnation. Devouring widows under cover of long prayers is hypocrisy at its worst, and greater pretence brings greater damnation. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

41 COMMENTARY: Jesus sat over against the treasury, and beheld how the people cast money into the treasury, and many rich cast in much. Sitting over against the treasury, Christ watched giving itself, for the manner and measure of gifts are seen by him. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

42 COMMENTARY: There came a certain poor widow, and she threw in two mites, which make a farthing. Two mites, a farthing's worth, are the smallest gift in the treasury, and the widow's poverty makes it the greatest. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

43 COMMENTARY: He called to him his disciples and said, Verily, this poor widow has cast more in than all they which have cast into the treasury. More is measured not by amount but by proportion, and the widow's farthing outweighs the rich men's much. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

44 COMMENTARY: For all they did cast in of their abundance, but she of her want did cast in all that she had, even all her living. Abundance gives without feeling it, but want giving all its living gives everything, and God counts the all, not the amount. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
