Study Notes — 1 Kings

Chapter 7

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Solomon spent thirteen years building his own house, longer than the temple's seven, a contrast Henry notes without excusing royal magnificence. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: The house of the forest of Lebanon, a hundred cubits long, fifty broad, and thirty high, was named for its forest of cedar pillars. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: It was covered with cedar on forty-five pillars, fifteen in a row, a hall whose pillars made it look like a planted forest. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: The three tiers of windows gave prospect against prospect, the pleasantness of a country retirement that even great princes have prized. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: He made a porch of pillars, fifty cubits long and thirty broad, an entrance hall before the main house. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: The porch of judgment held the king's bench, where causes were adjusted in the king's own presence. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: His wife's house, built of cedar like the porch, stood nearer his palace, yet had it been as near as it ought, Solomon would not have multiplied wives as he did. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: The stones were costly not only for size but for workmanship, finely wrought in all respects, the best of their kind throughout. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: The foundation was of costly great stones, ten and eight cubits, a foundation worthy of the weight above it. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: His own court's pattern followed God's courts, for he liked the model of God's courts and made his own by it. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Solomon sent and fetched Hiram out of Tyre, a craftsman distinct from the king of the same name, to work the temple's brass. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Hiram was a widow's son of Naphtali, his father a Tyrian worker in brass, and he was filled with wisdom and skill for all brass work. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: He cast two brass pillars, eighteen cubits high, twelve cubits round, the great entrance pillars of the temple porch. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: He made two chapiters of molten brass, five cubits high, to crown the pillars, the ornamental heads of the work. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Nets of checker work and wreaths of chain work adorned the chapiters, interwoven ornament befitting their place. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: He made the pillars with two rows of pomegranates round the network to cover the chapiters, fruitfulness carved upon strength. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: The chapiters in the porch were of lily work, four cubits, a flower crown on the pillars at the temple's door. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: The chapiters also had pomegranates, two hundred in rows round about, above the belly by the network, abundant ornament on both pillars. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: He set up the right pillar, Jachin, and the left, Boaz, names meaning he shall establish and in it is strength, a sermon in the porch. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: The pillars stood purely for ornament and significancy: Jachin, he will establish, and Boaz, in him is strength, a memorandum to depend on God only. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: He made a molten sea, ten cubits across and five high, thirty round, the great basin for the priests' washing. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Under its brim were knops, ten in a cubit, cast with the sea, an ornamental band round the whole basin. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: It stood on twelve oxen, three facing each quarter, their hinder parts inward, strength bearing the water of cleansing toward all the world. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: The sea held water for the priests' washing, a sea where the tabernacle had only a laver, intimating the fuller cleansing the gospel makes. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: He made ten bases of brass, four cubits square and three high, movable stands for the lavers of the temple. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: Each base had borders between ledges, a panelled frame that gave the stand both strength and ornament. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: On the borders were lions, oxen, and cherubim, and on the ledges a base of similar work, the creatures of God's dominion carved on the vessels of service. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: Every base had four brass wheels with plates and undersetters at the corners, so the heavy lavers could be moved where needed. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: The mouth within the chapiter was a cubit, round after the manner of a pedestal, a socket to receive the laver above. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: The bases stood on wheels, that the lavers might be removed as there was occasion, though ordinarily they stood in two rows. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: The wheels were like chariot wheels, axletrees, naves, spokes, and felloes all of brass, work fit for the king's house. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: Four undersetters at the corners, cast with the base itself, bore the weight of the laver above them. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: The bases bore lavers for the service of the temple, plentiful provision for cleansing, since those who bear the vessels of the Lord must be very clean. — Matthew Henry

37 COMMENTARY: All ten bases had one casting, one measure, and one size, uniformity marking the order of God's house. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: He made ten lavers of brass, each holding forty baths and four cubits, one upon each base, for washing the sacrifices. — Matthew Henry

40 COMMENTARY: Solomon provided abundance against the future, that when the first vessels wore out, a good stock stood ready for posterity. — Matthew Henry

41 COMMENTARY: The account then lists the two pillars and the bowls of their chapiters, the two networks covering them, the sum of the porch work. — Matthew Henry

42 COMMENTARY: Four hundred pomegranates, two rows for each network, covered the chapiters on the pillars, ornament counted because it was all God's work. — Matthew Henry

43 COMMENTARY: The ten bases and ten lavers upon them are listed again, closing the inventory of the movable vessels. — Matthew Henry

44 COMMENTARY: One sea and twelve oxen under it close the list of the great vessels, the whole set now complete. — Matthew Henry

45 COMMENTARY: All the brazen vessels were of bright brass, the best to be had, for God, who is the best, must be served and honoured with the best. — Matthew Henry

46 COMMENTARY: The king cast them in the plain of Jordan, in clay ground between Succoth and Zarthan, where the soil served the founders' moulds. — Matthew Henry

47 COMMENTARY: The vessels went unweighed because they were exceedingly numerous, and the brass so plentiful that no account was needed: Solomon's care in providing, not carelessness. — Matthew Henry

48 COMMENTARY: All within doors was gold and all made new, except the ark, and the altar of incense still but one, for Christ and his intercession are so. — Matthew Henry

49 COMMENTARY: Ten golden candlesticks spoke of the much greater plenty of heavenly light the gospel gives than the law could afford. — Matthew Henry

50 COMMENTARY: Bowls, snuffers, basins, spoons, and censers of pure gold, and the hinges of gold for the inner and outer doors, completed the golden service. — Matthew Henry

51 COMMENTARY: So all the work Solomon made for the Lord's house was ended, and he brought in David's dedicated silver, gold, and vessels into the temple treasuries. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 5, 11, 36, 39 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
