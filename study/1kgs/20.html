Study Notes — 1 Kings

Chapter 20

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Benhadad gathered his host with thirty-two kings and besieged Samaria — Henry notes the quarrel's ground is untold, but apostasy had made formidable again a Syria David once subdued, and Asa's earlier tempting of Syria into Israel shows how dearly posterity may pay for bringing a foreign force into the country. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: He sent messengers to Ahab in the city, saying, Thus saith Benhadad, a demand delivered as if already granted. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Thy silver and gold is mine, he claimed, and thy wives and children, even the goodliest, are mine, a conqueror's terms before any battle. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Ahab answered, My lord, O king, according to thy saying, I am thine, and all that I have, a surrender born of fear. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: The messengers came again, saying, Although I sent for thy silver, gold, wives, and children, yet tomorrow my servants shall search thy house and thy servants' houses. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: They would take whatever was pleasant in Ahab's eyes, a plunder without limit, worse than the first demand. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Ahab called the elders and asked them to mark how Benhadad sought mischief, owning how he had truckled before — Henry notes he spoke but poorly, and durst not have denied Benhadad at all if his people had not animated him. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: All the elders and all the people said, Hearken not to him, nor consent, the nation refusing a surrender its king had already offered. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Ahab told Benhadad's messengers that all he first sent for he would do, but this second thing he could not do, drawing a line at last. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Benhadad swore by his gods that the dust of Samaria would not suffice for handfuls for all the people following him, boasting before the battle. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Ahab answered, Let not him that girdeth on his harness boast himself as he that putteth it off, a proverb against boasting before victory. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Hearing the message while drinking with the kings in the pavilions, Benhadad ordered his servants to set themselves in array — Henry's moral is that drunkenness was the forerunner of his fall: he preferred pleasure before business and kept his kings drinking when they should have been at their posts. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: A prophet came to Ahab, saying, Hast thou seen this great multitude, behold, I will deliver it into thy hand today, and thou shalt know that I am the Lord. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Ahab asked by whom, and the prophet answered by the young men of the princes, with Ahab himself to order the battle — Henry stresses the instruments were the unlikeliest: pages and footmen, only 232, unacquainted with war, chosen so the excellency of the power might appear to be of God. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: He numbered the young men of the princes, two hundred and thirty-two, and after them all the people, seven thousand, a small army for a great deliverance. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: They went out at noon, while Benhadad drank himself drunk in the pavilions with the thirty-two kings, a drunken camp against a sober attack. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: The young men went out first, and Benhadad's scouts reported men come out of Samaria — Henry notes he despised so small a sally at first, and his drunken order to take them alive rather than kill them gave the attackers their opening. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: He said, Whether they come for peace or war, take them alive, contempt that would cost him the battle. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: So the young men of the princes of the provinces came out of the city, and the army that followed them, the small force leading the attack. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Every man slew his man, the Syrians fled, and Benhadad escaped on horseback — Henry's contrast is the boast undone: where now are the handfuls of Samaria's dust, when the proud man durst not face a sally but mounted, drunk as he was, and made the best of his way? — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: The king of Israel went out, smote the horses and chariots, and slew the Syrians with a great slaughter, completing the victory his small force began. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: The prophet warned Ahab to strengthen himself and mark what he did, for Syria would return at the year's turn — Henry notes Ahab was secure and looked but a little way before him: God had decreed the end, but Ahab must use the means, else he tempts God. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: Syria's servants told Benhadad their gods are gods of the hills, therefore Israel was stronger there, but let us fight against them in the plain, and we shall be stronger. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: They advised him to take the kings away and put captains in their rooms, replacing unreliable allies with professional soldiers. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: He was to number an army like the one lost, horse for horse and chariot for chariot, and fight in the plain, a plan that mistook God's power for geography. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: At the year's return Benhadad numbered the Syrians and went up to Aphek — Henry notes his counsellors chose the flat valley ground deliberately, fancying Jehovah a god of the hills only, who could not come down from them to the valley. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: Israel's force looked like two little flocks of kids against a Syrian host filling the country — Henry stresses the disproportion as deliberate staging: mean equipage and a contemptible figure on one side, numbers, noise, chariots and baggage on the other. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: A man of God told Ahab that because Syria said the Lord is God of the hills but not of the valleys, God would deliver the great multitude into his hand, that Israel might know he is the Lord. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: After seven days facing one another the battle was joined, and Israel slew a hundred thousand footmen in one day — Henry pictures the week before it: the Syrians boasting, the Israelites trembling, until the rout was total. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: The rest fled to Aphek, where a wall fell on twenty-seven thousand, and Benhadad hid in an inner chamber — Henry's moral is that they found their bane where they hoped for protection, and the greatest confidence ended in the greatest cowardice. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: His servants told him Israel's kings are merciful kings, and advised sackcloth on their loins and ropes on their heads, going out to Ahab in submission. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: So they girded sackcloth and ropes and came to Ahab, saying, Thy servant Benhadad saith, I pray thee, let me live, and Ahab said, Is he yet alive, he is my brother. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: The men diligently observed whether anything would come from Ahab, and hastily caught his word brother, saying, Thy brother Benhadad, and Ahab said, Go, bring him, and Benhadad came forth and was caused to come up into the chariot. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: Benhadad promised to restore the cities his father took from Ahab's father, and grant streets in Damascus as his father had in Samaria, and Ahab sent him away with a covenant. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: A son of the prophets bade his neighbour smite him in the Lord's word, and the man refused — Henry thinks the refusal came from a good principle, since good men receive a wrongful blow more easily than give one, yet disobedience to an express command cost him his life by a lion, a warning aimed straight at Ahab. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: Because he had not obeyed the Lord's voice, the prophet said, as soon as thou art departed, a lion shall slay thee, and as soon as he departed, a lion found and slew him. — Matthew Henry

37 COMMENTARY: The prophet found another man and said, Smite me, and the man smote him, wounding him, so that he could appear before the king disguised. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: Disguised with ashes on his face, the prophet waited for the king by the way — Henry explains the disguise was for the parable: wounded as a soldier, he would charge himself with the very crime Ahab had committed in sparing Benhadad, and oblige the king to condemn himself. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: As the king passed, he cried, Thy servant went into the battle, and a man brought a prisoner, saying, Keep this man, if he be missing, thy life shall be for his life, or pay a talent of silver. — Matthew Henry

40 COMMENTARY: When the servant pleaded he had been busy here and there and the prisoner was gone, Ahab answered that his judgment was his own deciding — Henry notes the trap: the prophet then turned it, 'Thou art the man; out of thy own mouth art thou judged,' for Ahab had wittingly dismissed the man God marked for destruction. — Matthew Henry

41 COMMENTARY: The prophet hasted, took the ashes from his face, and Ahab discerned him as one of the prophets, the parable now turned on the king himself. — Matthew Henry

42 COMMENTARY: Thus saith the Lord: because thou hast let go the man appointed to destruction, thy life shall go for his life, and thy people for his people — Henry adds the sting that Ahab did not carelessly lose Benhadad but wittingly dismissed him, and calls it foolish pity: there is a time when keeping back the sword from blood is doing the Lord's work deceitfully. — Matthew Henry

43 COMMENTARY: The king of Israel went to his house heavy and displeased, and came to Samaria, mercy to God's enemy now his own sentence. — Matthew Henry
