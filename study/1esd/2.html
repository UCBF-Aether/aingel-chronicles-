Study Notes — 1 Esdras

Chapter 2

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the Persian empire founded by Cyrus is dated to 536 B.C., and that the reference to Jeremiah's word being fulfilled is a parenthetic statement of the historian, not part of the proclamation itself. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: JFB notes Cyrus's proclamation, though issued in his first year, did not take effect until the year following, and that its phraseology shows he had seen the prophecies foretelling his name and career two hundred years before his birth. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: JFB notes Cyrus's claim to all the kingdoms of the earth, though in the Oriental style of hyperbole, was literally true in that the Persian empire was then the greatest ruling power in the world. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the existence of remarkable predictions led Cyrus to acknowledge that his kingdoms were gifts from the Lord God of heaven, and prompted him to fulfil the duty laid upon him long before his birth, which was the source of the great favor he showed the Jews. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the purport of the edict was to grant full permission to those Jewish exiles in every part of his kingdom who chose to return, and to recommend those who remained to aid the poor and feeble on their way and contribute liberally to rebuilding the temple. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the returning exiles were liberally assisted by multitudes of their captive countrymen who chose to remain, and by their neighbors, whether from favorable disposition toward the Jewish faith or imitation of court policy. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the emigrants were assisted with gifts and goods by neighbors and by countrymen born in Babylonia or comfortably established there by family connections or property. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the paternal and ecclesiastical chiefs of Judah and Benjamin, with some from other tribes who retained attachment to pure worship, naturally took the lead, and their example was followed by all whose piety and patriotism were strong enough to brave the discouragements of the enterprise. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the liberality of neighbors and remaining countrymen toward the emigrants displayed hearty good will and great liberality in aiding and promoting the views of those departing. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: JFB notes that though it is said the vessels were cut in pieces, that would not be done to the large and magnificent vases, and the Hebrew word may signify merely cut off, that is, from further use in the temple. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: JFB identifies Shesh-bazzar, the prince of Judah, as Zerubbabel son of Salathiel, born in Babylon, whose court name signified fire-worshipper, and who was recognized among the exiles as hereditary prince of Judah. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: JFB notes Shesh-bazzar, that is Zerubbabel, received the vessels from the treasurer and was recognized as hereditary prince of Judah among the exiles. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the vessels here specified amount only to 2,499, so it is probable only the larger vases are mentioned, while the inventory of the whole, including great and small, came to the gross sum stated. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the gross sum of the vessels covers great and small together, while the itemized list names only the larger vases. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: JFB notes not all exiles embraced the privilege granted, since the great proportion, born in Babylon, preferred their comfortable homes to a distant and hazardous journey, and the returning exiles did not all go at once, the first band going with Zerubbabel and others later with Ezra and Nehemiah. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the officers named were supposed to be deputy governors appointed by the Persian king over the provinces west of the Euphrates, and that their letter aimed to press upon the royal notice the inexpediency and danger of rebuilding the walls of Jerusalem. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the three officers were deputy governors over the provinces subject to Persia west of the Euphrates, writing in the Syrian, or Aramaean, language used by the Persians in decrees and communications relative to the Jews. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the object of the letter was to prejudice the king's mind against rebuilding the walls, laboring hard to make the measure appear inexpedient and dangerous. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: JFB defines the three revenues at stake as a poll tax, a property tax, and excise dues on articles of trade, and notes the letter and the edict that followed, commanding an immediate cessation of the work, form the exclusive subject of this narrative. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: JFB explains the writers' plea in terms of the Oriental phrase of eating a prince's salt, equivalent to receiving maintenance from him, which bound them in loyalty to warn the king of danger to his interests. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the writers appealed to their maintenance from the king's palace as the ground of their loyalty, and sought a search of the royal records to prejudice the king against the rebuilding. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the letter and the edict that followed commanded an immediate cessation of the work at the city walls, and that the historian then returns from this digression to resume the thread concerning the building of the temple. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the edict commanding cessation of the work was the exclusive subject of the letter and its answer, and that the stoppage first gave rise to the strong religious antipathy between Jews and Samaritans. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the work of the house of God then ceased, an occurrence that first gave rise to the strong religious antipathy between the Jews and the Samaritans, later aggravated by the rival temple on Mount Gerizim. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

No note for verse(s) 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
