Study Notes — 1 Esdras

Chapter 5

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Bissell notes this departure follows the contest victory, in which the third speaker secures the king's consent to the return of the Jews, so that the principal men of the families are now chosen to go up with their households and cattle. — E. C. Bissell

2 COMMENTARY: Bissell notes the Authorized Version's phrasing here is safely, and with musical instruments, tabrets and flutes, and that he reconstructed the sentence after Fritzsche's text, with the brethren playing as the company went up. — E. C. Bissell

3 COMMENTARY: Bissell notes all their brethren played with music as the company went up together, the same musical escort described in the preceding verse. — E. C. Bissell

4 COMMENTARY: Bissell flags the displacement in the book's arrangement, since this register of names belongs to the families that returned in the time of Cyrus, though it is placed here after the Darius episode. — E. C. Bissell

5 COMMENTARY: JFB identifies Jeshua the high priest and Zerubbabel as the leaders of the first band of returning exiles, whose register this list preserves, with the names extracted probably from the Persian archives. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Bissell notes the wise sentences spoken before Darius are the contest speeches themselves, and records Fritzsche's supposition that the original hero of that portion was not Zerubbabel but his son Joakim, whose name appears here among the leaders. — E. C. Bissell

7 COMMENTARY: JFB explains the returned exiles are called children of the province, that is, Judea, now reduced from an illustrious kingdom to an obscure tributary province of Persia, and that each returned to his own city, whether his ancestors' city or one newly rebuilt and allotted to him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: JFB notes Zerubbabel was the chief leader of the first band of returning exiles, with Jeshua the high priest and Nehemiah conspicuous among those associated in conducting the caravans. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the word children, used throughout this catalogue, means posterity or descendants, so each entry names a family and its number. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: JFB notes it is pleasant to see so many of Anathoth returning, since that Levite city had spurned Jeremiah's warning and been laid waste in the Assyrian conquest, but its people, brought to a better state of mind during captivity, returned and rebuilt their city. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: JFB notes each priestly family was ranged under its prince or head, as the whole body was divided into twenty-four courses, and that only four of the courses returned from Babylon, afterwards divided again into twenty-four under the original course names appointed by David. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: JFB explains the children of Solomon's servants as either the strangers Solomon enlisted in building the temple or those who lived in his palace, which was deemed a high honor. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

38 COMMENTARY: JFB notes Barzillai preferred that name to his own family name, deeming connection with so noble a family a greater distinction than the house of Levi, but by this worldly ambition he forfeited the dignity and advantages of the priesthood. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

39 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the forfeiture of priestly dignity followed from preferring a noble secular name to Levitical descent, which is why those whose kindred could not be found in the register were removed from executing the priesthood. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

40 COMMENTARY: JFB explains Tirshatha as a title borne by the Persian governors of Judea, derived from the Persian word for severe, and equivalent to your severity or your awfulness. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

41 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the gross total of 42,360 is twelve thousand more than the particular numbers in the catalogue amount to, and explains the deficiency as those who did not belong to Judah and Benjamin or to the priests, but to the other tribes. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

42 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the servants and singers, male and female, are reckoned separately from the congregation, so that putting all items together the company that went with Zerubbabel amounted to fifty thousand, with eight thousand beasts of burden. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

43 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the beasts of burden are counted with the whole caravan, amounting to eight thousand, alongside the fifty thousand persons who went up with Zerubbabel. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

44 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the sight of a place hallowed by sacred associations but now lying in desolation made the wellsprings of the chiefs' piety and patriotism gush out afresh, so that before providing for themselves they raised a large sum by voluntary contributions toward restoring the temple. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

45 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the gifts of gold coin, here darics, a Persian coin, and of priests' garments were very appropriate in the circumstances, and that presents of garments or usable commodities accord with established Eastern custom. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

46 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the exiles came again unto Jerusalem and Judah, every one unto his city, settling in Jerusalem, in the country, and in their villages as the work of restoration began. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

47 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the returning exiles, after preliminary work rearing habitations amid the ruins, addressed themselves to rebuilding the altar of burnt offering as the seventh month approached, resolving to celebrate the feast of tabernacles just as if the temple had been fully restored. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

48 COMMENTARY: JFB identifies Jeshua as the grandson of Seraiah, the high priest put to death at Riblah, and Zerubbabel as son of Pedaiah by nature but called Salathiel's son because brought up by him, and notes the altar was built of urgent necessity for atonement, blessing, and animating piety and patriotism for the national work. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

49 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the altar was built to offer burnt offerings for atonement and to obtain divine blessing on the preparations for the temple, animating the people's piety and patriotism for the national work. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

50 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the altar was reared upon its old foundation, occupying as nearly as possible its former site, and that the people resumed the daily service prescribed by the law without waiting till the temple should be rebuilt. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

51 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the feast of tabernacles was held on the fifteenth day of the seventh month, with the daily oblation revived at that time. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

52 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the daily oblation and the annual seasons of solemn observance were revived together, including the continual offerings and the sacrifices of sabbaths, new moons, and holy feasts. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

53 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the people did not wait till the temple should be rebuilt, but at the outset resumed the daily service prescribed by the law, offering vows from the first day of the seventh month. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

55 COMMENTARY: JFB notes negotiations were opened with the Tyrians for workmen as well as timber, on the same terms and with the same views as Solomon had done. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

58 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the Levites were appointed to set forward the work, that is, to act as overseers of the workmen and to direct and animate the laborers in the various departments. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

63 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the painful emotions of the aged who had seen the first temple were excited by the contrast between its prosperous founding and the desolate, reduced state of the country, the inferior stones and smaller foundation, and above all the absence of the ark, the shekinah, and the Urim and Thummim, though the second temple's glory would outshine the first by receiving the incarnate Savior within its walls. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

64 COMMENTARY: JFB notes that among Eastern people expressions of sorrow are always very loud and vehement, so that the wailing was sometimes not easily distinguishable from joyful acclamations. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

65 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the shout of joy and the noise of weeping mingled so that the people could not discern one from the other, since Eastern expressions of sorrow are loud and vehement. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

66 COMMENTARY: JFB identifies the adversaries of Judah and Benjamin as strangers settled in the land of Israel, whose hearing of the noise prompted their inquiry. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

67 COMMENTARY: JFB identifies the adversaries as the strangers settled in the land, who perceived the returned captives were building the temple unto the Lord God of Israel. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

68 COMMENTARY: JFB explains the offer to build together came from Assyrian settlers whose descendants, a mongrel race called Samaritans, claimed to seek God but served him in a superstitious way of their own, a claim strikingly confirmed by Esar-haddon's annals of deportation and colonization. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

69 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the settlers' claim rests on Esar-haddon's deportation of Israelites and settlement of Babylonian colonists in their place, as inscribed on a cylinder in the British Museum, and that though instructed in the knowledge of God they served him superstitiously. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

70 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the refusal to cooperate with the Samaritans, from whatever motives it sprang, was overruled by Providence for ultimate good, since working together would have brought familiar acquaintanceship and intermarriage, a relapse into idolatry, and confusion in the genealogical evidence proving the descent of the Messiah. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

71 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the refusal rested on the commission given to the Jews alone, and that in their hostile and separate condition the two peoples jealously watched each other's proceedings, guarding the purity and honor of Mosaic worship and the integrity of the sacred books. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

72 COMMENTARY: JFB notes that exasperated by this repulse, the Samaritans endeavored by every means to molest the workmen and obstruct the building, and by bribes and clandestine arts at court labored to frustrate the effects of Cyrus's edict. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

73 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the Samaritans' underhand dealings succeeded greatly, since Cyrus, frequently absent and absorbed in war, left government to Cambyses and then Smerdis, so that for a long period the progress of the work was very slow until the time of Darius. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
