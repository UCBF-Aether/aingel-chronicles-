Study Notes — 1 Esdras

Chapter 1

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the day appointed by the law was kept on this occasion, with the priests ranged in their courses and exhorted to be ready in the manner legal purity required. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: JFB explains the priests were set in their courses for the feast, and Bissell notes their long garments were their official costume. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown; E. C. Bissell

3 COMMENTARY: JFB explains the Levites, as ministers and instructors of the people in divine worship, were commanded to restore the holy ark to Solomon's house, whether it had been put away by an idolatrous king or removed during temple repairs. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: JFB notes each course was composed of those belonging to the same fathers' house, and that since worship was now fixed in a permanent temple the Levites were no longer required to bear the ark on their shoulders. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: JFB explains the Levites stood in the court of the priests where the victims were killed, with the people admitted by families in companies, while the Levites handed the blood and fat from one to another to the officiating priests. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the minute directions were meant to facilitate distribution of the paschal lambs, especially providing for the many reduced to poverty by the Assyrian devastations, so the lambs could be delivered in order to each family. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the lambs and kids were in all probability destined for the poor, since a lamb or a kid might be used at convenience, while the bullocks were offered after the lambs on each successive day of the feast. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: JFB identifies these temple governors as ecclesiastical princes, including Hilkiah the high priest, and notes their gifts to the priests followed the pattern of Hezekiah's princes. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: JFB notes that as the Levitical tribes were not yet sufficiently provided, eminent Levites distinguished in Hezekiah's time gave a large additional contribution for the use of the Levites exclusively. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the Levites took a prominent part in killing and stripping the sacrifices, properly priestly duties, because the priests could not overtake the extraordinary amount of work and the Levites had been duly sanctified for the service. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: JFB notes that once all preparations were complete and the appointed time arrived, the solemnity was celebrated with the Levites enlisted for the time in priestly employment because of the multitudes engaged. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: JFB explains the small cattle designed for burnt offerings were put apart from the paschal lambs, which were carefully selected and intended to be sacramentally eaten, and that the passover was roasted with fire according to the ordinance while other offerings were cooked in pots and pans. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the portions were divided speedily among the people, either because of the multiplicity of the priests' business or to preserve the heat and flavor of the viands, and that the meal included the meat of the thank offerings as well as the paschal lambs. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the Levites rendered this aid to the priests solely because the priests were so engrossed the entire day that they had no leisure to provide refreshments for themselves. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the choir sang Psalms 113 to 118, once, twice, and even a third time during each company of offerers, and since they could not leave their posts the Levites prepared food for them as the bands relieved each other in turn. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: JFB notes what distinguished this passover above all preceding solemnities was not grandeur or concourse but the ardent devotion of king and people and unusually strict adherence, even in the smallest minutiae, to the forms prescribed in the book of the law. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the reference to Samuel means the same as the reference in Kings to the days of the judges, since Samuel concluded the era of the judges, and that though most of the northern kingdom was in exile, some remaining inhabitants journeyed to Jerusalem for the feast. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: JFB notes that if the chronology rests on an historical basis, all the events from the temple repairs to this passover happened in about five and a half months, and that Josiah's eighteenth year was reckoned from the autumn. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: JFB explains Josiah likely calculated that restored worship and revived religion would bring settled peace and prosperity, but the bright interval was brief, not from unfaithfulness in the divine promise but from the national apostasy drawing down long-threatened judgments. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: JFB notes Necho informed Josiah he had no intention of molesting the Jews, since his expedition was directed solely against his old Assyrian enemy, and that commentators disagree whether his claimed divine commission came through Jeremiah or was merely an appeal to authority Josiah would respect. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: JFB notes that as Josiah could not know the truth of Necho's declaration, he did not sin in opposing him, or if he sinned at all it was a sin of ignorance, and that he disregarded the warning and joined battle. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: JFB notes Josiah, as a faithful vassal of Assyria, resolved to oppose Necho's march across the northern country, and that the armies met in the valley of Megiddo, the plain of Esdraelon, where Josiah could most conveniently intercept the Egyptian passage. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the engagement took place and Josiah was mortally wounded, with the Egyptian army striking across the country past Megiddo. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the wounded king was taken out of his war chariot and put into his second chariot, the carriage for ordinary use, which would be more comfortable for the royal sufferer than the war chariot. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the second chariot carried the suffering king back toward Jerusalem, and that the death of this good king was the subject of universal and lasting regret. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: JFB notes Jeremiah's elegy for Josiah has not reached us, but it seems to have been long preserved and chanted on public occasions, and the lamentation at Hadad-rimmon in the valley of Megiddo passed into a proverbial phrase for any great and extraordinary sorrow. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the prophet's lament was preserved among the people and sung by professional singers, probably from a collection of funeral odes composed on the death of good and great men of the nation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the people raised Shallum, afterwards called Jehoahaz, to the throne in preference to his older brother Eliakim, and that the royal anointing, not usually necessary in regular succession, was resorted to in order to impart greater validity to the popular election. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: JFB notes Jehoahaz's power lasted only three months, since Necho, deeming it expedient to have a king of his own nomination on the throne, deposed the popularly elected monarch and placed his brother Eliakim, or Jehoiakim, there as an obsequious vassal. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the tribute of a hundred talents of silver and a talent of gold amounted to about a hundred and ninety-five thousand dollars by their reckoning. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

37 COMMENTARY: JFB notes Necho laid a tribute on the country, raised Eliakim, or Jehoiakim, as his vassal to the throne, and on his departure brought Jehoahaz captive with him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

39 COMMENTARY: JFB notes Jehoiakim followed the course of his idolatrous predecessors, and that as the deputy of the king of Egypt he departed further than his predecessor from Josiah's principles, grinding exactions from his subjects to meet his master's cupidity. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

40 COMMENTARY: JFB explains this refers to Nebuchadnezzar's first expedition, when as joint sovereign with his infirm father he defeated the Egyptians at Carchemish, reduced the provinces west of the Euphrates, and made Jehoiakim a vassal who was bound in chains. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

41 COMMENTARY: JFB notes Nebuchadnezzar took Jerusalem, carried away a portion of the sacred vessels, perhaps in lieu of unpaid tribute, and deposited them in the temple of his god Belus at Babylon. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

43 COMMENTARY: JFB notes Jehoiachin, also called Jeconiah or Coniah, was eighteen rather than eight years old, as appears from Kings and from the full development of his ungodly principles, and that his appointment by the people gave umbrage to Nebuchadnezzar. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

44 COMMENTARY: JFB notes Jehoiachin's short reign cannot be considered at variance with the prophetic denunciation against his father, and that his ungodly principles were already fully developed. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

45 COMMENTARY: JFB notes that when the year was expired, in the spring when campaigns usually began, Nebuchadnezzar came in person against Jerusalem, captured the city, sent Jehoiachin in chains to Babylon, and pillaged the remaining treasures of temple and palace. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

46 COMMENTARY: JFB notes Nebuchadnezzar appointed Zedekiah, whose name was originally Mattaniah and was changed according to the custom of Oriental conquerors, and that though son of Josiah he is called Jehoiachin's brother in the latitude of Hebrew style for a kinsman. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

47 COMMENTARY: JFB notes Zedekiah's own pride and obdurate impiety, the incurable idolatry of the nation, and their reckless disregard of prophetic warnings brought down the long-threatened judgments of God. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

48 COMMENTARY: JFB notes Zedekiah received his crown on the express condition of a solemn oath of fealty to Babylon, so his revolt by joining a league with Pharaoh-hophra involved the crime of perjury. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

49 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the incurable idolatry of the nation and reckless disregard of prophetic warnings brought down judgment, with Nebuchadnezzar as the executioner of divine vengeance. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

52 COMMENTARY: JFB notes Nebuchadnezzar commenced a third siege of Jerusalem, which after holding out for a year and a half was taken in the eleventh year of Zedekiah, resulting in the burning of the temple and the overthrow of the kingdom of Judah. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

55 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the siege ended in the burning of the temple, with most probably the ark, and the overthrow of the kingdom of Judah. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

57 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the land was to enjoy her sabbaths after the divine institution of the sabbatic year had been neglected, and that Jeremiah's prophecy told it would remain desolate seventy years in divine retribution. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

58 COMMENTARY: JFB notes the sabbatic rest of the land, neglected for so long, was now enforced in desolation for seventy years, as Moses and Jeremiah had foretold, and that leaving Palestine uncolonized, contrary to Assyrian custom, must be ascribed to the overruling providence of God. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

No note for verse(s) 16, 17, 18, 19, 23, 24, 38, 42, 50, 51, 53, 54, 56 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
