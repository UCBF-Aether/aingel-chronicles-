Study Notes — 1 Esdras

Chapter 3

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Bissell notes the Greek for making a great feast is exactly reproduced in Luke 5:29, and that the household servants are literally those born in the house. — E. C. Bissell

2 COMMENTARY: Bissell compares the extent of the empire, from India unto Ethiopia, with the same formula in Esther 1:1 and 8:9 and in Daniel 6:1. — E. C. Bissell

3 COMMENTARY: Bissell notes the Greek word may mean either awoke or slept profoundly, though awoke is the natural and usual meaning, and that Josephus gives the narrative quite a different coloring, making the contest turn on an oration agreeable to truth and wisdom and attributing to Darius a prior vow to return the vessels of God to Jerusalem. — E. C. Bissell

4 COMMENTARY: Bissell notes this debate before Darius is the book's only independent portion, in which three young men of the king's body-guard dispute the question of what is mightiest, and that it became the compiler's principal point of attraction. — E. C. Bissell

5 COMMENTARY: Bissell notes the comparative here is used for the superlative, so that wiser than the other means wisest of all, and the victor was to receive great gifts in token of victory. — E. C. Bissell

7 COMMENTARY: Bissell notes being called the king's kinsman was simply an honorary title, as in the Maccabean books where the same title is bestowed as a mark of royal favor. — E. C. Bissell

9 COMMENTARY: Bissell notes the three princes of Persia correspond to the leading princely families of Persia, whose heads were supposed to form the king's council, and that three may have been selected here with reference to the number of contestants. — E. C. Bissell

12 COMMENTARY: Bissell notes this contest is the book's only independent portion, and its verdict, that truth bears away the victory above all things, is what secures the third speaker's victory and, with it, the king's consent to the Jews' return. — E. C. Bissell

14 COMMENTARY: Bissell notes the chief officers are the hypatoi, a term used by Latin writers to designate consuls and prefects, and that one critic thinks the term furnishes a key to the time of the book's composition. — E. C. Bissell

18 COMMENTARY: Bissell connects the wording with the pointing of the Greek text, so that wine seduces or deceives the mind of every man who drinks it. — E. C. Bissell

19 COMMENTARY: Bissell notes the fatherless child is literally the orphan, used figuratively for what is weak, in contradistinction to the king, so that wine levels the strongest and the weakest minds alike. — E. C. Bissell

20 COMMENTARY: Bissell compares the sentiment of the verse with Psalm 104:15, Ecclesiastes 10:19, Wisdom 2:9, and Ecclesiasticus 13:8, on wine gladdening the heart and turning thought to mirth. — E. C. Bissell

21 COMMENTARY: Bissell explains the phrase about speaking by talents to mean speaking as though one had to do only with talents, that is, talking like a millionaire, as Wahl renders it. — E. C. Bissell

23 COMMENTARY: Bissell explains the phrase about being from the wine as rising from the sleep produced by wine, that is, sobering up after drinking. — E. C. Bissell

No note for verse(s) 6, 8, 10, 11, 13, 15, 16, 17, 22, 24 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
