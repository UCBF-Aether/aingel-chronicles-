Study Notes — 1 Corinthians

Chapter 13

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: This chapter is the New Testament psalm of love. Though Paul speaks with the tongues of men and of angels — with the eloquence admired at Corinth and command of various languages, abused there for mere ostentation — if he has not charity, the principle of the ordinary and more important gifts of the Spirit, he is only sounding brass and a tinkling cymbal: sound without soul or feeling, sharp and piercing but empty. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Though he has prophecy and understands all mysteries — the deep counsels of God hitherto secret but now revealed — and all knowledge of truths long known, and faith that could remove mountains, the practical power of the will elevated by faith, yet without love these are not what they seem and fail of the heavenly reward. Paul, who teaches justification by faith only, thus agrees with James: by works — that is, by love, the spirit of faith — a man is justified, and not by faith only. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Though he dole out in food all his goods to the poor, one of the highest functions of helps, and give his body to be burned, as the three youths yielded their bodies, yet without love he is profited nothing. These are noble exemplifications of love in giving and suffering, but they may be without love, in which case goods and body are given but not the soul, which is love's sphere; without the soul God rejects all else. Men will fight and die for Christianity but not live in its spirit, which is love. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Love suffers long under provocations of evil from others — its negative side — and is kind, extending good to others — its positive side, matching the wisdom from above. It does not envy, including jealousy, does not vaunt itself in words even of gifts it really possesses, an indirect rebuke of display with tongues, and is not puffed up with party zeal. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Love does not behave itself unseemly — it is not uncourteous or inattentive to civility and propriety. It seeks not its own, and thinks no evil: it imputes not the evil that actually is there, but makes allowances for others' falls, puts a charitable construction on them, excuses the evil inflicted on it, does not meditate on evil done by another, and in doubtful cases takes the more charitable view. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Love does not rejoice in iniquity but rejoices with the truth — it exults not at others' perpetration of unrighteousness but sympathizes with truth in its triumphs. Truth is the gospel truth, the inseparable ally of love, and the false charity that compromises truth by glossing over iniquity is tacitly condemned. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Love bears all things without speaking of what it bears, enduring without divulging personal distress, holding fast like a watertight vessel and containing itself in silence under hardship. It believes all things, unsuspiciously believing all that is not palpably false to another's credit; it hopes what is good of another even when others have ceased to hope; and it endures persecutions in a patient and loving spirit. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Love never fails — it is never out of use and always holds its place. But prophecies shall be done away with and knowledge shall be done away with, superseded at the Lord's coming by more perfect heavenly analogues, knowledge by intuition, while tongues, still more temporary, shall cease. A primary fulfillment came when the church attained maturity and the collected New Testament Scriptures and ordinary preaching made the supernatural gifts no longer required. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: For we know in part and we prophesy in part — partially and imperfectly, in contrast to the perfect man, the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ. Our present knowledge and prophecy are fragmentary. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: But when that which is perfect is come, then that which is in part — fragmentary and isolated — shall be done away. The partial gives way to the complete. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: When Paul was a child, he spoke as a child, alluding to tongues; he understood as a child, alluding to prophecy; he thought, or reasoned, as a child, alluding to knowledge. Now that he has become a man, he has done away with childish things — the gifts of the present age belong to spiritual childhood beside the maturity to come. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Now we see through a glass darkly — in a mirror, in enigma. Ancient mirrors of polished brass gave only a dim reflected image, and an enigma conveys only a shadow to the ear; neither eye nor ear can fully represent the things God has prepared for those who love him. Even the revealed word now is a dark discourse beside direct vision. Then we shall see face to face, and know fully even as we are fully known — now we are known by God rather than knowing him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: But now, the case being so, there abide faith, hope, and charity, these three — while prophecy, tongues, and knowledge fail, these three abide permanently as necessary and sufficient for salvation at all times. Faith is toward God, hope is on our own behalf, and charity is love to God creating love toward our neighbor. In one sense faith and hope will be done away, superseded by sight and fruition, but in another sense they abide even hereafter as trust in God and anticipation of ever new joys; love alone in every sense abides, and is therefore the greatest, since it presupposes faith, which without love and its works is dead. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
