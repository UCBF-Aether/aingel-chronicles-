Study Notes — 1 Corinthians

Chapter 12

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Concerning spiritual gifts — the signs of the Spirit's continued presence in the church, Christ's body as the body complements the head — Paul would not have them ignorant, despite their boasts of knowledge. He sets forth in order the unity of the body, the variety of its members and functions, the grand principle of love for rightly exercising the gifts, and then the comparison of gifts with one another. Ordinary and extraordinary gifts alike flow from the indwelling Spirit, and extraordinary gifts did not even prove their possessors were in a safe state. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: When they were Gentiles, they were carried away to dumb idols, blindly transported at the will of false guides, led at random without any definite principle, as heathen oracles led their votaries. This past explains why they needed instruction about spiritual gifts: when blind, they went to the dumb, in contrast to the living God who speaks in the believer by his Spirit. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: The test of inspiration is the rejection or confession of Jesus as Lord. No one speaking in the Spirit of God calls Jesus accursed, as Jews and Gentiles treated him, and no one can say Jesus is Lord except in the Holy Spirit — 'say' meaning a spiritual and believing confession of the historical, living God-man, acknowledging oneself his servant, since 'Lord' is the Septuagint's rendering of Jehovah. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: There are diversities of gifts — varieties of spiritual endowments peculiar to the several members — but the same Spirit. The Holy Trinity appears in the passage: the Spirit here, Christ in the ministrations, and the Father in the operations, with gifts, administrations, and operations corresponding respectively to the Three. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: There are differences of administrations, the various functions and services performed by those having the gifts, but the same Lord, whom the Spirit glorifies by these ministrations. Gifts, administrations, and operations form an ascending climax from endowment to service to effect. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: There are diversities of operations, the actual effects resulting from gifts and administrations through the universally operative power of the one Father, who works all in all — all the gifts in all the persons who possess them, by his Spirit working. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: The manifestation of the Spirit is given to each member severally, varying in each individual though all gifts flow from the one God, Lord, and Spirit, and it is given with a view to the profit of the whole body, not of the individual alone. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: To one is given by the Spirit the word of wisdom — ready utterance of wisdom, new revelations of divine wisdom in redemption, as contrasted with human philosophy — and to another the word of knowledge, ready utterance supernaturally imparted of truths already revealed. Wisdom penetrates deeper than knowledge and belongs to prophets, while knowledge belongs to teachers and relates to things to be done. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: To another is given faith by the same Spirit — not faith in doctrines but miracle-faith, confidence by the Spirit's impulse that God would enable the performance of any required miracle — and to another gifts of healing, in the plural, for different kinds of disease needing different kinds of healing. Saving faith differs in object, not in principle, from this faith: both rely on God, and saving faith saves by the merits of its object, not its own merit. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: To another the working of miracles of special and extraordinary power, such as raising the dead or inflicting death by a word, distinct from healings that might in time be effected by human skill. To another prophecy, here the inspired disclosure of the future, depending on faith and ranking next to apostles. To another discerning of spirits, distinguishing God's Spirit from an evil spirit or unaided human spirit. To another diverse kinds of tongues, various languages and also a spiritual language uttered in ecstasy, and to another the interpretation of tongues. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: All these are worked by one and the same Spirit, dividing to every man severally as he will. The distribution is sovereign, not according to human preference. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: As the body, though one, has many members, and all the members of that one body, being many, are one body, so also is Christ — the whole Christ, head and body. Unity, not unvarying uniformity, is the law of God in grace as in nature; the many members compose an organic whole and none can be dispensed with. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: By one Spirit we are all baptized into one body, whether Jews or Greeks, bond or free, and all made to drink of one Spirit. Baptism's designed effect is realized when not frustrated by man's unfaithfulness, and the drinking of one Spirit alludes indirectly to the Lord's Supper, as Spirit, water, and blood combine the two outward signs with the inward grace signified. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: The body is not one member but many, and the analogy illustrates the mutual dependence of the various members in the one body, the church — like the fable of the belly and the members told by Menenius Agrippa to the seceding commons. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: If the foot says, 'Because I am not the hand, I am not of the body,' it is not therefore not of the body. The humbler members ought not to disparage themselves: the foot and hand represent active life, and each compares himself with those nearest him in gifts, not those far superior. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: If the ear says, 'Because I am not the eye, I am not of the body,' it is not therefore not of the body. The ear and eye represent contemplative life, and the ear speaks of the nobler eye as the foot speaks of the hand. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: If the whole body were an eye, where would the hearing be? If the whole were hearing, where would the smelling be? Superior as the eye is, it would not do if it were the sole member to the exclusion of the rest. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: But now God has set the members, every one of them, in the body as it has pleased him. Each member is severally placed by God's own appointment, not by self-selection. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: If all were one member, where would the body be? A body by its very idea has many members, so oneness of member would destroy the body itself. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: But now there are many members, yet one body — many mutually dependent members, not a heap of disconnected parts. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: The eye cannot say to the hand, 'I have no need of you,' nor the head to the feet, 'I have no need of you.' The higher cannot dispense with the lower members. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Those members of the body which seem more feeble — more susceptible of injury, like the brain, belly, and eye — are necessary. Their very feebleness calls forth greater care for their preservation, as being felt necessary. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Those members we think less honorable, we bestow more abundant honor on — putting shoes on the feet and clothes to cover the belly — and our uncomely parts have more abundant comeliness, since even the poorest cover those secret parts though unclad elsewhere. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Our comely parts have no need of such honor, but God has tempered the body together on the principle of mutual compensation, giving more abundant honor to the part that lacked, the deficient part. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: This tempering is so that there should be no schism in the body — no disunion, referring to the divisions at Corinth — but that the members should have the same care one for another, in behalf of one another. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Whether one member suffers, all the members suffer with it — when a thorn enters the heel the whole body feels it, the back bends, the hands come forward to draw it out, the head stoops and the eyes gaze intently. Or if one member is honored, all the members rejoice with it, as when the head is crowned the whole man feels honored. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: You are the body of Christ, and members in particular — severally members of it. Each church is in miniature what the whole aggregate of churches is collectively, the body of Christ, and its components are members each in his assigned place. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: God has set some in the church, first apostles — above even the prophets, and not merely the Twelve, but others such as Barnabas — secondarily prophets, thirdly teachers, who taught truths already revealed while prophets made new revelations, after that miracles, ranked below teachers since teaching is more edifying though less dazzling, then gifts of healings, helps, governments, and diversities of tongues. Helps are lower ministrations, such as deacons relieving the poor and helpers with time and means; governments are higher, presbyters or bishops guiding the helm of the church, ranked with lower functions despite outward status because occupied with external things. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Are all apostles? Are all prophets? Are all teachers? Are all workers of miracles? Surely not. The rhetorical questions establish that gifts are distributed severally, not universally. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: Have all the gifts of healing? Do all speak with tongues? Do all interpret? Surely not — the same distribution holds for the remaining gifts, and no member holds them all. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: Covet earnestly the best — the greatest — gifts, emulously desiring them by prayer, watchfulness, and cultivation of faculties, not in discontented coveting, since the Spirit divides as he will. And yet, besides this, Paul will show a still more excellent way to desire: the way of love, which includes faith and hope and bears the Spirit's fruits, and is more to be desired than gifts. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
