Study Notes — 1 Corinthians

Chapter 6

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Going to law before the unjust — Gentile judges, so called because, in the highest view of justice with regard to God as Supreme Judge, they do not regard him as its fountain — is treason against Christian brotherhood. Jews abroad were permitted Jewish arbitrators, and Christians likewise were allowed Christian arbitrators, rather than heathen courts. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Do you not know, as a truth universally recognized by Christians, that the saints will judge the world — that is, rule as assessors of Christ, administering the kingdom under him? The weightiest earthly questions are infinitely small compared with those to be decided on judgment day, so the saints are surely competent for the smallest matters between brethren. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: The saints will even judge angels, namely the bad angels, joining in approving the Judge's final sentence on them, and as administrators under Jesus putting down all rule hostile to God. Those now a spectacle to angels will then judge angels. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: If you have cases for judgment about this life, set them before those least esteemed in the church — anyone, however low in the church, rather than the heathen. Questions of earthly property are secondary in true Christians' eyes and may be delegated to those in a secondary position. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Paul says this to their shame, to check their puffed-up spirit. Is it so that not one wise man among so many reputed for wisdom can judge between brother and brother? Better even the least esteemed judge than litigation before the heathen; the arbitrator should be chosen from the church body, one gifted for church government. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: But, emphatically answering that question in the negative, brother goes to law with brother, and that before unbelievers. The fact itself answers Paul's challenge: there was no wise judge used because they would not use one. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Going to law at all is already a shortcoming, a falling short of their high privileges, and doing so before unbelievers aggravates it. Rather than litigate, they should suffer themselves to be wronged and defrauded, as Christ taught. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Instead of bearing wrong, the Corinthians themselves did wrong and defrauded their brethren — doing wrong by taking away, and defrauding by retaining what was entrusted to them. They not only refused to bear wrongs but inflicted them, contrary to their Lord's command to return good for evil. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Doers of wrong will not inherit the kingdom of God, which is a kingdom of righteousness. The list that follows names the wrongdoers, beginning with fornicators — recalling the Corinthian scandal — and the effeminate, those who submit to unnatural lusts. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: The exclusion continues: neither thieves, nor covetous, nor drunkards, nor revilers, nor extortioners will inherit the kingdom of God. The list of doers of wrong from the preceding verse is completed here, all barred from the kingdom of righteousness. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Such were some of the Corinthians, but now they are washed — they have had themselves washed, admitted to the benefits of Christ's salvation — sanctified, set apart from the world and adopted into the church, and justified from condemnation through God's righteousness in Christ by faith. Washing precedes both and points to the outward new birth of water, the sign of the inward setting apart by the Spirit, all in the name of Jesus and by the indwelling Spirit of their common God. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Paul's own words, that all things are lawful, had been made a pretext for excusing idol-meats and even fornication, as if bodily appetites proved the lawfulness of gratifying them. But his remark referred only to things indifferent. The wordplay is pointed: all things are in my power, but I will not be brought under the power of any of them — the fornicator steps aside from his own liberty and is brought under the power of a harlot, ceasing to be his own master. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: The argument from meats does not transfer to fornication. Meats and the belly are both to be destroyed, but the body is not created for fornication — it is for the Lord, and the Lord for the body as its Redeemer who assumed a body. Since God raised the Lord and will raise us, the body is not a temporary thing to be discarded but is destined for resurrection, so fornication is a sin against one's own body. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: God raised up the Lord and will also raise up believers out of the rest of the dead. Whether Paul is found in the grave or alive when Christ comes, the Lord's coming rather than death is the Christian's great expectation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Believers' bodies are members of Christ, so shall Paul take Christ's members and make them members of a harlot? They cannot be both at once. The moral and spiritual ruin caused by such sins was treated by untaught human wisdom as blameless as eating and drinking, but revelation shows it alienates the body from Christ. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: One joined to a harlot is cemented to her and becomes one body with her, as God said by Adam, the two shall be one flesh. This justifies calling fornicators members of a harlot. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: By contrast, one joined to the Lord becomes one spirit with him. In union with a harlot the fornicator becomes one body, not one spirit, since the spirit is overlaid by the sensual; but the believer's body is sanctified by union with Christ's body and he becomes one spirit with him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Flee fornication — flight is the only safety in such temptations. Every other sin is comparatively external to the body, injuring it from without, but the fornicator alienates the very body that is the Lord's and makes it one with a harlot's, sinning against his own body in its truth and nature, a contradiction wrought within itself. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Each believer's body is the temple of the Holy Ghost within him — not many temples but one temple collectively, each member in miniature what the whole is together. Fornication is therefore a sacrilegious desecration of God's temple to profane uses, and since none but God can claim a temple, the Holy Ghost here assigned one is God. Nor is the body our own to alienate: as a servant's person was wholly his master's property, so Christ has bought believers to himself. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Believers are bought with a price — Christ's blood is strictly a ransom paid to God's justice by God's love in Christ for redemption. Taking off the obligation to punishment, it lays on a new obligation to obedience: accepting him as Prophet and Priest requires accepting him as King, and glorifying God in the body as in his temple. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
