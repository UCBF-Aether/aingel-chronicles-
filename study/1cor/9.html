Study Notes — 1 Corinthians

Chapter 9

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Paul confirms his teaching on not stumbling a brother by his own example of forgoing undoubted apostolic rights. Am I not free? Am I not an apostle? He has seen Jesus bodily, not in a mere vision — a leading function of an apostle as witness of the resurrection — and the Corinthians themselves are his work in the Lord, the seal of his apostleship. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: If Paul is not an apostle to others, he is doubtless one to the Corinthians. Their conversion by his preaching, accompanied by miracles and gifts, vouches for his apostleship as a seal attests a document's genuineness. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: To those who call his apostleship in question, his answer is this: the Corinthians themselves are the seal of his apostleship. His defense rests on their own existence as a church. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Have we not the right to eat and drink without laboring with our hands? The question expects the answer, surely you will not say we have not. Paul's not exercising this right was twisted by opponents into an insinuation that he knew himself no true apostle. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Paul had the undoubted right to lead about a sister as a wife, as other apostles did — Cephas was a married man — and as the Lord's brethren did. He did not exercise it, both to save the church the expense of maintaining a wife on his wide circuits and to give himself undistractedly to building up the church, in contrast to the Corinthians' self-seeking liberty at the cost of destroying the church. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Barnabas, long Paul's associate, likewise forbore to claim the maintenance that is a minister's right, and Paul supported himself by tent-making. Only he and Barnabas among the apostles are singled out as working with their hands. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Who serves as a soldier at his own expense, plants a vineyard without eating its fruit, or feeds a flock without drinking its milk? The minister is spiritually soldier, vine-dresser, and shepherd alike, and each by common right lives from his work. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Paul speaks not merely according to human judgment but with the sanction of divine law. The rights he claims rest on Scripture, not on custom alone. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: The law says not to muzzle the ox treading out the corn, when oxen threshed in the open air by treading the sheaves. God cares for the lower animal, but the animal is not the ultimate object of the law; in the humane consideration shown to the ox we learn that still more is due to man, the head of creation, and that the human laborer, spiritual as well as temporal, is worthy of his hire. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: The lawgiver by all means says this for our sakes as the ultimate object in the lower world. The plowman ought to plow in hope and the thresher in hope of partaking — the obligation rests on the people not to let their minister labor without remuneration. The spiritual plowman first plants a church, and the thresher tends one already planted. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: If the apostles sowed spiritual treasures to the Corinthians, may they not at least reap the only thing the Corinthians have to give, the goods that nourish the flesh? The exchange is vastly unequal in the Corinthians' favor. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: If others partake of this right over the Corinthians, whether true or false apostles, how much more might Paul and Barnabas, considering their greater labors? Yet they suffered all things without complaint, concealing their straitened circumstances, lest they hinder the gospel by giving any handle for imputing self-seeking if they received support. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Those who minister about holy things — the Jewish priests and Levites — live from the temple, partakers with the altar, since part of the victims went to the altar's service and the rest was shared by the priests. The same principle of support applies to Christian ministers. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Even so the Lord has ordained that those who preach the gospel should live from the gospel. The only inference is the parallel of support, not that Christian ministry is sacrificial like the Jewish priesthood; the Christian minister's plain duty is preaching the gospel, in contrast to ministering about sacrifices and waiting at the altar. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Paul used none of these rights, and he has not written to have them now applied to his case, as with soldier, planter, shepherd, plowman, and priest. Rather than hinder the gospel by any pretext for a charge of interested motives, he would die of hunger than have his glorying — preaching without remuneration — made void. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: If Paul preaches the gospel, even without charge, he has nothing to glory of in the preaching itself, for necessity is laid on him, as on Jeremiah and Jonah. His sole ground for glorying is preaching without charge, since no necessity is laid on him as to that; it is his voluntary act for the gospel's sake. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: If Paul preached of his own accord he would have a reward, but as it is he preaches involuntarily, by the constraining grace of God, and has had a dispensation entrusted to him. He can claim no reward for doing only his duty, but would incur woe if he failed in it. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Paul's reward, then, is that by making the gospel without charge, where he might rightfully have claimed maintenance, he might win the more. He does not use his power to the full, and that self-denial is his glorying, while the ultimate reward is the gaining of souls. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Though free from all men, Paul made himself servant to all, that he might gain the more — as many of them as possible. Gain is a fitting word for a reward, and he repeats it through the passage. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: To the Jews Paul became as a Jew in matters of Jewish usage, not Judaizing in essentials, and to those under the law as under the law in ceremonies not then repugnant to Christianity, that he might gain those under the law. He himself belonged nationally to the Jews but not in creed to those under the law. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: To those without revealed law, the heathen, Paul became as without law, not urging ceremonies and works of the law but the hearing of faith, and discoursing in their own manner, as at Athens from their own poets. Yet he was not without law to God, but responsible to law in relation to Christ — no longer subject to the literal law from without, but to the inward law of faith in Christ, the law of love. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: To the weak Paul became weak, that he might gain the weak — establishing inexperienced Christians instead of being a stumbling-block to them, and keeping them from falling from faith. He conformed himself to the feelings of each class, that out of them all he might gain some, for the gain of even some is worth the expenditure of all means. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Paul does all this for the gospel's sake, that he may be a fellow partaker of its blessings promised at Christ's coming, together with those he has gained to the gospel. His self-denial aims at sharing the prize with his converts. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: The Isthmian games, well known and a patriotic pride to the Corinthians who lived nearby, picture Christian earnestness. In a race course all run but one receives the prize — yet even if only one could be saved it would be worth running — so run in such a way that you may fully obtain, persevering to the end, as candidates swore to ten months' training and lived on strict self-denying diet. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Every man who strives in wrestling, a severer contest than the race, is temperate in all things, as Paul abstained from claiming sustenance for the sake of gaining the more. The athletes strove for a corruptible crown, a chaplet of soon-withering pine leaves; believers strive for an incorruptible crown, a wreath that does not fade. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Paul runs not uncertainly, as a runner unsure of the goal, and fights not as one beating the air in sham-fight. In all his acts — becoming all things to all men, receiving no sustenance — he has a definite end, to gain the more, looking straight to the goal, casting away encumbrance, and indifferent to bystanders. The real adversary is Satan acting through the flesh. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Paul keeps his body under and brings it into subjection, bruising it as in boxing and leading it captive as a slave — not ascetic maceration, but keeping down natural self-seeking so as to lay himself out entirely for the work. Unless the soul keeps the body under, the body will get above the soul; the body makes a good servant but a bad master. He does this lest, after heralding the gospel to others as the herald summoned racers and crowned conquerors, he himself should be a castaway, rejected by God the Judge of the race. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
