Study Notes — 1 Corinthians

Chapter 1

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Paul calls himself an apostle by God's will, not by his own merit or choice — the same will that grounds his authority over the Corinthian church is also a reason for humility. Sosthenes, named beside him, was likely the former synagogue ruler of Corinth, now a brother whose standing would add weight to the letter. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Paul still calls the divided Corinthian church the church of God, since it held evangelical doctrine, baptism, and the Lord's Supper; apparent purity is not the test of a true church. Its members are sanctified, set apart as holy by union with Christ, and called saints by God's calling, not by their own achievement. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Grace and peace are wished to the church from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ, with peace peculiarly needed in a church torn by dissension. The blessing follows the order of grace first, then peace. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Paul opens with praise rather than reproof, putting the causes for thanksgiving first so as not to discourage the church and to appeal to its better self. His thanks are for the grace given them in Christ Jesus. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: The Corinthians were enriched in utterance and knowledge — in the preaching of the word and in apprehending it. Paul congratulates them on these very gifts before he deals with their abuse of them, winning goodwill before correction. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: The testimony about Christ was confirmed among the Corinthians by God's work through Paul's preaching, the miracles accompanying it, and their own acceptance of the gospel by the Spirit's inward power. Their faith itself was the seal of the message. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: The church came behind in no gift, though no single believer had every gift — different persons held different gifts. Their eager waiting for Christ's coming exercised faith, hope, and love together, and was the crowning proof of their enrichment. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: God himself will confirm the believers to the end, so that they stand blameless in the day of Christ. Now is the day of work and trial; then will be the day of Christ and of his glory in his saints, after which there is no more danger. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: God is faithful to his promises, and has called believers according to his purpose into fellowship with Jesus Christ — to be fellow heirs with him, sons of God and heirs of glory. Christ's name recurs so often in this epistle precisely to draw the Corinthians away from party admiration of teachers to Christ alone. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Paul urges them, since they already have knowledge, utterance, and hope, to maintain love as well. They are to speak the same thing, without splits or breaches, and to be perfectly joined together — the word pictures healing a wound or mending a rent — in mind and judgment, in what they believe and in what they decide to do. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Word of their contentions reached Paul from Chloe's household, people trusted by both Paul and the Corinthians. Paul names the family to show his charge rests on authority, but names no individuals, so as not to stir hatred against them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Each believer was boasting, 'I am of Paul,' 'I of Apollos,' 'I of Cephas,' 'I of Christ' — not necessarily fixed parties, but a party spirit attaching to favorite teachers. Paul will not be flattered even by those using his own name, since that dishonors Christ; Apollos, for his part, discouraged such preference and would not revisit just then. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Christ cannot be divided into parts, one under one leader and another under another, for all belong to him, the one Head. Nor was Paul crucified for them or they baptized into his name — the cross claims believers for Christ as their redeemer, and baptism dedicates them to him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Paul thanks God that he baptized only Crispus, the former synagogue ruler, and Gaius, his host at Corinth. Baptizing was chiefly the deacons' work of instruction and administration, while the apostles' work was to preach, found churches, and superintend them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: God so ordered it that no one could say Paul had baptized people into his own name. The restraint was providential, not a reason Paul had in mind at the time. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Paul also baptized the household of Stephanas, the firstfruits of Achaia among the first converts there. Such households likely included children, and the history of the church favors infant baptism as the usage from the earliest ages. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Christ sent Paul to preach the gospel, not to baptize — not to depreciate baptism, which he exalts elsewhere, but because the apostles' peculiar work was preaching and founding churches. He preached not with philosophical reasoning dressed in oratory, lest human eloquence empty the cross of Christ, the sum of the gospel, of its power. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: The message of the cross seems foolishness to those on the way to perishing, because they prefer human wisdom of words to the doctrine of the cross. To those on the way to salvation it is the power of God — his mighty instrument of salvation and the highest exhibition of his power. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Quoting Isaiah, Paul says God himself will destroy the wisdom of the wise and bring the understanding of the prudent to nothing. Worldly wisdom perishes not by accident but by God's own act. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Where is the wise man, the Jewish scribe, or the Greek disputer now? God has shown this age's philosophy to be folly, because it lacks faith in Christ crucified, and he has not used its help in converting and saving men. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: In God's wise arrangement, the world by its own philosophy never came to know God, whatever other knowledge it gained — nature's highest gifts cannot make the moral day dawn, any more than stars and moon can make it day. So it pleased God to save believers through preaching that the world calls foolishness. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Jews demanded signs from heaven as direct tokens that Jesus was Messiah, while Greeks sought a philosophical demonstration of Christianity. Christ instead demands faith on the ground of his word and reasonable evidence that the revelation is his — Christianity begins by satisfying the heart longing for forgiveness, not by solving every intellectual difficulty. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Paul and Apollos preached Christ crucified — the Greek marks not just the fact of crucifixion but the permanent character Christ now bears as Savior. To Jews this was a stumbling-block and to Gentiles foolishness, yet a religion so seemingly contemptible in origin could not have succeeded unless it were divine. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: To those effectually called, Jew and Greek alike, Christ is the power of God and the wisdom of God. The cross, a slave's death that scandalized Jews looking for a temporal Messiah, meets every reasonable demand for a sign; and Christ, received beyond the offense of the cross, is wisdom in the highest degree. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: What men call the foolishness of God — his plan of salvation — is wiser than men, and what they call his weakness — Christ crucified through weakness, yet living by God's power — is stronger than men. God perfects strength out of the weakness of his servants. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Consider your calling: not many wise by human study, not many mighty, not many noble were called. The stress falls on the weakness of the instruments God used, so that the church's expansion is a progressive victory of the lowly over the lofty. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: God chose the foolish things of the world to shame the wise, and the weak things to shame the mighty. The repeated 'chosen' marks the gracious deliberateness of his purpose: he effects through his instruments, without human wisdom, what the worldly wise cannot effect — bringing men to salvation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: God chose the lowborn and despised, things regarded as nothing, to bring to nothing the things that are. The clause gathers up the foolish, weak, and base as one class of nothings in the world's eyes. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: No flesh may glory in God's presence. Human greatness, like the flower of the field, is beautiful but frail, and those who boast in it are put to shame. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: Believers are in Christ not of themselves but of God, by living union with him. From God, Christ has been made to them wisdom — wise unto salvation where once they were fools — and righteousness, the ground of justification, and sanctification by his Spirit, and redemption, the final deliverance of the body. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: As Jeremiah wrote, whoever glories must glory in the Lord, not in flesh or in the world. Paul joins humility with the elevating consciousness of true dignity in Christ, against both self-glory and slavish self-abasement. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
