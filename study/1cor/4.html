Study Notes — 1 Corinthians

Chapter 4

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Paul and Apollos are to be regarded as ministers of Christ, not as heads of the church in whom believers glory — headship belongs to Christ alone. They are stewards, not depositories of grace but dispensers of it, proclaiming as heralds the deep truths of God, the mysteries known only by revelation, so far as hearers are disposed to receive them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: What is required in stewards, here on earth as in earthly service, is that a man be found faithful. The difference is that God's stewards do not await man's judgment to test them, but the testing of the day of the Lord — an argument against partial preferences for teachers based on their gifts. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Being judged by man's judgment amounts to a very small matter compared with God's, not because Paul despises their judgment but because man's day is nothing beside the Lord's day. He does not even assume the right of approving himself; the Lord alone is the Decider of his case. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Paul is conscious of no ministerial unfaithfulness, yet that does not justify him, for conscience is not an infallible guide. He does not consider his own conscience decisive; only the Lord's judgment can do that. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Therefore believers must not forestall God's prerogative by judging before the time, until the Lord comes. He will bring hidden things of darkness to light and manifest the counsels of hearts, since present judgments see only outward acts and cannot see motives; then each will receive his due praise, estimated by motives rather than exaggerated praise heaped on favorite teachers. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Paul has transferred to himself and Apollos, as a figure or type, what holds good of all teachers, naming the two whose names were used as party cries while meaning others he spares naming. The lesson is not to go beyond what is written — to revere the silence of Scripture as much as its declarations, and so dogmatize less on what is not expressly revealed — and not to be puffed up for one minister against another. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Who distinguishes one believer above another? Not himself, but God. To glory as if a gift had not been received is to forget that everything is owed to God, not to oneself. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: With irony Paul says they are already filled full, already rich, already seated as kings without him — as if they no longer needed to hunger and thirst after righteousness, and had already reached the kingdom for which Christians must strive and suffer. He truly wishes it were so, that he might reign with them, for their prosperity would redound to him as their father in Christ. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: God has set forth the apostles last, as criminals appointed to death and exhibited as a theatrical spectacle to the world, to angels and to men. Like condemned men in the amphitheater, set forth last to fight wild beasts, they are a gazing-stock — yet to angels they are a spectacle worthy of intense regard, though lowest in the world's judgment. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Ironically, the apostles are fools for Christ's sake while the Corinthians are wise in Christ, the apostles weak while they are strong, the apostles despised while they are honorable. The contrast turns on worldly philosophy and rhetoric, which the Corinthians prized and the apostles did not use. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Even to the present hour the apostles hunger and thirst, are insufficiently clad, and are buffeted as slaves — the reverse of the Corinthians reigning as kings. Paul's Master before him was likewise buffeted as a slave before dying a slave's death. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: The apostles labor with their own hands, a fact confirmed by Paul's later words to the Ephesian elders that these hands ministered to his necessities. The undesigned agreement between letter and history is incompatible with forgery. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Being defamed, the apostles entreat — praying for their defamers as Christ enjoined, and replying gently. They have become as the refuse and sweepings of the world, the rubbish thrown out after a cleaning, and not of the world only but of all things. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Paul writes not to shame them but to admonish them as beloved sons, as a father admonishes without provoking wrath. They might well be ashamed at the disparity between their father and themselves, his spiritual children. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Though they have ten thousand instructors — more than was desirable — they have not many fathers, for Paul begot them spiritually in Christ Jesus. The instructors were evangelical teachers, but tutors only, without a father's rights and peculiar affection; and since Paul spiritually regenerated them while baptizing almost none of them, regeneration cannot be inseparably in and by baptism. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Paul urges them to be imitators of him in his ways which are in Christ, not in his crosses and sufferings. Imitation means following his walk and teaching, not seeking his afflictions. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: For this reason Paul sent Timothy, his son in the faith, beloved and faithful in the Lord, best suited from his spiritual connection with Paul to remind them of the apostle's walk and teaching, which they had in some respects forgotten. What Paul taught everywhere in every church must be necessary at Corinth also. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Some were puffed up as though Paul would not come himself, misconstruing his sending of Timothy. A puffed-up spirit was the besetting sin of Corinth, and Paul guards against the misreading in advance. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Paul emphatically says he will come shortly, after Pentecost, if the Lord will — a wise proviso. He cares nothing for the high-sounding speech of the puffed up, but will take cognizance of their power, whether they are really powerful in the Spirit or not. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: The kingdom of God is not in word but in power. Not empty speeches but the manifest power of the Spirit attests the reign of the gospel, in a church or in an individual. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Paul leaves the manner of his coming to them: shall he come with a rod of displeasure, or in love and the spirit of meekness? The choice is theirs, by their repentance or refusal. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
