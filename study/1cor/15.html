Study Notes — 1 Corinthians

Chapter 15

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Paul makes known afresh the gospel he preached, in which they now stand — their present privilege, if they do not fall from it. That it must be declared anew carries a reproach, since some among them do not have the knowledge of God. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: By this gospel they are being saved, if they hold fast in memory and personal appropriation the very words with which he preached it. Otherwise their faith is vain, resting on Christ's resurrection as an objective reality that would not be real. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Paul delivered among the foremost points what he himself received from Christ by special revelation: Christ died for our sins, to atone for them and take them away, according to the Scriptures, which cannot be broken. Paul puts Scripture's testimony above even that of eyewitnesses, and the atonement is of primary importance. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: He was buried, and he rose again the third day according to the Scriptures. His burial connects closely with his resurrection: at his death his inextinguishable life exerted itself, and the grave was not a receptacle of corruption but an apartment fitted for entering into life. 'Hath risen' marks a state begun whose consequences still continue. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: He was seen by Cephas, Peter, and then by the twelve — the round number for the eleven, their ordinary appellation even when their number was not full, though Matthias may have been present. The appearances are listed as attested fact. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: After that he was seen by above five hundred brethren at once, probably on the mountain in Galilee at his most solemn public appearance, appointed remote from Jerusalem so believers might assemble freely and securely. Most of them remain to the present and may be thoroughly sifted for the trustworthiness of their testimony, while some have fallen asleep in the sure hope of awaking at the resurrection. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: After that he was seen by James, the Less, the Lord's brother, and then by all the apostles — a term including many besides the Twelve, perhaps the seventy disciples. Each appearance widens the circle of witnesses. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Last of all he was seen by Paul also, as one born out of due time — abortively born, the abortion in the family of apostles. As a prematurely born child is puny and scarcely worthy of the name, so Paul is the least of the apostles, a supernumerary taken in out of regular course, not led by long instruction but by sudden power, on the Damascus road. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Paul is the least of the apostles and not meet to be called an apostle, because he persecuted the church of God. Though God has forgiven him, he can hardly forgive himself at the remembrance of his past sin — his very name Paulus means 'least.' — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: By the grace of God he is what he is — occupying the apostle's office — and God's grace toward him was not in vain, for he labored more abundantly than they all. Yet it was not he but the grace of God with him: grace so preponderated that his own cooperation is regarded as nothing and grace as virtually the sole agent, though his will concurred with God when brought into conformity by the Spirit. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Whether it was Paul or the other apostles who labored more abundantly, so they preached and so the Corinthians believed — the substance of the preaching was the same truths just stated, Christ's death for sins and resurrection. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: If Christ is preached as risen from the dead, how can some among the Corinthians say there is no resurrection of the dead? The deniers were Gentile reasoners who would not believe what they could not see how to explain. The general resurrection is the necessary consequence of Christ's resurrection. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: If there is no resurrection of the dead, then Christ is not risen. Head and members stand on the same footing: what does not hold good of them does not hold good of him, for his resurrection and theirs are inseparably joined. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: If Christ is not risen, preaching is vain — empty and unreal — and faith is vain. The principal argument of the first preachers was that God raised Christ from the dead; if that fact were false, the faith built on it must be false too. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: The apostles would even be found false witnesses of God, testifying concerning God that he raised Christ, whom he did not raise if the dead rise not. It is not right to tell untrue stories even seemingly for God's glory. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: For if the dead rise not, then Christ is not risen — the repetition carries unanswerable force. The argument cannot be escaped by restating it. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: If Christ is not risen, faith is vain in a different sense — without use, frustrated — and believers are still in their sins, under everlasting condemnation even in the disembodied state, since Christ's resurrection is our justification and we are saved by his life. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Then those also who have fallen asleep in Christ have perished — their souls lost, in misery in the unseen world. In Christ's case Paul says 'death' to assure the reality of his suffering; in ours he says 'sleep' for consolation, since our resurrection is still a matter of hope. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: If in this life only we have hope in Christ, we are of all men most miserable — exposed to every trial and persecution while others live unmolested, and doomed to bitter disappointment in our most cherished hope, since all hope of salvation, even of the soul, hangs on Christ's resurrection, without which his death would avail nothing. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: But now — as the case really is — Christ is risen from the dead and become the firstfruits of those who slept. He is the earnest and pledge that the whole resurrection harvest will follow, so faith is not vain and hope is not limited to this life. The epistle was likely written about Passover, and the day after the Passover Sabbath for offering firstfruits was the day of Christ's resurrection. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Since by man came death, by man came also the resurrection of the dead. The firstfruits are of the same nature as the rest of the harvest, so Christ, bringer of life, is of the same nature as the race to whom he brings it, just as Adam, bringer of death, was of the same nature as those on whom he brought it. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: As in Adam all die, in union of nature with Adam as representative head in the fall, so in Christ shall all be made alive, in union of nature with Christ as representative head in recovery. The life brought by Christ is co-extensive with the death brought by Adam. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: But every man in his own order — his own rank, as troops in their own regiments. All shall rise, but not all shall be saved: Christ first, then those who are Christ's at his coming, in a separate band from the ungodly, and then the end. Christ's flock shall share his glory at his coming, which is not to be confounded with the general judgment. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Then comes the end, the general resurrection and final judgment and consummation, when he delivers up the kingdom to God the Father, having put down all rule and all authority and power. His giving up of the mediatorial kingdom when its end is accomplished is in harmony with its continuing everlastingly: the change is in the manner of administration, not in the kingdom itself, for God shall then come into direct connection with the earth instead of mediatorially. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: For he must reign until he has put all enemies under his feet, because Scripture foretells it. There will then be no further need of his mediatorial kingdom, its object having been realized, and meanwhile he rules in the midst of his enemies. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: The last enemy that shall be destroyed is death. Satan brought in sin and sin brought in death, so they shall be destroyed in the same order, rendered utterly powerless — death most of all for believers, though even for unbelievers death is done away with by the general resurrection. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: For he has put all things under his feet, quoting the Psalm: what God has said is as good as done, so sure is it. All things, including death, are in perfect and lasting subjection as his footstool. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: When all things are subdued to him, then the Son also himself shall be subject to him who put all things under him, that God may be all in all. The Son's subjection is not as creatures are subject, but as a Son voluntarily subordinate though co-equal with the Father; his mediatorial kingdom merges in the Father's, with no derogation from his honor, since the Father wills that all honor the Son as they honor the Father. Then, and not till then, shall all things be subject to the Son and the Son subordinate to the Father while sharing his glory, and each saint shall feel God is all to him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Else, if the dead rise not, what shall they do who are baptized for the dead? Paul uses an ad hominem argument from a Corinthian practice — whether baptizing a living person on behalf of a friend who died unbaptized, or being baptized immediately upon the dead, gathered to the dead straight after — without sanctioning it: why take the trouble if the dead rise not? The best punctuation reads, 'If the dead rise not at all, why are they then baptized for them?' — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: And why do we, the apostles, stand in jeopardy every hour? From those baptized at the point of death, who could enjoy this life only a little time, Paul rises to the apostles, who could enjoy it longer if they had not renounced the world for Christ. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: Paul protests by the rejoicing he has over the Corinthians, the fruit of his labors in the Lord, that he dies daily — day by day in sight of death, exposed to it and expecting it. His daily dying stands first in the sentence for emphasis. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: If after the manner of men Paul fought with beasts at Ephesus — with men resembling savage beasts, as Heraclitus had called his countrymen wild beasts — what advantage would it bring him, if the dead rise not? He was still at Ephesus with many adversaries menacing him, his life daily in danger. If the dead rise not, the skeptics' slogan follows: let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die — the reckless self-indulgence of those despising God's call to mourning, which Paul quotes to reprove both their theory and practice, for those who persuade themselves they shall die like beasts soon live like beasts too. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: Do not be deceived: evil communications corrupt good manners — a current saying from Menander, probably from Euripides. Evil communications mean intercourse with deniers of the resurrection, whose notion was that resurrection is merely spiritual and sin's seat is solely the body, left behind at death. Intimacy with profligate society corrupts the Corinthians' pliant principles. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: Awake out of the sleep of carnal intoxication into which the skeptics' influence has thrown them, awake to righteousness and sin not — do not give yourselves up to sinful pleasures in a continued state. Those who live in sinful pleasures readily persuade themselves of what they wish, that there is no resurrection. Some among them have not the knowledge of God — a willful habitual ignorance, preferring to keep their sins rather than part with them to know God — and Paul speaks this to the shame of Corinthians who boast of knowledge yet keep company with such deniers. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: But some will ask, how are the dead raised, and with what body do they come? It is folly to deny a fact of revelation because we do not know the 'how,' measuring God's power by petty intelligence and refusing even on his assurance what we cannot explain. Ezekiel's answer of faith is the wise one, and Jesus argues wholly from the power of God as declared by his Word. The dead are said to depart, those rising to come; the objector wonders whether the body will be the same, since resurrection bodies will not eat, drink, or beget children, and the old bodies have moldered to dust — yet in one sense it will be the same body, in another a distinct body: a body, but spiritual, not natural. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: Fool — with all your boasted philosophy — that which you sow is not quickened except it die. Paul converts the very objection into an argument: death, far from preventing quickening, is its necessary prelude, just as seed is not quickened into a new sprout with increased produce except a dissolution of its previous organization takes place. Christ by his death has not reprieved us from death as to the life we have from Adam; he permits the law to take its course on our fleshly nature, but brings new spiritual and heavenly life out of death. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

37 COMMENTARY: What you sow is not the body that shall be, but bare grain — a body beautiful no longer, no longer without stalk or ear, but clothed with blade and ears and yielding many grains instead of one. There is no identity of all the old particles in the new body, for matter perpetually transmutes; but a hidden germ constitutes identity amidst all outward changes, and every seed shall have its own body and be instantly recognized, as each plant is known from its seed. Death is the avenue not to mere revivification but to resurrection and regeneration. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

38 COMMENTARY: But God gives it a body as it has pleased him, and to every seed its own body — as at creation he gave each kind of seed a body of its own after its kind. So God can and will give the blessed at the resurrection their own appropriate body, such as pleases him and suits their glorified state, peculiar to the individual yet substantially the same as the body sown. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

39 COMMENTARY: All flesh is not the same flesh: there is one kind of flesh of men, another of beasts, another of fishes, another of birds. As kinds of flesh differ widely yet remain flesh, so kinds of bodies differ yet remain bodies — illustrating that the new celestial body differs from its terrestrial seed while retaining substantial identity, and that resurrection bodies shall be in some sense really flesh, not mere phantoms, though not flesh and blood in the animal sense. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

40 COMMENTARY: There are also celestial bodies and bodies terrestrial — the celestial being the bodies of angels, as distinguished from earthly creatures — but the glory of the celestial is one and the glory of the terrestrial is another. Each kind of body has its fitting glory. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

41 COMMENTARY: There is one glory of the sun, another glory of the moon, and another glory of the stars, for one star differs from another star in glory. The analogy does not prove different degrees of glory among the blessed, but this: as fountains of light so similar in aspect yet differ, so there is nothing unreasonable in present bodies differing from resurrection bodies while still continuing bodies. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

42 COMMENTARY: So also is the resurrection of the dead. The body is sown in corruption — liable to corruption, not merely a prey to it when dead — and raised in incorruption, not liable to corruption. 'Sown' is a delightful word for burial. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

43 COMMENTARY: It is sown in dishonor — answering to our vile body, our body of humiliation, liable to disease, injury, and decay — and raised in glory, the garment of incorruption like his glorious body which we shall put on. It is sown in weakness, liable to infirmities, and raised in power, not liable to the weaknesses of our frail bodies, answering to a spiritual body. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

44 COMMENTARY: It is sown a natural body — literally an animal body, molded to suit the animal soul which predominates in it — and raised a spiritual body, wholly molded by the Spirit and conformed to the higher spiritual life rather than the lower animal life. If there is a natural body, there is also a spiritual body; it is no more wonderful that there should be a body fitted to man's highest part, his spirit, than one fitted to his subordinate animal soul. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

45 COMMENTARY: So it is written that the first man Adam was made a living soul — endowed with an animal soul, the living principle of his body. The last Adam, the last Head of humanity, is a quickening spirit — not only living but making alive. As the natural body is the fruit of union with the first Adam, an animal-souled man, so the spiritual body is the fruit of union with the second Adam, who exhausted in his own person the sentence of death passed on all men and gives spiritual and everlasting life to whom he will. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

46 COMMENTARY: That was not first which is spiritual, but that which is natural, and afterward that which is spiritual. Adam had a living soul destined to live forever had he eaten of the tree of life, yet his body was still an animal-souled body, not a spiritual body such as believers shall have, and much less was he a life-giving spirit. As the first and lower Adam came before the second and heavenly Adam, so the animal-souled body comes first and must die before it is changed into the spiritual body. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

47 COMMENTARY: The first man is of the earth, earthy — sprung from heaped earth or clay, for Adam means red earth, not merely born upon the earth but of earth. The second man is the Lord from heaven: humanity in Christ is generic, the representative man and federal head of redeemed man, the true ideal God originally designed. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

48 COMMENTARY: As is the earthy, such are they also that are earthy — all Adam's posterity in their natural state. As is the heavenly, such are they also that are heavenly — his people in their regenerate state. As the former state precedes the latter, so natural bodies precede spiritual bodies. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

49 COMMENTARY: As we have borne the image of the earthy, we shall also bear the image of the heavenly — wearing it as a garment. The oldest manuscripts read 'we must also bear' or 'let us also bear,' implying divine appointment and faith assenting to it, an exhortation yet implying a promise. Conformity to the heavenly Representative is begun here in our souls in part and shall be perfected at the resurrection in bodies and souls. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

50 COMMENTARY: Flesh and blood of the same animal and corruptible nature as our present animal-souled bodies cannot inherit the kingdom of God, which is altogether spiritual, not merely animal. The change of body is not merely possible but necessary: the glorified man differs from the mortal man as spirit extracted from wine dregs differs from them, yet the resurrection body will still be a body, substantially retaining personal identity. The believer therefore acquiesces gladly in the law's sentence appointing death of the present body as the necessary preliminary to the resurrection body of glory, and dies daily to flesh and world as the condition of regeneration here and hereafter. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

51 COMMENTARY: Behold, Paul shows them a mystery heretofore hidden in God's purposes but now revealed, by the word of the Lord: we shall not all sleep, but we shall all be changed. The putting off of the corruptible body for an incorruptible by instantaneous change will, for the living, stand as equivalent to death appointed to all men, of which Enoch and Elijah are types. The 'we' implies Christians in every age were designed to stand waiting as if Christ might come in their time and they might be found among the living. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

52 COMMENTARY: In a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trump — the trumpet on the last day, as the law was given with trumpet sound and the Lord ascended with trumpet sound, so he shall descend. The trumpet convoked the people on solemn feasts, especially the first day of the seventh month, type of the completion of time. For the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised incorruptible, and we shall be changed, immediately in consequence. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

53 COMMENTARY: For this corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality, as a garment. 'This' points to Paul's own body and those he addresses. Nowhere is the immortality of the soul distinct from the body taught; Scripture does not contemplate the anomalous state brought about by death as the consummation to be looked for, but the resurrection. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

54 COMMENTARY: So when this corruptible has put on incorruption and this mortal has put on immortality, then shall be brought to pass the saying, Death is swallowed up in victory. Death still has a sting for the believer in that his body remains under its power till the resurrection, but then sting and power cease forever: Jehovah will swallow up death in victory, for ever, so altogether victoriously that it shall never regain its power. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

55 COMMENTARY: O death, where is your sting? O grave, where is your victory? 'Sting' answers to the Hebrew 'plagues,' a poisoned sting causing plagues, fitting the old serpent; 'victory' answers to 'destruction.' The 'where' implies their past victorious destroying power and sting, now gone forever, obtained through Satan's triumph in Eden, which enlisted God's law on the side of Satan and death against man. The souls in Hades being freed by the resurrection, death's sting and victory are gone. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

56 COMMENTARY: The sting of death is sin, and the strength of sin is the law. If there were no sin there would be no death, and man's transgression of the law gives death its lawful power. Without the law sin is not perceived or imputed, and the law makes sin more grievous by making God's will clearer; Christ's people are no longer under the law. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

57 COMMENTARY: But thanks be to God, who gives us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ. The victory was in no way due to ourselves, and 'gives' is a present certainty: the victory death and Hades aimed at, we have gained despite the opposition of law and sin. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

58 COMMENTARY: Therefore, beloved — sound doctrine kindles Christian love — be steadfast, not turning aside from the faith of the resurrection yourselves, and unmovable, not turned aside by others. Always abound in the work of the Lord, the promotion of Christ's kingdom, knowing that your labor is not in vain in the Lord: as being in the Lord by faith, you know your labor according to his will is not without its reward in the Lord, through his merits and gracious appointment. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
