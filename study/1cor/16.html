Study Notes — 1 Corinthians

Chapter 16

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Now concerning the collection for the saints at Jerusalem and in Judea — Paul says 'saints' rather than 'the poor' to remind the Corinthians that giving is to the Lord's people, their own brethren in faith. Toward the close of the Jews' national existence, Judea and Jerusalem were harassed with troubles affecting Jewish Christians, and the early community of goods, which gave temporary relief but ultimately impoverished all by paralyzing individual exertion, had been discontinued. It is a beautiful fruit of grace that Paul, who by persecutions robbed many of their all, became foremost in exertions for their relief. As he gave order to the churches of Galatia during his journey through Galatia and Phrygia, so the Corinthians are to do likewise, example passing from Galatians to Corinthians to Macedonians to Romans. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: On the first day of the week — already kept sacred as the day of the Lord's resurrection, the beginning day of the physical and the new spiritual creations, gradually superseding the Jewish Sabbath — let every one of you, even those in limited circumstances, lay by him in store a definite proportion of his weekly income, as God has prospered him. Though there is no weekly public collection, each is privately to set apart his gift, so that there are no gatherings when Paul comes, when time ought to be spent on more directly spiritual things; regular weekly laying by collects more than a single gift at once. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: When Paul comes, whomsoever the Corinthians approve he will send with letters — letters to several persons at Jerusalem as their credentials — to bring their liberality, literally their gracious free gift, to Jerusalem. The antithesis with Paul himself in the next verse favors reading 'with letters' rather than 'by your letters.' — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: If it is worth while — if the collections are large enough to be worth an apostle's journey, a stimulus to their liberality — Paul himself will go with the approved bearers instead of merely giving them letters of credential, and his accompanying them guards against all possible suspicion of evil in handling the gift. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Paul will come to them when he passes through Macedonia, for he does pass through Macedonia on his way to them. His first intention had been to pass through Corinth to Macedonia and return to them again, announced in the lost epistle; now he has laid that aside and announces his second plan of passing through Macedonia first, then tarrying a while at Corinth. He was not yet in Macedonia but at Ephesus, thinking of passing through it, not abiding as he purposed to do at Corinth. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: It may be that he will abide, yea, and winter with them, that they may bring him on his journey wherever he goes. He did indeed abide and winter the three winter months in Greece, at Corinth, before leaving about a month before Passover. His purpose to go to Judea from Corinth was not yet positively fixed. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: For he will not see them now by the way — he does not wish to pay this time what would be a merely passing visit, as his second visit was. He trusts to tarry a while with them, some time rather than in passing, if the Lord permits. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: But he will tarry at Ephesus, whence he writes this epistle, until Pentecost. He stayed as he purposed, leaving just as the tumult that drove him away broke out; combined with the Passover references, this fixes the epistle's date to a few weeks before Pentecost, very soon after Passover. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: For a great door and effectual is opened to him at Ephesus — an opening for extending the gospel, great in extent and opportune for effecting great results, though requiring great labors. And there are many adversaries, open adversaries both Jews and heathen, who would block the way; after Paul's long labors threatened the gains derived from idolatry, many adversaries arose. Where great good is, evil is sure to start up as its antagonist. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: If Timothy comes — and Timothy was not the bearer of the epistle, since Paul says 'if,' having sent him from Ephesus before writing, by way of Macedonia, so he might reach Corinth only after the letter — see that he may be with them without fear, for he works the work of the Lord as Paul does. The fear refers perhaps to a nervous timidity in Timothy's character, added to by his youth and his origin in Lystra, likely to be despised in refined Corinth. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Let no man despise Timothy — a charge given concerning no other messenger Paul sent, explained by his youth, younger than those usually employed in Christian missions. They are to conduct him forth in peace, setting him on his way with every mark of respect and whatever he needs, for Paul looks for him with the brethren, feeling him so necessary to himself; peace here is the Eastern salutation of kindness standing for every blessing, in contrast to Corinthian contentions. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: As for Apollos, Paul greatly desired him to come with the brethren, lest the Corinthians suspect Paul kept him away from jealousy, perhaps after expressly requesting him. But Apollos's will was not at all to come at this time, because, aware of the undue admiration of his rhetorical style that led many astray at Corinth, he did not wish to sanction it; Paul's freedom from selfish jealousy led him to urge Apollos, while Apollos perseveringly refused. He will come when he has convenient time, and did return when their divisions moderated. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Watch, stand fast in the faith, quit you like men, be strong. They ought to make their hopes of salvation depend not on Apollos or any teacher but on themselves: watch, for they are slumbering; stand, for they are tottering; quit themselves like men and be strong, for they are effeminate; stand in the faith assailed by deniers of the resurrection. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Let all your things be done with charity — not with strifes as at present. Love is to govern everything, as chapters 8 and 13 have shown. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Paul beseeches them concerning the house of Stephanas, the firstfruits of Achaia, the first Achaean converts, whose members Paul himself baptized, and who voluntarily set themselves to minister to the saints. He appeals to their knowledge of this household's devotion. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: That they submit themselves to such, and to every one who helps with them and labors — in their turn, in return for the household's self-devotion. Submission is owed to those who help and labor, not only to the named household. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Paul is glad of the coming of Stephanas, Fortunatus, and Achaicus — Achaicus probably of Stephanas's household — for what was lacking on the Corinthians' part they have supplied. So far as the Corinthians themselves, being absent, could not refresh Paul's spirit, these men supplied the means of intercourse by coming from Corinth, and seem to have carried this letter back. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: For they have refreshed Paul's spirit and the Corinthians' — the Corinthians' spirits will be refreshed on receiving this letter by knowing Paul's spirit is refreshed by their coming, and perhaps by the good report they gave of many Corinthians. Paul's refreshment redounds to theirs, as his disciples. Therefore acknowledge those who are such, rendering due acknowledgment by kind reception and treating them according to their true worth. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: The churches of Asia — Lydian Asia only, of which Ephesus was capital — salute them, and Aquila and Priscilla salute them much in the Lord, with the church in their house. Driven from Italy by Claudius, Aquila and Priscilla came to Corinth and then removed with Paul to Ephesus, where, as at Rome later, they set up an assembly in their house — a pattern to Christian husbands and wives, whose self-devoting love appears wherever they were, and to whom even the gifted Apollos owed much of his knowledge. Their salutation in the Lord reflects their union together in Christ. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: All the brethren greet them, and they are to greet one another with a holy kiss — the token of mutual Christian love, especially at the Lord's Supper, in which all Corinthian dissensions would be swallowed up. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: The salutation of Paul with his own hand — he therefore dictated all the rest of the epistle. His autograph marks the personal seal on the letter. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: If any man does not love the Lord Jesus Christ, let him be Anathema Maranatha — accursed, with the curse the Jews who call Jesus accursed are righteously bringing on their own heads; so far from saluting him, Paul bids him be accursed. Maranatha is Syriac for 'the Lord comes,' a motto and watchword urging preparedness for the Lord's coming. The Lord ought to be loved above Paul, Apollos, and all teachers, and love to one another is to be in connection with love to him above all. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with them. This is the salutation meant in Paul's own-hand greeting, from which unbelievers are excluded. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Paul's love be with them all in Christ Jesus. After severe rebukes he closes with expressions of love: his rebukes were prompted by love and are in harmony with this profession, for it was love in Christ Jesus and therefore embraced all who loved him. The subscription's claim that the epistle was written from Philippi is mistaken; chapter 16 verse 8 shows it was written at Ephesus. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
