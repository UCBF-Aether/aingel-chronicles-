Study Notes — 1 Corinthians

Chapter 14

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Follow after charity as your first and chief aim, since it is the greatest. Yet as a secondary aim desire zealously spiritual gifts, but chiefly that you may prophesy — speak and exhort under inspiration, whether foretelling future events or explaining obscure Scripture and setting forth Christian doctrine and practice. Modern preaching succeeds prophecy without the inspiration, and prophecy is to be desired more than tongues. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: He who speaks in an unknown tongue speaks not to men but to God, who alone understands all languages; no man generally understands him, since the few with the gift of interpretation are the exception. In the spirit, as opposed to the understanding, he speaks mysteries — unintelligible to hearers, exciting wonder rather than instructing them. Corinth's mixed merchant population gave scope for tongues, but their legitimate use was before an audience understanding the speaker's tongue, not in mere display. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: But he who prophesies speaks to men for edification, exhortation, and comfort. Edification's two principal species are exhortation to remove sluggishness and comfort or consolation to remove sadness. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: He who speaks in a tongue edifies himself, as he understands what the tongue expresses, but the church, the congregation, does not. The gift benefits the speaker alone when uninterpreted. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Paul wishes them all to speak with tongues — so far is he from objecting to tongues — but rather, as his ulterior and higher wish, that they should prophesy. Tongues must therefore mean languages, not ecstatic unintelligible rhapsodies, for Paul could never wish the latter for them. He who prophesies is greater, because more useful, than he who speaks with tongues, unless he interprets, so that the church may receive edifying. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: If Paul came speaking with tongues, what would he profit them unless he spoke by revelation or by knowledge or by prophesying or by doctrine? Revelation is the supernatural unveiling of divine truths, prophesying the enunciation of such revelations; knowledge corresponds to doctrine, the teaching of general obvious truths of salvation from the common storehouse of believers. Without interpretation there is no edification. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Even lifeless things, pipe or harp, if they give no distinction in the tones, how will what is piped or harped be known? An instrument must sound distinct notes, or its tune cannot be recognized. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: If the trumpet gives an uncertain sound, having no definite meaning, who will prepare for battle? One succession of notes should summon to attack, another to retreat, another to some other evolution; an indistinct call prepares no one. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: So likewise you, unless you utter by the tongue words easy to be understood, how will it be known what is spoken? You will be speaking into the air, in vain, like one beating the air — living speakers should be clearer than lifeless instruments. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: There are, it may be, so many kinds of voices in the world, kinds of articulate speech, and none of them is without signification — none is without its own distinct voice and meaning. Every language means something to someone. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Therefore, if I do not know the meaning of the voice, I shall be a barbarian — a foreigner speaking a foreign language — to him who speaks, and he a barbarian to me. Without shared meaning, speaker and hearer are strangers to each other. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Since you are zealous for spiritual gifts — emanations from the one Spirit — seek that you may excel to the edifying of the church. Seek the gifts in abundance precisely for building up the congregation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Let him who speaks in an unknown tongue pray that he may interpret. Though his words are unintelligible to hearers, he should pray to obtain the gift of interpreting, which will make them edifying to the church. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: If I pray in an unknown tongue, my spirit prays — my higher being, the passive object of the Spirit's operations and instrument of prayer — but my understanding, the active instrument of thought, is unfruitful in edifying others, since the vehicle of expression is unintelligible to them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: What then is Paul's determination? He will pray with the spirit, but with the understanding also, and sing with the spirit and with the understanding also. By inference he will keep silence altogether if he cannot pray with understanding so as to be understood by others — a prescient warning against liturgies kept in dead languages unintelligible to the masses. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Otherwise, if you bless with the spirit, how shall he who occupies the room of the unlearned — one who, lacking the gift of interpretation, is reduced to the position of a private person — say Amen at your giving of thanks, since he does not understand what you say? Prayer is not a vicarious duty done by others for us; the congregation must join with the leader and say aloud its responsive Amen in assent, as in Jewish and primitive Christian usage. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: You indeed give thanks well — the prayers of the church were called blessings and giving of thanks, as synagogue prayers were called eulogies with thanksgiving joined — but the other is not edified. Good thanksgiving that no one understands still fails the congregation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Paul thanks God that he speaks with tongues more than all of them — in a foreign tongue, singular. His correction of tongues comes not from lacking the gift but from possessing it abundantly. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Yet in the church Paul would rather speak five words with his understanding, that he might teach others, than ten thousand words in an unknown tongue. He would not wish at all to speak ten thousand words unintelligibly; five intelligible words outweigh them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Brethren — an appellation to conciliate their reception of the exhortation — do not be children in understanding, as preferring gifts abused to non-edification would make you. Be childlike, not childish: in malice be children, but in understanding be men, full-grown. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: In the law — the whole Old Testament — it is written from Isaiah that God will speak to this people with other tongues and other lips, yet they will not hear him. Israel heard God not in their familiar language, so he would speak through the tongues of foes sent against them, and even then they would not hearken. Paul applies it: it is a penalty to be associated with men of a strange tongue, yet the Corinthians impose this on the church, and speakers in foreign tongues appear like children just weaned, stammering unintelligibly, ridiculous as babbling drunkards or madmen. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Tongues are therefore a sign, not to believers but to unbelievers — often a condemnatory sign, since they will not understand, they shall not understand. Prophesying, by contrast, is not for the obstinately unbelieving but for believers: it makes believers of those not willfully unbelieving and spiritually nourishes those who already believe. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: If the whole church comes together and all speak with tongues, and unlearned persons or unbelievers come in from curiosity or better motive, will they not say you are mad? The more assembled and the more speaking in unknown tongues, the more the impression grows that the whole body is a mob of fanatics — like the builders of Babel after the confusion of tongues, or a cause tried between two deaf men before a deaf judge. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: But if all prophesy — speaking the truth by the Spirit intelligibly — and an unbeliever or unlearned person comes in, he is convinced by all and judged by all. Conviction falls on the unbeliever, and his secret character is searched into, as with the unlearned; the effect on any single entrant would be produced by each speaker in turn. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Thus the secrets of his heart are made manifest, as his inner character is opened out by the sword of the Spirit, the word of God in the prophet's hand. No argument is stronger for religion than its manifestation of men to themselves in their true character. So convicted, judged, and manifested to himself, he falls down and worships God, announcing openly that God is in you of a truth — and by implication that the God in you is the true God. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: What then is the true rule for the use of gifts? When you come together, each one has a psalm — an extemporary inspired song like those of Mary, Zechariah, Simeon, and Anna — a doctrine to impart, a tongue, a revelation, an interpretation. Let all things be done for edifying: that is the general rule under which the particular cases fall, and each is bound to obey church ordinances not adverse to Scripture. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: If anyone speaks in an unknown tongue, let it be by two, or at most three, at each meeting, and in turns, and let one interpret — one who has the gift, and not more than one. Order replaces the simultaneous clamor. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: If there is no interpreter, let the speaker in tongues keep silence in the church, and speak to himself and to God privately, not in the hearing of others. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Let the prophets speak two or three at one meeting, one by one in turn, and let the others judge — by their power of discerning spirits whether the speaker really speaks under the Spirit's influence. Paul gives prophets similar rules to tongue-speakers, though he does not add 'at the most,' lest he seem to quench prophesyings, the most edifying of gifts. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: If anything is revealed to another sitting by, a hearer, let the first hold his peace. He who came furnished with a previous revelation gives place to him who at the assembly is moved by a sudden revelation from the Spirit. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: For you can all prophesy one by one, if you will, giving way to one another, that all may learn and all may be comforted. The possibility of orderly succession justifies the command to yield. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: The spirits of the prophets are subject to the prophets: their own spirits, acted on by the Holy Spirit, are not so hurried away as to cease to be under their own control. They can if they will hear others and not demand to be heard alone. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: For God is not the author of confusion but of peace, as in all churches of the saints. God is a God of peace, and must not among the Corinthians be supposed a God of confusion. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: Let your women keep silence in the churches, for it is not permitted to them to speak; they are to be in subjection, as the law — the whole Old Testament, here Genesis — says. For women to speak in public would be an act of independence, as if not subject to their husbands. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: If they want to learn anything, let them ask their own husbands at home, not other men and not in public. It is a shame — indecorous — for women to speak in the church, and the anticipated objection about asking questions publicly is answered: ask at home. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: What! Did the word of God come out from you, or did it come to you only? Do the Corinthians pretend their church is the first from which the gospel came, that they should give law to all others, or the only church to which it has come? Their judgment is not above that of other churches. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

37 COMMENTARY: If anyone thinks himself a prophet or spiritual — one capable of discerning spirits, as Apollos's followers prided themselves on being spiritual — let him acknowledge that the things Paul writes are the commandments of the Lord, a direct assertion of inspiration: Paul's words as apostle are Christ's words. He appeals to the whole body for facts no body could mistake: their conversion by the Spirit's demonstration and power, the miraculous power then generally exercised, and the gift enabling the spiritual to decide whether his epistle was Scripture. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

38 COMMENTARY: If anyone is willfully ignorant, not wishing to recognize these ordinances and Paul's apostolic authority in enjoining them, let him be ignorant — left to his ignorance at his own peril. Paul feels it a waste of words to argue further, an argument with weight for Corinthians who admired knowledge so much. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

39 COMMENTARY: Therefore, brethren, covet earnestly to prophesy — earnestly desire it, stronger than merely 'forbid not' — and forbid not speaking with tongues. The contrast marks how much higher Paul esteemed prophecy than tongues. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

40 COMMENTARY: But let all things be done decently and in order. While desiring prophecy and not forbidding tongues, order governs all: church government is the best security for Christian liberty. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
