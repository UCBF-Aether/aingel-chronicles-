Study Notes — 1 Corinthians

Chapter 7

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The Corinthians had asked questions tending to disparage marriage, implying it might be better to break it off with an unbeliever. Paul replies that it is good — expedient — for a man not to touch a woman, because of the present distress, the unsettled state of the world and likelihood of persecutions tearing marriage ties asunder. This is expediency in exceptional times, not a denial that marriage is honorable in all, and celibacy's other advantage is attending on the Lord without distraction. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: The general rule is given to avoid fornication — more literally, on account of fornications, the irregular lusts very prevalent at Corinth and not even counted sins among the heathen. Let every man have his own wife and every woman her own husband, a positive command to all who lack the gift of continency, in fact the great majority; marriage signifies the mystical union between Christ and the church. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Husband and wife owe each other the conjugal duty due by the marriage contract. The duty of cohabitation belongs to both parties alike. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The wife has not power over her own body, but her husband does, and likewise the husband's body is his wife's — a paradox explained by the oneness of body in marriage. Each complements the other, and neither without the other realizes the perfect ideal of man. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Spouses must not defraud one another of the conjugal due except by mutual consent for a special season, to be at leisure for prayer. Satan often thrusts temptations to unholy thoughts even amid the holiest exercises, and would take advantage of incontinency, so they are to come together again lest Satan tempt them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: These directions about marriage and temporary separation are given by way of permission, not as a commandment. Paul allows them; he does not impose them as divine command. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Paul wishes all men were even as himself, having the gift of continence — but this wish holds only relatively to the present distress, not absolutely, else the extension of mankind and of the church would cease. Each has his proper gift from God, one in one way and another in another. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: To the unmarried in general, of both sexes, and to widows in particular, Paul says it is good for them to abide even as he does — unmarried. The counsel is for their good under present conditions, not a command. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: If they cannot contain, let them marry, for it is better to marry than to burn with the secret flame of lust, which lays waste the whole inner man. The dew of God's grace is needed to stifle that flame, which otherwise would thrust men at last into hell-fire. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: To the married Paul gives not merely his own word but the Lord's direct authority: let not the wife be separated from her husband. The separation on either side, whether owing to husband or wife, is forbidden, resting on the Lord's own teaching. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: If separation has nevertheless been committed, a second sin must not be added: the separated wife must remain unmarried or be reconciled to her husband, by appeasing his displeasure and recovering his good will. Likewise the husband must not put away his wife, the only exception being fornication. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: To the rest — those in mixed marriages with unbelievers, the other class the Corinthians asked about — Paul speaks with no direct command from the Lord, but with apostolic judgment. If an unbelieving husband consents to dwell with his believing wife, she must not put him away. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Likewise, if a believing woman has an unbelieving husband who consents to dwell with her, she must not put him away. Under Greek and Roman law the wife had power to effect divorce, but the believer must not use it here. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: The unbelieving husband is sanctified in the believing wife, and the unbelieving wife in the believing husband — those inseparably connected with God's people are hallowed thereby, so the believer may retain the connection without impairing his own sanctity, and the unbeliever is prepared for becoming inwardly sanctified by faith. Otherwise the children would be unclean, beyond the hallowed pale; but now they are holy, since the faith of one Christian parent gives children a near relationship to the church, just as children were included in the covenant made with Abraham and his seed. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: If the unbelieving partner wishes for separation, offended by the believer's Christianity and refusing to live with her unless she renounce it, let him depart. The brother or sister is not under bondage in such a case — not bound to renounce the faith to retain the unbelieving spouse — though the believer is not bound to separate when the unbeliever stays. God has called us to peace, so the believer should not ordinarily depart, nor force the departing party to stay in continual discord. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: What do you know but that by staying you may save your unbelieving partner? The precept to stay is enforced by this hope, as Ruth became a convert to her husband's faith and an unbelieving husband may be won by a believing wife. Conversely, if the unbeliever wishes to depart, let him go, for you cannot be sure of converting him so as to be obligated at all costs to stay against his will. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: The general principle stands: as the Lord has allotted to each and as God has called each, so let him walk. The heavenly calling does not set aside earthly callings, and this direction must not be made a ground for separating of oneself. Paul ordains the same in all churches. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: If a man was called being circumcised, let him not become uncircumcised, as some in excess of anti-Jewish feeling might attempt; if called in uncircumcision, let him not be circumcised, as Judaizers demanded. Outward religious marks are not to be anxiously reversed. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Circumcision is nothing and uncircumcision is nothing; what matters is the keeping of the commandments of God — defined elsewhere as faith working by love and the new creature. Circumcision was once God's commandment, but not forever, as love is. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Let every man abide in the same calling — the same condition, Jew, Greek, slave, or freeman — in which he was called. The station itself is not the obstacle to godliness. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: If called being a servant, let it not trouble him; he is to be content in his existing condition, since in union with Christ outward disparities are compensated. Yet if he may lawfully be made free, he should use the opportunity rather than neglect it — the gospel's spirit is against slavery, and the precept not to become servants of men shows slavery is abnormal, though unlawful means of escape are not advised. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: He who is called in the Lord being a servant is the Lord's freedman — spiritually made free from sin, from the law, and from circumcision. And he who is called being free is Christ's servant, for love makes Christ's service perfect freedom. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: All believers are bought with the blood of Christ, whatever their earthly state, so they must not become servants of men — neither externally, where that applies, nor spiritually, as servile adherents to party leaders at Corinth. The outward and inward conditions should correspond, with the former subservient to the latter. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Let every man abide with God in the calling in which he was called, chiefly careful of his footing toward God rather than toward men. This limits the earlier precept: if a calling is unfavorable to abiding with God in holy fellowship, a man may use lawful means to change from it. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Concerning virgins Paul has no express commandment from the Lord, but gives his judgment under the ordinary inspiration accompanying the apostles in their canonical writings — a recommendation the Corinthians are free to adopt, not a positive command like the Lord's own teaching on divorce. He attributes his apostleship and its gifts to God's mercy alone, and claims faithfulness in dispensing the directions he received. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Paul considers it good for a man to remain in his present state, by reason of the present distress — the troubles beginning to fall on believers, which would prevail before the destruction of Jerusalem, making the married state less desirable than the single. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Neither the married, those bound to a wife, nor the unmarried, those loosed from a wife, are to seek a change of state. The principle of abiding applies to both alike. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Those who marry do not sin, but they will have trouble in the flesh — in their outward state by reason of the present distress, not sin, which is the trouble of the spirit. Paul's motive in advising as he does is to spare them such trouble. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: The time, the season of this present dispensation up to the Lord's coming, is shortened, contracted. For the remaining time, those who have wives should be as though they had none — we ought to consider nothing as our own in real or permanent possession. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: Likewise those who weep should be as though they wept not, those who rejoice as though they rejoiced not, and those who buy as though they possessed not. The Christian will not hold fast to possessions as his enduring substance, for that is elsewhere; the men of Sodom were condemned not merely for open profligacy but for living as though their all were in this world. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: Those who use this world should not abuse it by using it to the full as their chief aim. We are to use it, not take our fill of its pursuits; as planets turn on their own axes yet revolve round the sun, so while we do our part in our worldly sphere, God is to be the center of all desires. The fashion of this world is a fleeting form, a shifting stage scene, actually passing away now. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: Paul would have them without distracting cares. The unmarried man, if he uses aright the advantages of his condition, cares for the things of the Lord, how he may please the Lord. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: But the married man cares for the things of the world, how he may please his wife, and his attention is divided. This is the counterpart to the unmarried man's undistracted care for the Lord, and the reason celibacy can be expedient in distressful times. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: The same difference holds for women: the unmarried woman and the virgin care for the things of the Lord, that she may be holy in body and spirit, while the married woman cares for the things of the world, how she may please her husband. A woman undergoes a greater change of condition than a man in marrying. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: Paul speaks for their own profit, not to display apostolic authority, and not to cast a snare over them — not to entangle them with fear of sin where there is no sin. His aim is what is comely under present circumstances, that they may attend upon the Lord without distraction, assiduously waiting on him as Mary sat and Anna served in the temple. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: If a father behaves uncomely toward his virgin daughter by leaving her unmarried beyond the flower of her age, debarring her from the lawful gratification of her feelings as a marriageable woman, and need so requires out of regard for her welfare, let them marry — the daughter and her suitor — and he does not sin. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

37 COMMENTARY: But the father who stands steadfast, not turned from his purpose by the world's obloquy, having no necessity arising from his daughter's inclinations, and having power over his own will because his daughter's will does not oppose his, and who has so determined in his heart, does well to keep her unmarried. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

38 COMMENTARY: So he who gives his virgin daughter in marriage does well, but he who gives her not in marriage does better, under the present distress and for undistracted attendance on the Lord. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

39 COMMENTARY: The wife is bound as long as her husband lives, but if her husband dies she is free to marry whom she will, only in the Lord — only a Christian. The restriction guards the believer against unequal alliance. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

40 COMMENTARY: In Paul's judgment, the widow is happier if she abides as she is, unmarried, for the reasons already given about trouble in the flesh and undistracted service. He adds that he also thinks he has the Spirit of God — 'think' here is not doubt but well-grounded assurance, his opinion given by inspiration. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
