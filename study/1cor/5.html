Study Notes — 1 Corinthians

Chapter 5

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: It was actually reported — and the Corinthians themselves had not told Paul, though they wrote to him about other matters — that a gross fornication existed among them not even found among the heathen: a man keeping his father's wife, his stepmother, while his father still lived. The woman was perhaps a heathen, which is why Paul's rebuke is directed at the man rather than her. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Instead of mourning, the Corinthians were puffed up with their own wisdom and their favorite teachers' eloquence, at a time when they ought to have mourned the scandal to religion. Paul mourned because they did not mourn; believers ought to mourn over others' transgressions and repent of their own. Their mourning should have led to the offender being removed from among them by excommunication — a discipline that in fact brought him to bitter repentance before the second epistle. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Though absent in body, Paul was present in spirit and had already judged the one who perpetrated so scandalous a deed while called a brother. The word for what he did is stronger than mere doing — it marks a deed done scandalously. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The sentence was to be passed in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, by his authority and as representing his person and will, when the church was gathered together with Paul's spirit and the power of the Lord Jesus. Christ promised his power to his church gathered in his name, and Paul by inspiration gave a special promise that his apostolic judgment, guided by the Spirit, would ratify their decree as if he were present in person. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Beyond excommunication, which the church itself could enact, Paul delegated his own apostolic power to deliver such a one to Satan for the destruction of the flesh — affliction of the body, even death, so as to destroy fleshly lust, not the body itself, which will share in redemption. The aim was that the spirit might be saved in the day of the Lord, since temporary affliction often leads to permanent salvation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Their glorying in their attainments and favorite teachers, while conniving at such a scandal, was unseemly. A little leaven leavens the whole lump, both by present complicity in the guilt and by the danger of future contagion. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: The old leaven — the remnant of heathenish and natural corruption — must be purged out, as Jews searched every corner of their houses for leaven before the Passover. Christians are continually to search and purify their hearts, and to correspond in fact with their normal state as unleavened, since Christ our Passover has already been sacrificed; the Christian feast of joy in his finished work lasts all our time, not one season, and in no part of it is the leaven of evil to be admitted. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: The feast is to be kept not with the old leaven of the unconverted state, but with unleavened bread of sincerity and truth. Malice is the opposite of sincerity, which allows no leaven of evil mixed with good; wickedness is the opposite of truth, which does not mistake evil for good. Malice is the evil habit of mind, wickedness its outworking in word and deed, and sincerity is what, examined by sunlight, is found pure and unadulterated. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Paul refers to a former epistle, now lost, in which he wrote not to keep company with fornicators. That he means a former letter, not the present one, is proved because no such direction occurs earlier in this epistle; it was probably a brief reply to Corinthian inquiries, superseded by this fuller epistle and not preserved because the Spirit did not design it for the church's general guidance. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: The prohibition is limited: it does not mean utterly foregoing intercourse with fornicators, covetous, extortioners, or idolaters of the unbelieving world, for in dissolute Corinth that would mean companying with almost no one. Believers are to remain in the world but keep themselves from its evil; attempting to get out of the world altogether led to monasticism and its evils. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: The case is different with a man called a brother who is a fornicator, covetous, an idolater, a railer, a drunkard, or an extortioner — with such a one believers are not even to eat, whether at love-feasts or in private, much less at the Lord's table. There is less danger in associating with open worldlings than with carnal professors, and covetousness is joined with fornication because both spring from the creature's fierce longing, turned from God, to fill itself with inferior objects of sense. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Paul's concern is not with unbelievers outside the church but with those within, whom the church itself must judge — you judge your fellow citizens, not strangers. God will judge those outside, and he is the Judge of the heathen's salvation, not believers. Paul here also anticipates his censure of their lawsuits before heathen tribunals instead of judging causes among themselves. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Those outside God judges; the church's sentence is to put away from among yourselves that wicked person, in language taken from Deuteronomy. Excommunication is the church's own act of purging. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
