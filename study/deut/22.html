Study Notes — Deuteronomy

Chapter 22

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: You shall not see your brother's ox or sheep go astray, and hide yourself from them; you shall in any case bring them again to your brother, religion teaching neighbourliness and readiness to do good offices. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: And if your brother is not near you, or if you do not know him, then you shall bring it to your own house, and it shall be with you until your brother seeks after it, and you shall restore it to him again. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: In like manner shall you do with his ass, and so with his raiment, and with all lost things of your brother's, which he has lost and you have found; you may not hide yourself from them. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Hiding from a fallen beast is the same failure as hiding from a stray one: Henry's neighbourliness point covers both, for religion forbids passing by when a lift would restore your brother's animal. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: The woman shall not wear that which pertains to a man, neither shall a man put on a woman's garment, for all that do so are abomination to the Lord; even in dress, all must be done with regard to preserving purity in heart and action. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: If a bird's nest chance to be before you in the way in any tree or on the ground, whether with young ones or eggs, and the dam sitting upon the young or eggs, you shall not take the dam with the young. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: But you shall in any wise let the dam go, and take the young to yourself, that it may be well with you, and that you may prolong your days; God's providence extends to the smallest affairs. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: When you build a new house, then you shall make a battlement for your roof, that you bring not blood upon your house, if any man fall from there, care for life extending to the structure of the home. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Sowing mixed seed is a small affair with a great meaning in Henry's section: even field work must show a single eye and a simple heart, keeping kinds distinct rather than mingling what God separated. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: An ox and an ass plow at different strengths: yoking them together is the section's small-affair example of a people whose behaviour must be all of a piece, not joined to what pulls another way. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Mixed cloth is the same lesson worn on the body: garments, like eating and drinking, must be done with regard to purity and to God's will rather than to fashion. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Even a garment's fringe falls under the section's rule: the smallest precepts count as great things when they keep God's people mindful of his will rather than of the world's fashions. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Henry's impurity section opens here with a husband's hatred turned into a public charge: the law restrains lust not only in the act but in the slander that follows a marriage gone cold. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: An evil name raised against a wife is itself the offence the elders must try: the tokens, not the husband's word, decide whether the charge stands. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Proof is public, at the gate before the elders: a private accusation cannot stand against evidence produced in the open. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: The father speaks for his daughter's honour before the court: the case is tried as a matter of a family's standing, not settled by the husband's say-so. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: The cloth spread before the elders makes the verdict visible: innocence is shown, not merely claimed, and the false accuser is answered in the same public place. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Henry groups this with the laws against impurity (vv 13-30), which lay restraint on fleshly lusts that war against the soul; here that restraint takes the form of the elders' public discipline of the false accuser. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: The penalty joins money to permanence: a hundred shekels to the father, and a marriage he may never dissolve, so the slanderer cannot profit by casting off the wife he defamed. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Where the tokens fail, the law's restraint falls the other way: the same public process that vindicates a falsely accused wife condemns one proved unfaithful before marriage. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: The sentence is carried out at her father's door, where the offence began: folly wrought in Israel is purged publicly, at the house whose honour was betrayed. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: Adultery takes both parties: the law refuses the double standard of sparing one and condemning the other, and purging evil means both deaths. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: Betrothal already binds: the damsel is treated as a wife in law, so the offence is against her husband's household before any marriage feast. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: The city is the test: help was within call, so her silence is read as consent, while his guilt needs no such test, for he humbled his neighbour's wife. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: The field reverses the presumption: no one could hear her cry, so the man alone dies and the damsel's innocence is presumed from the place itself. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: The damsel's case is likened to murder of the victim: as a slain man bears no guilt for being attacked, so she bears none for being forced. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: The field case shows the restraint running the other way too: the law distinguishes force from consent by place and cry, so that a woman overpowered where no help could come is not punished for another's lust. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: With no betrothal binding her, the law takes another course: the man does not die, but must marry and pay, so that force carries a lifelong obligation rather than a death sentence. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: The price and the permanence go together: fifty shekels to her father, and a wife he may never put away, so the humbling is answered by obligation, not by death. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: The chapter's restraint closes at the father's household: taking his father's wife breaches the nearest boundary, and Henry's section leaves such lusts under the same restraint as all the cases before it. — Matthew Henry
