Study Notes — Ezra

Chapter 4

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: When the adversaries of Judah and Benjamin heard that the returned exiles were building the temple, they took notice at once. Henry notes opposition begins the moment God's work visibly resumes. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: The adversaries offered to build with them, claiming they sought the same God and had sacrificed to him since Esarhaddon brought them up. JFB notes the offer was a snare: their worship was mixed, and union would have corrupted the work it pretended to help. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Zerubbabel, Jeshua and the chief fathers refused: 'Ye have nothing to do with us to build an house unto our God.' Henry defends the refusal: the temple was Israel's by Cyrus's commission, and a mixed building company would have made a mixed religion. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Then the people of the land weakened the hands of Judah and troubled them in building. Open help refused, the adversaries turned to discouragement, the usual second weapon against God's work. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: They hired counsellors against them to frustrate their purpose all the days of Cyrus, even until Darius. JFB notes the long, paid lobbying at court: opposition to the temple was a sustained political campaign, not a passing quarrel. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: In the beginning of Ahasuerus's reign they wrote an accusation against Judah and Jerusalem. Henry notes the accusers waited for a new king, hoping a new reign would mean a new policy. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: In the days of Artaxerxes, Bishlam, Mithredath, Tabeel and their companions wrote to the king, the letter written in the Syrian tongue and interpreted in Syrian. JFB notes the language detail shows this was a formal state paper, not a private complaint. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Rehum the chancellor and Shimshai the scribe wrote a letter against Jerusalem to Artaxerxes. The highest provincial officials now lead the charge, giving private enmity the weight of office. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: The letter is signed by Rehum, Shimshai and their companions, a roll of peoples settled in Samaria: Dinaites, Apharsathchites, Tarpelites, Apharsites, Archevites, Babylonians, Susanchites, Dehavites and Elamites. JFB identifies them as the mixed colonies planted by Assyria in the empty land. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: The rest of the nations whom the great and noble Asnapper brought over and set in Samaria and beyond the river join the complaint. Henry notes the accusers boast of an Assyrian king's settlement as their title, against Israel's title from God and Cyrus. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: This is the copy of the letter sent to Artaxerxes, from his servants beyond the river. The record preserves the enemy's own words, so that the reader can judge the charge on its face. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: The charge is that the Jews come from the king to Jerusalem are building 'the rebellious and the bad city,' setting up its walls and joining its foundations. JFB notes the slander turns temple-building into city-fortifying, a political threat rather than a religious work. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: They warn that if the city is built and the walls set up, toll, tribute and custom will cease, damaging the king's revenue. Henry notes the appeal is to the king's purse, the argument most likely to move a court. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Because they have maintenance from the king's palace, they say, it is not meet for them to see the king's dishonour, so they have certified the king. JFB notes the pretended loyalty: servants of the palace pose as guardians of the king's honour while serving their own hatred. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: They urge a search in the records of his fathers, which will show Jerusalem a rebellious city, hurtful to kings and provinces, moving sedition of old time, for which it was destroyed. Henry notes the half-truth: Jerusalem had rebelled, but against Babylon, not against Persia, and its punishment was God's, not a standing treason. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: They certify that if the city is built and its walls set up, the king will have no portion beyond the river. The warning escalates from lost revenue to lost empire, the standard exaggeration of accusers. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: The king sent an answer to Rehum, Shimshai and their companions in Samaria and beyond the river: 'Peace.' Henry notes the irony: a greeting of peace introduces an order that will stop God's work by force. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: 'The letter which ye sent unto us hath been plainly read before me.' The king's answer begins with procedure, showing the accusation was formally received and read in council. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Search was made, and it was found that Jerusalem of old time had made insurrection against kings, with rebellion and sedition therein. JFB notes the records did show revolts, but the king reads them without distinguishing the kings or the causes. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Mighty kings had ruled over Jerusalem and over all countries beyond the river, to whom toll, tribute and custom were paid. The king's fear is fed by Jerusalem's former greatness, not its present weakness. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: The king commands these men to cease, and the city not to be built, until another commandment is given from him. Henry notes the saving clause: the stop is not final in form, and God will use that opening in a later reign. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: 'Take heed now that ye fail not to do this: why should damage grow to the hurt of the kings?' The order presses for immediate obedience, leaving no room for appeal or delay. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: When the king's letter was read before Rehum, Shimshai and their companions, they went up in haste to Jerusalem and made the Jews cease by force and power. JFB notes the haste: enemies of God's work lose no time in executing a favourable decree. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Then the work of the house of God at Jerusalem ceased, until the second year of Darius. Henry notes the long pause: from Artaxerxes back to Darius the narrative leaps years, and the temple stood unfinished while the people waited on God's time. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
