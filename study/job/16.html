Study Notes — Job

Chapter 16

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Job answers Eliphaz, rejecting his second speech as he rejected the first. The reply names the friends' comfort for what it is. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Job has heard many such things: miserable comforters are you all. JFB leaves the verdict to stand on its own, the friends' speeches being the miserable comfort in question. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Shall vain words have an end, or what emboldens you that you answer? JFB notes Job retorts Eliphaz's reproach of words of wind, and emboldeneth as what wearies you so that you contradict, or what provoked you. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Job also could speak as the friends do, heap up words against them, and shake his head at them. JFB renders heap up as marshalling an army of words, and shaking the head as nodding in Eastern scorn. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: But Job would strengthen them with his mouth, and the moving of his lips would assuage their grief. JFB calls this bitter irony: Job could offer the same heartless mouth-strengthening, opposed to strengthening with the heart, real consolation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Though Job speaks, his grief is not assuaged, and though he forbears, what goes from him? JFB renders eased literally as what portion of his sufferings goes from him — nothing, either way. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: But now God has made Job weary, and has desolated all his company. JFB renders but now as ah, and company as a band of witnesses who could attest his innocence, his children and servants, now cut off. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: God has filled Job with wrinkles, which rise up against him as a witness, and his leanness testifies to his face. JFB explains the Hebrew as fettered: God has disabled him by pains from attesting his innocence, and his leanness, also meaning lie, is a false witness construed as proof of guilt. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: He tears Job in his wrath and hates him, gnashing upon him with his teeth, while his enemy sharpens his eyes upon him. JFB gives the wild beast image, and renders hateth as pursues hard, with God sharpening his eyes as an enemy. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Men have gaped upon Job with their mouth, smitten him upon the cheek reproachfully, and gathered themselves against him. JFB notes gaping is to mock, not devour, smiting the cheek is contemptuous abuse, and gathering is conspiring unanimously. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: God has delivered Job to the ungodly and turned him over into the hands of the wicked. JFB identifies the ungodly as his professed friends persecuting him with unkind speeches, and turned over as cast headlong into the wicked's hands. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Job was at ease, but God broke him asunder, took him by the neck, shook him to pieces, and set him up for his mark. JFB notes the animal seizing prey by the neck, and the mark as God letting Job recover strength only to torment him ceaselessly. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: God's archers compass Job round about, cleaving his reins asunder without sparing, and pouring out his gall upon the ground. JFB continues the mark image: God is accompanied by the three friends, whose words wound like sharp arrows, and gall stands for a vital part. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: God breaks Job with breach upon breach, running upon him like a giant. JFB gives the fortress image: storming walls by making breaches, with the giant as a mighty warrior. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Job has sewed sackcloth upon his skin and defiled his horn in the dust. JFB explains the sack as closely sewed to the body, and the horn, emblem of power, thrown in the dust as humiliation from former greatness, joined with the fury of a horned beast tearing earth. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Job's face is foul with weeping, and on his eyelids is the shadow of death. JFB renders foul as red, flushed and heated, and the shadow of death as darkening through many tears. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Not for any injustice in Job's hands, and his prayer is pure. The claim stands against the friends' charge: his hands hold no injustice, his prayer is pure. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: O earth, cover not Job's blood, and let his cry have no place. JFB explains his blood as his undeserved suffering, compared to a murdered man whose blood the earth refuses to drink until avenged, and no place as no resting-place for his cry. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Also now, behold, Job's witness is in heaven, and his record is on high. JFB notes that even now, greatly misunderstood on earth, God in heaven is sensible of his innocence, and the record is in the high places — his witness. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Job's friends scorn him, but his eye pours out tears unto God. JFB renders the friends as his mockers, a heart-cutting paradox, leaving God alone to attest his innocence. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Oh that one might plead for a man with God, as a man pleads for his neighbour! JFB renders one as He, God himself: God must support Job against God, since he makes him suffer yet alone knows his innocence, prefiguring the advocacy of Christ. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: When a few years are come, Job shall go the way whence he shall not return. JFB notes few as years of number, opposed to numberless: his remaining years are countable and short. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
