Study Notes — Job

Chapter 42

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Then Job answered the LORD, and said. JFB heads this chapter as Job's penitent reply, the answer to God's second address. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: I know that thou canst do every thing, and that no thought can be withheld from thee. JFB explains that in the first clause Job owns God to be omnipotent over nature, as contrasted with his own feebleness, and in the second that God is supremely just in all his dealings, with thought as purpose: while to Job's finite view God's plans seem bad, to the All-wise One they continue unhindered and will at last be seen to be as good as they are infinitely wise. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Who is he that hides counsel without knowledge? Therefore have I uttered that I understood not, things too wonderful for me, which I knew not. JFB notes Job in God's own words expresses his deep and humble penitence: God's word concerning our guilt should form the groundwork of confession, and Job in omitting by words goes even further than God's accusation, confessing not merely his words but his whole thoughts and ways were without knowledge, with too wonderful as his rash denial that God has any fixed plan in governing human affairs merely because that plan was too wonderful for his comprehension. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Hear, I beseech thee, and I will speak: I will demand of thee, and declare thou unto me. JFB notes that when Job said hear, his demand convicted him of being without knowledge: God alone could speak thus to Job, not Job to God, so Job quotes again God's words as the groundwork of retracting his own foolish words. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear, but now mine eye sees thee. JFB explains hearing and seeing as often in antithesis, and seeth not as God's face but his presence in the veil of a dark cloud: Job implies that besides this literal seeing he now saw spiritually what he had indistinctly taken on hearsay before, concerning God's infinite wisdom. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Wherefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes. JFB renders myself as I abhor, and retract the rash speeches made against God, marking the end of the poetical dialogue and the beginning of the prose epilogue. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: And it was so, that after the LORD had spoken these words unto Job, the LORD said to Eliphaz the Temanite, my wrath is kindled against thee, and against thy two friends: for ye have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job has. JFB explains to Eliphaz because he was the foremost of the three friends, their speeches being but the echo of his, and right literally as well-grounded, sure and true: their spirit towards Job was unkindly, and to justify themselves they used false arguments, namely that calamities always prove peculiar guilt, so though it was for God they spoke falsely, God reproves them; Job had spoken rightly in relation to them, denying their theory and the alleged fact of peculiar guilt and hypocrisy, but wrongly in relation to God, and that extreme he has now repented of, so God speaks of him as now altogether right. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Therefore take unto you now seven bullocks and seven rams, and go to my servant Job, and offer up for yourselves a burnt offering, and my servant Job shall pray for you: for him will I accept, lest I deal with you after your folly, in that ye have not spoken of me the thing which is right, like my servant Job. JFB notes seven as the number offered by the Gentile prophet, and that Job plainly lived before the legal priesthood, with patriarchs acting as priests for their families and sometimes as praying mediators, foreshadowing the true Mediator, though sacrifice accompanies and is the groundwork on which mediation rests, with him as his person only, which must first be accepted before God can accept his offering, and folly as impiety. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: So Eliphaz the Temanite and Bildad the Shuhite and Zophar the Naamathite went, and did according as the LORD commanded them: the LORD also accepted Job. JFB notes the forgiving spirit of Job foreshadows the love of Christ and of Christians to enemies. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: And the LORD turned the captivity of Job, when he prayed for his friends: also the LORD gave Job twice as much as he had before. JFB renders turned captivity as proverbial for restored, or amply indemnified for all he had lost, so that the future vindication of man against Satan at the resurrection has its earnest in the temporal vindication of Job by Jehovah in person, and twice as the glorious recompense following intercession for enemies. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Then came there unto him all his brethren, and all his sisters, and all they that had been of his acquaintance before, and did eat bread with him in his house, and they bemoaned him, and comforted him over all the evil that the LORD had brought upon him: every man also gave him a piece of money, and every one an earring of gold. JFB notes it was Job's complaint in his misery that his brethren were estranged from him, and these now return with the return of his prosperity, with swallow friends leaving in winter and returning with spring, eat bread in token of friendship, piece of money as presents usual in visiting a man of rank after a calamity, the Hebrew kesita, weighed out not coined, likely equal to four shekels, and earring whether for nose or ear, much gold in the East being in the shape of ornaments in the absence of banks. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: So the LORD blessed the latter end of Job more than his beginning: for he had fourteen thousand sheep, and six thousand camels, and a thousand yoke of oxen, and a thousand she asses. JFB notes the blessing came probably by degrees, not all at once. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: He had also seven sons and three daughters. JFB notes the same number as before, perhaps by a second wife, his first wife being last mentioned in chapter 19. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: And he called the name of the first, Jemima, and the name of the second, Kezia, and the name of the third, Keren-happuch. JFB explains the names as significant of restored prosperity: Jemima as daylight, after his night of calamity, or a dove, Kezia as cassia, an aromatic herb, instead of his offensive breath and ulcers, and Keren-happuch as horn of stibium, a paint with which females dyed their eyelids, in contrast to his horn defiled in the dust, the names also implying the beauty of his daughters. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: And in all the land were no women found so fair as the daughters of Job: and their father gave them inheritance among their brethren. JFB notes inheritance among brethren as an unusual favour in the East to daughters, who in the Jewish law only inherited if there were no sons, a proof of wealth and unanimity. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: After this lived Job a hundred and forty years, and saw his sons, and his sons' sons, even four generations. JFB notes the Septuagint makes Job live a hundred seventy years after his calamity and two hundred forty in all, which would make him seventy at the time of his calamity, and that sons' sons are a proof of divine favour. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: So Job died, being old and full of days. JFB renders full of days as fully sated and contented with all the happiness that life could give him, realizing what Eliphaz had painted as the lot of the godly, and notes the Septuagint adds that he will rise again with those whom the Lord will raise up, perhaps derived spuriously from Matthew 27. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
