Study Notes — Job

Chapter 10

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Job's soul is weary of his life, so he will leave his complaint upon himself and speak in bitterness. JFB renders it as giving loose to his complaint, as he resolved in chapter 7. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Job will say to God: do not condemn me; show me why you contend with me. JFB explains the plea: do not treat me as guilty by mere sovereignty without showing the reasons. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Is it good for God to oppress, to despise the work of his hands, and shine on the counsel of the wicked? JFB notes Job cannot think God takes pleasure in using power to oppress the weak, and shine means favouring with prosperity. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Has God eyes of flesh, or does he see as man sees? JFB asks whether God sees as feebly as man, with the same uncharitable eye as Job's friends. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Are God's days as the days of man, or his years as man's days? The question presses whether God's time is as short as man's, explaining the rapid succession of his strokes. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: God searches after Job's iniquity and seeks out his sin, as if time pressed. JFB links this to the preceding questions: the rapid strokes look as though God had no time to spare in overwhelming him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: God knows Job is not wicked, yet there is none who can deliver out of his hand. JFB notes the omniscient God knows, yet searches after sin, though he has no need to deal with the rapid violence man would use. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: God's hands have made Job and fashioned him together round about, yet he destroys him. JFB notes made implies a work of difficulty and art, and together round about implies a complete unity whose parts on all sides bear the closest scrutiny. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Remember that God made Job as clay, and will bring him into dust again. JFB notes the reference is less to perishable material than to wonderful fashioning by the divine potter. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: God poured Job out as milk and curdled him like cheese. JFB explains the body's organization from rude liquid beginnings gradually assuming solid consistency, like milk curdling into cheese. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: God clothed Job with skin and flesh and fenced him with bones and sinews. JFB renders fenced as inlaid or curiously wrought, the skin first, then flesh, then harder parts. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: God granted Job life and favour, and his visitation preserved his spirit. JFB explains visitation as God's watchful providence, and spirit as breath. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: These things God hid in his heart, and Job knows this is with him. JFB explains that all God's dealings with Job in creation, preservation, and present affliction were part of his secret counsel. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: If Job sins, God marks him and will not acquit him from his iniquity. JFB notes Job's perplexity: God marks every sin with ceaseless rigour, whether the man is wicked or comparatively righteous. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: If Job is wicked, woe to him; if righteous, he still cannot lift up his head, being full of confusion and seeing his affliction. JFB explains lifting the head as conscious innocence, and seeing affliction as seeing only what seems to prove him guilty. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: If Job's head increases, God hunts him as a fierce lion and shows himself marvellous upon him. JFB renders it as if he lifts his head God would hunt him, like a lion that does not kill at once but returns to torture again. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: God renews his witnesses against Job and increases indignation upon him, with changes and war against him. JFB explains the witnesses as accumulated trials, like witnesses brought to wear out the accused, and changes and war as host after host, afflictions then reproaches. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Why then did God bring Job forth out of the womb, that he might give up the ghost unseen? The question returns to chapter 3: birth itself seems the first wrong. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Oh that Job had been as though he had not been, carried from the womb to the grave. Unborn or stillborn, he would never have entered the suffering he now endures. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Are not Job's days few? Let God cease and leave him alone, that he may take comfort a little. JFB notes that since he was destined from birth to these ills, he asks at least a little breathing time during his few remaining days. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Before Job goes whence he shall not return, to the land of darkness and the shadow of death. The request for respite is bounded by the grave's finality. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: A land of darkness, as darkness itself, of the shadow of death, without any order, where light is as darkness. JFB notes three Hebrew words for darkness here, and that its only sunshine is thick darkness — a bold poetic figure, though Job in a better frame has brighter thoughts of the unseen world. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
