Study Notes — Job

Chapter 27

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Job continues his parable, after the friends fall silent. JFB notes it was Zophar's turn, but he and the others were silent, virtually admitting defeat, so after a pause Job proceeds. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: As God lives, who has taken away Job's judgment, and the Almighty, who has vexed his soul. JFB notes taken away judgment foreshadows Christ's taken judgment, and that God will not give Job his right by declaring his innocence, with vexed as made bitter. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: All the while Job's breath is in him, and the spirit of God is in his nostrils. JFB notes this implies knowledge that the living soul was breathed into man by God, and Maurer's reading: as yet all my breath is in me, the reason he can speak so boldly. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Job's lips shall not speak wickedness, nor his tongue utter deceit. JFB notes the deceit would be admitting guilt against his conscience's witness. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: God forbid that Job should justify the friends; till he dies he will not remove his integrity from him. JFB explains justify as approving their views, and integrity as what they deny on account of his misfortunes. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Job holds fast his righteousness and will not let it go; his heart shall not reproach him so long as he lives. JFB renders heart as conscience, reproaching not one of his days, not repenting of any day since he came into existence. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Let Job's enemy be as the wicked, and he that rises up against him as the unrighteous. JFB explains this not as a curse on enemies, but as accounting an opponent of his innocence as actuated by criminal hostility. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: For what is the hope of the hypocrite, though he has gained, when God takes away his soul? JFB notes gained is antithetic to taketh away, which literally draws the soul out of the body as from a scabbard, and that Job admits Bildad's and Zophar's point yet proves he is no hypocrite by still calling on God amid trials. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Will God hear the hypocrite's cry when trouble comes upon him? The hypocrite's cry in trouble goes unheard, unlike Job's persistent calling. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Will the hypocrite delight himself in the Almighty, and always call upon God? JFB notes he may call in prosperity to seem religious, but will not, as Job does, call in calamities verging on death. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Job will teach the friends concerning the hand of God, and what is with the Almighty he will not conceal. JFB notes verses 11-23 state not so much Job's own sentiments as what Zophar would have said, stated more strongly, concerning God's hand in governing men and the counsel regulating his dealings. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Behold, you all yourselves have seen it; why then are you thus altogether vain? JFB notes the friends themselves see the wicked often afflicted, though often the reverse, yet vainly make affliction an argument to prove Job wicked. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: This is the portion of a wicked man with God, and the heritage of oppressors received from the Almighty. JFB links portion and heritage to Zophar's argument in chapter 20, now stated by Job. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: If the wicked's children are multiplied, it is for the sword, and his offspring shall not be satisfied with bread. JFB notes his family only increases to perish by sword or famine. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Those that remain of him shall be buried in death, and his widows shall not weep. JFB explains those escaping war and famine shall be buried by the deadly plague, death personified performing funeral rites with no one else, and widows as their widows. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Though the wicked heaps up silver as the dust, and prepares raiment as the clay. JFB explains dust and clay as images of multitudes, and many changes of raiment as a chief constituent of Eastern wealth. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: He prepares it, but the just shall put it on, and the innocent shall divide the silver. The wicked's hoard passes to the just by introverted parallelism. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: He builds his house as a moth, and as a booth which the keeper makes. JFB explains the moth worm's house, broken whenever raiment is shaken out, and the booth as a bough-formed hut a vineyard guard raises for temporary shelter. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: The rich man shall lie down, but he shall not be gathered; he opens his eyes, and he is not. JFB notes the readings: honourably buried yet seeing destruction in the unseen world, or lying rich in bed with nothing robbed from him, opening his eyes to find nothing more there. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Terrors take hold on him as waters, and a tempest steals him away in the night. JFB compares the terrors to a sudden violent flood. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: The east wind carries him away, and he departs, and as a storm hurls him out of his place. The wind that carried others' hopes now carries the wicked himself. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: For God shall cast upon him, and not spare; he would fain flee out of his hand. JFB explains cast as thunderbolts cast upon him, unspared, with flight impossible. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Men shall clap their hands at him, and hiss him out of his place. JFB explains clapping as for joy at his downfall, and hissing as derision, alluding to Bildad's words. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
