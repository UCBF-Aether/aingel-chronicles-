Study Notes — Job

Chapter 40

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Moreover the LORD answered Job, and said. JFB introduces God's second address: he had paused for a reply, but Job was silent. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Shall he that contends with the Almighty instruct him? He that reproves God, let him answer it. JFB renders he that contendeth as Job, who had so often expressed a wish to contend with God, or rebuketh: does Job now still wish to set God right, and must he answer the questions God has asked? — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Then Job answered the LORD, and said. JFB notes the Lord here is JEHOVAH, now addressed by a humbled Job. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Behold, I am vile; what shall I answer thee? I will lay mine hand upon my mouth. JFB explains that it is a very different thing to vindicate ourselves before God from what it is before men: Job could do the latter, not the former, and laying hand upon mouth means having no plea to offer. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Once have I spoken, but I will not answer: yea, twice, but I will proceed no further. JFB renders once and twice as oftentimes, more than once, with I have spoken as namely against God, and not answer as not pleading against him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Then answered the LORD unto Job out of the whirlwind, and said. JFB notes again the Lord is JEHOVAH, speaking a second time from the whirlwind. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Gird up thy loins now like a man: God will demand of Job, and Job shall declare unto him. JFB notes that since Job has not only spoken against God but accused him of injustice, God challenges him to try whether he could govern the world as God by his power does, and punish the proud and wicked. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Wilt thou also disannul God's judgment? Wilt thou condemn him, that thou mayest be righteous? JFB explains that Job would not only contend with God but set aside his judgment or justice in the government of the world, and condemn as declaring God unrighteous in order that Job may be accounted righteous. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Hast thou an arm like God? Or canst thou thunder with a voice like him? JFB renders arm as God's omnipotence, and thunder as God's voice. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Deck thyself now with majesty and excellency, and array thyself with glory and beauty. JFB asks whether Job has power and majesty like God's to enable him to judge and govern the world. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Cast abroad the rage of thy wrath, and behold every one that is proud, and abase him. JFB renders rage as pouring out the redundant floods of wrath, and behold as trying whether Job, as God, by a mere glance can abase the proud. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Look on every one that is proud, and bring him low, and tread down the wicked in their place. JFB renders proud as high, and in their place as on the spot, suddenly, before they can move from their place. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Hide them in the dust together, and bind their faces in secret. JFB explains abase and remove them out of the sight of men, with bind faces as shutting up their persons, or rather the custom of binding a cloth over the faces of persons about to be executed, and in secret as consigning them to darkness. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Then will God also confess unto Job that his own right hand can save him. JFB renders confess as extol: since Job cannot do these works, he must instead of censuring extol God's government, and thine own hand save as eternal salvation by Christ. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Behold now behemoth, which God made with thee; he eats grass as an ox. JFB explains that God shows that if Job cannot bring under control the lower animals, of which he selects the two most striking, behemoth on land and leviathan in the water, much less is he capable of governing the world: behemoth agrees in part with the hippopotamus and in part with the elephant, but exactly with neither, being rather a poetical personification of the great Pachydermata, with the hippopotamus predominant, and made with thee as God made thyself, yet how great the difference. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Lo now, his strength is in his loins, and his force is in the navel of his belly. JFB renders navel as muscles of his belly, the weakest point of the elephant, therefore not meant. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: He moves his tail like a cedar: the sinews of his stones are wrapped together. JFB explains like a cedar as the tempest bends the cedar, so it can move its smooth thick tail, though the cedar implies straightness and length such as do not apply to the river horse's short tail, and stones as thighs, wrapped as firmly twisted together like a thick rope. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: His bones are as strong pieces of brass; his bones are like bars of iron. JFB renders strong as tubes of copper. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: He is the chief of the ways of God: he that made him can make his sword to approach unto him. JFB explains chief of the works of God, so ways, and can make his sword to approach as has furnished him with his sword, the sickle-like teeth with which he cuts down grain. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Surely the mountains bring him forth food, where all the beasts of the field play. JFB notes the mountain is not his usual haunt, and beasts play as a graphic trait: though armed with such teeth, he lets the beasts play near him unhurt, for his food is grass. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: He lies under the shady trees, in the covert of the reed, and fens. JFB renders lieth as leading an inactive life, and shady trees as lotus bushes. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: The shady trees cover him with their shadow; the willows of the brook compass him about. JFB again translates shady trees as lotus bushes. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Behold, he drinks up a river, and hastens not: he trusts that he can draw up Jordan into his mouth. JFB renders it as though a river be violent, he trembles not, for though living on land he can live in the water too, and is secure though a Jordan swell up to his mouth, with Jordan used for any great river as a poetical generalization. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: He takes it with his eyes: his nose pierces through snares. JFB renders it as will any take him by open force, before his eyes, or pierce his nose with cords? No, he can only be taken by guile, and in a pitfall. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
