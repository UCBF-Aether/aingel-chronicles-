Study Notes — Job

Chapter 6

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Job answers Eliphaz. The reply turns from Eliphaz's doctrine to Job's own weighed grief and the failure of his friends' comfort. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Oh that Job's grief were thoroughly weighed and his calamity laid in the balances together! JFB explains that Job asks Eliphaz to compare sorrow and misfortune accurately instead of censuring complaints he ought to have sympathized with. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Weighed together, his calamity would be heavier than the sand of the sea, and therefore his words are swallowed up. JFB reads swallowed up as spoken rashly: Job apologizes for speaking too much and too boldly. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The arrows of the Almighty are within Job, and their poison drinks up his spirit. JFB gives the poetic image of God armed with bow and arrows, the arrows poisoned like the burning pains penetrating Job's inmost parts, with God's terrors set in array against him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: The wild ass does not bray when it has grass, nor the ox low over its fodder. JFB argues that just as beasts cry only in want, Job's complaint is not without cause, though a rational being should show a better spirit than the brute. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Can tasteless food be eaten without salt, or is there taste in the white of an egg? JFB notes salt is a chief necessary of life in the East, and the white is literally spittle, which egg-white resembles. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: The things Job's soul refused to touch have become his sorrowful meat. JFB explains that his taste refused even to touch such insipid food, yet sickness itself is now his nourishment. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Oh that Job might have his request, and God would grant him the thing he longs for! JFB cautions that desiring death is no necessary proof of fitness for it: the godly desire to be with the Lord but patiently wait God's will. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Even that God would destroy him, let loose his hand, and cut him off! JFB explains destroy as grind or crush, and cut off as a weaver cutting the finished web from the loom. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Then Job would still have comfort, hardening himself in sorrow, because he has not concealed the words of the Holy One. JFB renders it as exulting in pain if it hastened death, and notes Job answers Eliphaz's insinuation of hypocrisy by claiming he never disowned God's commands. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: What is Job's strength that he should hope, and what is his end that he should prolong his life? JFB reads this as despair of restoration: with only a miserable end before him, why desire to prolong life? — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Is Job's strength the strength of stones, or his flesh brass? JFB notes disease has attacked him so that only stone-hard strength and brass-like flesh could stand under it, and he has only common flesh that must give way. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Is not Job's help in him? No: wisdom, or deliverance, is driven quite from him. JFB drops the interrogation: there is no help in me. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: To him who is ready to faint, pity should be shown from his friend, even if he forsakes the fear of the Almighty. JFB calls pity a proverb: charity judges indulgently, and withholding it renounces the fear of God. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Job's brethren have dealt deceitfully as a brook, passing away like streams that vanish. JFB compares them to Arabian wadies, full in winter but dry in summer, disappointing caravans that expected water, as loud past professions now fail when needed. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: The brooks are blackish by reason of ice, with snow hidden in them. JFB gives the vivid image of a stream turbid and black with melted ice and snow descending into the valley. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: In the time of heat the brooks vanish and are consumed out of their place. JFB traces the stages: the stream flows narrower, then silent, then every trace disappears by evaporation under the hot sun. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: The paths of their way are turned aside, and they go to nothing and perish. JFB explains that caravans turn aside by circuitous routes to waters they remember, only to find an empty wilderness and perish, unable to regain their road. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: The troops of Tema looked, and the companies of Sheba waited for them. JFB locates Tema north of Arabia-Deserta, named from Ishmael's son, and Sheba among southern merchants, giving living reality to the disappointed caravans. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: They were confounded because they had hoped, and came there and were ashamed. JFB notes each had hoped his companions would find water, and the greater the hope the bitterer the disappointment, their countenances burning with shame. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: For now you are nothing: you see my casting down and are afraid. JFB reads it as the dried brook to the caravan — Job's friends are a nothing to him — and puts a mild construction on their failure: they are struck aghast at his misery and lose presence of mind. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Did Job ask you to bring him a gift or pay a ransom from your substance? All he asked was affectionate treatment, not money or rescue. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Or to deliver Job from the enemy's hand and redeem him from the mighty? JFB explains the mighty as the oppressor or creditor in whose power the debtor stood. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Teach Job, and he will hold his tongue; cause him to understand where he has erred. JFB reads irony here: if the friends can teach the right view, Job will be set right, but their words prove feeble. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: How forcible are right words, but what does your arguing reprove? JFB reverses the English force: how feeble are the words of what you call the right view, and the reproofs proceed from you, who are not in Job's situation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Do you imagine reproving words and the speeches of one desperate, which are as wind? JFB notes the speeches of a desperate man are mere nothings, not to be narrowly taken to task. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: You would overwhelm the fatherless and dig a pit for your friend. JFB explains the hunting image: causing a net of sophistry to fall on the desolate, and digging a covered pit to catch Job in unguarded language. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Now therefore be content to look upon Job, for it is evident to you if he lies. JFB renders it as pleased to look: his countenance will betray him if he is the hypocrite they suppose. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Return, let it not be iniquity; return again, for Job's righteousness is in it. Job asks his friends to reconsider rather than persist in an unjust judgment. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: Is there iniquity in Job's tongue, and cannot his taste discern perverse things? He appeals to his own moral palate: he can tell right speech from perverse speech. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
