Study Notes — Job

Chapter 9

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Job answers Bildad, conceding Bildad's doctrine yet pressing his own question: how can a man be just with God? The reply accepts that God does not pervert justice, and asks how a mere man could assert his right before him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Job knows it is true that God does not pervert justice, but asks how man should be just with God. JFB notes the Gospel answers the question Job can only pose. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: If God will contend with man, man cannot answer him one of a thousand. JFB explains contend as deigning to enter into judgment, and that man would not dare utter one of a thousand ready answers, from awe of God's majesty. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: God is wise in heart and mighty in strength; who has hardened himself against him and prospered? JFB notes God confounds the ablest arguer by wisdom and the mightiest by power, and that to prosper one must fall in with God's arrangements. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: God removes mountains and they know it not, overturning them in his anger. JFB reads know not as suddenly, unexpectedly, before they are aware. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: God shakes the earth out of its place, and its pillars tremble. JFB gives the poetic image of the earth resting on pillars that tremble in an earthquake. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: God commands the sun and it rises not, and seals up the stars. JFB explains this by eclipse and earthquake darkness, and sealing as totally covering a room so its contents cannot be seen. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: God alone spreads out the heavens and treads on the waves of the sea. JFB stresses God's governing power over nature: he bows the heavens alone and walks majestically over the mountain waves as a conqueror taming their violence. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: God makes Arcturus, Orion, and the Pleiades, and the chambers of the south. JFB explains Arcturus as the great bear that never sets, Orion as the chained fool-hero, the Pleiades as the heap or knot of stars, and the chambers as the unseen southern stars. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: God does great things past finding out, and wonders without number. JFB notes the line is repeated from Eliphaz, now turned to Job's argument about God's unsearchable power. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: God goes by Job and Job sees him not; he passes on and Job perceives him not. JFB gives the image of a howling wind bursting invisibly: God is felt in the awful effects of his wrath but is not seen. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: If God takes away, who can hinder him, or who will say what are you doing? JFB notes God only takes his own, as an absolute king whom a mortal cannot call to account. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: If God will not withdraw his anger, the proud helpers stoop under him. JFB explains the proud helpers as arrogant would-be allies of one contending with the Almighty, who avail nothing against him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: How much less shall Job answer God and choose out words to reason with him? JFB notes the weak must stoop where the mighty stoop, and choosing words means using a well-chosen speech to reason. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Though Job were righteous, he would not answer, but would make supplication to his judge. JFB notes that even conscious of no sin, he would not dare say so, but would leave justification to God's judgment and mercy. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: If Job called and God answered, he would not believe God had hearkened to his voice. The next verse explains why: God breaks him with a tempest, like a tree stripped of its leaves. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: God breaks Job with a tempest and multiplies his wounds without cause. JFB links this to the preceding verse: the tempest-breaking explains Job's unbelief that God had heard him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: God will not suffer Job to take his breath, but fills him with bitterness. The complaint is of unrelieved affliction, breath itself denied between blows. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: If Job speaks of strength, God is strong; if of judgment, who shall set a time to plead? JFB notes the sense is the same whether the words are God's or Job's: lo expresses God's swift readiness for battle when challenged. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: If Job justifies himself, his own mouth shall condemn him; if he says he is perfect, it shall prove him perverse. JFB notes the condemning it may be his mouth or God himself. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Though Job were perfect, he would not know his soul and would despise his life. JFB renders it literally: I perfect, I should not know my soul, I would disown my life, compelled in contending with infinite God to ignore his own innocence. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: This is one thing: God destroys the perfect and the wicked alike. JFB calls this Job's central point against his friends: the righteous and wicked are alike afflicted, and great suffering here does not prove great guilt. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: If the scourge slays suddenly, God laughs at the trial of the innocent. JFB clarifies: while the scourge slays the wicked suddenly, God disregards, not derides, the gradual pining away of the innocent. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: The earth is given into the hand of the wicked, and he covers the faces of its judges. JFB contrasts the wicked oppressor who holds the earth with righteous judges led to execution, their faces covered as culprits were. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Job's days are swifter than a post, fleeing away and seeing no good. JFB explains the post as a fleet courier in the Persian empire, carrying royal commands on dromedaries or on foot, not a slow caravan. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Job's days pass as swift ships, as the eagle hastening to its prey. JFB explains the swift ships as light reed canoes or papyrus skiffs on the Nile, swift from their lightness. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: If Job says he will forget his complaint, leave off his heaviness, and comfort himself, still he fears all his sorrows will return. JFB gives the apodosis: he is afraid God will not hold him innocent even if his sufferings are removed. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Job is afraid of all his sorrows, knowing God will not hold him innocent. The fear is not of pain alone but of a verdict never cleared. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: If Job is wicked, why does he labour in vain? JFB notes Job submits not so much because convinced God is right as because God is powerful and he is weak. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: If Job washes himself with snow water and cleanses his hands, God would still plunge him in the ditch and his own clothes would abhor him. JFB notes snow water was thought more cleansing for its whiteness, and the cleansing agent is lye, alkali used as soap. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: Yet God would plunge Job in the ditch, and his own clothes would abhor him. Even the whitest washing cannot make him presentable before a God who treats him as guilty. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: God is not a man as Job is, that they should come together in judgment. The inequality of the parties makes a fair trial impossible on Job's terms. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: Neither is there any daysman between them to lay his hand on them both. JFB explains daysman as mediator or umpire, whose imposed hand expresses power to adjudicate, and notes Christians know such a Mediator on a level with both, the God-man Christ Jesus. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: Let God take his rod away from Job, and let not his fear terrify him. JFB explains the rod here not as punishment but as power: Job cannot meet God fairly while God deals with him on the footing of almighty power. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: Then Job would speak and not fear him, but it is not so with him. JFB notes that as things stand, with the rod not taken away, Job is not on a footing of equality to vindicate himself. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
