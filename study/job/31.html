Study Notes — Job

Chapter 31

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Job made a covenant with his eyes; why then should he think upon a maid? JFB explains think as casting a lustful look, which Job put out of the question by covenanting with his eyes against temptation, asserting his guard against being allured to sin by his senses. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: For what portion of God is there from above, and what inheritance of the Almighty from on high? JFB explains that had Job let his senses tempt him to sin, this is the portion he must have expected from God above. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Is not destruction to the wicked, and a strange punishment to the workers of iniquity? JFB calls this the answer to verse 2: strange as extraordinary, the expected lot of such sin. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Does not God see Job's ways, and count all his steps? JFB notes that knowing this, Job could only have expected destruction had he committed this sin. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: If Job has walked with vanity, or his foot has hasted to deceit, let God weigh him. JFB explains vanity as falsehood, in Job's abstinence from evil deeds. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Let God weigh Job in an even balance, that God may know his integrity. JFB renders it parenthetically: oh that God would weigh me, then would he know. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: If Job's step has turned out of the way, and his heart walked after his eyes, and any blot has cleaved to his hands. JFB connects this with verse 5, explaining the way as God's way, and heart after eyes as coveting what the eyes beheld. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Then let Job sow, and another eat, and his offspring be rooted out. JFB calls this the apodosis to verses 5 and 7, the curses he imprecates on himself if he did these things, with offspring as what he plants, his harvests. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: If Job's heart has been deceived by a woman, or he has laid wait at his neighbour's door. JFB introduces Job's assertion of innocence of adultery, with deceived as seduced, and laid wait until the husband went out. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Then let Job's wife grind unto another, and let others bow down upon her. JFB explains grind as turning the handmill, becoming the most abject slave and concubine. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: For this is a heinous crime, yea, an iniquity to be punished by the judges. JFB notes adultery was punished with death in earliest times and later, a sin against the community needing the judge's cognizance, whereas before Job spoke of sins against conscience. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: For it is a fire that consumes to destruction, and would root out all Job's increase. JFB notes no crime more provokes God to send destruction as consuming fire, and none so desolates the soul. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: If Job has despised the cause of his manservant or maidservant when they contended with him. JFB introduces Job's freedom from unfairness to servants and harshness to the needy, with despise as refusing to do them justice. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: What then shall Job do when God rises up, and when he visits, what shall he answer him? JFB explains parenthetically: had he despised his servants, what could he do when God arose to call him to account, and what answer when God visited to enquire? — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Did not he that made Job in the womb make his servant, and did not one fashion them in the womb? JFB notes slaveholders defend themselves by original inferiority, but common origin is the argument for brotherly love from master to servant. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: If Job has withheld the poor from their desire, or caused the eyes of the widow to fail. JFB explains fail as in vain expectation of relief. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Or eaten his morsel himself alone, and the fatherless has not eaten thereof. JFB notes Arabian hospitality requires the stranger to be helped first and to the best, which Job did not violate. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: For from Job's youth the fatherless was brought up with him as with a father, and he guided the widow from his mother's womb. JFB explains parenthetically that he did the contrary to verses 16-17, with he as the orphan, and guided her as the widow, by advice and protection. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: If Job has seen any perish for want of clothing, or any poor without covering. JFB explains perish as ready to perish, the perishing man Job clothed. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: If his loins have not blessed Job, and if he has not been warmed with the fleece of his sheep. JFB explains loins poetically as thanking him: the loins before naked, when clad by him, wished him every blessing. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: If Job has lifted up his hand against the fatherless, when he saw his help in the gate. JFB explains when as because: he might calculate on a powerful party's help in the court at the gate if summoned by the injured fatherless. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Then let Job's arm fall from his shoulder blade, and his arm be broken from the bone. JFB calls this the apodosis to verses 13 and 16-21, alluding to Eliphaz's charge, with the first arm as shoulder and the second as forearm, broken from the bone or reed, the upper arm above the elbow. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: For destruction from God was a terror to Job, and by reason of his highness he could not endure. JFB explains this as the reason Job guarded against such sins: fear of God, though he could escape man's judgment, with highness as majestic might, against which he could avail nothing. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: If Job has made gold his hope, or said to the fine gold, you are my confidence. JFB introduces Job's freedom from trust in money, turning to duty toward God after duty toward himself and neighbour, since covetousness is covert idolatry transferring the heart from Creator to creature. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: If Job rejoiced because his wealth was great, and because his hand had gotten much. Trust in money is tested not only by hope placed in it but by rejoicing in its increase. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: If Job beheld the sun when it shined, or the moon walking in brightness. JFB explains Sabaism, worship of the heavenly hosts, the earliest false worship: sun, moon, and stars as visible representatives of the invisible God, worshipped on high places and roofs, with light worshipped as emanation before its embodiments. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: And Job's heart has been secretly enticed, or his mouth has kissed his hand. JFB explains enticed as away from God to idolatry, and kissing the hand as adoration literally: worshippers kissed the hand and threw the kiss toward the object of worship. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: This also were an iniquity to be punished by the judge, for Job would have denied the God above. JFB notes the Mosaic law later embodied the godly feeling against idolatry as treason against the Supreme King, deserving judicial penalties. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: If Job rejoiced at the destruction of him that hated him, or lifted up himself when evil found him. JFB explains lifted up as malicious triumph. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: Neither has Job suffered his mouth to sin by wishing a curse to his soul. JFB explains mouth literally as palate, and wishing as demanding his enemy's life by a curse, confirming chapter 6, with Job realizing the Gospel spirit before the law. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: If the men of Job's tabernacle said not, oh that we had of his flesh, we cannot be satisfied. JFB explains the household's wish to devour Job's enemy, which Job refrained from even wishing, and better as who was not satisfied with the meat Job provided, since he never let a poor man leave his gate unfed. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: The stranger did not lodge in the street, but Job opened his doors to the traveller. JFB explains traveller literally as way, including wayfarers of every kind. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: If Job covered his transgressions as Adam, by hiding his iniquity in his bosom. JFB explains Adam as the representative of fallen man's propensity to concealment, with the same word for hiding used of Adam in Genesis 3, and Job not hiding his iniquity from God. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: Did Job fear a great multitude, or did the contempt of families terrify him, that he kept silence and went not out of the door? JFB renders it as the apodosis: then let him be fear-stricken before a multitude, the just retribution for hiding sin from God being exposure before man, which Job had not suffered, being esteemed in assemblies. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: Oh that one would hear Job! Behold his desire is that the Almighty would answer him, and that his adversary had written a book. JFB explains his desire as his mark of subscription, originally a cross or Tau, and adversary as whoever judicially opposes him, with written a book as writing down his charge. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: Surely Job would take it upon his shoulder, and bind it as a crown to him. JFB notes that far from hiding the adversary's charge through fear, he would take it as public honour on his shoulder, and as a crown of distinction, not shame. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

37 COMMENTARY: Job would declare unto him the number of his steps, and as a prince would he go near unto him. JFB notes a good conscience imparts princely dignity before man and free assurance in approaching God, realized only through Christ, not in Job's way. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

38 COMMENTARY: If Job's land cries against him, or its furrows likewise complain. JFB explains the personification: complaints of unjustly ousted proprietors transferred to the lands themselves, if Job unjustly acquired lands or paid not the labourer for tillage and fruits. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

39 COMMENTARY: If Job has eaten its fruits without money, or caused the owners thereof to lose their life. JFB explains lose life not literally but harassed to death, until the owner gave up land gratis, or languished by loss of livelihood. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

40 COMMENTARY: Let thistles grow instead of wheat, and cockle instead of barley. The words of Job are ended. The self-curse closes his defence: if guilty of land wrong, let the land itself bear weeds instead of crops, and with this his words end. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
