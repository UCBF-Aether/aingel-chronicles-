Study Notes — Job

Chapter 30

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: But now they that are younger than Job have him in derision, whose fathers he would have disdained to set with the dogs of his flock. JFB notes the deriders are not the three friends but a low, base-born brood, whose juniors ought to reverence him, with dogs regarded in the East as unclean, living on offal outside houses. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Yea, whereto might the strength of their hands profit Job, in whom old age was perished? JFB notes that if their fathers could be no profit, much less the feebler sons, in whom hope of old age is gone, and even strength of hands would be useless, since all Job wants now is sympathy. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: For want and famine they were solitary, fleeing into the wilderness in former time desolate and waste. JFB explains solitary as dried up and emaciated with hunger, describing the rudest Bedouins, gnawers of the wilderness in the yesternight of desolation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Who cut up mallows by the bushes, and juniper roots for their meat. JFB explains mallows as salt-wort eaten as salad by the poor, and juniper as broom, whose bitter roots the poor eat. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: They were driven forth from among men, and they cried after them as after a thief. JFB explains the cry raised as contempt from civilized Arabs, driving wild vagabonds away as thieves when they raid villages. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: To dwell in the cliffs of the valleys, in caves of the earth, and in the rocks. JFB explains the gloomy valleys or wadies, where dwelling marks wretchedness, with troglodytes living in caves. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Among the bushes they brayed, and under the nettles they were gathered together. JFB explains braying like the wild ass for food, with inarticulate tones little above beasts, gathered as sprinkled here and there behind thorn bushes. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: They were children of fools, children of base men, viler than the earth. JFB explains fools as impious and abandoned, base as nameless low-born rabble, driven out of the land like the Horites, cave-dwellers of Mount Seir. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: And now Job is their song, yea, he is their byword. JFB notes the derision Christ also underwent, with Job become a song of derision to such as these. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: They abhor Job, flee far from him, and spare not to spit in his face. JFB explains spitting before his face as deliberate contempt and abhorrence in the East. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Because God has loosed Job's cord and afflicted him, they have also let loose the bridle before him. JFB explains He as God, and cord as a bow unstrung, with each letting loose his reins in unrestrained insult. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Upon Job's right hand rise the youth, pushing away his feet, and raising up against him the ways of their destruction. JFB explains youth as a low brood, rising on the right as accusers in court, jostling him out of the way, and raising siege ways against him like an army approaching a city. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: They mar Job's path, set forward his calamity, and have no helper. JFB continues the fortress image: they tear up the path by which succour might reach him, with no helper as an Arabic proverb for contemptible persons, yet even such afflict him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: They came upon Job as a wide breaking in of waters, and in the desolation they rolled themselves upon him. JFB explains it as through a wide breach made by besiegers in a fortress wall, amid the crash of falling masonry. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Terrors are turned upon Job, pursuing his soul as the wind, and his welfare passes away as a cloud. JFB explains terrors as pursuing his dignity, with welfare as prosperity passing like a cloud. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: And now Job's soul is poured out upon him, and the days of affliction have taken hold upon him. JFB notes Job's outward calamities now affect his mind, with poured out as irrepressible complaints. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: His bones are pierced in him in the night season, and his sinews take no rest. JFB explains night personified as piercing his bones so they fall from him, with sinews as gnawers, gnawing pains from elephantiasis never ceasing. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: By the great force of his disease Job's garment is changed, binding him about as the collar of his coat. JFB explains the garment changed from a robe of honour to mourning, with affliction as both outer and inner garment, binding his throat as a coat collar. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: God has cast Job into the mire, and he has become like dust and ashes. JFB notes God is poetically said to do what the mourner did to himself, lying in ashes until he became like them in dirty colour. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Job cries unto God, but God does not hear him; he stands up, but God regards him not. JFB explains standing as the reverential attitude of a suppliant before a king, with God not only refusing aid but regarding him with a frown. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: God has become cruel to Job, with his strong hand opposing himself against him. The charge is direct: the strong hand once preserving now opposes. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: God lifts Job up to the wind, causing him to ride upon it, and dissolving his substance. JFB explains lifting to wind as a leaf or stubble, or pillars of sand raised to the clouds, with substance dissolved in the whirlwind's crash. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: For Job knows God will bring him to death, and to the house appointed for all living. JFB notes this shows chapter 30 cannot be restricted to hope of temporal deliverance: death is the realm of the dead, the house appointed for all. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Howbeit God will not stretch out his hand to the grave, though they cry in his destruction. JFB explains Job's faith after death: though one goes to the grave, God will no more afflict in the ruin of the body if one cried to him when destroyed, with stretching the hand answering to raising the cry. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Did not Job weep for him that was in trouble, and was not his soul grieved for the poor? JFB asks whether he may not be allowed to complain and beg relief, seeing he himself sympathized with those in trouble, those who had a hard time. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: When Job looked for good, then evil came unto him, and when he waited for light, there came darkness. JFB notes he may be allowed to crave help, seeing that when he looked for good on account of piety and charity, evil came instead. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Job's bowels boiled and rested not, and the days of affliction prevented him. JFB explains bowels as the seat of deep feeling, boiled as violently heated and agitated, with prevented as unexpectedly surprising him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Job went mourning without the sun, and stood up and cried in the congregation. JFB explains mourning as moving blackened, though not by the sun, but by the heat of God's wrath and elephantiasis blackness, standing as an innocent man crying for justice in court. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Job is a brother to dragons, and a companion to owls. JFB renders dragons and owls as jackals and ostriches, uttering dismal screams amid solitudes, whose desolation Job resembles. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: Job's skin is black upon him, and his bones are burned with heat. JFB explains skin black and falling away from him, with bones as in chapter 19, burned with fever heat. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: Job's harp also is turned to mourning, and his organ into the voice of them that weep. JFB explains organ as pipe, with instruments properly for joy now used in sorrow, sadder by contrast. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
