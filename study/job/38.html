Study Notes — Job

Chapter 38

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Then the LORD answered Job out of the whirlwind, and said. JFB notes Jehovah appears unexpectedly in a whirlwind, already gathering in chapter 37, the symbol of judgment to which Job had challenged him, asking him now to get ready for the contest: can he explain the phenomena of God's natural government, and how then hope to understand his moral government? — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Who is this that darkens counsel by words without knowledge? JFB explains this as Job, impugning God's divine wisdom in the providential arrangements of the universe: such words rather obscure than throw light on God's ways, and God is about to be Job's Vindicator but must first bring him to a right state of mind for receiving relief. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Gird up now thy loins like a man, for God will demand of Job, and Job shall answer him. JFB renders a man as hero, ready for battle, as Job had wished: the robe, usually worn flowing, was girt up by a girdle when men ran, laboured, or fought. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Where wast thou when God laid the foundations of the earth? Declare, if thou hast understanding. JFB notes that to understand the cause of things, man should have been present at their origin, and the finite creature cannot fathom the infinite wisdom of the Creator. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Who has laid the measures thereof, if thou knowest? Or who has stretched the line upon it? JFB explains measures as proportions, the image from an architect's plans of a building, and line as the line of measurement: the earth is formed on an all-wise plan. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Whereupon are the foundations thereof fastened? Or who laid the corner stone thereof? JFB explains fastened literally as made to sink, as a foundation-stone let down till it settles firmly in the clay, and notes gravitation makes and keeps the earth a sphere. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: When the morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy? JFB notes morning stars as especially beautiful, the creation morn being the commencement of this world's day, and the stars figuratively singing God's praises as symbols of the angels, answering to sons of God in the parallel. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Or who shut up the sea with doors, when it broke forth, as if it had issued out of the womb? JFB explains doors as floodgates, or else the shores, and womb as of chaos, the bowels of the earth, with the image from childbirth: ocean at its birth was wrapped in clouds as its swaddling bands. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: When God made the cloud the garment of the sea, and thick darkness a swaddling band for it. JFB's gloss on verse 8 gives the image: ocean at its birth was wrapped in clouds as its swaddling bands. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: And God broke up for it his decreed place, and set bars and doors. JFB renders brake up as appointed it, with shores generally broken and abrupt cliffs: God broke off or measured off for the sea his limit, the limit which he thought fit. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: And said, hitherto shalt thou come, but no further: and here shall thy proud waves be stayed? JFB renders stayed as a limit shall be set to the proud waves. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Hast thou commanded the morning since thy days, and caused the dayspring to know his place? JFB notes the passage passes from creation to phenomena in the existing inanimate world: hast thou, as God daily does, commanded the morning to rise, since thou hast come into being, with his place varying from day to day yet fixed each day according to fixed laws. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: That it might take hold of the ends of the earth, that the wicked might be shaken out of it? JFB explains take hold of the ends as spreading over the earth to its utmost bounds in a moment, with the wicked who hate the light and do their evil works in the dark shaken out of it as the corners or wings of a garment are taken hold of by the dayspring. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: It is turned as clay to the seal, and they stand as a garment. JFB explains that as the plastic clay presents the various figures impressed on it by a seal, so the earth, which in the dark was void of all form, when illuminated by the dayspring presents a variety of forms, hills and valleys, with turned alluding to the rolling cylinder seal, and they stand as the forms of beauty unfolded by the dawn standing forth as a garment in which the earth is clad. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: And from the wicked their light is withheld, and the high arm shall be broken. JFB explains their light as darkness, which is their day, extinguished by daylight, and high as the arm uplifted for murder or other crime, broken and falling down suddenly, powerless, through fear of light. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Hast thou entered into the springs of the sea? Or hast thou walked in the search of the depth? JFB renders springs as fountains beneath the sea, and search as the inmost recesses, literally that which is only found by searching, the deep caverns of ocean. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Have the gates of death been opened unto thee? Or hast thou seen the doors of the shadow of death? JFB notes man during life does not even see the gates of the realm of the dead, much less are they opened to him, but those are naked before God. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Hast thou perceived the breadth of the earth? Declare if thou knowest it all. As God does, man is asked whether he has perceived the earth's breadth. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Where is the way where light dwells? And as for darkness, where is the place thereof? JFB introduces the marvels in heaven, asking the origin of light and darkness: in Genesis light is created distinct from and previous to light-emitting bodies, the luminaries of heaven. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: That thou shouldest take it to the bound thereof, and that thou shouldest know the paths to the house thereof? JFB asks whether Job knows light's place so well as to guide it to its own boundary, the limit between light and darkness. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Knowest thou it, because thou wast then born? Or because the number of thy days is great? JFB notes the irony: then, when God created light and darkness, was Job already born? — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Hast thou entered into the treasures of the snow? Or hast thou seen the treasures of the hail? JFB renders treasures as storehouses from which God draws forth snow and hail: snow is vapour congealed in the air before it is collected in drops large enough to form hail, its shape a crystal in endless variety of beautiful figures. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Which God has reserved against the time of trouble, against the day of battle and war? JFB explains the time of trouble as the time when God designs to chastise men. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: By what way is the light parted, which scatters the east wind upon the earth? JFB explains is parted as parts, so as to diffuse itself over the whole earth though seeming to come from one point, and scattereth as the east wind personified spreading itself, light and east wind being associated as both coming from one quarter. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Who has divided a watercourse for the overflowing of waters, or a way for the lightning of thunder? JFB notes rain falls not in a mass on one spot but in countless separate canals in the air marked out for them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: To cause it to rain on the earth, where no man is, on the wilderness, wherein there is no man? JFB notes that since rain falls also on places uninhabited by man, it cannot be that man guides its course: such rain, though man cannot explain the reason for it, is not lost, for God has some wise design in it. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: To satisfy the desolate and waste ground, and to cause the bud of the tender herb to spring forth? JFB notes the personification: as though the desolate ground thirsted for God's showers, and the beauty imparted to the uninhabited desert pleases God, for whom primarily all things exist. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Has the rain a father? Or who has begotten the drops of dew? JFB asks whether any visible origin of rain and dew can be assigned by man: dew is moisture suspended in the air but condensed on reaching the lower temperature of objects on the earth in the night. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Out of whose womb came the ice? And the hoary frost of heaven, who has gendered it? The question turns to the origin of ice and frost, which no man can name. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: The waters are hid as with a stone, and the face of the deep is frozen. JFB explains the unfrozen waters are hid under the frozen, as with a covering of stone, and frozen literally as taken, the particles taking hold of one another so as to cohere. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: Canst thou bind the sweet influences of Pleiades, or loose the bands of Orion? JFB explains sweet influences as the joy diffused by spring, the time when the Pleiades appear, described as brilliant rosettes, and bands as the knot by which the seven stars are closely bound together, with Orion represented in the East as an impious giant chained to the sky. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: Canst thou bring forth Mazzaroth in his season? Or canst thou guide Arcturus with his sons? JFB explains Mazzaroth as the signs of the Zodiac at their respective seasons, the twelve lodgings in which the sun successively stays, and Arcturus as Ursa Major, with his sons as the three stars in his tail. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: Knowest thou the ordinances of heaven? Canst thou set the dominion thereof in the earth? JFB renders ordinances as those regulating the alternations of seasons, and dominion as the controlling influence of the heavenly bodies, the sun and moon, on the earth, on tides and weather. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: Canst thou lift up thy voice to the clouds, that abundance of waters may cover thee? The question asks whether Job's voice can command rain at will. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: Canst thou send lightnings, that they may go and say unto thee, here we are? JFB renders here we are as at thy disposal. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: Who has put wisdom in the inward parts? Or who has given understanding to the heart? JFB notes the alternative readings of dark clouds or meteor, but the Hebrew supports the English version: who has given thee the intelligence to comprehend in any degree the phenomena just specified, with heart from a root meaning to view, that is, perception. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

37 COMMENTARY: Who can number the clouds in wisdom? Or who can stay the bottles of heaven? JFB asks who appoints by his wisdom the due measure of the clouds, rendering stay as empty, literally lay down or incline so as to pour out, with bottles of heaven as rain-filled clouds. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

38 COMMENTARY: When the dust grows into hardness, and the clods cleave fast together? JFB renders groweth as pour itself into a mass by the rain, like molten metal, asking who can empty the rain vessels and bring down rain at such a time. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

39 COMMENTARY: Wilt thou hunt the prey for the lion? Or fill the appetite of the young lions? JFB introduces the instincts of animals, asking whether Job gives the lioness the instinct to hunt its prey, with appetite literally as life, which depends on the appetite. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

40 COMMENTARY: When they couch in their dens, and abide in the covert to lie in wait? JFB explains lie in wait as for their prey. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

41 COMMENTARY: Who provides for the raven his food? When his young ones cry unto God, they wander for lack of meat. JFB notes the transition from the noble lioness to the croaking raven: though man dislikes it as of ill omen, God cares for it, as for all his creatures. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
