Study Notes — Job

Chapter 19

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Job answers Bildad, protesting his friends' unkind usage. Henry notes the friends blamed him as wicked because he was afflicted, and Job shows their condemnation was capable of excuse. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: How long will the friends vex Job's soul and break him in pieces with words? Henry observes harsh language from friends greatly adds to the weight of afflictions, yet it is best not to lay it to heart lest resentment be harboured. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: These ten times the friends have reproached Job, and are not ashamed to make themselves strange to him. The repeated reproaches have become estrangement, not comfort. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: If Job has truly erred, his error remains with himself. The friends' case fails even on its own terms: an error of Job's would not justify their treatment. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: If indeed the friends magnify themselves against Job and plead his reproach against him, they use his affliction as their argument. Henry notes what they condemned was capable of excuse, yet they turned it into accusation. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Know now that God has overthrown Job and compassed him with his net. Job attributes the net to God himself, not merely to men: his affliction is divinely encompassed. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Job cries out of wrong, but is not heard; he cries aloud, but there is no judgment. The complaint is not only suffering but unheard appeal. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: God has fenced up Job's way so he cannot pass, and set darkness in his paths. Henry places this in the doleful complaints of verses 8-22, where Job traces his afflictions to God as their author. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: God has stripped Job of his glory and taken the crown from his head. Honour as well as wealth is removed. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: God has destroyed Job on every side and he is gone, uprooting his hope like a tree. The image is total: every side attacked, hope itself uprooted. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: God's wrath is kindled against Job, and he counts him as one of his enemies. Henry warns it is a common mistake to think those whom God afflicts he treats as enemies. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: God's troops come together, raise up their way against Job, and encamp round about his tabernacle. Affliction arrives as an army besieging his house. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: God has put Job's brethren far from him, and his acquaintance are verily estranged from him. Henry notes how uncertain is the friendship of men, though God as friend will not fail in need. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Job's kinsfolk have failed, and his familiar friends have forgotten him. Kinship and familiarity alike prove hollow. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Those who dwell in Job's house and his maids count him a stranger, and he is an alien in their sight. Even his household treats him as an outsider. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: Job calls his servant, and he gives no answer, though he entreats him with his mouth. The master who once commanded now begs and is ignored. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Job's wife finds his breath strange, though he entreats for the children's sake of his own body. Henry notes the body, after all care, is consumed by diseases it has in itself, and even the nearest bond is strained. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Young children despise Job, and when he arises they speak against him. Contempt reaches down to the youngest. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: All Job's inward friends abhor him, and those he loved are turned against him. The inner circle completes the desertion. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Job's bone cleaves to his skin and flesh, and he has escaped with the skin of his teeth. Wasting has left almost nothing between him and death. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Have pity upon Job, O his friends, for the hand of God has touched him. Henry notes Job recommends himself to his friends' compassion and justly blames their harshness. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: Why do the friends persecute Job as God does, and are not satisfied with his flesh? To add human persecution to divine affliction is to do God's work of wounding without God's right. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: Oh that Job's words were now written, printed in a book, graven with iron and lead in rock forever! Henry notes the Spirit powerfully wrought on Job here: he witnessed a good confession, declaring sound faith and assured hope. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Oh that his words were graven with an iron pen and lead in the rock forever! The permanence he seeks for his testimony matches the permanence of the hope it declares. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: For Job knows his Redeemer lives, and shall stand at the latter day upon the earth. Henry explains Job was taught to believe in a living Redeemer, his Redeemer, though not yet come in the flesh, who would appear as Judge to raise the dead. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: And though after his skin worms destroy this body, yet in his flesh shall Job see God. Henry notes Job comforted himself with expectation of the resurrection of the dead and the life of the world to come. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: Whom Job shall see for himself, and his eyes shall behold, and not another, though his reins are consumed within him. The seeing is personal and bodily: his own eyes, not another's, shall behold God. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: But the friends should say, why persecute we him, seeing the root of the matter is found in him? Henry explains the root as a living, quickening, commanding principle of grace in the heart, as necessary to religion as a root is to a tree's fixedness and fruitfulness. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Be afraid of the sword, for wrath brings the punishments of the sword, that you may know there is a judgment. Henry notes Job and his friends agreed in the root of the matter, the belief of another world, and the warning is that judgment will test persecutors too. — Matthew Henry
