Study Notes — Job

Chapter 1

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Job of Uz is presented as blameless and upright, fearing God and turning from evil. Henry stresses that his piety, not merely his prosperity, is the foundation of the whole book. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Job's seven sons and three daughters show a full, flourishing household. The family stands complete before any trial touches it, which makes the losses that follow all the sharper. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Job's vast flocks, herds, and household make him the greatest man of the east. Henry notes the rare point: he was prosperous and yet pious, since wealth's temptations had not overcome him by God's grace. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Job's sons feasted in turn in each other's houses, with their sisters invited. The picture is of a harmonious family enjoying its plenty together, not of riot or division. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: After each round of feasting Job sent for his children, sanctified them, and offered a burnt offering for each one, fearing they might have sinned in their hearts. Henry reads this as a father's care for his children's souls and his dependence on God's appointed way of acceptance. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: The scene moves to heaven, where the sons of God present themselves before the LORD and Satan comes among them. JFB treats Satan here as the adversary who appears only by permission, not as an independent power. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Asked where he has come from, Satan answers that he has been roaming the earth. The exchange shows him as a restless accuser already at work among men before he turns on Job. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: The LORD himself points to Job as without equal on earth, perfect, upright, fearing God, and shunning evil. The commendation comes from God, not from Job's own claim, and it sets up the test that follows. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Satan answers with a suspicion: does Job fear God for nothing? His charge is that Job's religion is bought by blessing, a bargain rather than love, and the whole trial will answer that charge. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Satan points to the hedge God has set around Job, his house, and all he owns, and to the blessing on his work. In Satan's telling, protection and prosperity are the only reasons for Job's obedience. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Satan wagers that if God touches all Job has, Job will curse him to his face. The claim is that suffering will strip away the disguise and expose self-interest underneath. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: The LORD gives Satan power over all Job has, but forbids him to lay a hand on Job himself. JFB stresses the limit: Satan has no power against man until God gives it, and even then it runs only as far as God allows. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: On a day when Job's children are feasting at their eldest brother's house, the first blow falls. Henry notes Satan chose the hour of greatest ease and pleasure, so the trouble would cut deepest. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: A messenger reports the oxen plowing and the donkeys feeding beside them when disaster struck. The ordinary workday setting underlines how sudden the ruin is. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Sabean raiders carry off the livestock and kill the servants with the sword, leaving one man alive to tell it. The human enemy is the first instrument of a trial permitted from above. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: While the first messenger is still speaking, a second reports fire from heaven consuming the sheep and servants. Blow follows blow without pause, so Job has no time to gather himself between losses. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: A third messenger tells of Chaldean bands raiding the camels and killing the servants. Henry observes the design: manifold troubles arriving together, wave upon wave, to overwhelm rather than merely grieve. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: The fourth messenger brings the heaviest news: Job's sons and daughters were eating and drinking in their eldest brother's house when calamity struck. The loss of property was grievous; the loss of the children is of another order. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: A great wind from the wilderness strikes the house's four corners, and it falls on the young people, killing them. The one survivor in each report exists only so Job hears the whole weight at once. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Job rises, tears his robe, shaves his head, falls to the ground, and worships. Henry reads this as humbling himself under God's hand: grief expressed openly, yet turned at once toward worship rather than rebellion. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Job confesses he came naked into the world and will return naked, and blesses the name of the LORD who gave and has taken away. JFB notes that the mother's womb is used poetically for the earth, and that instead of cursing, Job blesses the name of Jehovah. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: In all this Job did not sin or charge God foolishly. JFB explains the sense as Job committing no folly against God: hasty words against God in grief would themselves be folly, and Job refuses them. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
