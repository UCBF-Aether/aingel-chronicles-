Study Notes — Job

Chapter 15

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Eliphaz the Temanite answers a second time, now sharper than before. The second series of speeches abandons the mild opening for direct accusation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Should a wise man utter vain knowledge and fill his belly with the east wind? JFB explains vain knowledge as windy knowledge, and the east wind as the most destructive wind of the region — empty violence filling the inward parts. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Should he reason with unprofitable talk, or speeches with which he can do no good? Eliphaz charges Job's words as useless as well as empty. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Job casts off fear and restrains prayer before God. JFB explains fear as reverence, and prayer as devotion: if God disregarded the righteous and prospered the wicked, all devotion would end. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Job's mouth teaches his iniquity, and he chooses the tongue of the crafty. JFB reads this as Job's own sophistry proving his guilt. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Job's own mouth condemns him, not Eliphaz, and his own lips testify against him. JFB notes no pious man would utter such sentiments. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Was Job the first man born, or made before the hills? JFB asks whether Job is wisdom personified: wisdom existed before the hills, the eternal Son, and the farther back one existed the nearer he was to eternal wisdom. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Has Job heard the secret of God, and does he restrain wisdom to himself? JFB renders secret as a listener in God's secret council, on whose cushions Eastern counsellors sit, and asks whether Job borrowed his wisdom thence. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: What do the friends know that Job does not know, or understand that is not in them? JFB notes Eliphaz retorts Job's words upon himself, claiming the friends are equally aware. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: With the friends are the greyheaded and very aged men, much elder than Job's father. JFB notes the aged are on Eliphaz's side, and Eliphaz himself seems older than Job. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Are the consolations of God small with Job, or is there any secret thing with him? JFB explains the consolations as Eliphaz's revelatory reproof, and asks whether Job despises a gentle word addressed to him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Why does Job's heart carry him away, and what do his eyes wink at? JFB explains winking as eyes evincing pride. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Job turns his spirit against God and lets such words go out of his mouth. JFB renders it as fretting against God and letting fall rash words. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: What is man that he should be clean, and he born of a woman that he should be righteous? JFB notes Eliphaz repeats the revelation of chapter 4 in substance, using Job's own words to strike him with his own weapons. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Behold, God puts no trust in his saints, and the heavens are not clean in his sight. JFB notes the saints here are holy angels, as servants were in chapter 4. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: How much more abominable and filthy is man, who drinks iniquity like water? JFB explains filthy as sour in Arabic, corrupted from original purity. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Eliphaz will show Job, hear him, and declare what he has seen. Against Job's claim that the wicked prosper most, Eliphaz appeals to his own experience and the wisdom of the ancients. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Which wise men have told from their fathers, and have not hidden it. JFB renders it as wisdom handed down from fathers and not concealed. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Unto whom alone the earth was given, and no stranger passed among them. JFB notes Eliphaz boasts like a genuine Arab that his ancestors possessed the land unmixed with foreigners, opposing Job's claim that the earth is given into the wicked's hand. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: The wicked man travails with pain all his days, and the number of his years is hidden to the oppressor. JFB renders travaileth as trembling of himself with no real danger, because he knows not when his life must end. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: A dreadful sound is in the wicked man's ears, and in prosperity the destroyer shall come upon him. JFB explains an evil conscience conceiving alarm at every sudden sound even in a time of peace. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: He believes he shall not return out of darkness, and he is waited for by the sword. JFB explains darkness as danger or calamity, glancing at Job's despair of restoration, and the sword as his destiny, every sword seeming drawn against him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: He wanders abroad for bread, saying where is it, knowing the day of darkness is ready at his hand. JFB notes famine depicts sore need, and ready at his hand is an Arabic phrase for complete readiness, as if in the hand. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Trouble and anguish make him afraid, prevailing against him as a king ready to battle. JFB explains prevail as breaking upon him suddenly and terribly, as a king ready for battle. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Because he stretches out his hand against God and strengthens himself against the Almighty. JFB gives the image of wielding a spear as a bold rebel against God. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: He runs upon God with neck outstretched, upon the thick bosses of his bucklers. JFB explains the rebel and his fellows joining shields into a compact covering against weapons hurled from a fortress. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Because he covers his face with fatness and makes collops of fat on his flanks. JFB notes the well-nourished body is the sign of prosperity, pampered by sensual indulgence until it rebels against God. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: He dwells in desolate cities and in houses which no man inhabits, ready to become heaps. JFB identifies this class as robbers who plunder cities and seize houses of banished citizens, the class Job himself had chosen in chapter 12. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: He shall not be rich, neither shall his substance continue, nor shall he prolong perfection on the earth. JFB renders it as not increasing his riches: he has reached his highest point, and his acquired wealth shall not be extended. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: He shall not depart out of darkness, the flame shall dry up his branches, and by the breath of God's mouth he shall go away. JFB explains the branches as offspring, the flame as the sultry eastern wind shrivelling sappy plants, and the mouth as God's wrath. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: Let not him that is deceived trust in vanity, for vanity shall be his recompense. JFB renders it as not trusting in vanity or being deceived by it, since sin is its own punishment. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: It shall be accomplished before his time, and his branch shall not be green. JFB explains the tree shall not be filled up in its time, but ended before its time, with the withered tree imaging childless extinction. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: He shall shake off his unripe grape as the vine, and cast off his flower as the olive. JFB calls these images of incompleteness, the vine's own act poetically expressing that the sinner's ruin is the fruit of his own conduct. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: For the congregation of hypocrites shall be desolate, and fire shall consume the tabernacles of bribery. JFB explains the binding together of hypocrites as fruitless, and the tabernacles as dwellings of unjust judges, insinuating Job's fire may have come from bribery as an emir. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: They conceive mischief and bring forth vanity, and their belly prepares deceit. JFB calls this bitter irony on the unfruitfulness of the wicked: their conceptions and birthgivings consist solely in mischief. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
