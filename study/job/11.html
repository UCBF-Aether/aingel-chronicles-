Study Notes — Job

Chapter 11

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Zophar the Naamathite answers, opening the first and harshest speech of the third friend. His attack falls on Job's words as much as on his sufferings. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Should not the multitude of words be answered, and should a man full of talk be justified? JFB notes Zophar assails Job's empty words and indirectly the two friends' weak replies, since taciturnity is highly prized among Orientals. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Should Job's lies make men hold their peace, and when he mocks shall no man make him ashamed? JFB renders lies as vain boasting, and mocking as upbraiding God by complaints. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Job has said his doctrine is pure and he is clean in God's eyes. JFB notes doctrine is purposely used of speeches that sounded like lessons, and that Job maintained his sincerity against suspicion, not faultlessness. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Oh that God would speak and open his lips against Job! Zophar wishes for a divine verdict to settle the dispute his own words cannot. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: God would show Job the secrets of wisdom, that they are double to what is, and that God exacts less than Job's iniquity deserves. JFB renders it as God's ways, if their secret wisdom were shown, vastly exceeding men's wisdom, and exacting as consigning much guilt to oblivion in Job's favour. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Can Job by searching find out God, or find out the Almighty unto perfection? JFB renders it as penetrating to the perfections of the Almighty, which no searching reaches. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: God's wisdom is as high as heaven — what can Job do? Deeper than hell — what can he know? JFB notes the forcible abruptness: the heights of heaven, what can you do to attain them with your gaze? — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: The measure of God's wisdom is longer than the earth and broader than the sea. No line Job drops can sound it. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: If God cuts off, shuts up, or gathers together, who can hinder him? JFB renders cut off as passing over like a storm, shut up as imprisoning for trial, and gather together as holding a judicial assembly to pass sentence. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: God knows vain men, and sees wickedness and will consider it. JFB notes God's wisdom detects sin where man's eye cannot reach, seeing wickedness man does not perceive. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Vain man would be wise, though man be born like a wild ass's colt. JFB explains vain as hollow, and the wild ass's colt as a proverb for untamed wildness: man wishes to appear wisely obedient, but is unsubdued in spirit from birth. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: If Job prepares his heart and stretches out his hands toward God, the way to restoration opens. JFB notes the apodosis comes at verse 15, and preparation of heart is obtained by stretching out hands in prayer. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: If iniquity is in Job's hand, let him put it far away, and let not wickedness dwell in his tabernacles. JFB renders it as putting far away the iniquity in his hand, as Zacchaeus did, before the then of verse 15 follows. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Then Job shall lift up his face without spot, be steadfast, and not fear. JFB notes Zophar answers Job's own words, that he would not lift up his head: if he follows this advice he may lift up his face, steadfast like metals made firm by fusion. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Job shall forget his misery, remembering it as waters that pass away. JFB compares the dried stream: as its threatened waves are forgotten when it runs dry, so misery will be remembered only as passed water. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Job's age shall be clearer than noonday, and he shall shine forth, with darkness as the morning. JFB explains the age as his days of life, and the noonday as his former prosperity at its height, with any new darkness reduced to mere morning twilight. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Job shall be secure because there is hope, and shall dig about him and take his rest in safety. JFB notes experience will teach hope in every trial, and dig may mean digging wells, the chief necessity in the East. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Job shall lie down and none shall make him afraid, and many shall make suit unto him. JFB notes the oriental images of prosperity, and making suit as literally stroking his face or caressing him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: But the eyes of the wicked shall fail, they shall not escape, and their hope shall be as the giving up of the ghost. JFB calls this a warning if Job will not turn: the obdurate look in vain for relief, every refuge vanishes, and hope leaves them as breath leaves the body. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
