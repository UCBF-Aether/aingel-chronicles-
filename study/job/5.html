Study Notes — Job

Chapter 5

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Eliphaz asks whether Job will call on any holy one to answer him. JFB reads it as a challenge: after the revelation just given, Job cannot presume that God or any angel round his throne will vouchsafe a judicial reply to his rebellious complaint. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Wrath kills the foolish man, and envy slays the silly one. JFB explains the wrath and envy as Job's own fretful, passionate complaints, not God's wrath or envy. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Eliphaz has seen the foolish taking root, yet suddenly his habitation is cursed. JFB reads the foolish as the wicked, spreading his root wide in prosperity until circumstances suddenly leave his dwelling desolate. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The foolish man's children are far from safety and crushed in the gate, with none to deliver. JFB notes the gate was the place of judgment and public proceedings, and Eliphaz obliquely alludes to the calamity that cut off Job's children. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: The hungry eat up the wicked man's harvest, taking it even out of the thorns, and the thirsty swallow up his substance. JFB stresses how cleanly swept away the harvest is, since the gleaner does not grudge even grain hanging on thorn bushes. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Affliction does not come forth from the dust, nor does trouble spring out of the ground. JFB explains that affliction does not grow like a weed of its own accord, hinting that its cause lay with Job himself. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Yet man is born to trouble, as the sparks fly upward. JFB reads this as God's appointment for sin, passing from Job's particular case to mankind's universal sin and suffering, as necessary as sparks flying upward. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Eliphaz's counsel is that Job should seek God and commit his cause to him. Since affliction is ordered by God on account of sin, the remedy is to turn humbly to God rather than complain. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: God does great things and unsearchable, marvellous things without number. The praise of God's incomprehensible works prepares the call to trust him rather than dispute him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: God gives rain on the earth and sends waters on the fields. His providence reaches the common, life-giving gifts as well as the great wonders. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: God sets the lowly on high, and those who mourn are lifted to safety. JFB connects this with God's unsearchable dealings: they aim to raise the humble and abase the proud, so Job ought to turn humbly to him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: God disappoints the devices of the crafty, so their hands cannot perform their enterprise. JFB notes the Hebrew combines wisdom and happiness in the idea of enduring existence that the crafty fail to secure. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: God takes the wise in their own craftiness, and the counsel of the froward is carried headlong. JFB notes Paul quotes this clause as written Scripture, and that the schemers' plans are precipitated before they ripen. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: The crafty meet with darkness in the daytime and grope at noonday as in the night. JFB reads this as judicial blindness sent upon keen men of the world. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: God saves the poor from the sword of their mouth and from the hand of the mighty. JFB explains the sword as the sword proceeding from their mouth, the weapon of slander and accusation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: So the poor has hope, and iniquity stops her mouth. JFB reads this as God's interposition for the poor, silencing the offending mouth at last. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Happy is the man whom God corrects, so Job should not despise the Almighty's chastening. JFB cautions that the suffering itself is not joyous, but the righteousness of him who sends it and the end for which it is sent make it cause for thankfulness, not complaint. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: God makes sore and binds up, wounds and makes whole with his hands. JFB gives the image of binding a wound, the healing art of the time consisting much in external applications. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: God shall deliver Job in six troubles, and in seven no evil shall touch him. JFB explains the Hebrew idiom: a number is fixed on for emphasis, then raised by one, so the sense is deliverance in all possible troubles. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: In famine God shall redeem from death, and in war from the power of the sword. The sword is personified as a living agent whose hands are stayed. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Job shall be hidden from the scourge of the tongue and not afraid of destruction when it comes. The promise covers slander as well as open calamity. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: At destruction and famine Job shall laugh, and not be afraid of the beasts of the earth. JFB clarifies Eliphaz's meaning: not laughter in spite of calamity, but because those calamities shall not come upon him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Job shall be in league with the stones of the field, and the beasts of the field shall be at peace with him. JFB explains that stones shall not hurt his soil's fertility nor wild beasts his fruits, a picture whose full realization is yet future. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Job shall know his tabernacle is in peace, and shall visit his habitation and not sin. JFB renders it as resting in the assurance that his dwelling is an abode of peace, and that numbering his herd will not disappoint his expectations. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Job shall know his seed is great and his offspring as the grass of the earth. JFB notes the grass is properly herb-bearing seed, a picture of abundant increase. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Job shall come to his grave in a full age, like a shock of corn coming in its season. JFB explains full age as ripeness for death, not mere length of years, and the corn as lifted up and carried home as sheaves. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Lo, this we have searched, so it is; hear it and know it for your good. JFB notes the closing appeal is literally for thyself: the searched-out truth is offered for Job's own benefit. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
