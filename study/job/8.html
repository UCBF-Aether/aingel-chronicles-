Study Notes — Job

Chapter 8

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Bildad the Shuhite answers, more severe and coarse than Eliphaz. JFB frames the whole speech as Bildad's address, pressing Job's guilt from his sufferings. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: How long will Job speak these things, and how long shall his words be like a strong wind? JFB reads the wind as disregarding restraints and daring against God. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Does God pervert judgment, or does the Almighty pervert justice? JFB notes the repeated pervert galling Job, and that Bildad assumes Job's guilt from his sufferings, as if Job would have God let his sins go unpunished. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: If Job's children have sinned against God, he has cast them away for their transgression. JFB renders if as since, and calls this most cutting to the bereaved father: God leaves sinners to the consequence of their sin. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: If Job would seek God betimes and make supplication to the Almighty, God would respond. JFB explains betimes as early, making God the first and chief anxiety. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: If Job were pure and upright, God would surely awake for him and make his righteous habitation prosperous. JFB notes awake means arise to his help, and that Bildad assumes Job's former habitation was one of guilt. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Though Job's beginning was small, his latter end should greatly increase. JFB reads the beginning as the start of his new happiness after restoration, and the latter end as his greatly increased end. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Inquire of the former age, and prepare to the search of their fathers. JFB notes the sages of olden time reached ages beyond Job's contemporaries and could give testimony from fuller experience. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: We are but of yesterday and know nothing, because our days on earth are a shadow. JFB explains yesterday as a recent race, knowing nothing compared with the ancients because of life's brevity, with knowledge handed down in proverbs and tradition. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Shall not the fathers teach Job and utter words out of their heart? JFB notes Bildad answers Job's teach me with inquire of the fathers, whose words are well-considered, from observation and reflection, not merely from the mouth. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Can the rush grow without mire, or the flag grow without water? JFB identifies the rush as papyrus, growing only in marshy places: so the godless thrives only in external prosperity, with no inward stability. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: While the rush is yet green and not cut down, it withers before any other herb. JFB explains that before ripening for the scythe it withers suddenly once moisture is gone, as ruin seizes the godless in the zenith of prosperity. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: So are the paths of all who forget God, and the hypocrite's hope shall perish. JFB notes forgetting God is the distinguishing trait of the godless, and the paths are their ways. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: The hypocrite's hope shall be cut off, and his trust shall be a spider's web. JFB explains the confidence as laid in ruins, to accord with the spider's house metaphor. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: He shall lean on his house but it shall not stand; he shall hold it fast but it shall not endure. JFB notes the spider holds fast by its web and is sustained by it, but the godless is not sustained by the thin web he rests on, fine-spun from his own inventions. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: The godless is green before the sun, and his branch shoots forth in his garden. JFB reads the sun as smiling fortune, during which the hypocrite wondrously progresses like rank weeds spreading over stones and walls. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: His roots are wrapped about the heap and he sees the place of stones. JFB explains the house of stones as the garden wall, which the creeping parasite plant figuratively sees and regards as it spreads over it. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: If God destroys him from his place, then it shall deny him, saying it never saw him. JFB notes the personified place is ashamed of the withered weeds, as the godless man's nearest friends disown him when he falls. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Behold, this is the joy of his way, and out of the earth shall others grow. JFB calls it bitter irony: this is the hypocrite's joy at last, and a fresh crop of weeds always springs up where the old were torn away. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Behold, God will not cast away a perfect man, neither will he help evildoers. JFB notes Bildad regards Job as righteous but fallen into sin: if he repents, God will not cast him away, but those who persevere in sin God will not take by the hand. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Till God fills Job's mouth with laughing and his lips with rejoicing. JFB renders till as to the point that: God's blessing on the repentant will keep increasing until it reaches this joy. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: They who hate Job shall be clothed with shame, and the dwelling of the wicked shall come to naught. JFB identifies Job's haters as the wicked, shamed at the failure of their hope that he would utterly perish, because they instead come to naught. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
