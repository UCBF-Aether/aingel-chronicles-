Study Notes — Job

Chapter 3

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: After seven days of silence Job opens his mouth and curses his day. JFB notes the formula marks deliberation and gravity, and that Job curses his birthday, not his God. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Job speaks, answering not an actual question but the question his whole case virtually poses. JFB warns that desiring death to escape troubles is a mark of corruption, unlike desiring it to be free from sin, yet notes Job's trials were greater and his light less than ours. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Job wishes the day of his birth and the night that announced a man-child would perish. JFB notes night is personified as speaking, and that a male birth was a matter of joy in the East. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Let that day be darkness, unregarded by God and unlit by light. JFB renders it poetically: let God not stoop from his bright throne to seek it out of its dark hiding-place. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Let darkness and the shadow of death claim the day, with a gathered mass of dark clouds over it. JFB reads stain as the ancient chaotic night resuming its rights over light and claiming that day as its own. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Let darkness seize the night and dissolve it utterly, so it has no rejoicing place in the circle of days and months that form the year. JFB stresses the poetic personification: the night is not to rejoice among the days. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Let that night be solitary, with no joyful voice in it. JFB renders solitary as unfruitful: would that it had not given birth to me. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Let those who curse days, even magicians believed able by charms to make a day evil and to rouse leviathan, curse that day. JFB explains the cursers of the day as enchanters claiming power over wild beasts and evil omens. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Let the stars of that night's twilight be dark, let it look for light and have none, and not see the dawning. JFB notes the dawning is literally the eyelashes of morning, the sun's first rays breaking forth like opening eyelids. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Job curses the night because it did not shut the doors of his mother's womb or hide sorrow from his eyes. The complaint turns from the day itself to the birth it allowed. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Why did I not die from the womb, or give up the ghost as I came out of the belly? Job wishes his life had ended at its very beginning, before suffering could reach him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Why did the knees receive me, and why did the breasts nurse me? JFB explains the knees as the father's solemn recognition, placing the newborn on his knees as his own child whom he was bound to rear. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Had he died at birth, Job says, he would have lain quiet and slept. JFB traces the gradation — lain, then quiet, then slept — and notes death in Scripture is called sleep. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: There he would rest with kings and counsellors who built desolate places for themselves. JFB reads the irony: their grand buildings proved ruins, and the great ones, wearied with heaping perishable treasures, find rest only in death. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Or with princes who had gold and filled their houses with silver. Their wealth, like the kings' buildings, ends in the same quiet grave. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Or as a hidden untimely birth that never saw the light, like infants who never lived. JFB judges even that lot preferable to the restless miser's life Job now endures. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: There the wicked cease from troubling, and the weary are at rest. JFB explains the wicked as the ever-restless, full of desires, and the weary as those whose strength is worn out. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: There the prisoners rest together and do not hear the oppressor's voice. Death frees them from their chains. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: The small and great are there, and the servant is free from his master. JFB notes the slave is manumitted from slavery in death. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Why is light given to him in misery, and life to the bitter in soul? JFB notes God is reverentially omitted, and light means life, which ill suits mourners whose feelings accord with the grave. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: They long for death but it does not come, and dig for it more than for hidden treasure. The image turns death into a prize men mine for in vain. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: They rejoice exceedingly and are glad when they can find the grave. What others dread, the sufferer counts as discovery and joy. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Why is light given to a man whose way is hidden, whom God has hedged in? JFB pictures a wanderer who has lost his way and is hedged in with no exit of escape. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Job's sighing comes before he eats, and his roarings pour out like waters. JFB explains the sighing as preventing his eating, and the roarings as the rushing sound of streaming water. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: The thing Job greatly feared has come upon him, and what he was afraid of has overtaken him. JFB traces it through his trials: hearing of one loss he feared another, and now he fears his friends' ill opinion of him as a hypocrite. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Job was not in safety, had no rest, was not quiet, yet trouble came. JFB reads this not of his former state but of the beginning of his troubles, from which there was no intermission — and now a fresh trouble, his friends' suspicion, gives the starting-point of the whole controversy. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
