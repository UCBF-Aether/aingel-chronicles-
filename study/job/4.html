Study Notes — Job

Chapter 4

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Eliphaz the Temanite answers first. JFB calls him the mildest of Job's three accusers, driven to doubt Job's integrity by the greatness of his calamities, his complaints against God, and the belief that calamities prove guilt. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Eliphaz asks leave to attempt a word, and wonders whether Job will be grieved by it. JFB notes that pious friends often count only a touch what the sufferer feels as a wound. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Eliphaz reminds Job that he instructed many and strengthened weak hands. The past ministry is now turned into an argument: the counsellor should bear his own counsel. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Job's words upheld him who was falling, and he strengthened feeble knees. Eliphaz credits Job's former comfort of others before pressing the contrast with his present collapse. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Now trouble has come on Job himself and he faints; it touches him and he is troubled. JFB renders troubled as unhinged, having lost self-command. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Eliphaz asks whether Job's fear of God is not his confidence, and the uprightness of his ways his hope. JFB, by transposition, reads it as an appeal: if fear is hope and uprightness is confidence, then who ever perished being innocent? — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Remember, Eliphaz urges: who ever perished being innocent, or where were the righteous cut off? The question assumes suffering always traces to guilt, though JFB cautions that outward appearance alone cannot judge a life. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Those who plow iniquity and sow wickedness reap the same. JFB cites the harvest proverb running through Scripture: the sowing determines the reaping. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: By the blast of God they perish, and by the breath of his nostrils they are consumed. JFB explains the breath of his nostrils as God's anger, figured by the fiery winds of the East. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: The roaring of the lion, the voice of the fierce lion, and the teeth of young lions are broken. JFB reads the lions as wicked men: calamity falls on them despite all their resources, just as destruction falls on the lion despite its strength. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: The old lion perishes for lack of prey, and the stout lion's whelps are scattered abroad. JFB notes the five Hebrew terms for lion express phases of wickedness, and the race becomes extinct when its strength fails. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: A thing was secretly brought to Eliphaz, and his ear received a little of it. JFB explains that Eliphaz confirms his view by a divine declaration secretly imparted, a whisper conveying more than articulate words could utter. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: In thoughts from the visions of the night, when deep sleep falls on men, fear came upon Eliphaz. JFB reads this as manifold thoughts before the night visions began, so the experience is not presented as a mere delusive dream. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Fear and trembling seized Eliphaz, making all his bones shake. The terror of the vision is felt in the body before any word is spoken. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: A spirit passed before his face, and the hair of his flesh stood up. The unseen presence is sensed before it is heard. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: The apparition stood still, yet its form was indistinct, and then came a gentle voice. JFB explains that it first glides, then stands still in shadowy indistinctness, and the voice is a gentle murmur, not silence. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: The voice asks whether mortal man shall be more just than God, or a man more pure than his Maker. JFB notes two Hebrew words for man — one for feebleness, one for strength — and neither, feeble or strong, is righteous before God. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: God puts no trust in his servants, and charges his angels with folly. JFB explains folly as imperfection attributed even to angels in comparison with God, whose creaturely holiness at best wants moral consideration. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: How much less, then, those who dwell in houses of clay, whose foundation is dust, crushed before the moth? JFB notes Eastern houses of sun-dried clay bricks wash away easily, and man, who cannot stand before the moth physically, cannot stand before God morally. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: They are destroyed from morning to evening, perishing forever without any regarding it. JFB reads the day as one short day, and the destruction as what would follow if God withdrew his loving protection. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Does not their excellency go away, so that they die even without wisdom? JFB notes the images of a bow unstrung and a tabernacle's cords taken down, and concludes that man's continued existence proves God's inconceivable wisdom and holiness, which alone save him from ruin. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
