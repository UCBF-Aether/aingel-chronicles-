Study Notes — Job

Chapter 37

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: At this also Elihu's heart trembles, and is moved out of his place. JFB explains at this as when he hears the thundering of the Divine Majesty, perhaps the storm already begun out of which God was to address Job. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Hear attentively the noise of God's voice, and the sound that goes out of his mouth. JFB renders hear attentively as the thunder, and sound as the muttering of the thunder: hearing it, one feels good reason to tremble. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: God directs it under the whole heaven, and his lightning unto the ends of the earth. JFB explains directeth as applying to the pealing roll of the thunder, showing God's all-embracing power, and ends literally as wings or skirts, the habitable earth compared to an extended garment. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: After it a voice roars: God thunders with the voice of his excellency, and he will not stay them when his voice is heard. JFB notes the thunderclap follows at an interval after the flash, and stay them as not holding back the lightnings, or the rain and hail that accompany thunder. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: God thunders marvellously with his voice; great things does he, which we cannot comprehend. JFB notes the sublimity lies in God being everywhere in the storm, directing it whither he will. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: For God says to the snow, be thou on the earth; likewise to the small rain, and to the great rain of his strength. JFB renders be as more forcible than fall, and notes the shower increasing from small to great is expressed by the plural showers following the singular shower, as winter rain. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: God seals up the hand of every man, that all men may know his work. JFB explains that in winter God stops man's out-of-doors activity, sealing or closing up hands, so that all men whom he has made may be brought to acknowledgment of his work, in antithesis to man's own work which at other times makes men forget their dependence on God. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Then the beasts go into dens, and remain in their places. JFB notes it is beautifully ordered that during the cold, when they could not obtain food, many lie torpid, a state wherein they need no food. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Out of the south comes the whirlwind, and cold out of the north. JFB renders south literally as chambers, the whirlwinds poetically pent up by God in his southern chambers whence he sends them forth, and north literally as scattering, the north wind scattering the clouds. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: By the breath of God frost is given, and the breadth of the waters is straitened. JFB explains breath of God poetically as the ice-producing north wind, frost as ice, and straitened as physically accurate: frost compresses the expanded liquid into a congealed mass. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Also by watering God wearies the thick cloud: he scatters his bright cloud. JFB explains how the thunderclouds are dispersed or employed by God either for correction or mercy: by loading the cloud with water he burdens it so that it falls in rain, and bright cloud literally as cloud of his light, that is, of his lightning. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: And it is turned round about by God's counsels, that they may do whatsoever he commands them upon the face of the world in the earth. JFB explains it as the cloud of lightning, counsels as guidance or steering: the clouds obey God's guidance as the ship does the helmsman, and neither is haphazard in its movements. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: God causes it to come, whether for correction, or for his land, or for mercy. JFB renders it literally as he makes the rain-cloud find place, whether for correction if destined for his land, the part inhabited by man, or for mercy. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Hearken unto this, O Job: stand still, and consider the wondrous works of God. Elihu pauses the storm description to bid Job stop and consider. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Dost thou know when God disposed them, and caused the light of his cloud to shine? JFB renders when as how, and disposed as laying his charge on these wonders to arise, asking how light arises from the dark thundercloud. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Dost thou know the balancings of the clouds, the wondrous works of him which is perfect in knowledge? JFB renders it as hast thou understanding of the balancings, how the clouds are poised in the air so that their watery gravity does not bring them to earth, the descending moisture meeting warmer temperature which dissipates it into vapour and counteracts the descending force. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: How thy garments are warm, when God quiets the earth by the south wind? JFB explains it as dost thou know how thy body grows warm so as to affect thy garments with heat, when God makes still and sultry the earth by the south wind. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Hast thou with God spread out the sky, which is strong, and as a molten looking glass? JFB explains with him as like as he does, spread out as given expanse to, strong pieces as firm, whence the term firmament, and molten looking glass as the bright smiling sky, mirrors then being formed of molten polished metal, not glass. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Teach us what we shall say unto God, for we cannot order our speech by reason of darkness. JFB notes men cannot explain God's wonders, so we ought to be dumb and not contend with God, and order as frame, with darkness as ignorance of mind. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Shall it be told God that Elihu speaks? If a man speak, surely he shall be swallowed up. JFB explains that what a mortal says against God's dealings is not worthy of being told him, in opposition to Job's wish to speak before God. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: And now men see not the bright light which is in the clouds, but the wind passes, and cleanses them. JFB renders cleanseth as clearing the air of clouds: when the bright light of the sun, previously not seen through clouds, suddenly shines out owing to the wind clearing them away, the effect is dazzling, so if God's majesty, now hidden, were suddenly revealed it would spread darkness over Job's eyes. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Fair weather comes out of the north: with God is terrible majesty. JFB renders it as golden splendour, with brightness chiefly associated with the north because the north wind clears the air, and majesty as splendour upon God as a garment. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Touching the Almighty, we cannot find him out: he is excellent in power, and in judgment, and in plenty of justice: he will not afflict. JFB explains afflict as oppressively, so as to pervert judgment as Job implied, and notes the reading he answers not, that is, gives no account of his dealings, is like a transcriber's correction from chapter 33. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Men do therefore fear him: he respects not any that are wise of heart. JFB renders do as ought, and wise as in their own conceits. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
