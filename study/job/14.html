Study Notes — Job

Chapter 14

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Man born of a woman is of few days and full of trouble. JFB notes woman means feeble and, in the East, looked down upon, so man born of one so frail must be frail himself, and few days is literally short of days. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Man comes forth like a flower and is cut down, flees as a shadow and continues not. The flower and shadow give his brevity two images: beauty cut down, and a shape that does not stay. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Does God open his eyes upon such a one and bring him into judgment with himself? JFB explains open eyes not as graciousness but as sharply fixing eyes upon one so frail, asking whether he is worthy of such constant watching by the Almighty. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Who can bring a clean thing out of an unclean? Not one. JFB calls this a plea in mitigation, resting on the doctrine of original sin held from the first: man unclean from birth cannot be expected to present perfect cleanness. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Seeing man's days are determined, the number of his months with God, and he has appointed bounds he cannot pass. The limits are fixed by God, not negotiable by man. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Turn from man, that he may rest, till he accomplishes as a hireling his day. JFB renders turn as turning God's eyes from jealous watching, and accomplish as enjoy: at least the hireling's measure of rest and reward. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: For there is hope of a tree, if it is cut down, that it will sprout again and its tender branch will not cease. Man may claim a peaceful life the more, JFB notes, since death separates him from it without return to the present condition — yet this does not deny a future life. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Though its root waxes old in the earth and its stock dies in the ground, yet through the scent of water it will bud and bring forth boughs like a plant. JFB notes the scent is the exhalation causing germination, and the tree is personified with volition in antithesis to man. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Yet through the scent of water it will bud, and bring forth boughs like a plant. The tree's renewal stands against man's finality in this present life. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: But man dies and wastes away, gives up the ghost, and where is he? JFB contrasts two Hebrew words: the mighty man dies, the man of earth gives up the ghost, reduced to nothing, unable to revive in the present state as the tree does. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: As waters fail from the sea and the flood decays and dries up, so man lies down and rises not. JFB explains sea as a lake or pool from a river's spreading, like the Euphrates, utterly dried up, answering to the outstretched corpse of the once living man. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: So man lies down and rises not till the heavens be no more, nor is he raised out of his sleep. JFB notes this implies only no hope of living again in the present order, not no hope in a new order, since the resurrection is preceded by the dissolution of the heavens. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Oh that God would hide Job in the grave until his wrath is past, appointing a set time and remembering him. JFB notes Job wishes to be kept hidden until God's wrath passes, with a decreed set time. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: If a man dies, shall he live again? All the days of Job's appointed time he will wait till his change comes. JFB explains the implied answer as hope of living, though not in the present order, and change as release, like a soldier relieved from duty by the guard. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: God shall call, and Job will answer; God will have a desire to the work of his hands. JFB reads this of the resurrection, and desire literally as becoming pale with anxious longing, implying God would not leave his wonderfully made creature in oblivion. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: For now God numbers Job's steps, but does not watch over his sin. JFB renders it as a future hope: God will number his steps to guard him from wandering, not jealously watch over his sin as now. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Job's transgression is sealed up in a bag, and God sews up his iniquity. JFB explains sealed purses in the East, and sewing as covering: to cover sins is to forgive them completely and think of them no more. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: And surely the mountain falling comes to naught, and the rock is removed out of its place. JFB notes the turn back to gloomy bodings: even the solid mountain fades, so man cannot hope to escape decay in the present world. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: The waters wear the stones, and floods wash away the dust of the earth, and God destroys the hope of man. JFB traces the gradation from mountains to rocks to stones to dust, until the solid mountain disappears utterly. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: God prevails forever against man, passes him, changes his countenance, and sends him away. JFB explains prevail as overpowering by superior strength, passeth as dying, and changed countenance as the visage altered at death. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Job's sons come to honour and he knows it not, or are brought low and he perceives it not. JFB selects the severance of parents and children as the striking trait of the dead's separation from all that passes in the world. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: But his flesh upon him shall have pain, and his soul within him shall mourn. JFB notes flesh and soul describe the whole man, and that in his gloomy frame Job anticipates the dead limited to thought of their own misery, with pain attributed by personification to the dead. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
