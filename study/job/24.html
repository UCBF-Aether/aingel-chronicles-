Study Notes — Job

Chapter 24

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Why, seeing times are not hidden from the Almighty, do they that know him not see his days? JFB explains times as times of punishment, and days as days of vengeance: Job does not doubt they are appointed, but wishes God would let all now see it is so. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Some remove the landmarks, violently take away flocks, and feed thereof. JFB introduces verses 2-24 as instances of the wicked doing the worst deeds with seeming impunity, with landmarks as boundaries between pastures. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: They drive away the ass of the fatherless and take the widow's ox for a pledge. JFB notes others really do, with impunity, what Eliphaz falsely charged the afflicted Job with. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: They turn the needy out of the way, and the poor of the earth hide themselves together. JFB explains it literally as pushing the poor out of their road, taking advantage by force, with the poor hiding from oppressors who robbed them of all and drove them to unfrequented places. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Behold, as wild asses in the desert, the poor go forth to their work, rising betimes for a prey, the wilderness yielding food for them and their children. JFB explains the wild asses as Bedouin robbers with unbridled desert wildness, whose lawless work is robbery, rising early as Eastern travel begins before heat. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: They reap every one his corn in the field, and gather the vintage of the wicked. JFB explains reaping metaphorically as the robbers' various grain, and the vintage as the vintage of robbery, not honest industry, gathered late in autumn. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: They cause the naked to lodge without clothing, having no covering in the cold. JFB notes the robbers are regardless of life's comforts, passing the night naked and uncovered, with frost not uncommon at night in those regions. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: They are wet with the showers of the mountains, and embrace the rock for want of a shelter. JFB explains the plundered travellers embracing the rock as taking refuge under it. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: They pluck the fatherless from the breast, and take a pledge of the poor. JFB notes kidnapping children for slaves, passing from desert wrongs to wrongs among habitations, with pledge as the poor debtor's garment. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: They cause the naked to go without clothing, taking away the sheaf from the hungry. JFB contrasts open desert robbery of garments with refined robbery in civilized life under a pledge, where the poor labour in harvest fields and are denied even the corn they carry. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Which make oil within their walls, and tread their winepresses, and suffer thirst. JFB explains the poor pressing oil within the oppressor's walled vineyards and olive gardens, yet not allowed to quench thirst with grapes and olives. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Men groan from out of the city, and the soul of the wounded cries out, yet God lays not folly to them. JFB reads men as the dying, answering to the wounded, oppressed sufferers in the city crying in vain, with God taking no account by punishing their oppressors' sin, the gist of the whole list. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: They are of those that rebel against the light, knowing not its ways nor abiding in its paths. JFB notes the turn from open sins to sins done in the dark, with light both literal and figurative, and paths as places where light shines. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: The murderer rising with the light kills the poor and needy, and in the night is as a thief. JFB explains the robber lying in wait at early dawn, when travellers set out and labourers go to work, while thieves steal at night: the same man steals at night and murders at dawn to escape detection. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: The eye of the adulterer waits for the twilight, saying no eye shall see me, and disguises his face. JFB notes the veil put on to escape detection. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: In the dark they dig through houses, which they had marked for themselves in the daytime, knowing not the light. JFB explains Eastern houses of sun-dried mud bricks dug through, and marked as sealed up in their houses by day for their own ends, shunning light. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: For the morning is to them even as the shadow of death; if one knows them, they are in the terrors of the shadow of death. JFB notes they shrink from morning light as other men shrink from blackest darkness, familiar with the terrors of darkness. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: He is swift as the waters, his portion is cursed in the earth, and he beholds not the way of the vineyards. JFB notes Job here quotes his adversaries ironically, as before: the sinner swiftly passes away like a thing floating on waters, cursed by witnesses, turning not to enjoy his pleasant possessions. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Drought and heat consume the snow waters, so does the grave those which have sinned. JFB gives the Arabian image: melted snow quickly dries in sunburnt sand leaving no trace, expressing the swift, utter destruction of the godless. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: The womb shall forget him, the worm shall feed sweetly on him, and he shall be no more remembered, with wickedness broken as a tree. JFB notes even the mother who last forgets her child shall dismiss him, the worm feeding sweetly as on a delicate morsel, and wickedness broken utterly like a staff. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: He evil entreats the barren that bears not, and does not good to the widow. JFB explains the friends' reason for the sinner's fate: the barren without sons to protect her, and the widow without a husband to support her, are his victims. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: He draws also the mighty with his power, rising up though no man is sure of life. JFB gives Job's reply to the friends' opinion: experience proves the contrary, for God prolongs the mighty with his power, and the wicked rises from his sickbed though he had given up hope of life. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Though it is given him to be in safety, whereon he rests, yet God's eyes are upon their ways. JFB notes God gives the wicked safety and security, and Job's wonder is that God so favours them while his eyes are open to their wicked ways. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: They are exalted for a little while, but are gone and brought low, taken out of the way as all others, and cut off as the tops of the ears of corn. JFB repeats Job's point that sinners die in exalted positions by a quick, easy, natural death, gathered as ripe corn in full age, not prematurely. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: And if it be not so now, who will make Job a liar, and make his speech nothing worth? The challenge closes the catalogue: disprove the observed facts, or the friends' theory falls. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
