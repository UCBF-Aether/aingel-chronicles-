Study Notes — Job

Chapter 21

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Job answers, asking for a hearing before the friends mock again. The chapter is his closest approach to the disputed question: whether prosperity marks the true church. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Hear diligently Job's speech, and let this be your consolations. JFB notes that listening calmly will itself count as consolations, alluding to Eliphaz's boasted consolations Job felt as aggravations. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Suffer Job to speak, and after he has spoken, mock on. JFB renders it literally as begin your mockings after he speaks, accepting their scorn as the price of a hearing. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: As for Job, is his complaint to man? If it were so, why should his spirit not be troubled? JFB notes his difficulty is not with man but with God: why God afflicts him as if he were the guilty hypocrite alleged. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Mark Job, be astonished, and lay your hand upon your mouth. JFB explains the hand on the mouth as the gesture of silence, enough in Job's case to awe them into silence. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Even when Job remembers, he is afraid, and trembling takes hold on his flesh. JFB asks whether they can wonder he broke out in complaints, when the struggle was not with men but with the Almighty, reconciling innocent woes with divine justice. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Why do the wicked live, become old, and grow mighty in power? JFB notes old opposes the friends' assertion that sinners are cut off early. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Their seed is established in their sight, and their offspring before their eyes. JFB notes this opposes Bildad and Eliphaz on cut-off children: the wicked's children flourish visibly. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Their houses are safe from fear, and the rod of God is not upon them. JFB renders it literally peace from fear: their house is peace itself, far removed from fear. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Their bull genders and fails not, their cow calves and casts not her calf. JFB explains the first clause as easy conception, the second as happy birth. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: They send forth their little ones like a flock, and their children dance. JFB explains the little ones as lambkins sent out to happy sports under the skies, dancing not formally but skipping like lambs. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: They take the timbrel and harp, and rejoice at the sound of the organ. JFB renders take as lifting up the voice in song, timbrel as tambourine, and organ as pipe, so voice, strings, and winds enumerate all kinds of music. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: They spend their days in wealth, and in a moment go down to the grave. JFB notes wealth is old English for prosperity, and in a moment means not by lingering disease: lengthened life, prosperity, and a sudden painless death. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Therefore they say unto God, depart from us, for we desire not the knowledge of thy ways. JFB renders therefore as and yet: they say this not in words but virtually by conduct. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: What is the Almighty that we should serve him, and what profit should we have if we pray unto him? JFB notes sinners ask not what is right but what profits self, forgetting that wanting religion will cost self infinitely more. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Lo, their good is not in their hand; the counsel of the wicked is far from Job. JFB explains good is in God's hand, not theirs, which is Job's difficulty, and renders the second clause as may the counsel of the wicked be far from me, lest he be thought to admire it. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: How often is the candle of the wicked put out, and how often does their destruction come upon them? JFB notes Job here quotes the friends' assertion to refute it, borrowing Bildad's lamp image to question its truth. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: They are as stubble before the wind, and as chaff that the storm carries away. JFB notes Job alludes to Bildad's sentiment, using his own previous words against him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: God lays up the wicked's iniquity for his children, rewarding him so he shall know it. JFB calls equally questionable the friends' assertion that children are punished if the father is not, and that God rewards him here so he knows it. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: His eyes shall see his destruction, and he shall drink of the wrath of the Almighty. JFB calls questionable the assertion that the sinner sees his own and his children's destruction in his lifetime, with drink as the cup of wrath. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: For what pleasure has he in his house after him, when the number of his months is cut off in the midst? JFB explains the friends' argument: what has he to do with his children when dead, unless his eyes see their destruction, and cut off as the allotted months fulfilled. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Shall any teach God knowledge, seeing he judges those that are high? JFB replies that in these assertions the friends try to teach God how he ought to deal with men, rather than prove he does so in fact, since God gives prosperity and adversity as he pleases on inscrutable principles. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: One dies in his full strength, being wholly at ease and quiet. JFB renders it literally in the bone of his perfection, the full strength of unimpaired prosperity. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: His breasts are full of milk, and his bones are moistened with marrow. JFB explains breasts as skins or vessels for fluids, or stations of herds near water, with the body compared to a well-watered field, abundant substance and vigorous health. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: And another dies in the bitterness of his soul, and never eats with pleasure. The same death meets opposite lives: fullness for one, bitterness without pleasure for the other. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: They shall lie down alike in the dust, and the worms shall cover them. Prosperous and bitter alike share dust and worms. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Behold, Job knows the friends' thoughts, and the devices they wrongfully imagine against him. JFB notes their wrongful thoughts are stated but not honestly named: they insinuate his guilt without naming him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: For the friends say, where is the house of the prince, and where are the dwelling places of the wicked? JFB notes house refers to the fall of Job's eldest son's house, and prince in a bad sense as tyrant or oppressor, with dwelling places as pavilions of a great emir. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Have you not asked them that go by the way, and do you not know their tokens? JFB notes Job appeals to travellers' observation, impartial witnesses unconnected with him, opposing Bildad and Zophar. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: The wicked is reserved to the day of destruction, and shall be brought forth to the day of wrath. JFB notes travellers testify the wicked is spared now and reserved hereafter: Job does not deny future punishment, only punishment in this life, with lots reversed hereafter. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: Who shall declare his way to his face, and who shall repay him what he has done? JFB asks who dares charge him openly in this present life, granting he shall be repaid hereafter. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: Yet shall he be brought to the grave, and shall remain in the tomb. JFB renders yet as and, with solemn pomp to the graves, and remain as watching on the tomb, his remembrance preserved by monument, opposing Bildad's forgotten name. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: The clods of the valley shall be sweet unto him, and every man shall draw after him, as innumerable before him. JFB notes the earth is light upon him and his repose sweet, sharing the common lot of mortals, with most men following in his steps. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: How then do the friends comfort Job in vain, seeing in their answers there remains falsehood? JFB explains falsehood as transgression: their consolations are contradicted by facts and betray evil intent against him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
