Study Notes — Job

Chapter 13

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Job's eye has seen all this, his ear has heard and understood it. JFB notes all this refers to the dealings of Providence discussed in chapter 12. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: What the friends know, Job knows also; he is not inferior to them. The claim of equality, made before, is now the ground for appealing past them to God. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Surely Job would speak to the Almighty and desire to reason with God. JFB notes Job wishes to plead his cause before God, increasingly convinced his would-be physicians are valueless. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: But the friends are forgers of lies and physicians of no value. JFB renders forgers as artful twisters of vain speeches. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Oh that the friends would altogether hold their peace, and it should be their wisdom! JFB cites the Arab saying that the wise are dumb, and silence is wisdom. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Hear now Job's reasoning, and hearken to the pleadings of his lips. He demands a hearing for his own defence, not their verdict. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Will the friends speak wickedly for God and talk deceitfully for him? JFB explains deceitfully as using fallacies to vindicate God, as if the end justified the means, by asserting Job is a sinner because he is a sufferer. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Will they accept God's person and contend for God? JFB explains accepting his person as a judge favouring one party for personal reasons, and contends that partiality can never please the impartial God, nor a good cause excuse unfair arguments. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Is it good that God should search them out, or as one man mocks another, do they mock him? JFB asks whether the issue will be good when God searches their arguments, and renders mock as deceiving God as one man deceives another. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: God will surely reprove the friends if they secretly accept persons. JFB notes God can vindicate his own acts successfully and needs no fallacious argument from man. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Shall not God's excellency make them afraid, and his dread fall upon them? JFB asks whether God's majesty will not frighten them from employing sophisms in his name. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: The friends' remembrances are like unto ashes, and their bodies to bodies of clay. JFB explains remembrances as well-remembered proverbial maxims, parables of ashes in their lightness, and bodies as clay entrenchments easily destroyed, which will not shelter them when God reproves their injustice to Job. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Hold their peace, let Job alone, that he may speak, and let come on him what will. JFB notes Job wishes to be spared their speeches so he can speak out all his mind about his wretchedness, happen what will. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Why does Job take his flesh in his teeth and put his life in his hand? JFB calls it a proverb for anxiously desiring to save one's life, imaged by a wild beast carrying prey in its teeth and a man holding in his hand what he would keep secure. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Though God slay Job, yet will he trust in him, but he will maintain his own ways before him. JFB notes the textual reading is not, agreeing with Job's hopeless passages, yet he will vindicate himself before God as not a hypocrite. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: He also shall be Job's salvation, for a hypocrite shall not come before him. JFB explains that this very desire to come before God speaks for his acquittal, since a hypocrite would not wish to come before him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Hear diligently Job's speech and his declaration with their ears. JFB notes the declaration is his wish to justify himself immediately before God, heard attentively. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Behold, Job has ordered his cause, and knows he shall be justified. JFB explains ordered as constant preparation for defence in confidence of innocence. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Who is he that will plead with Job? If any can contend and prove him false, he will hold his tongue and give up the ghost. JFB renders it as staking his life on it: silence and death if disproved. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Only do not two things unto Job, and then he will not hide himself from God. JFB notes the address turns to God, and not hide means standing forth boldly to maintain his cause. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Withdraw God's hand far from Job, and let not his dread make him afraid. The two conditions for a fair hearing are removal of the crushing hand and of terror. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Then call, and Job will answer, or let Job speak and God answer him. JFB explains the trial language: call is a challenge to the defendant, answer the defence, speak the plaintiff's plea, answer the reply to the plea. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: How many are Job's iniquities and sins? Make them known to him. JFB notes the catalogue ought to be great to judge by the severity crushing one already bowed down, and that Job claims unconsciousness of a single particular sin, much less many. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Why does God hide his face and hold Job for his enemy? JFB explains hiding the face by the gloomy impression of the sun suddenly clouded over, and enemy as one robbed of power by ceaseless sufferings. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Will God break a leaf driven to and fro, and pursue the dry stubble? JFB compares Job to a fallen leaf the storm still chases, and break literally to shaking with terrors. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: God writes bitter things against Job and makes him possess the iniquities of his youth. JFB explains writest as a judicial phrase for noting down determined punishment, and possessing as inheriting in old age the punishments of sins acquired in youth. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: God puts Job's feet in stocks, looks narrowly to all his paths, and sets a print upon his heels. JFB explains the stocks as a prisoner's fastening until execution, the narrow look as an overseer's watch, and the print as a line drawn round his soles beyond which he must not move. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: And he, as a rotten thing, consumes, as a garment moth-eaten. JFB notes Job now speaks of himself in the third person, forming the transition to the general lot of man in chapter 14. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
