Study Notes — Job

Chapter 7

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Is there not an appointed time to man on earth, and are not his days like the days of a hireling? JFB renders appointed time as warfare, a hard conflict with evil, and notes Job details the miseries his friends will see if they look on him as he asked. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: As a servant earnestly desires the shadow, and a hireling looks for his reward, so Job longs for the close of his hard service. JFB notes Easterners measure time by shadow length, and that longing for the end proves Job did not regard the grave as mere sleep. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Job is made to possess months of vanity, and wearisome nights are appointed to him. JFB calls it an ironic inheritance: to be heir is usually joy, but here it is the entail of a dismal, involuntary lot, numbered out for him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: When Job lies down he asks when he shall arise and the night be gone, and he is full of tossings till dawn. JFB renders it literally: when shall be the flight of the night? — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Job's flesh is clothed with worms and clods of dust, his skin broken and loathsome. JFB notes maggots breed in elephantiasis sores, the clods are a crust of dried filth, and the skin heals and breaks out again with running matter. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Job's days are swifter than a weaver's shuttle and spent without hope. JFB explains that each day like the shuttle leaves a thread behind, but Job's thought is that his web must swiftly be cut off, without hope of recovery. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Remember, Job tells God, that his life is wind, and his eye shall no more see good. JFB notes this address to God marks a softer mood: a beam from former prosperity draws regret at leaving a world of light. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: The eye of him that has seen Job shall see him no more, and God's eyes are upon him but he is not. JFB stresses that Job disappears even while God looks on him, unable to survive the gaze of Jehovah. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: As the cloud is consumed and vanishes, so he who goes down to the grave shall come up no more. JFB notes Sheol is the place of departed spirits, and the sense is only that he shall not come up in the present order of things. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: He shall return no more to his house, nor shall his place know him any more. JFB notes the Eastern love of dwelling, whose desertion is a frequent theme of sorrow in Arabian elegies. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Therefore Job will not refrain his mouth, but speak in the anguish of his spirit and complain in the bitterness of his soul. JFB reads therefore I, at all events, as claiming at least the melancholy satisfaction of venting his sorrow in words. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Am I a sea, or a whale, that God sets a watch over me? JFB explains the sea as a violent rebel against the Lord of nature who curbs it, and the whale as a sea monster like the crocodile the Egyptians watched most carefully. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: When Job says his bed shall comfort him and his couch ease his complaint, God instead scares him with dreams and terrifies him through visions. The comfort he hoped for in sleep turns to fright. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: God scares Job with dreams and terrifies him through visions. JFB attributes the frightful dreams to his elephantiasis, which he ascribes to God, since the common belief assigned all night visions to God. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: So Job's soul chooses strangling and death rather than his life. JFB notes one rendering as wishing to strangle himself, and the simpler sense as choosing any violent death rather than his wasted, diseased frame. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Job loathes his life and would not live always; let God alone, for his days are vanity. JFB renders let me alone as ceasing to afflict him for the few vain days still left. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: What is man that God should magnify him and set his heart upon him? JFB notes Job wonders why God expends such attention on man as to make him the subject of severe trials, where David used the same words to wonder God does so much for insignificant man. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: God visits man every morning and tries him every moment. JFB gives the shepherd image: counting the flock every morning to see all are there, though Job complains it is trials, not mercies, that are new every morning. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: How long will God not depart from Job, nor let him alone till he swallows down his spittle? JFB explains the jealous keeper's gaze and the Arabic proverb for a brief respite, like our till I draw my breath. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Job confesses he has sinned, yet asks what he can do against God, the preserver of men, and why he is set as a mark. JFB notes Job bore his trials patiently as sent by God, and only his reason cannot reconcile their ceaseless continuance with his ideas of the divine nature. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Why does God not pardon Job's transgression and take away his iniquity, for soon he shall sleep in the dust and God shall seek him in the morning but he shall not be? JFB explains the morning as a sick man sought at dawn and found dead in the night: if help does not come at once, it will be too late. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
