Study Notes — Judith

Chapter 1

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The opening "Now" points to the author's internal purpose, and many books begin with "And," showing their connection with a general history; the building of Ecbatana took place soon after the destruction of Nineve mentioned in the preceding book. Arphaxad is probably the Dejoces of Herodotus, to whom he attributes the building of Ecbatana, capital of Media, or rather resembles Phraortes, who fortified and embellished the city; Ecbatana, or Agbata, signifies "variegated" in Arabic, as its seven walls rising one above another were marked white, black, red, blue, dark red, silver, and gold. — George Leo Haydock

2 COMMENTARY: The Greek adds that the hewn stones were "three cubits broad and six long," and the ancients aimed at solidity in architecture, as their ruins show; the height and breadth figures vary between the versions — Greek 70 high and 50 broad, Old Vulgate 60 high and 50 broad — and the Greek adds towers above the gates 100 cubits, with foundations 60 cubits broad and gates 70 cubits high and 40 broad, to send out armies and draw up infantry. — George Leo Haydock

4 COMMENTARY: On Arphaxad's glorying, the note recalls, "Fool, this night wilt thou perish" (Luke 12:20) — pride in such power is answered by sudden death. — George Leo Haydock

5 COMMENTARY: This Nabuchodonosor is not the king of Babylon who took and destroyed Jerusalem, but another of the same name who reigned in Nineve, called Saosduchin by profane historians, who succeeded Asarhaddon among the Assyrians and was contemporary with Manasses, king of Juda; the Jews frequently give foreign princes names different from those in profane history. — George Leo Haydock

6 COMMENTARY: Ragau lies near Rages, and Jadason is Mount Jason above the Caspian gates, unless it be the city Jassu in Armenia; the Greek has "the Hydaspes" here, and various battles were fought during this war, which the Greek intimates lasted seven years, ending with Arphaxad attacked, Nineve sacked, the king slain, and Nabuchodonosor abandoning himself with his army to pleasure in the conquered city for 120 days. — George Leo Haydock

8 COMMENTARY: Esdrelon is in Syriac "Jezrael," the usual name in Scripture (Joshua 17:16). — George Leo Haydock

9 COMMENTARY: Jesse, or Gessen, is where Joseph placed his brethren (Genesis 46:34). — George Leo Haydock

11 COMMENTARY: On their refusal, the Greek adds that they "did not come to help him in the war, because they feared him not," supposing he would have enough to do with Arphaxad, as he was but like their equal, or as one man. — George Leo Haydock

12 COMMENTARY: Those subject to the king did wrong in refusing aid, but the Jews were under no such obligation, and God espoused their cause the more as the king set up for a deity (chapter 3); elated by victory like other conquerors, he had at first entertained thoughts of universal dominion. — George Leo Haydock

No note for verse(s) 3, 7, 10, 13, 14, 15, 16 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
