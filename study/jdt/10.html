Study Notes — Judith

Chapter 10

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

2 COMMENTARY: She rose where she had fallen down, and called her maid, and went down into the house in which she abode in the sabbath days and in her feast days; the Greek adds, "where she spent the sabbath and festival days, she rolled up her" sackcloth and widow's garments — she remained in her oratory therefore only on other days. — George Leo Haydock

3 COMMENTARY: And pulled off the sackcloth which she had on, and put off the garments of her widowhood, and washed her body all over with water, and anointed herself with precious ointment, and combed her hair, and put on her bonnet; "body" is in Complutensian Greek "mouth," but other editions seem more accurate, and "best" ointment is in Greek "thick," probably the myrobalanum of Pliny. The bonnet, or mitre, was tied with ribbands hanging down behind, like those of bishops. — George Leo Haydock

4 COMMENTARY: And she took sandals upon her feet, and put about her her bracelets, and her chains, and her rings, and her earrings, and all her ornaments, and decked herself to be admired; sandals were highly ornamented and worn by people of quality, and lilies hung pendent from the neck. The Greek reads, "and she was richly adorned, so that she might captivate (literally, deceive) the eyes of whatever men should behold her." The fathers highly extol her virtue, and she was no doubt actuated by the purest motives, yet she might be guilty of some indiscretion; God inspired her with the laudable design of freeing her country, but the means were left to her own choice. The making use of rich attire was not, however, sinful in itself, and we have no right to suspect that Judith gave way either to vanity or to sensuality; provided they observe due modesty, who will blame ladies of quality for rich attire or perfumes? Judith might lawfully desire to marry Holofernes for the good of her nation, as Esther became the wife of Assuerus, though an infidel, and the general seemed even willing to become a convert (chapter 11:21). — George Leo Haydock

5 COMMENTARY: Then she gave her maid a bottle of wine, and a cruse of oil, and filled a bag with parched corn, and lumps of figs, and with fine bread; the bottle was made of leather, used for carrying wine, the corn was eaten either steeped in water or mixed with oil, and "cheese" is so read in Syriac while Greek reads "pure loaves." Why she took her provisions with her, she explains in chapter 12:2. — George Leo Haydock

7 COMMENTARY: And when they saw her, that her countenance was altered, and her apparel was changed, they wondered at her beauty very greatly; the Greek adds, "with her countenance and robes changed." — George Leo Haydock

8 COMMENTARY: The God, the God of our fathers give thee favour, and accomplish thine enterprises to the glory of the children of Israel, and to the exaltation of Jerusalem; the Greek reads, "And they said to her, May God, the God of" our fathers give thee favour, "For the glory of the Israelites, and the exaltation of Jerusalem; and they adored God." — George Leo Haydock

9 COMMENTARY: And she said unto them, "Command the gates of the city to be opened unto me, that I may go forth to accomplish the things whereof ye have spoken with me"; the Greek continues, "And she said to them, Order the gate of the city to be opened for me, and I will go out to accomplish what you were talking about to me. And they commanded the young men to open for her; and they did so." — George Leo Haydock

10 COMMENTARY: And when they had done so, Judith went out, she, and her maid with her, and the men of the city looked after her, until she was gone down the mountain, and had passed the valley; the Greek continues, "But Judith and her maid went out. And the citizens looked at her while she descended the hill, till she had passed the valley, when they lost sight of her; and they passed along straight through the vale." It appears that there was still day-light, or the moon shone brightly. — George Leo Haydock

12 COMMENTARY: And took her, and asked her, "Of what people art thou? and whence comest thou? and whither goest thou?" And she said, "I am a woman of the Hebrews, and am fled from them"; the Greek reads, "Because they are about to be given up a prey to you. Therefore, I come to Holofernes, general in chief of your army, to inform him of the truth." — George Leo Haydock

13 COMMENTARY: And I am coming before Holofernes the chief captain of your army, to declare words of truth, and I will show him a way whereby he shall go and win all the hill country; in this and the following chapter, some things related to have been said by Judith seem hard to reconcile with truth, but all that is related in Scripture of God's servants is not approved by Scripture, and even saints in their enterprises may sometimes slip into venial sins. By means of mental reservation we may exculpate her from lying, but this expedient is bad and childish; she might speak ironically or prophetically, or it might be an untruth to be excused like stratagems in war — how often does an enemy give out that he is going to march to some place, when he intends to go quite another way! She spoke many things certainly true, and others which would probably have taken place if God had not sent relief. — George Leo Haydock

15 COMMENTARY: Thou hast saved thy life, in that thou hast hasted to come down to the presence of our lord: now therefore come to his tent, and some of us shall conduct thee unto him; the Greek adds, "and now proceed to his tent, and some of us shall give thee into his hands." — George Leo Haydock

16 COMMENTARY: And when thou standest before him, be not afraid in thine heart, but show unto him according to thy word, and he will entreat thee well; the Greek continues, "But if thou stand before him, let not thy heart fear, but tell him this, and he will treat thee well." — George Leo Haydock

17 COMMENTARY: Then they chose out of them an hundred men to accompany her and her maid, and they brought her to the tent of Holofernes; the Greek continues, "And they selected 100 men, and surrounded her and her servant-maid, and conducted her to the tent of Holofernes." — George Leo Haydock

18 COMMENTARY: Then was there a concourse throughout all the camp, for her coming was noised among the tents, and they came about her, as she stood without the tent of Holofernes, till they had told him of her; the Greek continues, "And they ran from all parts of the camp, as soon as her coming was made known, and they came round her as she stood without the tent of Holofernes, till they had told him of her. And they were astonished at her beauty, and at the Israelites, and said to each other, Who," and so on. — George Leo Haydock

19 COMMENTARY: And they wondered at her beauty, and admired the children of Israel because of her, and every one said to his neighbour, "Who would despise this people, that have among them such women?"; the Greek reads, "For it is not expedient to leave one man of them alive, lest they should delude all the earth" (the Old Vulgate omits the negation). — George Leo Haydock

20 COMMENTARY: And they that lay near Holofernes went out, and all his servants, and they brought her into the tent; the Greek reads, "And all who slept near Holofernes, and all his servants, came out and introduced her into the tent," which was divided into two apartments. — George Leo Haydock

21 COMMENTARY: Now Holofernes rested upon his bed under a canopy, which was woven with purple, and gold, and emeralds, and precious stones; the Greek reads that Holofernes "was reposing on a bed in the canopy (or inner room)," and the curtain was intended to keep off gnats — a delicacy Mark Antony imitated, as Horace notes. — George Leo Haydock

22 COMMENTARY: So they showed him of her, and he came out before his tent with silver lamps going before him; the Greek reads, "And they told him of her, and he came out to the forepart of the tent, and silver lamps preceded him," either for grandeur, as they did the kings of Persia and the emperors of Rome and of the Turks, or because it was still dark. — George Leo Haydock

23 COMMENTARY: And when Judith was come before him and his servants they all marvelled at the beauty of her countenance, and she fell down upon her face, and did reverence unto him; the Greek reads, "But as soon as Judith came in sight of him and of his ministers, they all were astonished at the beauty of her face; and falling prostrate, she adored him (with civil respect) and his servants lifted her up." St. Fulgentius judges, with great probability, that Judith was now about 40 years old. — George Leo Haydock

No note for verse(s) 1, 6, 11, 14 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
