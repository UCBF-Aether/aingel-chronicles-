Study Notes — Judith

Chapter 8

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Now at that time Judith heard thereof, daughter of Merari, son of Ox, son of Joseph, son of Ozel, son of Elcia, and so on through her genealogy; "Idox" is in Greek "ox," and all versions disagree, as copyists probably took in part of verse 3 too soon, supposing a woman's genealogy was never given. St. Fulgentius differs from all, giving Manasses' ancestors as the same as Judith's. "Simeon, the son of Ruben" is in Greek "the son of Israel," for Simeon the patriarch was not Ruben's son but his brother; more probably the Simeon and Ruben here are descendants of patriarch Simeon, not the patriarchs themselves, as with Elcana's genealogy (1 Samuel 1:1) and Saul's (1 Samuel 9:1). Others think Judith descended from Ruben by her father and Simeon by her mother, or that we should read "brother" for "son of Ruben"; but as Judith calls Simeon her father (chapter 9:2), it is more likely Ruben was placed for Israel, as in Syriac and St. Fulgentius — a correction maintained by Bellarmine, Salien, and Menochius. Thus both Judith and her husband were of the same tribe, she by Salathiel, he by Sarisadai. Innumerable are the mistakes in proper names in Scripture, and Greek has Sarasdai (Complutensian "Saladai," Alexandrian Septuagint "Salasadai") instead of Simeon son of Israel, and Alexandrian "Jeel," probably a contraction for Israel. — George Leo Haydock

2 COMMENTARY: And Manasses was her husband, of her tribe and kindred, who died in the barley harvest; the Greek adds, "of her tribe and family, and he died in the days" of harvest. — George Leo Haydock

3 COMMENTARY: For as he stood overseeing them that bound sheaves in the field, the heat came upon his head, and he fell on his bed, and died in Bethulia; the Greek reads, "fell upon his couch" (Grabe adds, "and died in Betuloua, his own city"), "and they buried him with his fathers, in the field between Dothaim and Balamo" — places added without reason, as they were too remote from Bethulia. — George Leo Haydock

4 COMMENTARY: So Judith was a widow in her house three years and four months; on the duration, the old Vulgate, Greek, and Syriac have four months, and Judith led a most religious life, in prayer, hair-cloth, and fasting, all the year except on the festivals. — George Leo Haydock

5 COMMENTARY: And she made her a tent upon the top of her house, and put on sackcloth upon her loins and wore her widow's apparel; the Greek reads "a tent on the roof," that she might look towards Jerusalem and pray with less distraction, as Absalom lay in such a situation (2 Samuel 16:22), and "she put sackcloth" upon her loins — a rough sort of garment. — George Leo Haydock

6 COMMENTARY: And she fasted all the days of her widowhood, save the eves of the sabbaths, and the sabbaths, and the eves of the new moons, and the new moons, and the feasts; the Greek reads, "except the day before the sabbath, and the sabbaths, and the new moons, and feasts, and days of rejoicing of the house of Israel," and she had on the garments of her widowhood. Syriac and old Vulgate omit "the day before the sabbath," which perhaps was not in the original, the Greek translator having inserted it conformably to the practice of his times: it was prescribed to abstain from fasting then, that the festival's joy might not be impaired and because it would be necessary to taste what was made ready for the sabbath, or because the festival began on Friday between the two vespers. New moons were days of rejoicing (1 Samuel 20:5), and the Jews kept two days together for fear of missing the day the moon really appeared. — George Leo Haydock

7 COMMENTARY: She was also of a goodly countenance and very beautiful to behold, and her husband Manasses had left her gold, silver, menservants, maidservants, cattle, and possessions; the Greek, Syriac, and others add, "and fields, and she lived on them." — George Leo Haydock

8 COMMENTARY: And there was none that gave her an ill word, for she feared God greatly; "the reputation of chastity in women is tender, and, like a beautiful flower, presently withers at the least blast, and perishes; particularly when they are at an age prone to vice, and destitute of the authority of a husband, whose shadow is the safeguard to a wife" (St. Jerome). Scripture could not give Judith a greater character, and how few can obtain it at present, though their virtue may be most pure, detraction is so keen! — George Leo Haydock

9 COMMENTARY: Now when she heard the evil words of the people against the governor, that they fainted for lack of water, for Judith had heard all the words that Ozias had spoken; the Greek and Syriac read, "and she heard of the evil discourses of the populace against the magistrate, as they lost courage on account of the scarcity of water; and Judith heard of all the words of Ozias to them, how he had sworn to them that he would deliver up the city to the Assyrians after five days." — George Leo Haydock

10 COMMENTARY: Then she sent her waitingwoman, that had the government of all things that she had, to call Ozias and Chabris and Charmis, the ancients of the city; the Greek and Syriac continue that, "sending her maid, whom she had placed at the head of all her possessions, she called Ozias" and the other ancients. — George Leo Haydock

13 COMMENTARY: And now try the Lord Almighty, but ye shall never know any thing — she blames the magistrates for taking a rash oath, pretending to fathom the designs of God and to fix a time for him, and not attempting any resistance, though the welfare of the whole nation and the sacred things depended on their exertions. They had been assured God had heard their prayer (chapter 4:9) and had often witnessed miraculous interference of Providence; in other circumstances the magistrates would not have been to blame, though Judith might think them so, and in effect they ought rather to have died than thus to have exposed all to certain ruin. — George Leo Haydock

16 COMMENTARY: Do not bind the counsels of the Lord our God, for God is not as man, that he may be threatened, neither is he as the son of man, that he should be wavering; the Greek reads, "But do not you govern the designs of the Lord, our God, for God is not to be menaced like a man, nor forced to give an account like the son of man" (Numbers 23:19). — George Leo Haydock

18 COMMENTARY: For there arose none in our age, neither is there any now in these days, neither tribe, nor family, nor people, nor city among us, which worship gods made with hands; the Greek reads, "As there has not arisen in our families generations, nor is there at this day either tribe, or family, or populace, or city among us, adoring gods made with hands, as it happened in days past," in the former part of Manasses' reign, when all had begun to open their eyes at the sight of the dreadful catastrophe. — George Leo Haydock

20 COMMENTARY: But we know none other god, therefore we trust that he will not despise us, nor any of our nation; the Greek reads, "Wherefore we hope that he will not despise us, nor those of our race." — George Leo Haydock

21 COMMENTARY: For if we be taken so, all Judea shall lie waste, and our sanctuary shall be spoiled, and he will require the profanation thereof at our mouth; the Greek continues, "nor, if we be taken, will Judea be so styled, our sacred things will be plundered. He will require his profanation at our mouth" — she shows the dread consequences which will ensue from the rash oath and from such dastardly conduct under trial. — George Leo Haydock

24 COMMENTARY: Now therefore, O brethren, let us show an example to our brethren, because their hearts depend upon us, and the sanctuary, and the house, and the altar are supported by us; the Greek adds, "Let us show our brethren that their soul depends on us, and the holy things, and the house (temple) and the altar, are supported by us" — these were not therefore demolished (chapter 5:22). "Yea, let us give thanks, above all, to the Lord our God, who tries us like our fathers. Remember," and so on. — George Leo Haydock

26 COMMENTARY: Remember what things he did to Abraham, and how he tried Isaac, and what happened to Jacob in Mesopotamia of Syria, when he kept the sheep of Laban his brother; the Greek adds, "in Mesopotamia....because he does not take vengeance on us, as he made them pass through fire to examine their heart." — George Leo Haydock

27 COMMENTARY: For he hath not tried us in the fire, as he did them, for the examination of their hearts, neither hath he taken vengeance on us; but the Lord doth scourge those that come near unto him, for an admonition — the Greek addition continues that the Lord, for an admonition, chastises those who approach to him. — George Leo Haydock

29 COMMENTARY: For this is not the first day wherein thy wisdom is manifested, but from the beginning of thy days all the people have known thy understanding, because thy heart is good; the Greek adds, "for it is not to-day only that thy wisdom has appeared, but from thy earliest days all the people has known thy prudence, as the formation (Syriac, the thought) of thy heart is good." Hence it appears Judith was not very young, and as many desired to marry her (last chapter, Greek), we may conclude she was of middle age, about thirty-five. — George Leo Haydock

30 COMMENTARY: But the people were very thirsty, and compelled us to do unto them as we have spoken, and to bring an oath upon ourselves, which we will not break; the Greek continues, "But the people was much oppressed with thirst, and they forced us to....take an oath, which we shall not break. Now," and so on. — George Leo Haydock

31 COMMENTARY: Therefore now pray thou for us, because thou art a godly woman, and the Lord will send us rain to fill our cisterns, and we shall faint no more; the Greek adds, "and the Lord will send rain to fill our reservoirs, and we shall faint no longer." — George Leo Haydock

32 COMMENTARY: Then said Judith unto them, "Hear me, and I will do a thing, which shall go throughout all generations to the children of our nation"; the Greek continues, "And Judith replied: Hear me, and I will perform an action which shall come to the ears of all future generations of our race. You," and so on. She did not tell what she was going to do. — George Leo Haydock

33 COMMENTARY: Ye shall stand this night in the gate, and I will go forth with my waitingwoman; "servant" is in Greek abra, commonly denoting a maid of honour or companion, though sometimes applied to people of loose morals, and this servant probably had the administration of her mistress's affairs (verse 9). The Greek reads, "as you....the Lord will by my hand look," and so on. — George Leo Haydock

34 COMMENTARY: But enquire not ye of mine act, for I will not declare it unto you, till the things be finished that I do; the Greek reads, "for I shall not tell you, till what I am about be accomplished. And Ozias, with the princes, said to her." — George Leo Haydock

35 COMMENTARY: Then said Ozias and the princes unto her, "Go in peace, and the Lord God be before thee, to take vengeance on our enemies" — Ozias with the princes so answered her. — George Leo Haydock

36 COMMENTARY: So they returned from the tent, and went to their wards; the Greek adds, "from the tent to their respective stations." — George Leo Haydock

No note for verse(s) 11, 12, 14, 15, 17, 19, 22, 23, 25, 28 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
