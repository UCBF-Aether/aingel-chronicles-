Study Notes — Wisdom of Solomon

Chapter 14

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Again, one preparing to sail, about to pass through raging waves, calls upon a piece of wood more rotten than the vessel carrying him. Haydock's image is the sailor praying to a wooden idol rottener than his ship. — George Leo Haydock

2 COMMENTARY: Desire of gain devised that, and the workman built it by his skill. Haydock notes gain devised the idol, and skill built it; worship follows commerce. — George Leo Haydock

3 COMMENTARY: But your providence, O Father, governs it, for you made a way in the sea, and a safe path in the waves. Haydock's contrast is providence: the Father, not the idol, makes the sea path safe. — George Leo Haydock

4 COMMENTARY: Showing that you can save from all danger, yea, though a man went to sea without art. Haydock observes that God can save even the unskilled sailor. — George Leo Haydock

5 COMMENTARY: Nevertheless you would not that the works of your wisdom should be idle, and therefore men commit their lives to a small piece of wood, and passing the rough sea in a weak vessel are saved. Haydock notes God wills that wisdom's works be used: men trust a small wood vessel and are saved. — George Leo Haydock

6 COMMENTARY: For in old time, when proud giants perished, the hope of the world, governed by your hand, escaped in a weak vessel, and left to all ages a seed of generation. Haydock reads the ark: hope escaped in a weak vessel and left a seed. — George Leo Haydock

7 COMMENTARY: Blessed is the wood by which righteousness comes. Haydock's blessing is on the wood that bears righteousness, the ark and, by figure, the cross. — George Leo Haydock

8 COMMENTARY: But the idol made with hands is cursed, as well it as he who made it: he, because he made it, and it, because, being corruptible, it was called god. Haydock notes the double curse: maker and made, the corruptible called god. — George Leo Haydock

9 COMMENTARY: The ungodly and his ungodliness are both alike hateful to God. Haydock stresses both are hateful: the man and his impiety. — George Leo Haydock

10 COMMENTARY: What is made shall be punished together with him who made it. Haydock observes punishment falls on both maker and made. — George Leo Haydock

11 COMMENTARY: Therefore there shall be a visitation also upon the heathen idols, because in God's creation they became an abomination, and stumbling blocks to men's souls, and a snare to the feet of the unwise. Haydock notes idols are visited because they became stumbling blocks and snares. — George Leo Haydock

12 COMMENTARY: The devising of idols was the beginning of spiritual fornication, and the invention of them the corruption of life. Haydock's maxim is that idol-devising begins spiritual fornication. — George Leo Haydock

13 COMMENTARY: They were neither from the beginning, nor shall they be for ever. Haydock observes idols have no eternity: they began, and they will end. — George Leo Haydock

14 COMMENTARY: By men's vanity they entered into the world, and therefore shall they come shortly to an end. Haydock notes vanity brought them in, and a short end awaits them. — George Leo Haydock

15 COMMENTARY: A father afflicted with untimely mourning, having made an image of his child soon taken away, now honoured him as a god, and delivered to those under him ceremonies and sacrifices. Haydock traces idolatry's origin in grief: a dead child honoured as god, with rites handed down. — George Leo Haydock

16 COMMENTARY: Thus in process of time an ungodly custom grown strong was kept as a law, and graven images were worshipped by kings' commandments. Haydock observes custom hardened into law, and kings commanded image worship. — George Leo Haydock

17 COMMENTARY: Whom men could not honour in presence, because they dwelt far off, they took the counterfeit of his visage from far, and made an express image of a king whom they honoured, that by this flattery they might worship as present him who was absent. Haydock notes the absent king's image: flattery makes a present god of an absent ruler. — George Leo Haydock

18 COMMENTARY: Also the singular diligence of the artificer helped to set forward the ignorant to more superstition. Haydock observes the artificer's skill pushes the ignorant further into superstition. — George Leo Haydock

19 COMMENTARY: He, willing to please the ruler, forced all his skill to make the resemblance of the best fashion. Haydock notes the craftsman flatters the ruler by making the image as fair as skill allows. — George Leo Haydock

20 COMMENTARY: And so the multitude, allured by the grace of the work, took him now for a god who a little before was honoured as a man. Haydock's point is that beauty of workmanship turns a man into a god in the multitude's eyes. — George Leo Haydock

21 COMMENTARY: This was a trap to life, that men, under calamity or tyranny, decreed to give the incommunicable name to stones and stocks. Haydock calls this the trap: under calamity or tyranny, God's incommunicable name is given to stone and stock. — George Leo Haydock

22 COMMENTARY: Moreover, this was not enough for them, that they erred in the knowledge of God, but whereas they lived in great war of ignorance, they call such evils peace. Haydock observes error compounds: ignorance at war with itself is called peace. — George Leo Haydock

23 COMMENTARY: They slew their children in sacrifices, or used secret ceremonies, or made revellings of strange rites. Haydock lists the rites: child sacrifice, secret ceremonies, strange revellings. — George Leo Haydock

24 COMMENTARY: They kept neither lives nor marriages undefiled, but either slew one another by treachery, or grieved one another by adultery. Haydock notes the social fruit: life and marriage both defiled. — George Leo Haydock

25 COMMENTARY: So that there reigned in all men without exception blood, manslaughter, theft, dissimulation, corruption, unfaithfulness, tumults, perjury. Haydock's catalogue is the reign of idolatry's fruits without exception. — George Leo Haydock

26 COMMENTARY: Disquieting of good men, forgetfulness of good turns, defiling of souls, changing of kind, disorder in marriages, adultery, and shameless uncleanness. Haydock continues the catalogue: good men disquieted, souls defiled, kinds changed. — George Leo Haydock

27 COMMENTARY: The worshipping of idols not to be named is the beginning, the cause, and the end of all evil. Haydock's summary is total: idolatry is beginning, cause, and end of all evil. — George Leo Haydock

28 COMMENTARY: Either they are mad when they are merry, or prophesy lies, or live unjustly, or readily forswear themselves. Haydock notes the worshippers' conduct: madness in mirth, lying prophecy, unjust life, ready perjury. — George Leo Haydock

29 COMMENTARY: For insomuch as their trust is in idols, which have no life, though they swear falsely, they look not to be hurt. Haydock observes false swearing seems safe because the idols sworn by have no life. — George Leo Haydock

30 COMMENTARY: Howbeit for both causes shall they be justly punished: both because they thought not well of God, giving heed to idols, and because they swore unjustly in deceit, despising holiness. Haydock gives the twofold cause of punishment: wrong thoughts of God, and deceitful swearing. — George Leo Haydock

31 COMMENTARY: It is not the power of the idols they swore by, but the just vengeance of sinners, which always punishes the offence of the ungodly. Haydock closes: vengeance comes not from idols' power but from God's justice on sinners. — George Leo Haydock
