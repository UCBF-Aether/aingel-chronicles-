Study Notes — Wisdom of Solomon

Chapter 15

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: But you, O God, are gracious and true, longsuffering, and in mercy ordering all things. Haydock's contrast opens the chapter: against idolatry's evils stands a gracious, true, longsuffering God. — George Leo Haydock

2 COMMENTARY: For if we sin, we are yours, knowing your power; but we will not sin, knowing that we are counted yours. Haydock notes the double knowledge: knowing God's power and knowing we are his. — George Leo Haydock

3 COMMENTARY: To know you is perfect righteousness, and to know your power is the root of immortality. Haydock's maxim is that knowledge of God is perfect righteousness and the root of immortality. — George Leo Haydock

4 COMMENTARY: Neither has the malicious invention of men deceived us, nor an image spotted with divers colours, the painter's fruitless labour. Haydock observes Israel was not deceived by malicious invention or painted image. — George Leo Haydock

5 COMMENTARY: The sight of it entices fools to lust after it, and they desire the form of a dead image without breath. Haydock notes the sight entices fools to lust after a breathless form. — George Leo Haydock

6 COMMENTARY: Both they who make them, they who desire them, and they who worship them, are lovers of evil things, and are worthy to have such things to trust upon. Haydock's judgment covers maker, desirer, and worshipper alike. — George Leo Haydock

7 COMMENTARY: The potter, tempering soft earth, fashions every vessel with much labour for our service, and of the same clay fashions vessels that serve for clean uses and likewise for contrary uses, but what is the use of either sort, the potter himself is judge. Haydock notes the potter judges the vessels' uses; the same clay serves clean and contrary ends. — George Leo Haydock

8 COMMENTARY: With evil labour he fashions a vain god of the same clay, he who a little before was made of earth himself, and within a little while returns to the earth from whence he was taken, when his life, lent him, shall be demanded. Haydock's irony is that the earth-made potter makes an earth god, and will himself return to earth. — George Leo Haydock

9 COMMENTARY: His care is not that he shall have much labour, nor that his life is short, but he strives to outdo goldsmiths and silversmiths, and endeavours to counterfeit things of brass, counting it glory to make counterfeit things. Haydock observes the potter's ambition: to outdo goldsmiths in counterfeits, counting counterfeit-making glory. — George Leo Haydock

10 COMMENTARY: His heart is ashes, his hope more vile than earth, and his life less value than clay. Haydock's verdict is that heart, hope, and life are all baser than the clay he works. — George Leo Haydock

11 COMMENTARY: For he knew not his Maker, and him who inspired into him an active soul, and breathed in a living spirit. Haydock notes the root: not knowing the Maker who breathed a living spirit. — George Leo Haydock

12 COMMENTARY: But they counted our life a pastime, and our time here a market for gain, for, they say, we must be getting every way, though it be by evil means. Haydock observes the idol-makers count life a pastime and a market, getting by evil means. — George Leo Haydock

13 COMMENTARY: This man, who makes brittle vessels and graven images of the same clay, knows himself to offend above all others. Haydock notes the maker knows, above all, that he offends. — George Leo Haydock

14 COMMENTARY: Most foolish are all the enemies of your people, and the heathen who deem the idols of the nations to be gods. Haydock's judgment is that Israel's enemies are most foolish for deeming idols gods. — George Leo Haydock

15 COMMENTARY: For they did not know you, the true God, nor acknowledge you. Haydock's point is that folly is ignorance of the true God. — George Leo Haydock

16 COMMENTARY: They are more miserable than a child, for their gods are more foolish than a child, and their hopes are in dead things. Haydock observes their gods are more foolish than a child, and their hope in dead things. — George Leo Haydock

17 COMMENTARY: They are most miserable of all, for they are most foolish of all, and their gods are most miserable of all. Haydock's superlative sums it: most miserable, most foolish, most miserable gods. — George Leo Haydock

18 COMMENTARY: For they worshipped even creeping things, which are most hateful, and their gods are most miserable. Haydock notes the lowest worship: creeping things, most hateful. — George Leo Haydock

19 COMMENTARY: Neither are they beautiful, so much as to be desired in respect of beasts, but they went without the praise of God and his blessing. Haydock closes: even beasts have more beauty than these gods, which have neither beauty nor God's praise and blessing. — George Leo Haydock
