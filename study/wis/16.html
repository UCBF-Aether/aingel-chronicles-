Study Notes — Wisdom of Solomon

Chapter 16

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Therefore by the like were they punished worthily, and by the multitude of beasts tormented. Haydock's rule applies again: punishment by the like, here a multitude of beasts. — George Leo Haydock

2 COMMENTARY: Instead of this punishment, dealing graciously with your own people, you prepared for them meat of a strange taste, even quails to stir up their appetite. Haydock contrasts gracious dealing with punishment: strange-tasting quails instead of beasts. — George Leo Haydock

3 COMMENTARY: To the end that they, desiring food, might for the ugly sight of the beasts sent among them loathe even what they must needs desire, but these, suffering penury for a short space, might be made partakers of a strange taste. Haydock notes the purpose: Egypt loathes food at the sight of beasts, Israel tastes strangeness after short penury. — George Leo Haydock

4 COMMENTARY: It was necessary that upon them should come inexorable want, but upon these only a showing of how their enemies were tormented. Haydock observes the asymmetry: inexorable want for Egypt, only a showing of torment for Israel. — George Leo Haydock

5 COMMENTARY: When the fierce poison of beasts came upon your people, and they were stung by crooked serpents, your wrath endured not for ever. Haydock notes the serpents' sting was real, but wrath did not endure for ever. — George Leo Haydock

6 COMMENTARY: They were troubled for a small season, that they might be admonished, having a sign of salvation, to put them in remembrance of your law's commandment. Haydock reads the brazen serpent as a sign of salvation and a reminder of the commandment. — George Leo Haydock

7 COMMENTARY: He who turned toward it was not saved by the thing seen, but by you, the Saviour of all. Haydock stresses the sign saves nothing by itself; the Saviour of all saves. — George Leo Haydock

8 COMMENTARY: In this you showed your enemies that you deliver from all evil. Haydock notes the deliverance itself preached to enemies. — George Leo Haydock

9 COMMENTARY: They were killed with the stings of grasshoppers and flies, and found no remedy for their life, for they were worthy to be punished by such. Haydock's correspondence is exact: Egypt, worthy of punishment by such small things, is killed by grasshoppers and flies. — George Leo Haydock

10 COMMENTARY: But your sons were not overcome by the very fangs of venomous dragons, for your mercy was ever by them and healed them. Haydock contrasts mercy by Israel's side with the dragons' fangs. — George Leo Haydock

11 COMMENTARY: They were pricked, that they should remember your words, and were quickly saved, lest, falling into deep forgetfulness, they should be drawn from your benefit. Haydock notes the pricking was mnemonic: lest forgetfulness draw them from benefit. — George Leo Haydock

12 COMMENTARY: Neither herb nor mollifying plaster restored them to health, but your word, O Lord, which heals all things. Haydock stresses the healer is God's word, not herb or plaster. — George Leo Haydock

13 COMMENTARY: You have power of life and death, and lead to the gates of hell and bring up again. Haydock's point is sovereignty over life, death, and the gates below. — George Leo Haydock

14 COMMENTARY: A man indeed kills another by his malice, and the spirit, when gone forth, returns not, neither can the soul received up come again. Haydock observes man's killing is irreversible, unlike God's power to bring up again. — George Leo Haydock

15 COMMENTARY: But your hand no man can escape. Haydock's short maxim is that God's hand cannot be escaped. — George Leo Haydock

16 COMMENTARY: The ungodly, denying to know you, were scourged by the strength of your arm, with strange rains, hails, and showers, which they could not avoid, and were consumed by fire. Haydock lists the scourges: strange rains, hails, showers, consuming fire. — George Leo Haydock

17 COMMENTARY: Most strange was it that fire had more force in water, which quenches all things, for the world fights for the righteous. Haydock notes the wonder: fire stronger in water, because the world fights for the righteous. — George Leo Haydock

18 COMMENTARY: Sometimes the flame was mitigated, that it might not burn up the beasts sent against the ungodly, but themselves might see and perceive they were persecuted with God's judgment. Haydock observes the flame was tempered so beasts survived to show judgment. — George Leo Haydock

19 COMMENTARY: At another time it burns even in the midst of water, above the power of fire, that it might destroy the fruits of an unjust land. Haydock notes fire burning in water above fire's power, to destroy an unjust land's fruits. — George Leo Haydock

20 COMMENTARY: Instead thereof you fed your people with angels' food, and sent them from heaven bread prepared without labour, able to content every man's delight and agreeing to every taste. Haydock contrasts manna, angels' food, with the destroyed fruits. — George Leo Haydock

21 COMMENTARY: Your sustenance declared your sweetness to your children, and, serving to the appetite of the eater, tempered itself to every man's liking. Haydock notes manna's sweetness tempered itself to each eater's liking. — George Leo Haydock

22 COMMENTARY: But snow and ice endured the fire and melted not, that they might know fire burning in hail and sparkling in rain did destroy their enemies' fruits. Haydock observes snow and ice enduring fire taught Egypt that hail-fire destroyed their fruits. — George Leo Haydock

23 COMMENTARY: The fire forgot its own strength, that the righteous might be nourished. Haydock notes fire itself changes nature at God's command, abating for the righteous. — George Leo Haydock

24 COMMENTARY: The creature that serves you, the Maker, increases strength against the unrighteous for their punishment, and abates strength for the benefit of those who put trust in you. Haydock's rule is that the creature strengthens or abates according to whom it serves. — George Leo Haydock

25 COMMENTARY: The creature was altered into all fashions and was obedient to God's grace, which nourishes all things, according to the need of those who ask. Haydock's point is that creation obeys grace, changing form to meet need. — George Leo Haydock

26 COMMENTARY: Your children, O Lord, whom you love, might know that it is not the growing of fruits that nourishes man, but your word preserves those who trust in you. Haydock's lesson is that nourishment comes from God's word, not from fruit-growing. — George Leo Haydock

27 COMMENTARY: That which was not destroyed by the fire, being warmed with a little sunbeam, soon melted away. Haydock notes the manna melted in a sunbeam, a sign that thanksgiving must come early. — George Leo Haydock

28 COMMENTARY: That it might be known that we must prevent the sun to give God thanks, and at the day-spring pray to him. Haydock's lesson is prayer before sunrise, taught by the melting manna. — George Leo Haydock

29 COMMENTARY: The hope of the unthankful shall melt away as the winter's hoar frost, and shall run away as unprofitable water. Haydock's close is that unthankful hope melts like frost and runs like unprofitable water. — George Leo Haydock
