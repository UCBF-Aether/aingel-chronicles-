Study Notes — Wisdom of Solomon

Chapter 17

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Great are your judgments, and cannot be expressed; therefore unnurtured souls have erred. Haydock's opening is that judgments too great for expression mislead the unnurtured. — George Leo Haydock

2 COMMENTARY: When unrighteous men thought to oppress the holy nation, they, shut up in their houses, prisoners of darkness and fettered with bonds of a long night, lay exiled from the eternal providence. Haydock pictures Egypt as prisoners of darkness, exiled from providence while thinking to oppress. — George Leo Haydock

3 COMMENTARY: While they supposed to lie hid in their secret sins, they were scattered under a dark veil of forgetfulness, being horribly astonished and troubled with strange apparitions. Haydock notes secret sins do not hide; they scatter under forgetfulness and apparitions. — George Leo Haydock

4 COMMENTARY: The cave that held them did not protect them from fear, for sounds on every side troubled them, and sad visions appeared with heavy countenances. Haydock observes that no hiding place protects from fear when darkness itself is the prison. — George Leo Haydock

5 COMMENTARY: No power of fire could give them light, nor could bright flames of stars endure to lighten that horrible night. Haydock notes fire and stars both fail to lighten this night. — George Leo Haydock

6 COMMENTARY: Only there appeared to them a fire kindled of itself, very dreadful, for being much terrified, they thought the things seen to be worse than the sight was. Haydock notes the self-kindled fire made terror worse than sight. — George Leo Haydock

7 COMMENTARY: As for the illusions of art magic, they were put down, and their vaunting in wisdom was reproved with disgrace. Haydock observes magic's illusions collapse and vaunted wisdom is disgraced. — George Leo Haydock

8 COMMENTARY: They who promised to drive away terrors and troubles from a sick soul were sick themselves of fear, worthy to be laughed at. Haydock's irony is that the promised drivers of fear are themselves sick with fear. — George Leo Haydock

9 COMMENTARY: Though no terrible thing frightened them, yet, scared by beasts passing by and hissing of serpents, they were troubled. Haydock notes passing beasts and hissing serpents suffice to terrify in darkness. — George Leo Haydock

10 COMMENTARY: They died for fear, denying that they saw the air, which could of no side be avoided. Haydock observes they died of fear while denying the very air around them. — George Leo Haydock

11 COMMENTARY: Wickedness, condemned by her own witness, is very timorous, and, pressed with conscience, always forecasts grievous things. Haydock's maxim is that wickedness makes fear, and conscience condemns. — George Leo Haydock

12 COMMENTARY: Fear is nothing else but a betraying of the succours which reason offers. Haydock defines fear as reason's succours betrayed. — George Leo Haydock

13 COMMENTARY: Expectation from within, being less, counts the ignorance more than the cause which brings the torment. Haydock notes inward expectation magnifies ignorance above the torment's cause. — George Leo Haydock

14 COMMENTARY: They slept the same sleep that night, which was indeed intolerable, and came upon them out of the depths of inevitable hell. Haydock pictures the intolerable night coming from the depths below. — George Leo Haydock

15 COMMENTARY: They were partly vexed with monstrous apparitions, and partly fainted, their heart failing them, for a sudden fear, not looked for, came upon them. Haydock notes apparitions, fainting hearts, and sudden fear. — George Leo Haydock

16 COMMENTARY: Whoever there fell down was straitly kept, shut up in a prison without iron bars. Haydock notes the prison needs no bars; darkness itself holds them. — George Leo Haydock

17 COMMENTARY: Whether he were husbandman, or shepherd, or a labourer in the field, he was overtaken, and endured that necessity which could not be avoided, for all were bound with one chain of darkness. Haydock stresses all are bound by one chain, whatever their trade. — George Leo Haydock

18 COMMENTARY: Whether it were a whistling wind, or a melodious noise of birds among spreading branches, or a pleasing fall of waters, all made them swoon for fear. Haydock's catalogue begins with ordinary sounds turned to terrors. — George Leo Haydock

19 COMMENTARY: Or a terrible sound of stones cast down, or a running that could not be seen of skipping beasts, or a roaring of wild beasts, or a rebounding echo from hollow mountains, all made them swoon for fear. Haydock continues the catalogue: stones, unseen beasts, roaring, and echo. — George Leo Haydock

20 COMMENTARY: The whole world shined with clear light, and none were hindered in their labour. Haydock's contrast is stark: the world works in light, while Egypt alone lies in darkness. — George Leo Haydock

21 COMMENTARY: Over them only was spread a heavy night, an image of that darkness which should afterward receive them, but yet they were unto themselves more grievous than the darkness. Haydock notes the night's grievousness is that it images the final darkness, and the sufferers are worse to themselves than darkness itself. — George Leo Haydock
