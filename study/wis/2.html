Study Notes — Wisdom of Solomon

Chapter 2

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The ungodly reason with themselves, but not aright: life is short and tedious, death has no remedy, and none was known to return from the grave. Haydock identifies this as the epicureans' reasoning, which rejects resurrection and future reward. — George Leo Haydock

2 COMMENTARY: We are born at all adventure, and shall be hereafter as though we had never been; breath is smoke, and a spark moves the heart. Haydock notes the epicurean fancy that man arose by chance, like a spark, and vanishes like smoke. — George Leo Haydock

3 COMMENTARY: When the spark is extinguished, the body turns to ashes and the spirit vanishes as soft air. Haydock's reading is that this materialist account leaves the soul no more endurance than air. — George Leo Haydock

4 COMMENTARY: Our name shall be forgotten in time, no man remembers our works, and life passes like a cloud's trace, scattered as mist before the sun. Haydock notes the same reasoning concludes that nothing of man survives to be remembered. — George Leo Haydock

5 COMMENTARY: Our days are the passing of a shadow, with no return after the end, sealed and none returning. Haydock compares the sealed state to one in a tomb, and notes the epicureans were convinced of nature's fragility yet drew the false inference that present enjoyment is all. — George Leo Haydock

6 COMMENTARY: Come therefore, let us enjoy the good things that are, and use the creature speedily as in youth. Haydock observes that denial of future reward produces the epicure's life of indulgence. — George Leo Haydock

7 COMMENTARY: Let us fill ourselves with costly wine and ointments, and let no flower of spring pass by us. Haydock notes youth is life's spring, and the voluptuous wrongly argue from transience to use rather than to contempt of such things. — George Leo Haydock

8 COMMENTARY: Let us crown ourselves with rosebuds before they wither. Haydock reads this as the same plea: seize pleasure before it fades, because, on their view, nothing follows. — George Leo Haydock

9 COMMENTARY: Let none of us go without his part of voluptuousness, leaving tokens of joy everywhere, for this is our portion and lot. Haydock's point is that the epicurean lot is deliberately chosen pleasure, shared as a programme. — George Leo Haydock

10 COMMENTARY: Let us oppress the poor righteous man, spare not the widow, nor reverence the ancient's grey hairs. Haydock notes that maxims of oppression follow from the denial of judgment: the weak have no protector on this view. — George Leo Haydock

11 COMMENTARY: Let our strength be the law of justice, for what is feeble is found worth nothing. Haydock calls these strange maxims, which few dare proclaim though many act by them. — George Leo Haydock

12 COMMENTARY: Let us lie in wait for the righteous, because he is unprofitable to us, contrary to our doings, and upbraids us with offences against the law. Haydock notes infidels are not content with riot; they persecute the just, and this passage strikingly foreshadows the treatment of Christ. — George Leo Haydock

13 COMMENTARY: He professes to have God's knowledge and calls himself God's child, and his life reproves our thoughts. Haydock observes that prophets, and Christ above all, irritated sinners precisely by speaking in God's name. — George Leo Haydock

14 COMMENTARY: He is grievous to behold, for his life is unlike others' and his ways are different. Haydock's reading is that mere difference of life is enough to offend those wedded to pleasure. — George Leo Haydock

15 COMMENTARY: We are esteemed by him as counterfeits, he abstains from our ways as from filthiness, and pronounces the righteous end blessed, boasting God as father. Haydock notes the just man's judgment on their ways, and his hope, are what they resent. — George Leo Haydock

16 COMMENTARY: Let us examine him with despitefulness and torture, that we may know his meekness and prove his patience. Haydock's point is that the persecutors propose a test of the very virtues they hate. — George Leo Haydock

17 COMMENTARY: Let us condemn him to a shameful death, for by his own sayings he shall be respected. Haydock reads this as the persecutors' appeal to the just man's own claim of divine protection. — George Leo Haydock

18 COMMENTARY: Such things they imagined, and were deceived, for their own wickedness blinded them. Haydock notes the blindness is self-inflicted: wickedness darkens the mind that reasons so. — George Leo Haydock

19 COMMENTARY: They did not know God's mysteries, nor hope for the reward of holiness, nor discern the honour of blameless souls. Haydock's point is that ignorance of the mysteries, not lack of evidence, is their ruin. — George Leo Haydock

20 COMMENTARY: They shall be examined by their own words, for the just man's claim shall be put to proof. Haydock notes the persecutors will be judged by the very words and test they proposed. — George Leo Haydock

21 COMMENTARY: These things they thought and erred, for their wickedness blinded them, and they knew not God's secrets. Haydock stresses the repeated moral: error follows wickedness, not the other way round. — George Leo Haydock

22 COMMENTARY: They knew not God's mysteries, nor hoped for holiness's reward, nor discerned the prize of blameless souls. Haydock observes that disbelief of mysteries leads both to dissolute life and to persecution of the just. — George Leo Haydock

23 COMMENTARY: God created man to be immortal, and made him in the image of his own eternity. Haydock's contrast is sharp: against the epicurean account of chance, man is made for immortality. — George Leo Haydock

24 COMMENTARY: Through the devil's envy death entered the world, and those of his side experience it. Haydock traces the envy to the fallen angel who grudged man's destined honour and strove to be like the Most High. — George Leo Haydock
