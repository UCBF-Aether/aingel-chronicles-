Study Notes — Wisdom of Solomon

Chapter 13

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Surely vain are all men by nature who are ignorant of God, and could not out of the good things seen know him who is, nor by considering the works acknowledge the workmaster. Haydock's opening is that nature's good things should lead to the Maker, and failure to follow them is vanity. — George Leo Haydock

2 COMMENTARY: But they deemed fire, or wind, or swift air, or the circle of stars, or violent water, or lights of heaven, to be the gods governing the world. Haydock lists the elements mistaken for gods. — George Leo Haydock

3 COMMENTARY: With whose beauty, if being delighted they took them for gods, let them know how much better the Lord of them is, for the first author of beauty created them. Haydock's argument is from beauty: the author of beauty exceeds the beautiful thing. — George Leo Haydock

4 COMMENTARY: If they were astonished at their power and virtue, let them understand by them how much mightier he is who made them. Haydock argues from power likewise: the Maker's power exceeds the made thing's. — George Leo Haydock

5 COMMENTARY: For by the greatness and beauty of creatures, proportionably, the Maker of them is seen. Haydock states the proportion: greatness and beauty in creatures reveal the Maker proportionably. — George Leo Haydock

6 COMMENTARY: But yet for this they are the less to be blamed, for they perhaps err seeking God and desiring to find him. Haydock notes a mitigation: those who err while seeking God are less blameworthy. — George Leo Haydock

7 COMMENTARY: For being conversant in his works, they search him diligently, and believe their sight, because the things seen are beautiful. Haydock observes they search diligently among works, and are misled by beauty itself. — George Leo Haydock

8 COMMENTARY: Howbeit neither are they to be pardoned. Haydock's qualification is sharp: mitigation is not pardon. — George Leo Haydock

9 COMMENTARY: For if they were able to know so much that they could aim at the world, how did they not sooner find out the Lord thereof? Haydock's question is that knowledge enough to aim at the world should have found its Lord sooner. — George Leo Haydock

10 COMMENTARY: But miserable are they, and in dead things is their hope, who called them gods which are the works of men's hands, gold and silver, to show art in, and resemblances of beasts, or a stone good for nothing, the work of an ancient hand. Haydock contrasts the seekers with idol-makers, whose hope is in dead things made by hands. — George Leo Haydock

11 COMMENTARY: Now a carpenter that fells timber, saws out a tree, and with skill planes it, uses all its offal for preparation of food, and then takes a crooked piece of wood good for nothing, carves it diligently, and shapes it by skill. Haydock begins the carpenter's story: the same tree feeds him and furnishes his god. — George Leo Haydock

12 COMMENTARY: The carpenter's offal he uses for food, and a useless crooked piece he carves into an image. Haydock notes the division: one part for use, one part for worship, from the same wood. — George Leo Haydock

13 COMMENTARY: He takes pains with carving, shapes it with skill, and makes it like the image of a man, or a vile beast. Haydock observes the skill spent on making a man-like or beast-like image. — George Leo Haydock

14 COMMENTARY: He colours it with vermilion and paints its face, covering every spot in it. Haydock notes the painting: vermilion and cosmetics hide the wood's spots. — George Leo Haydock

15 COMMENTARY: He makes a convenient room for it, sets it in a wall, and fastens it with iron, that it cannot fall. Haydock pictures the god fastened with iron, lest it fall. — George Leo Haydock

16 COMMENTARY: He provides for it lest it fall, knowing it cannot help itself, for it is an image needing help. Haydock's irony is that the god needs provision against falling. — George Leo Haydock

17 COMMENTARY: Then he prays to it for his goods, his wife, and his children, and is not ashamed to speak to that which has no life. Haydock notes the shame: prayer for family goods addressed to a lifeless thing. — George Leo Haydock

18 COMMENTARY: For health he calls upon what is weak, for life prays to what is dead, for aid humbly beseeches what has no experience, and for a good journey asks what cannot use its feet. Haydock's catalogue of absurd prayers is the chapter's centre: each request is addressed to a powerless thing. — George Leo Haydock

19 COMMENTARY: For gaining and getting, and for good success of his hands, he asks ability of him who is most unable to do anything. Haydock closes the catalogue with the same irony: success of hands is asked of what has no hands to help. — George Leo Haydock
