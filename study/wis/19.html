Study Notes — Wisdom of Solomon

Chapter 19

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: As for the ungodly, wrath came upon them without mercy to the end, for he knew before what they would do. Haydock's opening is merciless wrath to the end, grounded in foreknowledge. — George Leo Haydock

2 COMMENTARY: How that, having given them leave to depart and sent them hastily away, they would repent and pursue them. Haydock notes foreknowledge of the pursuit: leave given, yet pursuit follows. — George Leo Haydock

3 COMMENTARY: While they were yet mourning and making lamentation at the graves of the dead, they added another foolish device and pursued as fugitives those whom they had entreated to be gone. Haydock observes the folly added to mourning: pursuing as fugitives those entreated to leave. — George Leo Haydock

4 COMMENTARY: For the destiny whereof they were worthy drew them to this end, and made them forget the things that had already happened, that they might fulfil the punishment wanting to their torments. Haydock notes destiny draws them to forget past plagues, so punishment may be fulfilled. — George Leo Haydock

5 COMMENTARY: While your people passed through a wonderful way, they themselves found a strange death. Haydock contrasts the wonderful way with a strange death. — George Leo Haydock

6 COMMENTARY: The whole creature in his proper kind was fashioned again anew, serving your commandments, that your children might be kept without hurt. Haydock's point is that creation itself was refashioned to keep the children unhurt. — George Leo Haydock

7 COMMENTARY: The cloud shadowed the camp, and where water stood before, dry land appeared, and out of the Red Sea a way without impediment, and out of the violent stream a green field. Haydock pictures the sea road: cloud shadow, dry land, green field from violent stream. — George Leo Haydock

8 COMMENTARY: Through this the whole nation went, guarded by your hand, seeing your marvellous strange wonders. Haydock notes the whole nation passed guarded, seeing wonders. — George Leo Haydock

9 COMMENTARY: They went at large like horses, and leaped like lambs, praising you, O Lord, who had delivered them. Haydock's image is joy: horses at large, lambs leaping, praising the Deliverer. — George Leo Haydock

10 COMMENTARY: They were mindful of the things done in their sojourn, how the ground brought forth flies instead of cattle's breed, and the river cast up frogs instead of fishes. Haydock notes memory of the sojourn: flies for cattle, frogs for fishes. — George Leo Haydock

11 COMMENTARY: Afterward they saw a new generation of birds, when, being led by appetite, they asked delicate meats. Haydock observes the new generation of birds answering appetite's request. — George Leo Haydock

12 COMMENTARY: Quails came up to them from the sea for their contentment. Haydock notes quails from the sea contented them. — George Leo Haydock

13 COMMENTARY: Punishments came upon the sinners not without former signs by the force of thunders, for they suffered justly according to their wickedness, inasmuch as they used a more hard and hateful behaviour toward strangers. Haydock notes thunders gave former signs, and punishment fits the hard behaviour toward strangers. — George Leo Haydock

14 COMMENTARY: The Sodomites did not receive those whom they knew not when they came, but these brought friends into bondage, who had well deserved of them. Haydock contrasts Sodom's refusal of strangers with Egypt's bondage of benefactors. — George Leo Haydock

15 COMMENTARY: Not only so, but peradventure some respect shall be had of those, because they used strangers not friendly. Haydock notes even Sodom's unfriendliness may find some respect beside Egypt's cruelty. — George Leo Haydock

16 COMMENTARY: But these very grievously afflicted them, whom they had received with feastings, and who were already made partakers of the same laws with them. Haydock's point is the aggravation: guests received with feasting and made sharers of laws were grievously afflicted. — George Leo Haydock

17 COMMENTARY: Therefore even with blindness were these stricken, as those were at the doors of the righteous man, when, compassed with horrible great darkness, every one sought the passage of his own doors. Haydock notes blindness like Sodom's: each seeking his own door in darkness. — George Leo Haydock

18 COMMENTARY: The elements were changed in themselves by a kind of harmony, like as in a psaltery notes change the name of the tune, and yet are always sounds, which may well be perceived by the sight of the things done. Haydock's image is musical: elements change like psaltery notes, keeping the sound. — George Leo Haydock

19 COMMENTARY: Earthly things were turned into watery, and the things that before swam in the water now went upon the ground. Haydock notes the exchange: land to water, water to land. — George Leo Haydock

20 COMMENTARY: The fire had power in the water, forgetting his own virtue, and the water forgot its own quenching nature. Haydock observes fire forgetting its virtue in water, and water forgetting to extinguish. — George Leo Haydock

21 COMMENTARY: On the other side, the flames wasted not the flesh of the corruptible living things, though they walked therein, neither melted they the icy kind of heavenly meat that was of nature apt to melt. Haydock notes flames sparing flesh and sparing melting manna. — George Leo Haydock

22 COMMENTARY: O Lord, you magnified your people in all things, and did not despise them, but stood by them in every time and place. Haydock's doxology is that God magnified his people and stood by them everywhere. — George Leo Haydock
