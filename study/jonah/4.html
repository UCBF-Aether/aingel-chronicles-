Study Notes — Jonah

Chapter 4

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: It displeased Jonah exceedingly, and he was very angry, that Nineveh had repented and found mercy. Henry notes he had no rule over his own spirit, too little reverence for God to accept what pleased God, and too little affection for men to rejoice at their conversion, grudging mercy through pride and concern for his own honour. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Jonah prayed, yet awkwardly: he justified his flight, saying he had foreseen that Nineveh would repent and God would forgive them. Henry notes the strange perversity of dreading the success of his own ministry, and of making God's gracious, merciful character, his greatest glory, a matter of reproach. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: In passion Jonah begged God to take his life, saying it was better to die than to live. Henry calls this folly and strong corruption: Jonah was in the midst of usefulness, yet wished to die while out of temper and unfit to die, though life had just been restored to him as a mercy. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: The Lord answered mildly, Doest thou well to be angry? Henry notes God might justly have rejected Jonah or taken him at his word, but instead reasoned with him, as the father reasoned with the elder brother, teaching us to restore the fallen with meekness and to ask ourselves whether anger at God's mercy can ever be justified. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Jonah went out of the city, sat on its east side, made a booth and sat under its shadow, waiting to see what would become of the city. Henry notes he still hoped some judgment might save his credit, and that fretful spirits industriously create inconveniences so they may still have something to complain of. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: The Lord God prepared a gourd that grew up over Jonah to be a shadow over his head and deliver him from his grief, and Jonah was exceedingly glad of it. Henry notes God's tenderness even to a foolish and froward servant: as he had prepared a fish against the water, so he prepared a plant against the air, for a small thing coming seasonably may be a valuable blessing. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: God prepared a worm that smote the gourd, so that it withered. Henry notes the affliction came by divine appointment in the very thing that was his comfort: creature-comforts are withering things, and a small unseen worm at the root can destroy a large gourd. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: A vehement east wind drove the sun's heat upon Jonah's head, so that he fainted and wished to die. Henry notes the passion that ran to transport when the gourd flourished now ran to grief when it withered: inordinate affection lays the foundation for inordinate affliction. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: God asked, Doest thou well to be angry for the gourd, and Jonah answered, I do well to be angry, even unto death. Henry notes that speaking amiss in haste may be recalled, but standing to it after God's rebuke is worse: passion overruled conscience and made Jonah fly in the face of God's authority. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: God argued from Jonah's own pity: Jonah had pity on the gourd, for which he did not labour and which came up in a night. Henry notes the contrast: the gourd was one, not his own, of sudden growth and short life, while Nineveh's people were the work of God's own hands, grown over many years. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Should not God spare Nineveh, that great city, with more than a hundred and twenty thousand who cannot discern between their right hand and their left, and also much cattle? Henry notes God's tender regard to little children and to immortal souls, and that one soul is worth more than many gourds, so God's mercy is justified against every murmurer at it. — Matthew Henry
