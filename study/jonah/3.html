Study Notes — Jonah

Chapter 3

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The word of the Lord came to Jonah the second time, evidence of a thorough reconciliation after a controversy that had run high. Henry notes that employing Jonah again was equivalent to a pardon: when God forgives, he forgets, and restores the disobedient servant to his former service. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Jonah must arise, go to Nineveh, and preach the preaching God bids him, the very message he had fled from, unaltered to suit him. Henry stresses that God's word will not bend to the humours of preachers or hearers; they must comply with it, and God would go along with him step by step. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Jonah arose and went to Nineveh according to the word of the Lord, without murmuring or disputing. Henry reads this as repentance itself: a change of mind and way, and a return to the work and duty from which he had turned aside, with affliction having brought him back to his place. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Entering the exceedingly great city, Jonah cried at once, Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown. Henry notes the forty days were a reprieve in which God waited to see if they would humble themselves, slow to wrath even when wickedness cried for vengeance. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: The people of Nineveh believed God, and proclaimed a fast, and put on sackcloth from the greatest to the least. Henry calls this a wonder of grace: they believed there was one living and true God to whom they were accountable, that ruin would come at a fixed time unless prevented by timely repentance, and that he might yet be merciful. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Word reached the king of Nineveh, and he rose from his throne, laid aside his royal robe, covered himself with sackcloth and sat in ashes. Henry notes the king acknowledged he had forfeited throne and robe by misusing his power, and that it well becomes the greatest of men to abase themselves before the great God. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: By the king's decree and his nobles', a fast was proclaimed: neither man nor beast was to taste anything, and beasts too were covered with sackcloth. Henry notes the whole legislative power and the whole people concurred, making it a national act to prevent a national ruin. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: The decree joined prayer and reformation to fasting: let them cry mightily to God, and turn every one from his evil way and from the violence in his hands. Henry stresses it is not enough to fast for sin; we must fast from sin, restoring what was unjustly taken and ceasing to oppress or defraud. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Who can tell if God will turn and repent, and turn from his fierce anger, that we perish not? Henry notes they dared not presume, yet would not despair: a general sense of God's goodness gave them hope that mercy might rejoice against judgment, and hope of mercy is the great encouragement to repentance. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way, and he repented of the evil he had said he would do, and did it not. Henry notes God looked beyond good words to fruits meet for repentance: fasting and sackcloth alone would have been nothing without a general turning from sin. — Matthew Henry
