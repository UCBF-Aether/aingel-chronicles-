Study Notes — Jonah

Chapter 1

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The word of the Lord came to Jonah son of Amittai, an honour in itself: God gave his prophet a commission. Henry notes Jonah means a dove, a fitting name for God's people, who should be harmless as doves, and Amittai means my truth, for God's prophets should be sons of truth. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Jonah must arise and go to Nineveh, that great city, and cry against it, because its wickedness had come up before God. Henry stresses Nineveh's greatness in people, wealth and power, yet great cities as well as great men stand under God's government and judgment, and its sin was a bold, open affront. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Instead of rising to go to Nineveh, Jonah rose to flee to Tarshish, away from the presence of the Lord and the spirit of prophecy that pressed him to the work. Henry warns that providence may seem to favour a wrong course, a ship ready to sail, but the ready way is not always the right way. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: God sent out a great wind and a mighty tempest, so that the ship was likely to be broken. Henry calls the storm a pursuer sent after Jonah to fetch him back to God and his duty, and counts it mercy to be reclaimed when we go astray, even by a tempest. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: The mariners, afraid, cried every man to his god and cast the wares overboard to lighten the ship, valuing life above wealth. Henry contrasts their alarm with Jonah, the one concerned, lying fast asleep below, unawakened by the noise without or the guilt within. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: The shipmaster roused Jonah with a just chiding, What meanest thou, O sleeper, urging him to arise and call upon his God, if so be God would think upon them and they perish not. Henry calls this seasonable reproof the first step toward Jonah's recovery, and notes that those who hope to share a common mercy should contribute their prayers toward it. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Concluding the storm was divine justice arresting some enormous crime aboard, the mariners cast lots to know for whose cause the evil was upon them. Henry notes they appealed to God's disposal of the lot, and that God has many ways of bringing concealed sins and deserting servants to light. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: Questioned calmly rather than attacked, Jonah was asked for whose cause the evil had come, what his occupation was, and of what people he was. Henry notes their compassion toward a discovered offender, and that finding the cause of trouble requires descending to particulars in diligent search. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Jonah answered that he was a Hebrew who feared the Lord, the God of heaven who made the sea and the dry land. Henry notes he owned his religion as his calling, condemning his own folly in fleeing from such a God, and pointing the mariners from their many gods to the one living and true God. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: The men were exceedingly afraid when they learned Jonah had fled from the presence of the Lord. Henry notes their fear doubled: the tempest came from an offended God, and if a prophet was so pursued for one offence, they feared what would become of themselves for many. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: With the sea still growing more tempestuous, the mariners asked Jonah himself what they should do to him that the sea might be calm. Henry notes they treated him tenderly as a penitent, and that discovering sin as the cause of trouble is not enough: the sin itself must be confessed, detested and forsaken. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Jonah read his own doom: take me up and cast me into the sea, and the sea would be calm. Henry reads this as a true penitent's language, desiring that none but himself should suffer for his sin, and submitting even to a sentence of death, as a type of Christ giving himself that the storm might cease. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: The mariners rowed hard to bring the ship to land and spare Jonah, but they could not, for the sea wrought against them. Henry notes they rowed against the wind of God's vengeance and the tide of his counsels, and that it is vain to seek safety any way other than by destroying the sin. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Before casting Jonah over, the mariners cried to the Lord, no longer to their many gods, begging that innocent blood not be laid on them. Henry notes their dread of blood-guiltiness, especially a prophet's blood, and their plea that God himself had done as it pleased him in wind and lot. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: They took up Jonah and cast him into the sea, and the sea ceased from its raging. Henry's application is that the sin that raises the storm must itself be cast forth and drowned, and then God, by pardoning mercy, casts our iniquities into the depths of the sea as well. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: The men then feared the Lord exceedingly, and offered sacrifice and made vows to him. Henry reads this as confirmation in the true God: his power in raising and laying the tempest, his justice on his own servant, and his goodness in saving them moved them to worship him only. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: The Lord had prepared a great fish to swallow Jonah, and he was three days and three nights in its belly. Henry stresses the fish swallowed him not to devour but to protect him, a monument of mercy, a preparation for preaching to Nineveh, and a type of Christ three days in the heart of the earth. — Matthew Henry
