Study Notes — 1 Samuel

Chapter 14

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Upon a day Jonathan said to his armourbearer, Come, let us go over to the Philistines' garrison on the other side, but he told not his father. Jonathan felt a Divine impulse putting him upon this bold adventure, Henry notes; God directs the steps of those who acknowledge him in all their ways. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

2 COMMENTARY: Saul tarried in the uttermost part of Gibeah under a pomegranate tree in Migron, with about six hundred men. Saul seemed quite at a loss and unable to help himself, Henry observes; those who see themselves out of God's protection can never think themselves safe. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

3 COMMENTARY: Ahiah, son of Ahitub, Ichabod's brother, son of Phinehas, son of Eli, the LORD's priest in Shiloh, wearing an ephod, was with Saul, and the people knew not that Jonathan was gone. Saul now sent for a priest and the ark, hoping, Henry says, to make up matters with the Almighty by a partial reformation, as many do whose hearts are unhumbled. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

4 COMMENTARY: Between the passages by which Jonathan sought to go over to the Philistine garrison there was a sharp rock on each side, Bozez and Seneh. The ground itself made the venture look impossible. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

5 COMMENTARY: The forefront of one rock was northward over against Michmash, and the other southward over against Gibeah. The pass lay between the two camps, watched from both sides. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

6 COMMENTARY: Jonathan said to his armourbearer, Come, let us go over to the garrison of these uncircumcised; it may be that the LORD will work for us, for there is no restraint to the LORD to save by many or by few. God will direct the steps of those who seek him with full purpose of heart to follow his guidance. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

7 COMMENTARY: His armourbearer said, Do all that is in thine heart; turn thee, behold, I am with thee according to thy heart. The servant's faith matched his master's. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

8 COMMENTARY: Jonathan said, Behold, we will pass over unto these men and discover ourselves unto them. The plan put both men openly in the enemy's hands, trusting the sign God would give. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

9 COMMENTARY: If they say, Tarry until we come to you, then we will stand still and not go up. The first possible answer would mean God had not given the garrison into their hand. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

10 COMMENTARY: But if they say, Come up unto us, then we will go up, for the LORD hath delivered them into our hand, and this shall be a sign unto us. Sometimes, Henry notes, we find most comfort in that which is least our own doing, led by the unexpected but well-observed turns of Divine providence. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

11 COMMENTARY: Both discovered themselves to the garrison, and the Philistines said, Behold, the Hebrews come forth out of the holes where they hid themselves. The enemy mocked the two men climbing out of hiding. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

12 COMMENTARY: The men of the garrison said, Come up to us, and we will show you a thing, and Jonathan said, Come up after me, for the LORD hath delivered them into the hand of Israel. The sign was given exactly as Jonathan had framed it. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

13 COMMENTARY: Jonathan climbed up on hands and feet, his armourbearer after him, and they fell before Jonathan, and his armourbearer slew after him. Two men against a garrison, and the garrison fell. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

14 COMMENTARY: That first slaughter by Jonathan and his armourbearer was about twenty men, within about half an acre. The small beginning shook the whole host. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

15 COMMENTARY: There was trembling in the host, in the field, and among all the people; the garrison and the spoilers trembled, and the earth quaked, so it was a very great trembling. It is called a trembling of God, Henry notes, signifying not only a great trembling they could not resist, but that it came at once from the hand of God, who made the heart and knows how to make it tremble. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

16 COMMENTARY: Saul's watchmen in Gibeah looked, and behold, the multitude melted away, and they went on beating down one another. The Philistines were, by God's power, set against one another. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

17 COMMENTARY: Saul told his people to number and see who was gone, and when they numbered, Jonathan and his armourbearer were not there. The missing two explained the whole rout. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

18 COMMENTARY: Saul said to Ahiah, Bring hither the ark of God, for the ark was at that time with Israel. The more evident it was that God did all, Henry observes, the more reason Saul had to enquire whether God would give him leave to do anything. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

19 COMMENTARY: While Saul talked to the priest, the noise in the Philistine host went on and increased, and Saul said to the priest, Withdraw thine hand. But he was in such haste to fight a fallen enemy, Henry notes, that he would not stay to end his devotions nor hear what answer God would give; he that believeth will not make such haste, nor reckon any business so urgent as not to allow time to take God with him. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

20 COMMENTARY: Saul and all his people assembled and came to the battle, and behold, every man's sword was against his fellow, and there was a very great discomfiture. The victory was already God's before Israel struck a blow. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

21 COMMENTARY: Moreover the Hebrews who were with the Philistines turned to be with the Israelites with Saul and Jonathan. The turncoats turned back when the tide turned. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

22 COMMENTARY: Likewise all the men of Israel who had hid in Mount Ephraim, when they heard the Philistines fled, followed hard after them in battle. The hidden came out of their holes to join the pursuit. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

23 COMMENTARY: So the LORD saved Israel that day, and the battle passed over unto Bethaven. The day's salvation is credited to the LORD, not to Jonathan's daring or Saul's army. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

24 COMMENTARY: The men of Israel were distressed that day, for Saul had adjured the people, saying, Cursed be the man that eateth any food until evening, that I may be avenged on mine enemies, so none tasted food. Saul's severe order was very unwise, Henry says: if it gained time, it lost strength for the pursuit. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

25 COMMENTARY: All they of the land came to a wood, and there was honey upon the ground. The land itself offered the famished army food. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

26 COMMENTARY: When the people came into the wood, behold, the honey dropped, but no man put his hand to his mouth, for the people feared the oath. Hunger yielded to fear of the curse. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

27 COMMENTARY: But Jonathan heard not when his father charged the people with the oath, so he dipped the end of his rod in a honeycomb, put it to his mouth, and his eyes were enlightened. Innocent refreshment, taken in ignorance of the oath, revived the very man who had won the day. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

28 COMMENTARY: One of the people told him his father had straitly charged the people with an oath, Cursed be the man that eateth any food this day, and the people were faint. The warning came too late for Jonathan, but it showed the army's condition. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

29 COMMENTARY: Jonathan said, My father hath troubled the land; see how mine eyes have been enlightened because I tasted a little of this honey. Such is the nature of our bodies, Henry notes, that daily work cannot be done without daily bread, which our Father in heaven graciously gives. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

30 COMMENTARY: How much more, if the people had eaten freely today of the spoil of their enemies, would there not now have been a much greater slaughter among the Philistines? The rash oath had cost Israel a fuller victory. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

31 COMMENTARY: They smote the Philistines that day from Michmash to Aijalon, and the people were very faint. A long pursuit on empty stomachs ended in exhaustion. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

32 COMMENTARY: The people flew upon the spoil, took sheep, oxen, and calves, slew them on the ground, and ate them with the blood. Famished men broke the law in their haste to eat. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

33 COMMENTARY: They told Saul the people sinned against the LORD in eating with the blood, and he said, Ye have transgressed; roll a great stone unto me this day. The king who had caused the faintness now condemned its consequence. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

34 COMMENTARY: Saul told the people to bring every man his ox and sheep, slay them there, and eat, and sin not against the LORD in eating with the blood, and all the people brought their oxen that night and slew them there. The great stone gave them a lawful place to slaughter. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

35 COMMENTARY: Saul built an altar unto the LORD, the same being the first altar he built unto the LORD. Saul was turning aside from God, Henry observes, and now began to build altars, being most zealous for the form of godliness when denying its power. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

36 COMMENTARY: Saul said, Let us go down after the Philistines by night and spoil them until morning, leaving not a man, and the people agreed, but the priest said, Let us draw near hither unto God. The priest's counsel checked the king's haste with a call to enquire of God first. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

37 COMMENTARY: Saul asked counsel of God whether to go down after the Philistines and whether God would deliver them into Israel's hand, but God answered him not that day. If God turns away our prayer, Henry says, we have reason to suspect some sin harboured in our hearts, which we should find out and put away. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

38 COMMENTARY: Saul said, Draw near, all chiefs of the people, and know and see wherein this sin hath been this day. The silence was treated as proof of hidden sin in the camp. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

39 COMMENTARY: As the LORD liveth, which saveth Israel, though it be in Jonathan my son, he shall surely die, Saul swore, but no man among the people answered him. We should always first suspect and examine ourselves, Henry warns, but an unhumbled heart suspects every other person and looks everywhere but at home for the cause of calamity. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

40 COMMENTARY: He said to all Israel, Be ye on one side, and I and Jonathan my son on the other, and the people said, Do what seemeth good unto thee. The lot was arranged to isolate the king's house from the people. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

41 COMMENTARY: Saul said to the LORD God of Israel, Give a perfect lot, and Saul and Jonathan were taken, but the people escaped. The lot pointed at the royal house. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

42 COMMENTARY: Saul said, Cast lots between me and Jonathan my son, and Jonathan was taken. The lot narrowed from the nation to the household to the son. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

43 COMMENTARY: Saul said to Jonathan, Tell me what thou hast done, and Jonathan told him, I did but taste a little honey with the end of my rod, and lo, I must die. Jonathan was discovered to be the offender, Henry notes, though his offence was unwitting. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

44 COMMENTARY: Saul answered, God do so and more also, for thou shalt surely die, Jonathan. Those most indulgent to their own sins are most severe upon others, Henry observes, and those who most disregard God's authority are most impatient when their own commands are slighted. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

45 COMMENTARY: The people protested that Jonathan, who had wrought this great salvation in Israel, should not die, swearing that not one hair of his head should fall since he had wrought with God that day, and so they rescued Jonathan. The people overruled the king's rash oath to save the day's hero. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

46 COMMENTARY: Then Saul went up from following the Philistines, and the Philistines went to their own place. The pursuit ended in an unfinished victory and a rescued son. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

47 COMMENTARY: So Saul took the kingdom over Israel and fought against all his enemies on every side — Moab, Ammon, Edom, Zobah, and the Philistines — and whithersoever he turned himself, he vexed them. Here begins a general account of Saul's court and camp. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

48 COMMENTARY: He gathered a host and smote the Amalekites and delivered Israel out of the hands of them that spoiled them. War on every border marked his reign. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

49 COMMENTARY: The sons of Saul were Jonathan, Ishui, and Melchishua, and his daughters were Merab the firstborn and Michal the younger. The family list sets the persons who will fill the rest of the book. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

50 COMMENTARY: Saul's wife was Ahinoam daughter of Ahimaaz, and the captain of his host was Abner son of Ner, Saul's uncle. Abner, the king's kinsman and general, enters the story here. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

51 COMMENTARY: Kish was Saul's father, and Ner, Abner's father, was the son of Abiel. The genealogy ties king and captain as cousins. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

52 COMMENTARY: There was sore war against the Philistines all the days of Saul, and when Saul saw any strong or valiant man, he took him unto him. He had little reason to be proud of his royal dignity, Henry notes, nor had his neighbours cause to envy him, for he had little enjoyment after he took the kingdom; often men's earthly glory makes a blaze just before the dark night of disgrace and woe. — Matthew Henry (Concise)
