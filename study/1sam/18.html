Study Notes — 1 Samuel

Chapter 18

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: When David had made an end of speaking unto Saul, the soul of Jonathan was knit with the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul. The friendship of David and Jonathan was the effect of Divine grace, Henry notes, which produces in true believers one heart and one soul; this union of souls comes from partaking in the Spirit of Christ. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

2 COMMENTARY: Saul took David that day and would let him go no more home to his father's house. The victor was kept at court, in the king's sight and service. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

3 COMMENTARY: Then Jonathan and David made a covenant, because he loved him as his own soul. Where God unites hearts, Henry observes, carnal matters are too weak to separate them. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

4 COMMENTARY: Jonathan stripped himself of his robe and gave it to David, with his garments, his sword, his bow, and his girdle. The heir gave the shepherd his own arms and robes, a pledge of the covenant. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

5 COMMENTARY: David went out whithersoever Saul sent him and behaved himself wisely, and Saul set him over the men of war, and he was accepted by all the people and by Saul's servants. It was a great proof of God's grace in David, Henry says, that he bore all this respect and honour without being lifted up above measure. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

6 COMMENTARY: As they came, when David returned from the slaughter of the Philistine, the women came out of all Israel's cities singing and dancing to meet King Saul, with tabrets, joy, and instruments of music. Triumph brought a public song. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

7 COMMENTARY: The women answered one another as they played, Saul hath slain his thousands, and David his ten thousands. The song's arithmetic set the servant above the king. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

8 COMMENTARY: Saul was very wroth, and the saying displeased him, and he said, They have ascribed to David ten thousands, and to me but thousands; and what can he have more but the kingdom? David's troubles, Henry notes, not only immediately followed his triumphs but arose from them — such is the vanity of what seems greatest in this world. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

9 COMMENTARY: Saul eyed David from that day and forward. Envy fixed its eye on the young captain and never looked away. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

10 COMMENTARY: On the morrow the evil spirit from God came upon Saul, and he prophesied in the midst of the house, while David played as at other times, and there was a javelin in Saul's hand. Compare David with his harp, aiming to serve Saul, and Saul with his javelin, aiming to slay David, Henry says, and observe the sweetness of God's persecuted people and the barbarity of their persecutors. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

11 COMMENTARY: Saul cast the javelin, saying, I will smite David even to the wall, and David avoided out of his presence twice. It is a sign the Spirit of God is departed from men, Henry observes, if, like Saul, they are peevish, envious, suspicious, and ill-natured; but David's safety must be ascribed to God's providence. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

12 COMMENTARY: Saul was afraid of David, because the LORD was with him and was departed from Saul. The very presence that had once drawn Saul to David now terrified him. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

13 COMMENTARY: Therefore Saul removed him from himself and made him captain over a thousand, and he went out and came in before the people. Exile from the court became a wider command in the field. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

14 COMMENTARY: David behaved himself wisely in all his ways, and the LORD was with him. For a long time David was kept in continual apprehension of falling by Saul's hand, Henry notes, yet he persevered in meek and respectful behaviour toward his persecutor. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

15 COMMENTARY: When Saul saw that he behaved himself very wisely, he was afraid of him. Wisdom itself became a fresh cause of fear to the king. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

16 COMMENTARY: But all Israel and Judah loved David, because he went out and came in before them. The people's love grew as the king's fear grew. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

17 COMMENTARY: Saul said to David, Behold my elder daughter Merab, her will I give thee to wife; only be thou valiant for me and fight the LORD's battles. For Saul said, Let not mine hand be upon him, but let the hand of the Philistines be upon him. The offer of marriage was a trap baited with honour. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

18 COMMENTARY: David said, Who am I, and what is my life or my father's family in Israel, that I should be son-in-law to the king? If David magnified the honour of being son-in-law to King Saul, Henry asks, how should we magnify the honour of being sons to the King of kings? — Matthew Henry (Concise)

19 COMMENTARY: But at the time Merab should have been given to David, she was given to Adriel the Meholathite to wife. The promised bride was given to another, and the trap was reset. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

20 COMMENTARY: Michal Saul's daughter loved David, and they told Saul, and the thing pleased him. A daughter's love became the king's new instrument. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

21 COMMENTARY: Saul said, I will give him her, that she may be a snare to him, and that the hand of the Philistines may be against him; wherefore he said, Thou shalt this day be my son-in-law in the one of the twain. The snare was laid openly in Saul's own counsel. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

22 COMMENTARY: Saul commanded his servants to commune secretly with David, saying, Behold, the king hath delight in thee, and all his servants love thee; now therefore be the king's son-in-law. Flattery was sent ahead of the demand. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

23 COMMENTARY: Saul's servants spoke those words to David, and David said, Seemeth it to you a light thing to be a king's son-in-law, seeing I am a poor man and lightly esteemed? David's humility counted the honour too great for a poor shepherd. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

24 COMMENTARY: The servants told Saul what David had spoken. The humble answer was carried back to the king. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

25 COMMENTARY: Saul said, Thus shall ye say to David, The king desireth not any dowry, but an hundred foreskins of the Philistines, to be avenged of the king's enemies. But Saul thought to make David fall by the hand of the Philistines. The dowry was a death sentence disguised as a bride-price. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

26 COMMENTARY: When his servants told David these words, it pleased David well to be the king's son-in-law, and the days were not expired. David accepted the perilous terms without delay. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

27 COMMENTARY: David arose and went, he and his men, and slew two hundred Philistines, and brought their foreskins in full tale to the king, that he might be the king's son-in-law, and Saul gave him Michal his daughter to wife. The demanded hundred was doubled, and the trap returned its maker empty. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

28 COMMENTARY: Saul saw and knew that the LORD was with David, and that Michal his daughter loved him. The evidence was now undeniable, even to Saul. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

29 COMMENTARY: Saul was yet the more afraid of David, and Saul became David's enemy continually. Knowledge of God's presence with David hardened, rather than softened, the king. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

30 COMMENTARY: Then the princes of the Philistines went forth, and after they went forth, David behaved himself more wisely than all Saul's servants, so that his name was much set by. How uncommon is such prudence under insults and provocations, Henry asks; opposition in others will not excuse wrong tempers in us, but should increase our care in the duties of our station. — Matthew Henry (Concise)
