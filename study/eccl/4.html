Study Notes — Ecclesiastes

Chapter 4

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: He returned and considered all the oppressions done under the sun, and saw the tears of the oppressed with no comforter, while power stood on the side of the oppressors. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Therefore he praised the dead more than the living, not as a wish for death itself, but because the dead are past the reach of oppression that the living still endure. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Better than both is he who has not yet been, who has not seen the evil work done under the sun, because he has neither suffered oppression nor witnessed it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Again he considered all travail and every right work, and saw that for this a man is envied of his neighbour, so that even honest success breeds envy, which is also vanity and vexation of spirit. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: The fool folds his hands together and eats his own flesh, because sloth consumes a man as surely as labour wearies him, and idleness is its own ruin. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Better is a handful with quietness than both hands full with travail and vexation of spirit, because a little held in peace is worth more than abundance held in turmoil. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: He returned and saw vanity under the sun in a solitary labourer, a picture of toil without fellowship or heir, which Henry makes the next instance of earthly emptiness. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: There is one alone, with neither child nor brother, yet no end to his labour and his eye never satisfied with riches, and he never asks for whom he labours and bereaves his soul of good, which is also vanity and sore travail. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Two are better than one, because they have a good reward for their labour, sharing both the work and its fruit, which the solitary man cannot do. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: If one falls, the other lifts up his fellow, but woe to him that is alone when he falls, for he has not another to help him up. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: If two lie together they have heat, but how can one be warm alone, a homely proof that fellowship supplies what solitude cannot. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: If one prevails against a single man, two shall withstand him, and a threefold cord is not quickly broken, because union gives a strength that no solitary strength possesses. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Better is a poor and wise child than an old and foolish king who will no more be admonished, because wisdom, even in youth and poverty, outranks power grown deaf to counsel. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Out of prison he comes to reign, whereas also he that is born in his kingdom becomes poor, because fortune raises the low and lowers the high without regard to merit. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: He considered all the living who walk under the sun with the second child that shall stand up in the king's stead, because crowds transfer their favour to the rising heir as quickly as they gave it to the old king. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: There is no end of all the people, even of all that have been before them, yet they also that come after shall not rejoice in him, so that popularity itself is vanity and vexation of spirit. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
