Study Notes — Exodus

Chapter 12

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The LORD spake to Moses and Aaron in Egypt, giving the passover law before the deliverance itself. Henry observes God makes all things new to those he delivers: the night of departure would begin a new life for his people. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: This month would be the beginning of months, the first month of the year to Israel. Henry notes the calendar itself was reset: redemption became the starting point from which Israel counted time. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: On the tenth day of the month every household was to take a lamb, a lamb for a house. Henry notes the lamb was to be chosen and set apart four days beforehand, denoting the Saviour marked out in God's purpose and promise. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: If a household was too little for a lamb, it was to join with its neighbour, reckoning according to what each man could eat. Henry observes the provision was communal: no house was left without the sacrifice, and none was to take more than it needed. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: The lamb was to be without blemish, a male of the first year, taken from the sheep or the goats. Henry notes the lamb was to be in its prime and without spot, as Christ offered himself in the midst of his days, a Lamb without spot. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: They were to keep it until the fourteenth day, when the whole assembly of Israel would kill it in the evening. Henry observes the set time taught them to wait upon God's appointment, and the whole congregation acted together. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: They were to take of the blood and strike it on the two side posts and the upper door post of the houses where they ate it. Henry notes the blood was sprinkled openly on the door, denoting the open profession of faith in Christ, but never upon the threshold, lest the blood of the covenant be trampled. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: They were to eat the flesh that night, roast with fire, with unleavened bread and bitter herbs. Henry observes the meal was to be fed upon, not merely looked at: believers must by faith make Christ their own food and strength. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: It was not to be eaten raw or sodden with water, but roast with fire, its head with its legs and its purtenance. Henry notes the roasting denoted Christ's painful sufferings unto death, for the wrath of God is as fire, and Christ was made a curse for us. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Nothing was to remain until the morning; what remained was to be burnt with fire. Henry observes the whole lamb was to be eaten at once: today Christ is offered, and is to be accepted while it is called today, before we sleep the sleep of death. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: They were to eat it with loins girded, shoes on feet, and staff in hand, eating in haste, for it was the LORD's passover. Henry notes the posture preached readiness: those who feed on Christ must sit loose to the world, ready to depart at his call. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: That night God would pass through Egypt and smite all the firstborn of man and beast, and execute judgment against all the gods of Egypt. Henry observes the stroke was both punishment and unmasking: Egypt's gods stood judged by the death of Egypt's sons. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: The blood would be a token upon the houses, and when God saw the blood he would pass over them, and no destroying plague would fall on them. Henry notes the safety was not a reward of their righteousness but the gift of mercy, through the shedding and sprinkling of blood. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: This day would be a memorial, kept as a feast to the LORD throughout their generations by an ordinance forever. Henry observes the feast looked both ways: backward to deliverance remembered, and forward to the great sacrifice of the Lamb of God. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Seven days they were to eat unleavened bread, putting away leaven from the first day, on pain of being cut off from Israel. Henry notes the strictness taught sincerity: the feast must be kept without the leaven of malice and hypocrisy. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: The first day and the seventh day were holy convocations, in which no work was to be done save preparing food. Henry observes the week was hedged with rest, that holy joy might not be crowded out by common care. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: They were to observe the feast of unleavened bread, because in that selfsame day God had brought their armies out of Egypt. Henry notes the reason given was the deed itself: the calendar remembered the deliverance that had made them a people. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: From the fourteenth day at even until the twenty-first day at even they were to eat unleavened bread. Henry observes the fixed dates bound every household to the same remembrance, year after year. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Seven days no leaven was to be found in their houses, and whoever ate leavened bread would be cut off, whether stranger or homeborn. Henry notes the severity showed how seriously God takes sincerity in worship: hidden leaven spoiled the whole feast. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: They were to eat nothing leavened; in all their habitations they were to eat unleavened bread. Henry observes the rule reached every house: holiness was not a temple matter only, but a household discipline. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Moses called for all the elders of Israel and told them to draw out and take a lamb for each family and kill the passover. Henry notes the elders received the charge first, that the whole people might learn it through their heads. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: They were to take hyssop, dip it in the blood in the bason, and strike the lintel and the two side posts, and none was to go out of his house until morning. Henry notes faith was the bunch of hyssop, applying the blood to the soul, and safety lay in staying under its protection. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: The LORD would pass through to smite the Egyptians, and seeing the blood he would pass over the door and not suffer the destroyer to enter. Henry observes the blood was the barrier: where it was, the destroyer had nothing to do. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: They were to observe this thing for an ordinance to themselves and their sons forever. Henry notes the perpetuity: the ordinance was not for one night but for all generations, until the true Lamb came. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: When they came to the promised land they were still to keep this service. Henry observes the feast would outlive the wilderness, a standing memorial in the land of rest itself. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: When their children asked, What mean ye by this service? they were to be told. Henry notes it is good for children to ask about the things of God, and good for parents to have an answer ready: they that ask for the way will find it. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: They were to answer, It is the sacrifice of the LORD's passover, who passed over Israel's houses, smote the Egyptians, and delivered their own houses, and the people bowed their heads and worshipped. Henry observes instruction ended in worship: the story told became praise. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: The children of Israel went away and did as the LORD had commanded Moses and Aaron, so did they. Henry notes the simple obedience: the whole people kept the ordinance exactly as given, on the very night of judgment. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: At midnight the LORD smote all the firstborn in Egypt, from Pharaoh's firstborn on his throne to the firstborn of the captive in the dungeon, and all the firstborn of cattle. Henry observes the stroke reached from throne to dungeon: prince and peasant stood on the same level before God's judgments. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh rose in the night, he and all his servants and all the Egyptians, and there was a great cry in Egypt, for there was not a house where there was not one dead. Henry bids the reader imagine the cry that rang through the land, a long loud shriek of agony from every dwelling. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh called for Moses and Aaron by night, saying, Rise up and get you forth from among my people, you and the children of Israel, and go serve the LORD as ye have said. Henry notes pride was now abased and he yielded: God's word will stand, and men get nothing by disputing or delaying submission. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: Take your flocks and herds, as ye have said, and be gone, and bless me also, said Pharaoh. Henry observes the king who had bargained over every hoof now surrendered all, and even begged a blessing from the men he had oppressed. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: The Egyptians were urgent upon the people to send them out in haste, for they said, We be all dead men. Henry notes terror made Egypt pay Israel's wages: the people were provided for their journey by those who had enslaved them. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: The people took their dough before it was leavened, their kneadingtroughs bound up in their clothes upon their shoulders. Henry observes the haste was literal: the bread went unleavened because there was no time to wait. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: Israel did according to Moses's word and borrowed of the Egyptians jewels of silver and gold and raiment. Henry notes the borrowing fulfilled the promise of chapter 3: they would not go out empty. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: The LORD gave the people favour in the sight of the Egyptians, so that they lent them such things as they required, and they spoiled the Egyptians. Henry observes the Lord took care that hard-earned wages should be paid at last. — Matthew Henry

37 COMMENTARY: Israel journeyed from Rameses to Succoth, about six hundred thousand on foot that were men, besides children. Henry notes the host set forward without delay, a nation born in a night. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: A mixed multitude went up also with them, with flocks and herds, even very much cattle. Henry observes some came out of curiosity or love of Israel's religion, but there were always those among Israel who were not Israelites, as there are still hypocrites in the church. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: They baked unleavened cakes of the dough brought out of Egypt, for it was not leavened, because they were thrust out and could not tarry, nor had they prepared victuals. Henry notes the cakes themselves became a memorial of haste: deliverance came too swiftly for bread to rise. — Matthew Henry

40 COMMENTARY: The sojourning of Israel in Egypt was four hundred and thirty years. Henry notes the great event came 430 years from the promise to Abraham: though God's promises are not performed quickly, they will be performed in their season. — Matthew Henry

41 COMMENTARY: At the end of the four hundred and thirty years, even the selfsame day, all the hosts of the LORD went out from Egypt. Henry observes the punctuality: the promise kept its very day, after more than four centuries. — Matthew Henry

42 COMMENTARY: It is a night to be much observed unto the LORD for bringing them out of Egypt, a night to be observed by all Israel in their generations. Henry notes the great things God does for his people are to be remembered throughout all ages, especially the work of redemption by Christ. — Matthew Henry

43 COMMENTARY: This is the ordinance of the passover: no stranger shall eat thereof. Henry notes the feast belonged to the covenant people, and sharing in God's mercies meant joining his covenant. — Matthew Henry

44 COMMENTARY: But every man's servant bought for money, when circumcised, might eat thereof. Henry observes the door stood open to the household servant who entered the covenant sign, for grace reaches beyond blood. — Matthew Henry

45 COMMENTARY: A foreigner and a hired servant shall not eat thereof. Henry notes the distinction: the sojourner who would not join the covenant could not share its feast. — Matthew Henry

46 COMMENTARY: In one house shall it be eaten; none of the flesh was to be carried abroad out of the house, neither was a bone to be broken. Henry notes the unbroken bone was fulfilled in Christ, denoting his unbroken strength. — Matthew Henry

47 COMMENTARY: All the congregation of Israel shall keep it. Henry notes the universality: the passover was not for priests alone but for the whole congregation. — Matthew Henry

48 COMMENTARY: When a stranger sojourned and would keep the passover, all his males were to be circumcised, and then he might come near and keep it, as one born in the land. Henry notes here an early indication of favour to the Gentiles: privilege rested on covenant, not on descent from Abraham. — Matthew Henry

49 COMMENTARY: One law shall be to him that is homeborn and unto the stranger that sojourneth among you. Henry observes the single law taught Israel that God's favour was not a matter of birth but of covenant obedience. — Matthew Henry

50 COMMENTARY: Thus did all Israel, as the LORD commanded Moses and Aaron, so did they. Henry notes the obedience was complete and corporate, the whole people acting as one. — Matthew Henry

51 COMMENTARY: The selfsame day the LORD brought Israel out of Egypt by their armies. Henry closes the chapter on the day itself: the night of the passover became the day of the Exodus, and a people went out in order, like an army. — Matthew Henry
