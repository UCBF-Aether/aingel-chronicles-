Study Notes — Exodus

Chapter 2

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: A man of Levi married a daughter of Levi, so Moses was born of a priestly line on both sides. Henry notes that God raised his deliverer from the very tribe that would afterward serve at the altar. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: The mother hid her son three months because she saw he was a goodly child. JFB reads her faith as seeing in the child's beauty a pledge of God's purpose, while Henry stresses that parental love here served providence, not mere fondness. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: When hiding failed, she set the child in an ark of bulrushes daubed with slime and pitch by the river's brink. Henry observes that faith did its utmost and then committed the child to God, rather than sitting idle under Pharaoh's edict. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: His sister stood afar off to watch what would be done to him. JFB notes Miriam's watchful station by the river, the quiet human means by which providence guided the child into Pharaoh's own house. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh's daughter came to wash at the river and saw the ark among the flags. Henry marks the providence: the very house that ordered the slaughter became the shelter of the deliverer. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Opening the ark, she saw the weeping babe and had compassion, knowing him to be a Hebrew child. JFB notes that compassion overcame both her father's edict and the prejudice against a Hebrew infant. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: The sister offered to call a Hebrew nurse, a proposal the princess accepted. Henry observes how providence put the very words into a child's mouth that secured Moses a godly nursing. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: The maiden fetched the child's own mother, so Moses was nursed by her who had risked all for him. JFB notes the irony and mercy: Pharaoh's house paid the mother to raise the child Pharaoh had condemned. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh's daughter hired the mother to nurse the child, promising wages. Henry draws the lesson that God often makes even enemies pay for the upbringing of his servants. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: When the child grew, his mother brought him to Pharaoh's daughter, who adopted him and named him Moses, because she drew him out of the water. JFB notes the Egyptian name fits the deliverance, and that Moses thus learned both Hebrew faith at home and Egyptian learning at court. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Grown to manhood, Moses went out to his brethren and saw their burdens. Henry reads this as the first stirrings of his calling: he refused court ease to share his people's affliction. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Seeing an Egyptian smiting a Hebrew, Moses slew the Egyptian and hid him in the sand. JFB and Henry agree his zeal was right in its aim but rash in its manner, done without a call or commission and therefore hidden rather than owned. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: The next day Moses found two Hebrews striving and asked the wrongdoer why he smote his fellow. Henry notes the sad sight: the oppressed quarrelling among themselves, a wound to their common cause. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: The offender retorted, asking who made Moses a prince and judge, and whether he would kill him as he killed the Egyptian. JFB observes that Israel's rejection of Moses here foreshadowed their later rejection of him as deliverer. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Moses feared, for the thing was surely known, and Pharaoh sought to slay him. Henry notes that a good deed done in a wrong way brings fear rather than peace, and Moses fled from Egypt. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: Reuel, priest of Midian, had seven daughters who came to draw water and fill the troughs for their father's flock. Henry notes the daughters' industry in a shepherd's household, a humble station for the future lawgiver's kin. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: When shepherds drove the maidens away, Moses stood up, helped them, and watered their flock. JFB sees the same generous temper that had defended the Hebrew now defending strangers, and Henry notes that helpfulness opened his way in Midian. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: The daughters returned early to Reuel, who asked why they had come so soon. Their report of an Egyptian who delivered them set up Moses's introduction to the household. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: They told their father an Egyptian delivered them from the shepherds and drew water for the flock. Henry notes Moses was known by his dress as an Egyptian, though his heart was wholly Hebrew. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Reuel asked why they had left the man behind and bade them call him to eat bread. JFB notes eastern hospitality here rewarded the stranger, and Henry observes that small kindnesses often open great doors. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Moses was content to dwell with Reuel, who gave him Zipporah his daughter to wife. Henry notes Moses settled into a shepherd's life, the very training God would use for leading Israel. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: Zipporah bore a son whom Moses named Gershom, for he had been a stranger in a strange land. JFB notes the name kept his exile and his hope together: a stranger here, but heir of a promise elsewhere. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: In process of time the king of Egypt died, yet Israel's bondage continued and they sighed and cried under it. Henry notes that a change of princes brought no change of oppression, driving the people from man to God. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: God heard their groaning and remembered his covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. JFB stresses that God's remembering is not forgetfulness repaired but covenant faithfulness acting in its season. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: God looked upon Israel and had respect unto them. Henry closes the chapter on this comfort: the people's cry reached heaven, and God's eye was already on their deliverance. — Matthew Henry
