Study Notes — Exodus

Chapter 8

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The LORD told Moses to go to Pharaoh again with the demand: Let my people go, that they may serve me. Henry notes the demand never changed, though the judgments grew heavier, for God's claim on his people admits no compromise. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: If Pharaoh refused, God would smite all his borders with frogs. Henry observes the second plague was fitted to Egypt's worship, for the frog was among their sacred creatures, now turned into a torment. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: The river would bring forth frogs abundantly, invading houses, bedchambers, beds, servants' houses, ovens, and kneadingtroughs. Henry notes no place was private or clean enough to escape: the plague entered where life was most domestic. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: The frogs would come up upon Pharaoh himself, upon his people, and upon all his servants. Henry observes the plague honoured no rank: king and servant alike were overrun. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Aaron was to stretch his hand with the rod over the streams, rivers, and ponds, that frogs might come up upon Egypt. Henry notes the same waters that had run blood now bred the next plague, judgment following judgment from one source. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Aaron stretched out his hand over Egypt's waters, and the frogs came up and covered the land. Henry observes the completeness: not a few frogs, but a covering, as the dust had covered the ground in the plague before. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: The magicians did so with their enchantments and brought up frogs upon Egypt. Henry notes the counterfeit again added to the plague without removing it, and Pharaoh still took imitation for power. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh called Moses and Aaron and asked them to intreat the LORD to take away the frogs, promising to let the people go and sacrifice. Henry observes this was the first yielding, wrung from him by discomfort rather than conviction. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Moses answered, Glory over me: appoint when I shall intreat for thee, thy servants, and thy people. Henry notes Moses let Pharaoh name the time, that the removal might be plainly God's doing and not chance. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh said, To morrow, and Moses answered, Be it according to thy word, that thou mayest know there is none like the LORD our God. Henry observes Pharaoh's choice of tomorrow betrayed his heart: he would endure one more night of frogs rather than seek God at once. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: The frogs would depart from Pharaoh, his houses, his servants, and his people, remaining only in the river. Henry notes the removal was as precise as the plague: the river kept its frogs, the houses were cleared. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Moses and Aaron went out from Pharaoh, and Moses cried unto the LORD because of the frogs. Henry observes Moses prayed for his enemy at his enemy's request, the pattern of intercession for persecutors. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: The LORD did according to the word of Moses, and the frogs died out of the houses, villages, and fields. Henry notes the answer came exactly as prayed, that Pharaoh might know the LORD hearkens to his servants. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: They gathered the dead frogs together upon heaps, and the land stank. Henry observes the plague's aftermath was itself a judgment: deliverance left a stench, that Egypt might remember what it had endured. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: When Pharaoh saw there was respite, he hardened his heart and hearkened not, as the LORD had said. Henry notes the pattern of all his repentances: they lasted exactly as long as the pain did. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: The LORD told Moses to have Aaron stretch out his rod and smite the dust of the land, that it might become lice. Henry observes this plague came without warning, for Pharaoh had been warned enough, and judgment now fell unannounced. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Aaron smote the dust, and it became lice upon man and beast, all the dust of the land becoming lice. Henry notes the meanness of the instrument: dust underfoot became a torment, that Egypt might see God can arm the smallest things. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: The magicians tried with their enchantments to bring forth lice, but they could not. Henry observes the counterfeit failed here: imitation reached its limit, and the magicians stood exposed before their king. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: The magicians told Pharaoh, This is the finger of God, yet Pharaoh's heart was hardened and he hearkened not. Henry notes even his own wise men confessed what their king denied, and still denial held. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: The LORD told Moses to rise early and stand before Pharaoh as he came forth to the water. Henry notes the morning confrontation repeated: Pharaoh was met at his daily worship before the day's business began. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Else, if Pharaoh would not let God's people go, God would send swarms of flies upon him, his servants, his people, and his houses. Henry observes the fourth plague invaded the houses again, but now with stinging swarms rather than creeping frogs. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: God would sever the land of Goshen, where his people dwelt, so that no swarms would be there, that Pharaoh might know God was in the midst of the land. Henry notes the new feature: distinction, for the plagues now spared Israel while striking Egypt. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: God would put a division between his people and Pharaoh's people, and tomorrow this sign would be. Henry observes the division was itself the sign: the same land, the same air, yet one people plagued and the other untouched. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: The LORD did so, and a grievous swarm came into Pharaoh's house and his servants' houses, and the land was corrupted by reason of the swarm. Henry notes the corruption spread beyond the houses to the whole land, a plague of ruin, not mere annoyance. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh called Moses and Aaron and said, Go ye, sacrifice to your God in the land. Henry observes the compromise offered: worship permitted, but only within Egypt, where Pharaoh could still control it. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: Moses answered it was not meet to sacrifice the abomination of the Egyptians before their eyes, lest they stone them. Henry explains the sacrifices Israel offered were animals Egypt worshipped, so public sacrifice in Egypt would provoke murder. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: They would go three days' journey into the wilderness and sacrifice as God commanded. Henry notes Moses held the line: the place and manner of worship were God's to appoint, not Pharaoh's to bargain. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh yielded so far: he would let them go to sacrifice in the wilderness, only they must not go very far away, and they must intreat for him. Henry observes the bargain still clung to a chain: leave, but stay within reach. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Moses promised to intreat the LORD that the swarms would depart tomorrow, but warned Pharaoh not to deal deceitfully any more in not letting the people go. Henry notes the warning was plain: a second deceit would be worse than the first refusal. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: Moses went out from Pharaoh and intreated the LORD. Henry observes Moses again prayed for the king who had twice broken faith, and God again heard him. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: The LORD did according to Moses's word and removed the swarms, so that not one remained. Henry notes the completeness of the removal left Pharaoh without even the excuse of a lingering fly. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh hardened his heart at this time also, neither would he let the people go. Henry closes the chapter on the same sentence as before: mercy received, promise broken, heart harder than ever. — Matthew Henry
