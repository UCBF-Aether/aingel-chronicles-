Study Notes — Exodus

Chapter 15

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Then sang Moses and Israel this song unto the LORD: I will sing unto the LORD, for he hath triumphed gloriously, the horse and his rider hath he thrown into the sea. Henry notes this most ancient song is holy throughout, honouring God only and magnifying no man, and it is typical of the final destruction of the church's enemies. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: The LORD is my strength and song, and he is become my salvation; he is my God, and I will prepare him a habitation, my father's God, and I will exalt him. Henry observes the song's theology: God's people are weak in themselves, but his grace is their strength, and in sorrow he is their song. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: The LORD is a man of war; the LORD is his name. Henry notes the title is not of cruelty but of almighty power: woe to those that strive with their Maker, for he fights for his people. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh's chariots and his host hath he cast into the sea, and his chosen captains are drowned in the Red Sea. Henry observes the song names the best of Egypt's army, that none might say only the weak perished. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: The depths have covered them; they sank into the bottom as a stone. Henry notes the image of weight: the army that trusted in iron sank like stone, while unarmed Israel walked on dry ground. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Thy right hand, O LORD, is become glorious in power; thy right hand hath dashed in pieces the enemy. Henry observes the song ascribes all to God's hand, not to Israel's sword, for Israel had not lifted one. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: In the greatness of thine excellency thou hast overthrown them that rose up against thee, and sent forth thy wrath, which consumed them as stubble. Henry notes God's holiness appears in his wrath against obstinate sinners, as surely as in his deliverance of Israel. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: With the blast of thy nostrils the waters were gathered together, the floods stood upright as a heap, and the depths were congealed in the heart of the sea. Henry observes the wonder was wrought by God's breath alone: the sea stood as a wall at his command. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: The enemy said, I will pursue, I will overtake, I will divide the spoil, my lust shall be satisfied, I will draw my sword, my hand shall destroy them. Henry notes the song quotes Pharaoh's boast in full, that his pride might be measured against his fall. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Thou didst blow with thy wind, the sea covered them, and they sank as lead in the mighty waters. Henry observes the same wind that parted the sea for Israel closed it upon Egypt: one breath saved and judged. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Who is like unto thee, O LORD, among the gods? Who is like thee, glorious in holiness, fearful in praises, doing wonders? Henry notes the heart of the song: God is glorious in holiness, and his holiness is his glory, fearful to enemies and matter of praise to his servants. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Thou stretchedst out thy right hand, and the earth swallowed them. Henry observes the song compresses the whole deliverance into one gesture: God's hand stretched out, and Egypt was gone. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Thou in thy mercy hast led forth the people which thou hast redeemed, and guided them in thy strength unto thy holy habitation. Henry notes the song turns from judgment to guidance: mercy that redeemed will also lead, and the journey's end is God's own dwelling. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: The people shall hear and be afraid, and sorrow shall take hold on the inhabitants of Palestina. Henry observes the song prophesies: the news of the sea would go before Israel, and fear would prepare their way. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Then the dukes of Edom shall be amazed, the mighty men of Moab shall tremble, and all the inhabitants of Canaan shall melt away. Henry notes the neighbouring powers are named in order, each undone by report before Israel ever met them. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: Fear and dread shall fall upon them, and by the greatness of thine arm they shall be as still as a stone, till thy people pass over, the people which thou hast purchased. Henry observes the stillness was God's doing: terror held Israel's enemies motionless until the purchased people had passed. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Thou shalt bring them in and plant them in the mountain of thine inheritance, in the place thou hast made for thee to dwell in, the sanctuary which thy hands have established. Henry notes the song ends in settlement: a wandering people planted, and a sanctuary established by God's own hands. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: The LORD shall reign for ever and ever. Henry observes the song's final line outlasts every kingdom named in it: Pharaoh's reign ended in the sea, and the LORD's has no end. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: For the horse of Pharaoh went in with his chariots and horsemen into the sea, and the LORD brought the waters back upon them, but Israel went on dry land in the midst of the sea. Henry notes the verse restates the history plainly after the poetry, that the song might never float free from the fact. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Miriam the prophetess, the sister of Aaron, took a timbrel in her hand, and all the women went out after her with timbrels and dances. Henry observes the women answered the men's song with their own, for deliverance belonged to the whole people. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Miriam answered them, Sing ye to the LORD, for he hath triumphed gloriously; the horse and his rider hath he thrown into the sea. Henry notes the refrain returned: the women's chorus repeated the song's first line, that all Israel might carry it. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: Moses brought Israel from the Red Sea into the wilderness of Shur, and they went three days in the wilderness and found no water. Henry observes the song ended and the thirst began: three days from the sea, the people who had sung were already in want. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: When they came to Marah, they could not drink of the waters of Marah, for they were bitter, and therefore the name of it was called Marah. Henry notes God can make bitter to us that from which we promise ourselves most, that disappointment in the creature may drive us to the Creator. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: And the people murmured against Moses, saying, What shall we drink? Henry observes even true believers, in sharp trial, are tempted to fret and murmur, and hypocrites who showed high affections fall away when temptation comes. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: Moses cried unto the LORD, and the LORD showed him a tree, which when he had cast into the waters, the waters were made sweet, and there God made a statute and an ordinance, and proved them. Henry notes Moses did what the people had neglected: he cried to the Lord, and God provided graciously, and some make the tree typical of the cross, which sweetens affliction's bitter waters. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: God said, If thou wilt diligently hearken to the voice of the LORD thy God, and do that which is right, and give ear to his commandments, and keep his statutes, I will put none of the diseases of Egypt upon thee, for I am the LORD that healeth thee. Henry notes the promise is expressed and the threatening only implied: God is the great Physician, and if we are kept well, it is he that keeps us. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: They came to Elim, where were twelve wells of water and threescore and ten palm trees, and they encamped there by the waters. Henry observes that though God may order his people to encamp by bitter Marah for a time, that shall not always be their lot, and Elim's wells were enough, and to spare. — Matthew Henry
