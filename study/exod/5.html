Study Notes — Exodus

Chapter 5

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Moses and Aaron went in to Pharaoh with the LORD's demand: Let my people go, that they may hold a feast unto me in the wilderness. Henry notes the first demand was modest and reasonable, a feast in the wilderness, not yet full departure. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh answered, Who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice? I know not the LORD, neither will I let Israel go. Henry observes proud ignorance: Pharaoh owned no god above himself and treated the LORD as a stranger. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: They replied that the God of the Hebrews had met with them, and asked leave for three days' journey to sacrifice, lest God fall upon them with pestilence or sword. Henry notes the plea was framed humbly, as a religious duty, to leave Pharaoh without excuse. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: The king charged Moses and Aaron with letting the people from their works, bidding them return to their burdens. Henry observes tyrants call godly labour idleness and religious liberty a hindrance to profit. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh said the people of the land were now many, and Moses and Aaron made them rest from their burdens. Henry notes his fear was the people's numbers, and his remedy was more work, not justice. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: The same day Pharaoh commanded the taskmasters and the officers of the people. Henry observes the speed of oppression: the answer to a prayer for liberty was a heavier yoke within the day. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: No more straw was to be given for brick-making; the people must go and gather straw themselves. Henry notes the cruelty was deliberate: the material was withdrawn but the demand was not. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: The tale of bricks from former days was still to be laid upon them, undiminished. Henry observes this was oppression refined: the same output required with less supplied, so that failure itself became the crime. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: More work was to be laid upon the men, that they might labour therein and not regard vain words. Henry notes Pharaoh's policy: keep the people too weary to listen to Moses, and call God's word vain talk. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: The taskmasters and officers went out and told the people Pharaoh's command: no straw would be given, yet the tale must be delivered. Henry observes the messengers of tyranny delivered the sentence as if it were justice. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: The people were told to go and get straw where they could find it, yet nothing of their work would be diminished. Henry notes the impossible command was itself the punishment, designed to break their spirit. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: So the people were scattered throughout Egypt to gather stubble instead of straw. Henry observes the whole land saw Israel's scattering, and the work that once was ordered became a desperate search. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: The taskmasters hasted them, demanding their daily tasks as when straw was given. Henry notes oppression knows no Sabbath of mercy: the daily tale was exacted even when the means were gone. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: The Israelite officers set over the people were beaten and asked why they had not fulfilled the brick tale. Henry observes the officers, caught between Pharaoh and their brethren, suffered for a failure they could not prevent. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: The officers cried to Pharaoh, asking why he dealt so with his servants. Henry notes they appealed to the very power that oppressed them, the common refuge of the helpless when no higher help is yet seen. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: They pleaded that no straw was given, yet they were told to make brick, and were beaten as if at fault. Henry observes the plea was just, but justice was not to be had from a heart hardened against God. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh answered, Ye are idle, ye are idle, therefore ye say, Let us go and sacrifice to the LORD. Henry notes the charge was repeated for emphasis: religion itself was branded as idleness by a king who measured men by bricks. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Go and work, said Pharaoh; no straw would be given, yet the tale of bricks must be delivered. Henry observes the sentence closed every plea, leaving Israel no remedy in man. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: The officers saw they were in evil case when the tale could not be diminished. Henry notes the moment when human hope failed was the moment God would begin to work. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Coming from Pharaoh, the officers met Moses and Aaron standing in the way. Henry observes the messengers of deliverance were met not with thanks but with reproach, the common lot of those who bring hard news of freedom. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: The officers said, The LORD look upon you and judge, because ye have made our savour abhorred to Pharaoh and put a sword in his hand to slay us. Henry notes their complaint mistook the cause: Moses had not made the yoke heavier, Pharaoh had. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: Moses returned unto the LORD and asked why he had so evil entreated this people, and why he had sent him at all. Henry does not excuse the complaint, but notes its honesty: Moses carried the people's grief straight to God rather than to Pharaoh. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: Since Moses came to Pharaoh in God's name, Pharaoh had done evil to Israel, and God had not delivered them at all. Henry observes the chapter ends in darkness, that the coming deliverance might shine the brighter against it. — Matthew Henry
