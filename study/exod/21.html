Study Notes — Exodus

Chapter 21

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Now these are the judgments which thou shalt set before them. Henry notes the chapter expounds the fifth and sixth commandments, and though these laws differ from our times and are not binding on us, they explain the moral law and the rules of natural justice. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: If thou buy a Hebrew servant, six years he shall serve, and in the seventh he shall go out free for nothing. Henry notes the servant in bondage was an emblem of man's bondage to sin, Satan, and the law, and in being made free, an emblem of the liberty wherewith Christ makes his people free indeed, without money and without price. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: If he came in by himself, he shall go out by himself, and if he were married, then his wife shall go out with him. Henry observes the law preserved what the servant brought in: marriage before servitude was not dissolved by it. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: If his master gave him a wife, and she bore sons or daughters, the wife and her children would be her master's, and he would go out by himself. Henry notes the hardship was real, for a wife given in servitude belonged to the house, and freedom could mean parting from family. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: If the servant plainly said, I love my master, my wife, and my children, and I will not go out free, his choice was to be honoured. Henry observes love could bind where law released: the servant might choose perpetual service for his family's sake. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Then his master was to bring him unto the judges, and to the door or the door post, and bore his ear through with an awl, and he would serve forever. Henry notes the public act before judges and at the door made the choice solemn and witnessed, a mark borne for life. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: If a man sold his daughter to be a maidservant, she would not go out as the menservants did. Henry observes the daughter's case was governed by marriage law rather than by the servant's term, for she was sold with a view to betrothal. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: If she pleased not her master, who had betrothed her to himself, then he was to let her be redeemed, and had no power to sell her to a strange nation, seeing he had dealt deceitfully with her. Henry notes the protection: a master who broke faith could not profit by selling her abroad. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: If he had betrothed her unto his son, he was to deal with her after the manner of daughters. Henry observes the law raised her standing: betrothed to the son, she was to be treated as a daughter, not as a servant. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: If he took another wife, her food, her raiment, and her duty of marriage were not to be diminished. Henry notes the threefold provision guarded the first wife against neglect when a second was taken. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: If he did not these three unto her, then she would go out free without money. Henry observes the remedy matched the wrong: failure to provide freed her, and freedom cost her nothing. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: He that smiteth a man, so that he die, shall be surely put to death. Henry notes God, who by providence gives and maintains life, by his law protects it, and wilful murder was capital without exception. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: If a man lay not in wait, but God delivered another into his hand, God would appoint a place whither he might flee. Henry notes the cities of refuge protected those whose unhappiness, not their fault, it was to cause a death, as when a man doing a lawful act without intent of hurt happens to kill another. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: But if a man came presumptuously upon his neighbour to slay him with guile, he was to be taken even from God's altar, that he might die. Henry observes premeditation removed all sanctuary: a wilful murderer would be taken even from God's altar. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: He that smiteth his father or his mother shall be surely put to death. Henry notes children should hear this sentence and remember God will requite it if they have ever lifted hands against their parents, except they repent and flee to the Saviour. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: He that stealeth a man, and selleth him, or if he be found in his hand, shall surely be put to death. Henry notes man-stealing, whose object is to force another into slavery, is ranked in the New Testament with the greatest crimes. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: He that curseth his father or his mother shall surely be put to death. Henry observes the sentence reaches even the heart: cursing parents, even in the heart, stands under God's judgment, and parents must learn to train children by example and prayer, not provoking them to wrath. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: If men strove together, and one smote another with a stone or with his fist, and he died not but kept his bed, the case was not murder but injury. Henry notes care is taken here that satisfaction be made for hurt done to a person, though death do not follow. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: If he rose again and walked abroad upon his staff, then he that smote him would be quit, only he must pay for the loss of his time and cause him to be thoroughly healed. Henry observes the law of restitution: the smiter bore the cost of idleness and cure, so that nobody lost by his violence. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: If a man smote his servant or his maid with a rod, and he died under his hand, he would be surely punished. Henry notes the gospel teaches masters to forbear and moderate threatenings, considering, with Job, what they shall do when God riseth up. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Notwithstanding, if he continued a day or two, he would not be punished, for he is his money. Henry observes the distinction turned on presumption: a death after days did not prove murderous intent as a death under the hand did. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: If men strove and hurt a woman with child, so that her fruit departed, and yet no mischief followed, he would be punished as the woman's husband laid upon him, and pay as the judges determined. Henry notes the unborn life was protected by a fine assessed by judges, not by private revenge. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: And if any mischief followed, then thou shalt give life for life. Henry observes the lex talionis set the measure: punishment must match the harm, neither exceeding it in revenge nor falling short in justice. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot. Henry notes the formula governed judges, not private vengeance, fixing the penalty to the member lost. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: Burning for burning, wound for wound, stripe for stripe. Henry observes the list extends the same measure to every hurt, teaching that we must be very careful to do no wrong, either directly or indirectly. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: If a man smote the eye of his servant or his maid, that it perished, he would let him go free for his eye's sake. Henry notes even a servant's eye had its price: maiming freed the servant, for the body was not the master's to break. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: If he smote out his manservant's tooth or his maidservant's tooth, he would let him go free for his tooth's sake. Henry observes the same rule for the smaller member: the law valued the servant's body member by member. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: If an ox gored a man or a woman, that they died, then the ox would surely be stoned, and his flesh not eaten, but the owner of the ox would be quit. Henry notes the beast was stoned as a sign of abhorrence, and the flesh forbidden, while the owner who had no warning bore no guilt. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: But if the ox were wont to push with his horn in time past, and it had been testified to his owner, and he had not kept him in, but he killed a man or a woman, the ox would be stoned, and his owner also would be put to death. Henry observes known danger changed everything: a warned owner who neglected his beast shared the guilt of its killing. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: If there were laid on him a sum of money, then he would give for the ransom of his life whatsoever was laid upon him. Henry notes the ransom allowed the judges to weigh the case: where death was by negligence rather than malice, money could redeem the owner's life. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: Whether he had gored a son or a daughter, according to this judgment it would be done unto him. Henry observes the law made no distinction of age or sex in the victim: son and daughter stood equal before it. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: If the ox pushed a manservant or a maidservant, he would give unto their master thirty shekels of silver, and the ox would be stoned. Henry notes the fixed price for a servant's death, thirty shekels, the value set upon a servant's life in that age. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: If a man opened a pit, or dug a pit and did not cover it, and an ox or an ass fell therein, the fault was the digger's. Henry observes the law taught care for neighbours' property: an uncovered pit was a trap left in the common way. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: The owner of the pit shall make it good, and give money unto the owner of them; and the dead beast shall be his. Henry notes restitution was complete but not double: the dead beast went to the payer, so that nobody lost by the accident and nobody profited by it. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: If one man's ox hurt another's, that he died, then they would sell the live ox and divide the money, and the dead ox also they would divide. Henry observes where neither owner was at fault, the loss was shared: the living ox sold and the dead divided, that both bore alike. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: Or if it were known that the ox had used to push in time past, and his owner had not kept him in, he would surely pay ox for ox, and the dead would be his own. Henry closes on the rule of the chapter: known fault bears the whole loss, and if we have done wrong, we must be very willing to make it good. — Matthew Henry
