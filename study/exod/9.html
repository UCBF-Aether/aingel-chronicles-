Study Notes — Exodus

Chapter 9

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The LORD told Moses to go in to Pharaoh with the same demand from the LORD God of the Hebrews: Let my people go, that they may serve me. Henry notes the fifth plague was prefaced by the same unchanging claim, so Pharaoh could not say he was unwarned. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: If Pharaoh refused and held Israel still, the judgment would follow. Henry observes the condition was now explicit: continued detention was itself the crime that drew the stroke. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: The hand of the LORD would fall upon Egypt's cattle in the field, horses, asses, camels, oxen, and sheep, with a very grievous murrain. Henry notes the plague struck Egypt's wealth and its work, for cattle were the nation's riches and its gods. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: The LORD would sever between the cattle of Israel and the cattle of Egypt, so that nothing of Israel's would die. Henry observes the distinction was now extended to beasts: God's care marked his people's property as well as their persons. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: The LORD appointed a set time: tomorrow he would do this thing in the land. Henry notes the appointed time removed all excuse of chance, for the plague came on schedule, not by accident. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: The LORD did that thing on the morrow, and all the cattle of Egypt died, but of Israel's cattle there died not one. Henry observes the completeness on both sides: total loss in Egypt, total preservation in Goshen. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh sent and found not one of Israel's cattle dead, yet his heart was hardened and he would not let the people go. Henry notes inquiry confirmed the wonder and still did not soften him: evidence alone does not convert. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: The LORD told Moses and Aaron to take handfuls of ashes of the furnace and sprinkle them toward heaven in Pharaoh's sight. Henry observes the sign came from Egypt's own furnaces, the instruments of Israel's brick-making, now turned to judgment. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: The ashes would become small dust over all Egypt, and a boil breaking forth with blains upon man and beast. Henry notes the plague touched the body itself: after property and comfort, judgment reached the flesh. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: They took the ashes, stood before Pharaoh, and Moses sprinkled them toward heaven, and boils broke out upon man and beast. Henry observes the wonder was wrought in the king's presence, that he might see his own skin bear the sentence. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: The magicians could not stand before Moses because of the boils, for the boil was upon them and upon all Egyptians. Henry notes the final humiliation of the counterfeiters: they who imitated wonders could not endure this one. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: The LORD hardened Pharaoh's heart, and he hearkened not, as the LORD had spoken to Moses. Henry observes the hardening is now stated as God's act, the judicial sealing of a heart that had hardened itself six times over. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Moses was told to rise early and stand before Pharaoh with the demand from the LORD God of the Hebrews. Henry notes the morning summons came again, for Pharaoh was to hear God's claim before every new stroke. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: At this time God would send all his plagues upon Pharaoh's heart, his servants, and his people, that he might know there is none like God in all the earth. Henry observes the coming hail was announced as a plague upon the heart itself, aimed at pride. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: God said he could now stretch out his hand and smite Pharaoh and his people with pestilence, cutting them off from the earth. Henry notes the restraint shown: Egypt was spared utter destruction only that God's power might be displayed. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: For this very cause God had raised Pharaoh up, to show his power in him and declare his name throughout all the earth. Henry observes Pharaoh's greatness was itself God's instrument: the mightier the tyrant, the louder the deliverance. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: As yet exaltest thou thyself against my people, that thou wilt not let them go? Henry notes the charge went to the root: Pharaoh's sin was self-exaltation, setting his throne against God's people. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Tomorrow about this time God would cause a very grievous hail, such as had not been in Egypt since its foundation. Henry observes the warning was precise in time and unmatched in severity, leaving no room for doubt when it fell. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh was told to send and gather his cattle and all he had in the field, for every man and beast left in the field would die. Henry notes mercy mingled with judgment: even now a way of escape was offered to whoever would heed the word. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: He that feared the word of the LORD among Pharaoh's servants made his servants and cattle flee into the houses. Henry observes the first Egyptians who believed: fear of God's word saved them, and their obedience marked them out. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: He that regarded not the word of the LORD left his servants and cattle in the field. Henry notes the division in Egypt was now moral, not only national: those who despised the warning perished by their own contempt. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: Moses was told to stretch his hand toward heaven, that hail might fall on man, beast, herb, and tree throughout Egypt. Henry observes the plague reached from heaven itself, where Egypt's gods were supposed to reign. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: Moses stretched his rod toward heaven, and the LORD sent thunder and hail, and fire ran along upon the ground. Henry notes the storm joined two elements at war with each other, hail and fire together, a wonder no nature could explain. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: There was hail and fire mingled with the hail, very grievous, such as there was none like in Egypt since it became a nation. Henry observes the unprecedented severity matched the unprecedented warning: God had said it would be unmatched, and it was. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: The hail smote throughout Egypt all in the field, both man and beast, and every herb, and broke every tree. Henry notes the ruin was universal in the open country, sparing only what shelter had hidden. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: Only in the land of Goshen, where Israel was, there was no hail. Henry observes the storm drew a line on the map: one province calm while the nation shook, that all might see whose people Israel were. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh sent and called Moses and Aaron, confessing, I have sinned this time; the LORD is righteous, and I and my people are wicked. Henry notes the confession was fuller than any before, naming both sin and God's righteousness. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh asked them to intreat the LORD that there be no more mighty thunderings and hail, promising to let them go and stay no longer. Henry observes the promise was absolute this time, yet made under thunder, and thunder would soon pass. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Moses answered that as soon as he was gone out of the city he would spread abroad his hands, and the thunder would cease and the hail be no more, that Pharaoh might know the earth is the LORD's. Henry notes Moses prayed outside the city, away from idols, and the prayer claimed the whole earth for God. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: But as for thee and thy servants, said Moses, I know ye will not yet fear the LORD God. Henry observes Moses spoke the truth to Pharaoh's face even while interceding for him: the prayer was granted, but the prophet did not flatter. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: The flax and the barley were smitten, for the barley was in the ear and the flax was bolled. Henry notes the detail of the ruin: the early crops perished, a blow at Egypt's bread and linen alike. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: But the wheat and the rie were not smitten, for they were not grown up. Henry observes a double providence: the later crops survived both to feed Egypt and to be smitten later, if Pharaoh persisted. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: Moses went out of the city, spread abroad his hands unto the LORD, and the thunders and hail ceased, and the rain was not poured upon the earth. Henry notes the answer was immediate and complete, matching the prayer exactly. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: When Pharaoh saw the rain, hail, and thunders cease, he sinned yet more, and hardened his heart, he and his servants. Henry observes the saddest line in the chapter: mercy received became the occasion of greater sin. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh's heart was hardened, neither would he let Israel go, as the LORD had spoken by Moses. Henry closes on the fixed pattern: the king who confessed under thunder denied under sunshine. — Matthew Henry
