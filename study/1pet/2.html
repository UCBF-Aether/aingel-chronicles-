Study Notes — 1 Peter

Chapter 2

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Lay aside all malice, all guile, hypocrisies, envies, and evil speakings. Henry notes evil-speaking is a sign of malice and guile in the heart and hinders profiting by the word of God, so the old temper must be put off before the new food can nourish. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: As newborn babes, desire the sincere milk of the word, that you may grow thereby. A new life needs suitable food, and as infants desire milk with their best endeavours, so must a Christian's desire after the word of God be eager and constant. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: If indeed you have tasted that the Lord is gracious, that taste should draw you on to the word. Even the best of God's servants in this life have only a taste of his consolations, yet Christ is very merciful to miserable sinners and has a fullness of grace. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Coming to Christ as to a living stone, disallowed indeed by men but chosen of God and precious, believers find their protection and security. He is called a Stone to teach his servants that he is the foundation on which they are built, precious in the excellence of his nature, the dignity of his office, and the glory of his services. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Believers also, as living stones, are built up a spiritual house, a holy priesthood, to offer spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God by Jesus Christ. All true believers are sacred to God and serviceable to others, endowed with heavenly gifts, yet even their best sacrifices in prayer and praise are acceptable only through Christ. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Scripture says, Behold, I lay in Zion a chief cornerstone, elect and precious, and whoever believes on him shall not be confounded. Christ is the chief cornerstone who unites all believers into one everlasting temple and bears the weight of the whole fabric, chosen for a foundation that is everlasting. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: To believers, therefore, he is precious, but to the disobedient the stone the builders disallowed has become the head of the corner. To be built on Christ means to believe in him, and many deceive themselves by not considering what that is, nor the necessity of partaking of the salvation he wrought. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: To the disobedient he is also a stone of stumbling and a rock of offence, even to those who stumble at the word, being disobedient. The same foundation that secures the believer confounds those who reject it, and though the frame of the world were falling, the person built on this foundation may hear it without fear. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: But believers are a chosen generation, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a peculiar people, to show forth the praises of him who called them out of darkness into his marvellous light. They make one family, distinct from the world in spirit, principle, and practice, which they could never be unless chosen in Christ and sanctified by his Spirit. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: In time past they were not a people, but are now the people of God, having not obtained mercy but now having obtained mercy. Their first state was gross darkness, but they are called into joy, pleasure, and prosperity, and their obligations to him who made them his people and showed them mercy are vast. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Dearly beloved, as strangers and pilgrims, abstain from fleshly lusts which war against the soul. Even the chosen generation and people of God need exhortation against the worst sins, and fleshly lusts are most destructive to the soul, a sore judgment to be given up to them. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Keep your conversation honest among the Gentiles, so that, whereas they speak against you as evildoers, they may by your good works, which they behold, glorify God in the day of visitation. Holy lives may promote the happy change when God calls people to repentance by his word and grace. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Submit to every ordinance of man for the Lord's sake, whether to the king as supreme or to governors sent by him. Regard for relative duties is the will of God and the Christian's duty, and the way to silence the base slanders of ignorant and foolish people. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Governors are sent for the punishment of evildoers and the praise of those who do well, so submission to them belongs to an honest Christian conversation. The apostle treats these duties distinctly, because a Christian life cannot be honest without a just and careful discharge of all relative duties. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: For so is the will of God, that with well doing you may put to silence the ignorance of foolish men. Well-doing, not argument alone, answers slander, and Christians must behave aright in all relations so as not to make liberty a cloak for wickedness or neglect of duty. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: Live as free people, not using liberty for a cloak of maliciousness, but as servants of God. Freedom in Christ is service to God, not licence, and believers must remember in every relation that they are God's servants. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Honour all people, love the brotherhood, fear God, honour the king. These four short commands sum the relative duties: universal respect, special brotherly love, reverent fear of God, and honour to lawful authority. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Servants, be subject to your masters with all fear, not only to the good and gentle but also to the froward. Servants in those days were generally slaves with heathen masters who often used them cruelly, yet the apostle directs subjection to masters placed over them by providence, with a fear of dishonouring God. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: This is thankworthy, if a person for conscience toward God endures grief, suffering wrongfully. The sinful misconduct of one relation does not justify sinful behaviour in the other, and the servant remains bound to duty though the master is froward and perverse. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: What glory is it to take patiently buffeting for your faults, but if, when you do well and suffer for it, you take it patiently, this is acceptable with God? Patience under deserved correction earns no distinction, but patient endurance of ill treatment for well-doing, without peevish complaints or purposes of revenge, is a distinguishing effect of grace. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Even hereunto were you called, because Christ also suffered for us, leaving us an example that we should follow his steps. Christ's death was designed not only as an example of patience under suffering, but as the bearing of our sins, and from it believers draw both example and power for a holy life. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: Christ did no sin, neither was guile found in his mouth. His perfect sinlessness is the pattern: no sin in deed, no deceit in speech, even under the provocation his followers also face. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: When reviled, he reviled not again, and when he suffered, he threatened not, but committed himself to him who judges righteously. Henry's example of patience is precisely this refusal of revenge, leaving judgment to God rather than answering evil with evil. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: He himself bore our sins in his own body on the tree, that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness, and by his stripes we were healed. Christ bore the punishment of our sins and thereby satisfied divine justice, taking them away from us, and the fruits of his sufferings are the death of sin and a new holy life of righteousness. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: You were as sheep going astray, but are now returned to the Shepherd and Bishop of your souls. Before conversion, sinners are always going astray, a continued error away from pasture, Shepherd, and flock, exposing themselves to numberless dangers, and return is the effect of divine grace. — Matthew Henry
