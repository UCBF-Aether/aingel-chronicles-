Study Notes — Jubilees

Chapter 12

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: In the sixth week, in the seventh year thereof, Abram said to Terah his father, saying, 'Father,' and he said, 'Behold, here am I, my son.' The confrontation opens in the formal address of son to father, the setting for Abram's argument against idols. — R. H. Charles

2 COMMENTARY: This verse opens Abram's speech: and he said. The sentence is completed by the idol argument of the following verses. — R. H. Charles

3 COMMENTARY: 'What help and profit have we from those idols which thou dost worship, and before which thou dost bow thyself?' Abram's first question is practical: what help or profit comes from the idols at all? — R. H. Charles

4 COMMENTARY: 'For there is no spirit in them.' The answer begins with the idols' emptiness: no spirit dwells in them, so no help can come from them. — R. H. Charles

5 COMMENTARY: 'For they are dumb forms, and a misleading of the heart.' Dumb forms mislead the heart: speechlessness and deception go together in Abram's charge. — R. H. Charles

6 COMMENTARY: This verse completes the charge: the idols are a misleading of the heart. The heart misled is the seat of the error Abram discerned at fourteen. — R. H. Charles

7 COMMENTARY: 'Worship them not.' The charge becomes a command: the idols are not to be worshipped. — R. H. Charles

8 COMMENTARY: 'Worship the God of heaven.' Against the idols Abram sets the God of heaven, the true object of worship. — R. H. Charles

9 COMMENTARY: The God of heaven causes the rain and the dew to descend on the earth. Rain and dew, the farmer's life, come from God, not from idols. — R. H. Charles

10 COMMENTARY: He does everything upon the earth. God's universal agency replaces the idols' pretended local powers. — R. H. Charles

11 COMMENTARY: He has created everything by his word. Creation by word marks the Creator off from creatures made by hands, the contrast sharpened in verse 14. — R. H. Charles

12 COMMENTARY: 'Why do ye worship things that have no spirit in them?' The question returns with its reason: spiritless things cannot deserve worship. — R. H. Charles

13 COMMENTARY: This verse completes the question: why worship things with no spirit in them? The repetition presses the point home to Terah. — R. H. Charles

14 COMMENTARY: 'For they are the work of men's hands.' Idols are artefacts: made by hands, they cannot be makers or helpers. — R. H. Charles

15 COMMENTARY: 'And on your shoulders do ye bear them, and ye have no help from them.' The worshippers carry their gods, reversing true worship: the borne god cannot bear or help its bearers. — R. H. Charles

16 COMMENTARY: 'But they are a great cause of shame to those who make them.' The makers are shamed by their own work: the idol reflects dishonour back on its maker. — R. H. Charles

17 COMMENTARY: 'And a misleading of the heart to those who worship them: worship them not.' The speech closes where it began: idols mislead the heart, and the command stands, worship them not. — R. H. Charles

18 COMMENTARY: This verse completes the closing command: worship them not. Abram's argument ends in prohibition. — R. H. Charles

19 COMMENTARY: His father said, 'I also know it, my son, but what shall I do with a people who have made me to serve before them?' Terah concedes the truth but pleads compulsion: the people made him serve before the idols. — R. H. Charles

20 COMMENTARY: 'And if I tell them the truth, they will slay me; for their soul cleaves to them to worship them and honour them.' Truth-telling means death, because the people's souls cleave to the idols they worship and honour. — R. H. Charles

21 COMMENTARY: This verse completes Terah's plea: their souls cleave to the idols to worship and honour them. Cleaving is the people's bondage, matching Terah's own servitude. — R. H. Charles

22 COMMENTARY: 'Keep silent, my son, lest they slay thee.' Terah's counsel is silence for safety: speech against idols will get Abram slain as truth would slay Terah. — R. H. Charles

23 COMMENTARY: These words he spoke to his two brothers, and they were angry with him, and he kept silent. Abram carries the argument to his brothers, meets anger, and obeys his father's counsel of silence for a season. — R. H. Charles

24 COMMENTARY: In the fortieth jubilee, in the second week, in the seventh year thereof, Abram took to himself a wife, Sarai, daughter of his father, and she became his wife. Abram's marriage to Sarai, his father's daughter, is dated to the fortieth jubilee. — R. H. Charles

25 COMMENTARY: Haran his brother took to himself a wife in the third year of the third week, and she bare him a son in the seventh year of this week, and he called his name Lot. Haran's son Lot is born, the nephew Abram will later take as his own son. — R. H. Charles

26 COMMENTARY: Nahor his brother took to himself a wife. The brief notice pairs Nahor's marriage with Abram's and Haran's, completing the three brothers' marriages. — R. H. Charles

27 COMMENTARY: In the sixtieth year of Abram's life, that is, in the fourth week, in the fourth year thereof, year 1936 AM, Abram arose by night and burned the house of the idols, and burned all that was in the house, and no man knew it. At sixty Abram moves from speech to act: the idol house burns by night, secretly, fulfilling the silence of verse 23 with fire. — R. H. Charles

28 COMMENTARY: They arose in the night and sought to save their gods from the midst of the fire. The idol-savers rush to rescue gods that cannot save themselves, proving Abram's charge that the idols give no help. — R. H. Charles

29 COMMENTARY: Haran hasted to save them, but the fire flamed over him, and he was burnt in the fire, and he died in Ur of the Chaldees before Terah his father, and they buried him in Ur of the Chaldees. Haran dies saving the idols, burnt in Ur before his father, and is buried there; Genesis likewise records Haran's death before Terah in Ur. — R. H. Charles

30 COMMENTARY: This unit is the heading for the narrative that follows: the family of Terah in Haran, Abram's experiences there, and his journey to Canaan. It divides the Ur idol-burning from the Haran sojourn. — R. H. Charles

31 COMMENTARY: Terah went forth from Ur of the Chaldees, he and his sons, to go into the land of Lebanon and into the land of Canaan, and he dwelt in the land of Haran. Terah's migration aims at Lebanon and Canaan but halts at Haran, as in Genesis. — R. H. Charles

32 COMMENTARY: Abram dwelt with Terah his father in Haran two weeks of years. Fourteen years in Haran follow the departure from Ur, the sojourn in which Abram's night observation and call will come. — R. H. Charles

33 COMMENTARY: In the sixth week, in the fifth year thereof, Abram sat up throughout the night on the new moon of the seventh month to observe the stars from evening to morning, in order to see what would be the character of the year with regard to the rains, and he was alone as he sat and observed. Abram the star-observer keeps a lone night vigil on the seventh month's new moon to read the year's rains from the stars. — R. H. Charles

34 COMMENTARY: A word came into his heart, saying: 'All the signs of the stars, and the signs of the moon and of the sun are all in the hand of the Lord.' The vigil turns: star, moon and sun signs are all in the Lord's hand, not independent omens, against the Watcher astrology of chapter 8 and the Chaldean divination of chapter 11. — R. H. Charles

35 COMMENTARY: 'Why do I search them out?' The rhetorical question rejects the star-search itself: if the signs are in God's hand, searching them apart from him is vain. — R. H. Charles

36 COMMENTARY: 'If he desires, he causes it to rain, morning and evening.' Rain comes at God's desire, morning and evening alike, not by stellar compulsion. — R. H. Charles

37 COMMENTARY: 'And if he desires, he withholds it, and all things are in his hand.' Withholding is equally God's act, and all things rest in his hand: the weather's whole course is divine will. — R. H. Charles

38 COMMENTARY: He prayed that night. The night of observation becomes a night of prayer, introducing the petition that follows. — R. H. Charles

39 COMMENTARY: 'My God, God Most High, thou alone art my God, and thee and thy dominion have I chosen.' Abram chooses God Most High alone and his dominion, the exclusive allegiance his idol argument demanded. — R. H. Charles

40 COMMENTARY: 'And thou hast created all things.' The Creator claim grounds the choice: the God who made all things outranks all made things. — R. H. Charles

41 COMMENTARY: 'And all things that are are the work of thy hands.' All existing things are God's handiwork, the reverse of the idols as the work of men's hands in verse 14. — R. H. Charles

42 COMMENTARY: 'Deliver me from the hands of evil spirits who have sway over the thoughts of men's hearts.' Abram asks deliverance from the evil spirits, Mastema's tenth, who sway the thoughts of men's hearts toward idolatry. — R. H. Charles

43 COMMENTARY: 'And let them not lead me astray from thee, my God.' The plea continues: the spirits must not lead Abram astray from his chosen God. — R. H. Charles

44 COMMENTARY: This verse completes the plea: let them not lead me astray from thee, my God. Deliverance and non-erring are the prayer's double ask. — R. H. Charles

45 COMMENTARY: 'And stablish thou me and my seed forever, that we go not astray from henceforth and forevermore.' The prayer ends by asking perpetual establishment for Abram and his seed, never to go astray again. — R. H. Charles

46 COMMENTARY: This verse completes the establishment plea: that Abram and his seed go not astray from henceforth and forevermore. The seed's future fidelity is prayed for in advance. — R. H. Charles

47 COMMENTARY: 'Shall I return unto Ur of the Chaldees, who seek my face that I may return to them, or am I to remain here in this place?' Abram asks his practical question: return to Ur's seekers or remain in Haran? — R. H. Charles

48 COMMENTARY: 'The right path before thee prosper it in the hands of thy servant that he may fulfil it, and that I may not walk in the deceitfulness of my heart, O my God.' Abram asks God to prosper the right path in his servant's hands and to keep him from his own heart's deceit. — R. H. Charles

49 COMMENTARY: He made an end of speaking and praying, and behold the word of the Lord was sent to him through the angel, saying: 'Get thee up from thy country, and from thy kindred and from the house of thy father unto a land which I will show thee, and I shall make thee a great and numerous nation.' JFB on Genesis 12:1 notes the call included two promises, the land of his future posterity and blessing to all the earth in his posterity, and Abraham obeyed, an instance of faith frequently mentioned in the New Testament. — R. H. Charles; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

50 COMMENTARY: 'And I shall bless thee.' The first blessing of the call: God himself will bless Abram. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

51 COMMENTARY: 'And I shall make thy name great.' The name made great in Chaldea by the ravens in chapter 11 is now promised greatness by God himself. — R. H. Charles

52 COMMENTARY: 'And thou wilt be blessed in the earth.' Abram's blessing extends to his earthly life, not only to his name. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

53 COMMENTARY: 'And in thee will all families of the earth be blessed.' The universal promise: all earth's families are blessed in Abram, the second promise JFB notes in the call. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

54 COMMENTARY: 'And I shall bless them that bless thee, and curse them that curse thee.' Blessing and curse follow men's treatment of Abram, making him the measure of nations. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

55 COMMENTARY: 'And I shall be a God to thee and thy son, and to thy son's son, and to all thy seed: fear not, from henceforth and unto all generations of the earth I am thy God.' The covenant closes the call: God to Abram, son, son's son and all seed, from henceforth to all generations, with fear not. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

56 COMMENTARY: The Lord God said: 'Open his mouth and his ears, that he may hear and speak with his mouth, with the language which hath been revealed,' for it had ceased from the mouths of all the children of men from the day of the overthrow of Babel. The revealed language, lost since Babel's overthrow, is to be opened again in Abram's mouth and ears. — R. H. Charles

57 COMMENTARY: The angel opened his mouth, ears and lips, and began to speak with him in Hebrew, in the tongue of the creation. Hebrew returns as the tongue of creation, the first language taught again by the angel, reversing Babel's confusion for Abram. — R. H. Charles

58 COMMENTARY: This verse opens the books notice: and he took the books of his fathers. The sentence is completed by the transcription and study of the next verse. — R. H. Charles

59 COMMENTARY: He took the books of his fathers, written in Hebrew, and transcribed them, and began from henceforth to study them, and the angel made known to him what he could not understand, and he studied them during the six rainy months. The fathers' Hebrew books are transcribed and studied for six rainy months, with angelic explanation of the hard places, grounding Abram's knowledge in written tradition. — R. H. Charles

60 COMMENTARY: In the seventh year of the sixth week he spoke to his father and informed him that he would leave Haran to go into the land of Canaan to see it and return to him. Abram announces a Canaan reconnaissance, promising to return to his father. — R. H. Charles

61 COMMENTARY: Terah his father said to him: 'Go in peace.' The father's first word is permission: go in peace. — R. H. Charles

62 COMMENTARY: 'May the eternal God make thy path straight.' Terah's blessing asks the eternal God to straighten Abram's path. — R. H. Charles

63 COMMENTARY: 'And the Lord protect thee from all evil.' Protection from all evil is the blessing's second petition. — R. H. Charles

64 COMMENTARY: 'And grant unto thee grace, mercy and favour before those who see thee.' Grace, mercy and favour before onlookers are asked for the traveller. — R. H. Charles

65 COMMENTARY: 'And may none of the children of men have power over thee to harm thee.' No man is to have power to harm Abram, the blessing's guarding petition. — R. H. Charles

66 COMMENTARY: 'Go in peace.' The blessing closes as it opened: go in peace. — R. H. Charles

67 COMMENTARY: 'And if thou seest a land pleasant to thy eyes to dwell in, then arise and take me to thee and take Lot with thee, the son of Haran thy brother, as thine own son: the Lord be with thee.' If Canaan pleases Abram's eyes, he is to fetch Terah and take Lot as his own son, with the Lord's presence wished upon him. — R. H. Charles

68 COMMENTARY: 'And Nahor thy brother leave with me till thou returnest in peace, and we go with thee all together.' Nahor stays with Terah until Abram's peaceful return, when all will go together: the father's plan keeps the family joined across the reconnaissance. — R. H. Charles
