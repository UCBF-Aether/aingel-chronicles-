Study Notes — Jubilees

Chapter 31

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: On the new moon Jacob told his whole household to purify themselves, change garments, go up to Bethel where he had vowed his vow when fleeing Esau, and put away the strange gods among them. Henry stresses that masters of families should use their authority to keep up religion in their families, and that outward cleansing signified purifying and changing the heart. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: The household gave up the strange gods, the ornaments of ears and necks, and the idols Rachel had stolen from Laban, giving them wholly to Jacob. Henry notes that in families where there is a face of religion, yet many times there is much amiss, and more strange gods than one would suppose. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Jacob burnt, broke, and destroyed the idols, hiding them under an oak in the land of Shechem. Henry's rule applies: we must be wholly separated from our sins, as we are from those that are dead and buried out of sight. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Jacob went up on the new moon of the seventh month to Bethel. Henry notes that Bethel had been forgotten, but God reminds those he loves of neglected duties, and that when we have vowed a vow to God, it is best not to defer the payment. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Jacob built an altar at the place where he had slept, set up a pillar, and sent word to his father Isaac and mother Rebecca to come to his sacrifice. Henry observes that the comfort of holy ordinances lies not in Bethel the house of God, but in El-bethel, the God of the house, whom Jacob now worshipped with his family gathered. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Isaac answered that his son Jacob should come, so that he might see him before he died. Henry notes that Isaac lived long after making his will, and that timely setting of heart and house in order is the wise course for an aged father. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Jacob went to his father Isaac and mother Rebecca at the house of his father Abraham, taking two sons, Levi and Judah, with him. The choice of these two sons is the point: Jubilees is preparing the priestly and royal lines, the same two tribes Henry finds singled out in Jacob's later blessing. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: Rebecca came forth from the tower to kiss and embrace Jacob, for her spirit had revived when she heard her son had come. The reunion answers the long separation that began with the flight to Haran, and Henry's account of Isaac's household stresses the agreement and affection that God had restored between its members. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Rebecca saw Jacob's two sons, recognised them, and asked whether these were his sons. The grandmother's question opens her blessing on the next generation, the seed through whom the covenant would continue. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Rebecca embraced and kissed the boys, blessing them that in them the seed of Abraham would become illustrious and prove a blessing on the earth. Henry's reading of the tribal blessings fits: these addresses declare God's purpose respecting the tribes, traced in their histories, not private affection alone. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Jacob went in to Isaac's chamber with his two sons, took his father's hand, kissed him, and Isaac clung to Jacob's neck and wept. The embrace reverses the blind blessing scene: the father who once felt for a son in disguise now holds his true son openly. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: The darkness left Isaac's eyes, and he saw Levi and Judah, asking whether these were Jacob's sons, for they were like him. Henry's account of Isaac's dim eyes in the blessing chapter makes this restoration pointed: the father who could not discern now sees clearly, and the blessing that follows is given with open eyes. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Jacob affirmed that the boys were truly his sons, and that Isaac had truly seen them. The affirmation removes any shadow of the old deception: these are Jacob's sons, presented plainly before their grandfather. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: The boys came near, and Isaac turned, kissed, and embraced them both together. The embrace joins the two lines, priestly and royal, in one grandfather's arms before their separate blessings are spoken. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: The spirit of prophecy came down into Isaac's mouth, and he took Levi by his right hand and Judah by his left. Henry insists that such blessings are the language of the Holy Ghost, declaring God's purpose for the tribes, not the expression of private partiality. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: Isaac turned to Levi first and began to bless him first, praying that the God of all, the Lord of all the ages, would bless him and his children throughout all the ages. Jubilees puts in Isaac's mouth the priestly destiny that Genesis gives to Levi through Jacob, and Henry agrees on the substance: Levi's sentence was turned into a blessing, and the tribe was set apart to God as priests. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Isaac prayed that the Lord would give Levi and his seed greatness and great glory, and cause them to approach him to serve in his sanctuary as the angels of the presence and the holy ones. Henry notes that Levi's tribe, set apart to God as priests, was scattered through the nation in that character, serving before the Lord continually. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: The blessing continued that Levi's seed would serve in the sanctuary as the angels of the presence and the holy ones. The comparison lifts the priesthood to the heavenly pattern: earthly service mirrors the worship of the angels. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Even as the angels, Levi's sons would be for glory, greatness, and holiness, made great unto all the ages. The priesthood's honour is not wealth or land but nearness to God, which Henry calls the acceptable service of the tribe set apart to him. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Levi's sons would be princes, judges, and chiefs of all the seed of Jacob. The priestly tribe would also govern and judge, teaching and ruling the nation it served, a breadth of office Henry sees in Levi's scattering through Israel. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: They would speak the word of the Lord in righteousness, the priest's first office being to declare God's word truly. The blessing makes speech, not sacrifice alone, the centre of Levi's service. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: They would judge all God's judgments in righteousness, deciding cases by his law rather than by favour or bribery. Judgment and teaching go together in the blessing, as they did in the tribe's later office. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: They would declare God's ways to Jacob, carrying the knowledge of his dealings to the whole people. The tribe that knew the law would be the tribe that taught it. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: They would declare God's paths to Israel, completing the pair: his ways to Jacob, his paths to Israel. The two names for the people frame the teaching office as covering the whole nation under both its names. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: The blessing of the Lord would be given in Levi's mouth, to bless all the seed of the beloved. The priest blesses in God's name, and the blessing he speaks is the Lord's own, not his invention. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: The blessing in Levi's mouth is for all the seed of the beloved, the whole chosen people. No tribe is excluded from the priestly blessing, though one tribe is set apart to pronounce it. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: Isaac played on Levi's name, which his mother had justly called him, for he would be joined to the Lord. Henry notes the same name-play in the tribal blessings, where names declare character and destiny. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: Levi would be the companion of all the sons of Jacob, joined to every tribe rather than settled in one territory. Henry explains the scattering of Levi as the form his blessing took: priests spread through the nation, companions to all. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: The Lord's table would be Levi's, the portion of the priest being the offerings of the altar. The table stands for the priest's livelihood from the sanctuary, not from land of his own. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: Levi and his sons would eat of the Lord's table, sharing the holy food of the sanctuary. The eating is fellowship as well as provision: the priest eats where he serves. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: Levi's table would be full unto all generations, and his food would not fail unto all the ages. The promise secures the priesthood's support across time, matching the blessing's extension to all ages. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: The blessing adds that Levi's food would not fail unto all the ages, repeating the assurance for emphasis. Perpetuity is the mark of this blessing: what God gives the priesthood, no age takes away. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: All who hate Levi would fall before him, his adversaries would be rooted out and perish, and the ancient formula would guard him: blessed be he that blesseth thee, and cursed be every nation that curseth thee. The formula first guarded Abraham, and now it guards the priestly tribe. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: The blessing's closing curse and blessing are then gathered up in the next verse's turn to Judah. The formula binds Levi's enemies to the same fate as Abraham's, and his friends to the same blessing. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: Isaac turned to Judah, the second son to be blessed. The turn from Levi to Judah sets the priestly and royal blessings side by side, as Genesis sets them in Jacob's blessing of his sons. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: Isaac prayed that the Lord would give Judah strength and power to tread down all that hate him. Henry describes Judah as a strong and courageous tribe, a lion enjoying the satisfaction of his power without creating vexation to others. — Matthew Henry

37 COMMENTARY: Judah would tread down all that hate him, the royal tribe prevailing over its enemies. Henry notes that Judah's brethren would praise him, for his name signifies praise, and God was praised in him. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: Judah would be a prince, he and one of his sons, over the sons of Jacob. Henry states the substance: Judah should be the royal tribe, the tribe from which Messiah the Prince should come. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: Judah's name and his sons' names would go forth and traverse every land and region. The renown of the royal tribe would spread beyond Israel, as the name of its greatest Son would reach all nations. — Matthew Henry

40 COMMENTARY: The Gentiles would fear before Judah's face, and all nations would quake. The fear of the nations answers the praise of the brethren: Judah's royalty would be felt abroad as well as at home. — Matthew Henry

41 COMMENTARY: The nations would quake before Judah, completing the picture of a royalty acknowledged by the world. Henry sees this fulfilled in the Prince who came from Judah, in whom the earth should be blessed. — Matthew Henry

42 COMMENTARY: In Judah would be the help of Jacob, and in him the salvation of Israel. Henry applies the point directly: much said concerning Judah is to be applied to our Lord Jesus, the Shiloh in whom the earth should be blessed, the peaceable and prosperous Saviour. — Matthew Henry

43 COMMENTARY: In Judah would be found the salvation of Israel, the deliverance the nation waited for. Jacob himself, Henry notes, waited for the salvation of the Lord, the promised Seed to whom the gathering of the people would be. — Matthew Henry

44 COMMENTARY: When Judah sat on the throne of the honour of his righteousness, there would be great peace for all the seed of the sons of the beloved. The throne of righteousness brings peace, the fruit Henry finds in the Prince of Peace from Judah. — Matthew Henry

45 COMMENTARY: The throne's honour is righteousness, and its fruit is peace for the whole beloved seed. The blessing thus joins kingship, justice, and peace in one reign, the pattern of the Messiah's rule. — Matthew Henry

46 COMMENTARY: Great peace would cover all the seed of the sons of the beloved under Judah's righteous throne. Peace is the blessing's end, not conquest alone, and Henry's Shiloh is the peaceable One who brings it. — Matthew Henry

47 COMMENTARY: Blessed would be he that blessed Judah, and all that hated, afflicted, or cursed him would be rooted out, destroyed from the earth, and accursed. The Abrahamic formula closes Judah's blessing as it closed Levi's, guarding the royal tribe as it guarded the priestly one. — Matthew Henry

48 COMMENTARY: Those who hate, afflict, and curse Judah would be rooted out and destroyed from the earth and accursed. The triple description of enmity meets a triple judgment, leaving no form of opposition unanswered. — Matthew Henry

49 COMMENTARY: The curse on Judah's enemies closes with the word accursed, sealing the blessing's protection. The formula's severity matches the blessing's height: the throne it guards is the throne of the promised Prince. — Matthew Henry

50 COMMENTARY: Isaac turned, kissed Judah again, embraced him, and rejoiced greatly, for he had seen Jacob's sons in very truth. The joy closes the prophetic scene: the blind father's restored sight has confirmed what his spirit spoke. — Matthew Henry

51 COMMENTARY: Jacob went forth from between his father's feet, fell down, and worshipped him. The son's reverence answers the father's blessing, honour given where honour was received. — Matthew Henry

52 COMMENTARY: Isaac blessed them, sealing the blessings of Levi and Judah with a final blessing on them all. The seal confirms that the prophetic words were not a father's wishes but a blessing given in God's name. — Matthew Henry

53 COMMENTARY: Jacob rested that night with his father Isaac, and they ate and drank with joy. The shared meal after the blessings is fellowship restored, the household eating together in peace. — Matthew Henry

55 COMMENTARY: During the night Jacob told his father everything: how the Lord had shown him great mercy, prospered all his ways, and protected him from all evil. Henry's account of Jacob's return stresses the same testimony: God had been with him, brought him into the land in peace, and renewed the covenant with him. — Matthew Henry

56 COMMENTARY: Isaac blessed the God of Abraham, who had not withdrawn his mercy and righteousness from the sons of his servant Isaac. The father's praise answers the son's testimony: mercy kept through exile is mercy proved. — Matthew Henry

58 COMMENTARY: In the morning Jacob told Isaac the vow he had vowed, the vision he had seen, and that he had built an altar with everything ready for the sacrifice, and that he had come to set his father on an ass. Henry notes that when we have vowed a vow to God, it is best not to defer the payment, and Jacob now came to pay his Bethel vow in full. — Matthew Henry

59 COMMENTARY: Isaac answered that he could not go with Jacob, for he was old and unable to bear the way, being one hundred and sixty-five years old that day; Jacob should go in peace, and set his mother on an ass instead. Henry notes Isaac's great age and the timely ordering of his house, and the father's release of his son to fulfil the vow without him. — Matthew Henry

60 COMMENTARY: Isaac said he knew Jacob had come on his account, and blessed the day on which he had seen his son alive, and his son had seen him. The mutual sight is itself the blessing: father and son alive together after years of separation. — Matthew Henry

61 COMMENTARY: Isaac charged Jacob to prosper and fulfil the vow he had vowed, not to put it off, for he would be called to account concerning it, and to make haste to perform it. Henry's warning stands behind the charge: better late than never, but a vow deferred is a duty left unpaid, and God will require it. — Matthew Henry

62 COMMENTARY: Isaac told Rebecca to go with Jacob, and Rebecca went with Jacob and Deborah, and they came to Bethel. Deborah, Rebecca's nurse, travels with the household to the place of the vow, the faithful old servant still in the family's company. — Matthew Henry

63 COMMENTARY: Jacob remembered the prayer with which his father had blessed him and his two sons, Levi and Judah, and rejoiced, blessing the God of Abraham and Isaac. The memory of the blessing became itself a cause of praise on the road to Bethel. — Matthew Henry

64 COMMENTARY: Jacob said he now knew he had an eternal hope, and his sons also, before the God of all, and that this was ordained concerning the two, recorded as an eternal testimony on the heavenly tables. Henry's reading of the tribal blessings agrees on the substance: these words declare God's purpose for the tribes, traced through their histories to the end. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 54, 57 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
