Study Notes — Jubilees

Chapter 14

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: After these things, in the fourth year of this week, on the new moon of the third month, the word of the Lord came to Abram in a dream, saying: 'Fear not, Abram; I am thy defender, and thy reward will be exceeding great.' JFB on Genesis 15:1 notes that when the excitement of the conquest was over, Abram had become prey to despondency and terror at probable revenge, and the word, connected with a vision, denotes a prophetic message given to dispel his fear. — R. H. Charles; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: This verse opens Abram's reply: 'Lord, Lord, what wilt thou give me, seeing I go hence childless.' The sentence is completed by the Eliezer complaint of the following verses. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: This verse continues the complaint: the son of Maseq, the son of his handmaid, is the Damascene Eliezer. The heir in view is a household figure, not a son. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: This verse continues the complaint: Eliezer will be his heir. JFB on Genesis 15:3 notes that by nomadic usage the chief confidential servant would be heir to his possessions and honours, but only by adoption, falling sadly short of the parental hopes Abram had been encouraged to entertain. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: 'And to me thou hast given no seed.' The complaint closes: no seed has been given, so the household servant stands as heir by default. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: This verse is the hinge to God's answer: and he said unto him. The reply reverses the complaint in the next verses. — R. H. Charles

7 COMMENTARY: 'This man will not be thy heir.' The servant-heir is refused outright. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: 'But one that will come out of thine own bowels.' The heir will be of Abram's own body, not of his household. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: 'He will be thine heir.' The promise closes: the own-bowels son will be the heir. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: This verse opens the star sign: God brought him forth abroad and said unto him. The sentence is completed by the numbering command. — R. H. Charles

11 COMMENTARY: 'Look toward heaven and number the stars, if thou art able to number them.' The stars are set as the measure of the promised seed. — R. H. Charles

12 COMMENTARY: This verse completes the command: number the stars, if thou art able to number them. The condition already implies innumerability. — R. H. Charles

13 COMMENTARY: This verse completes the star command: if thou art able to number them. Human numbering fails before the stars, as it will before the seed. — R. H. Charles

14 COMMENTARY: He looked toward heaven and beheld the stars. Abram obeys the sign, looking up to behold what he cannot number. — R. H. Charles

15 COMMENTARY: This verse completes the looking: he beheld the stars. The beholding prepares the promise of the next verses. — R. H. Charles

16 COMMENTARY: God said unto him. The promise follows the beholding. — R. H. Charles

17 COMMENTARY: 'So shall thy seed be.' Seed like stars: innumerable as the host Abram just beheld. — R. H. Charles

18 COMMENTARY: He believed in the Lord. Abram's belief answers the star promise with trust. — R. H. Charles

19 COMMENTARY: It was counted to him for righteousness. Belief itself is reckoned as righteousness, the chapter's centre: Abram is righteous by trusting the promise before any son is born. — R. H. Charles

20 COMMENTARY: God said unto him: 'I am the Lord that brought thee out of Ur of the Chaldees, to give thee the land of the Canaanites to possess it forever.' The self-identification recalls the exodus from Ur and joins it to the perpetual land gift. — R. H. Charles

21 COMMENTARY: This verse continues the identification: the Lord brought Abram out of Ur to give him the Canaanites' land forever. — R. H. Charles

22 COMMENTARY: This verse continues the land promise: the land is to be possessed forever. — R. H. Charles

23 COMMENTARY: 'And I shall be God unto thee and to thy seed after thee.' The land promise is sealed by a God-promise extending to Abram and his seed after him. — R. H. Charles

24 COMMENTARY: This verse completes the God-promise: God unto Abram and his seed after him. The covenant's personal bond frames the land gift. — R. H. Charles

25 COMMENTARY: He said: 'Lord, Lord, whereby shall I know that I shall inherit it?' JFB on Genesis 15:8 notes Abram, having renewed his address in a spirit of more becoming submission, asked whereby he would know he would inherit it, and was delighted by a most explicit promise of Canaan, immediately confirmed by a remarkable ceremony. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: This verse completes the question: whereby shall I know that I shall inherit it? The sign Abram asks for is answered by the sacrifice of the next verses. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: This verse opens the answer: and he said unto him. The sacrifice command follows. — R. H. Charles

28 COMMENTARY: This verse opens the command: take me an heifer. The list is completed by the goat, sheep and birds of the next verses. — R. H. Charles

29 COMMENTARY: 'Take me an heifer of three years, and a goat of three years, and a sheep of three years, and a turtle-dove, and a pigeon.' The fivefold offering is specified: three three-year-old animals and two birds. — R. H. Charles

30 COMMENTARY: This verse completes the list: a turtle-dove and a pigeon. Birds close the offering, and they alone will remain undivided. — R. H. Charles

31 COMMENTARY: This verse completes the offering list: a turtle-dove and a pigeon. The five kinds are now all commanded. — R. H. Charles

32 COMMENTARY: He took all these in the middle of the month. The taking is dated to mid-month, the book's precise reckoning for covenant acts. — R. H. Charles

33 COMMENTARY: This verse continues the preparation: he dwelt at the oak of Mamre, which is near Hebron. The covenant place is Mamre's oak near Hebron. — R. H. Charles

34 COMMENTARY: This verse completes the place notice: the oak of Mamre near Hebron. Hebron frames the covenant as Shechem framed the first altar. — R. H. Charles

35 COMMENTARY: This verse opens the sacrifice: he built there an altar and sacrificed all these. The sentence is completed by the blood and division of the next verses. — R. H. Charles

36 COMMENTARY: This verse continues the sacrifice: he poured their blood upon the altar. Blood on the altar precedes the division of the pieces. — R. H. Charles

37 COMMENTARY: He divided them in the midst and laid them over against each other, but the birds divided he not. JFB on Genesis 15:9-21 notes that on great occasions when parties joined a compact they observed precisely these rites, and the Lord condescended to enter into covenant with Abram, while the patriarch did not pass between the sacrifice, because in this transaction he was bound to nothing, whereas God gave a sign binding himself. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

38 COMMENTARY: This verse continues the division: the pieces were laid over against each other. The divided halves face each other, awaiting the passing sign. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

39 COMMENTARY: This verse completes the division notice: the birds he divided not. Birds alone stay whole, as in the offering list's distinction. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

40 COMMENTARY: Birds came down upon the pieces. The covenant pieces are threatened from above, as Abram's seed was threatened by ravens in chapter 11. — R. H. Charles

41 COMMENTARY: Abram drove them away and did not suffer the birds to touch them. Abram guards the pieces as he once guarded the seed, driving the birds away untouched. — R. H. Charles

42 COMMENTARY: This verse completes the guarding: Abram did not suffer the birds to touch the pieces. The covenant sacrifice is kept intact until sunset. — R. H. Charles

43 COMMENTARY: When the sun had set, an ecstasy fell upon Abram, and lo, an horror of great darkness fell upon him. Sunset brings ecstasy and horror of great darkness, the dread that precedes the prophecy. — R. H. Charles

44 COMMENTARY: This verse continues the sunset dread: an horror of great darkness fell upon him. The darkness is the veil for the word that follows. — R. H. Charles

45 COMMENTARY: This verse opens the prophecy: it was said unto Abram. The sentence is completed by the surety of strangerhood and bondage. — R. H. Charles

46 COMMENTARY: 'Know of a surety that thy seed shall be a stranger in a land that is not theirs.' The prophecy begins with sure knowledge: strangerhood in a foreign land. — R. H. Charles

47 COMMENTARY: This verse continues the prophecy: they will bring them into bondage and afflict them. Strangerhood becomes bondage and affliction. — R. H. Charles

48 COMMENTARY: This verse gives the bondage its length: four hundred years. The affliction is measured, not endless. — R. H. Charles

49 COMMENTARY: This verse completes the bondage sentence: affliction for four hundred years. The measured years bound the darkness of the prophecy. — R. H. Charles

50 COMMENTARY: 'And the nation also to whom they will be in bondage shall I judge, and after that they will come forth thence with much substance.' The oppressor nation will be judged, and the seed will come out with much substance, as Abram came out of Egypt enriched. — R. H. Charles

51 COMMENTARY: 'And thou wilt go to thy fathers in peace, and be buried in a good old age.' Abram's own end is peaceful: to his fathers in peace, buried in a good old age, while his seed's bondage still lies ahead. — R. H. Charles

52 COMMENTARY: 'But in the fourth generation they will return hither; for the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet full.' Return waits on the fourth generation, when Amorite iniquity reaches its fullness: judgment on Canaan is timed by iniquity, not by haste. — R. H. Charles

53 COMMENTARY: He awoke from his sleep, and he arose, and the sun had set, and there was a flame, and behold, a furnace was smoking, and a flame of fire passed between the pieces. The smoking furnace and flame of fire pass between the pieces, the divine sign passing where Abram did not, binding God himself to the covenant. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

54 COMMENTARY: On that day the Lord made a covenant with Abram, saying: 'To thy seed will I give this land, from the river of Egypt unto the great river, the river Euphrates, the Kenites, the Kenizzites, the Kadmonites, the Perizzites, and the Rephaim, the Phakorites and the Hivites and the Amorites, and the Canaanites, and the Girgashites, and the Jebusites.' The covenant's land runs from Egypt's river to the Euphrates, over ten peoples named in full. — R. H. Charles

55 COMMENTARY: The day passed, and Abram offered the pieces, and the birds, and their fruit offerings, and their drink offerings, and the fire devoured them. After the covenant, the pieces, birds, fruit offerings and drink offerings are offered and devoured by fire. — R. H. Charles

56 COMMENTARY: On that day the angels made a covenant with Abram, according as they had covenanted with Noah in this month, and Abram renewed the festival and ordinance for himself forever. The covenant falls in the third month, as Noah's did, and Abram renews the festival and ordinance forever: the feast of weeks renewed in Abram's covenant. — R. H. Charles

57 COMMENTARY: This unit is the heading for the narrative that follows: the birth of Ishmael. It divides the covenant dream from the Hagar story. — R. H. Charles

58 COMMENTARY: Abram rejoiced and made all these things known to Sarai his wife, and he believed that he would have seed, but she did not bear. Joy and belief meet barrenness: Abram believes he will have seed, yet Sarai bears none. — R. H. Charles

59 COMMENTARY: Sarai advised her husband Abram, saying: 'Go in unto Hagar, my Egyptian maid: it may be that I shall build up seed unto thee by her.' Sarai's plan builds seed through her Egyptian maid, the human device beside the star promise. — R. H. Charles

60 COMMENTARY: Abram hearkened unto the voice of Sarai his wife, and said unto her, 'Do so.' Abram agrees to Sarai's voice, as Adam once hearkened to his wife's voice in chapter 3. — R. H. Charles

61 COMMENTARY: Sarai took Hagar, her maid, the Egyptian, and gave her to Abram her husband to be his wife. JFB on Genesis 16:1 notes the handmaid was a female slave, one of those obtained in Egypt; Sarai gives her to Abram as wife. — R. H. Charles; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

62 COMMENTARY: He went in unto her, and she conceived and bare him a son, and he called his name Ishmael, in the fifth year of this week, and this was the eighty-sixth year in the life of Abram. Ishmael is born when Abram is eighty-six, dated to the fifth year of the week. — R. H. Charles
