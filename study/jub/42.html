Study Notes — Jubilees

Chapter 42

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: In the first year of the third week of the forty-fifth jubilee the famine began in the land, and the rain was refused to the earth, for none whatever fell. Henry notes that Egypt's plenty depended on the Nile's overflow, while Canaan depended on rain, and that providence dispenses its gifts in many ways, yet our dependence on the First Cause is the same. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: The earth grew barren, but in Egypt there was food, for Joseph had gathered the seed of the land in the seven years of plenty and preserved it. Henry stresses the goodness of God in sending plenty before famine, that provision might be made. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: The Egyptians came to Joseph for food, and he opened the storehouses of the first year's grain and sold it to the people for gold. Henry notes that Joseph was diligent in laying up while plenty lasted, and prudent in giving out when famine came. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: The famine was very sore in Canaan, and Jacob heard that there was food in Egypt. Henry observes that Jacob saw the corn his neighbours had bought in Egypt, and that seeing where help is to be had should spur us to apply for it without delay. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Jacob heard there was food in Egypt and sent his ten sons to procure food, but Benjamin he did not send, and the ten arrived among those who went there. The sending of ten and the keeping of one is the father's fear after Joseph's loss, the same fear Henry finds in Jacob's refusal to trust his sons. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Joseph recognised his brothers, but they did not recognise him, and he spoke to them, questioned them, and charged them with being spies come to explore the land, and put them in ward. Henry explains that Joseph was hard upon them not from revenge but to bring them to repentance, and that God in providence sometimes seems harsh with those he loves. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: After that Joseph set them free again, detained Simeon alone, and sent off his nine brothers. Henry notes that Joseph settled at last that one should be left and the rest go home and fetch Benjamin, adding the encouraging word, I fear God, as assurance he would do them no wrong. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: Joseph filled their sacks with corn and put their gold in their sacks, and they did not know. Henry notes that the brethren came for corn and corn they had, and not only so, but every man had his money given back, as Christ gives supplies without money and without price. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Joseph commanded them to bring their younger brother, for they had told him their father was living and their younger brother. The demand for Benjamin was the test: Joseph, not seeing him, suspected they had made away with him as they had with Joseph. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: The brothers went up from Egypt to Canaan and told their father all that had befallen them, how the lord of the country had spoken roughly to them and seized Simeon until they should bring Benjamin. Henry notes that the report troubled the good man, and that even the returned money frightened him. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Jacob cried that his sons had bereaved him of his children. The cry gathers Joseph, Simeon, and Benjamin into one loss, the father's grief speaking before his reason. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Jacob said Joseph was not, and Simeon also was not, and they would take Benjamin away. Henry observes that Jacob gave up Joseph for gone, and Simeon and Benjamin as in danger, concluding that all these things were against him, though they proved to be for him. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Jacob charged his sons that their wickedness had come upon him. The charge looks back to the sons' conduct: the father distrusted them, remembering he had never seen Joseph since he had been with them. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Jacob said his son would not go down with them, lest he fall sick, for their mother bore two sons, one had perished, and this one also they would take from him. The two sons of Rachel are the father's whole remaining treasure, and the loss of one makes the other doubly precious. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Jacob feared that if Benjamin took a fever on the road, they would bring down his old age with sorrow to death. The fear is the same grief Henry traces through the chapter: great affection prepares for great affliction when its object is taken away. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: Jacob saw that their money had been returned to every man in his sack, and for this reason he feared to send Benjamin. Henry notes that guilty consciences take good providences in a bad sense, and that Jacob, knowing his sons, feared they had wrongfully brought home the money. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: The famine increased and became sore in Canaan and in all lands save Egypt, for many Egyptians had stored up their seed for food when they saw Joseph gathering and preserving it in storehouses. The wisdom of Joseph's storing taught even the Egyptians, and the stored seed became the difference between life and death. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: The Egyptians had stored their seed from the time they saw Joseph gathering, and their foresight is recorded as the fruit of his example. Providence used one man's prudence to teach a nation. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: The storing continued through the years of famine, the seed preserved in storehouses against the years without harvest. The sentence closes the account of Egypt's preparation, set against Canaan's growing need. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: The people of Egypt fed themselves from the stored seed during the first year of their famine. The first year of famine was met from the first year's storing, the plenty of the past feeding the want of the present. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: The Egyptians fed themselves thereon, the stored food sustaining the nation while the famine deepened elsewhere. The contrast with Canaan sharpens: Egypt eats, Canaan starves, and the road to Egypt becomes the only road. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: The feeding continued through the first year of the famine, the storehouses holding against the dearth. The first year is named because the famine would last seven, and the stores must stretch across them all. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: When Israel saw the famine was very sore in the land and there was no deliverance, he told his sons to go again and procure food so that they would not die. Henry notes that Jacob urged his sons to buy a little food, for in dearth a little must suffice, and that necessity at last yielded to the demand for Benjamin. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Israel's command is completed: go again and procure food for us, that we die not. The command is stark, hunger against fear, and the sons' answer will force the father's choice. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: The command closes with its reason: that the household might not die. Bread, not dainties, was the need, and Henry notes that we may live well enough upon plain food, but cannot live upon dainties without plain food. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: The sons answered that they would not go unless their youngest brother went with them. The condition was the lord of Egypt's own: without Benjamin they would not see his face, and the journey would be useless. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: The sons' refusal is completed: unless the youngest brother went with them, they would not go. The condition stood between the household and its bread, and Jacob had to choose. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: The refusal is stated once more, that without Benjamin there would be no journey. The repetition presses the father: the food is in Egypt, and Benjamin is the price of reaching it. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Israel saw that if he did not send Benjamin with them, they would all perish by reason of the famine. Henry notes that Jacob saw the necessity of the case and yielded, his prudence appearing in sending back the returned money, sending double money, and sending a present. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: Israel saw the whole household would perish by famine if Benjamin stayed. The choice was no longer between risks but between certain death at home and possible danger abroad. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: The perception is completed: without Benjamin they would perish by famine. Necessity, not trust, moved the father at last, and Henry notes that children may humbly advise and reason with parents when need requires. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: The famine is named again as the reason: they would perish by reason of the famine, not by any other cause. Hunger was the argument that overcame fear. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: Reuben said to give Benjamin into his hand, and if he did not bring him back, Jacob might slay his two sons instead of Benjamin's soul. The pledge offers the pledgor's own sons as surety, a desperate bargain from the first-born who had once saved Joseph. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: Reuben's pledge is completed: if he failed to bring Benjamin back, his two sons would die instead. The offer shows Reuben's earnestness, though Jacob would not accept it. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: The pledge closes with its sanction: slay my two sons instead of his soul. The life-for-life bargain is the measure of Reuben's determination to restore his standing with his father. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: Jacob answered Reuben that Benjamin would not go with him. The refusal of Reuben's pledge is the father's distrust, the same distrust Henry finds throughout the chapter: Jacob plainly distrusted his sons. — Matthew Henry

37 COMMENTARY: Judah came near and said to send Benjamin with him, and if he did not bring him back, he would bear the blame before his father all his days. Henry notes that Judah urged Benjamin should go, and that it is not against children's duty humbly to advise their parents when need requires. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: Judah's offer is completed: send Benjamin with me, and if I fail, let me bear the blame for ever. The surety of blame, not of sons' lives, is what Jacob accepts, and Judah's pledge will be tested at the cup. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: The surety is stated once more: Judah would bear the blame before his father all his days if Benjamin did not return. The lifelong blame is a heavier bond than a single payment, and it holds Judah to his word. — Matthew Henry

40 COMMENTARY: Jacob sent Benjamin with them in the second year of this week, on the first day of the month, and they came to Egypt with all who went, carrying presents of stacte, almonds, terebinth nuts, and pure honey. Henry notes Jacob's prudence: he sent a present of such things as the land afforded and were scarce in Egypt, though honey and spice could never make up the want of bread-corn. — Matthew Henry

41 COMMENTARY: The sending is dated to the second year of the week, on the first day of the month, and the company came to Egypt with presents in hand. The date marks the second journey as precisely as the first was marked by the famine's beginning. — Matthew Henry

42 COMMENTARY: They came to Egypt with all who went, and the presents were in their hands. The company is complete this time: eleven brothers, with Benjamin among them, and the gifts for the lord of the land. — Matthew Henry

43 COMMENTARY: The presents are named: stacte, almonds, terebinth nuts, and pure honey, the best fruits of a famine land. Henry notes that providence dispenses its gifts not alike to all, and that in famine men willingly barter gold and luxuries for bread. — Matthew Henry

44 COMMENTARY: The presents are completed with pure honey, the sweetest of the land's gifts, offered to the lord of Egypt. The gift was meant to soften the ruler who had spoken roughly before. — Matthew Henry

45 COMMENTARY: They went and stood before Joseph, and he saw Benjamin his brother. The standing before the lord of the land is the moment the first journey had prepared: the brothers face the brother they sold, still unrecognised. — Matthew Henry

46 COMMENTARY: Joseph saw Benjamin his brother and knew him. The recognition is one-sided, as it was on the first journey, and Joseph's question will test whether the brothers have changed. — Matthew Henry

47 COMMENTARY: Joseph knew Benjamin and asked whether this was their youngest brother. Henry notes Joseph's special regard for Benjamin, shown to try whether his brethren would envy him, as they had once envied Joseph. — Matthew Henry

48 COMMENTARY: The question is pressed: is this your youngest brother? The brothers must own Benjamin before the ruler, the brother whose loss their father feared and whose presence was the condition of their coming. — Matthew Henry

49 COMMENTARY: The brothers answered that it was he, owning Benjamin as their youngest brother. The answer satisfies the condition and opens the feast that follows. — Matthew Henry

50 COMMENTARY: The answer is completed: it is he, the youngest brother, standing before the lord of Egypt. The identification is simple, but it carries the whole weight of Jacob's surrender. — Matthew Henry

51 COMMENTARY: Joseph said the Lord be gracious to thee, my son, blessing Benjamin in the hearing of his brothers. The blessing is Joseph's first kind word to his own brother, spoken while still a stranger to him. — Matthew Henry

52 COMMENTARY: The blessing on Benjamin is completed: the Lord be gracious to thee, my son. Grace, not favour earned, is the word Joseph speaks over the brother he has not seen since childhood. — Matthew Henry

53 COMMENTARY: Joseph sent Benjamin into his house, brought forth Simeon to them, made a feast for them, and they presented the gift they had brought. Henry notes that Joseph showed great kindness to them and treated them nobly, and that in famine it is enough to be fed, but they were feasted. — Matthew Henry

54 COMMENTARY: The feast is prepared: Simeon is restored, the house is opened, and the gift is presented. Henry notes that their cares and fears were now over, and they ate their bread with joy, reckoning themselves on good terms with the lord of the land. — Matthew Henry

55 COMMENTARY: Simeon was brought forth to his brothers, the hostage of the first journey released at last. The restoration of Simeon is the first proof that the second journey has changed their standing. — Matthew Henry

56 COMMENTARY: The feast was made for them, and they presented the gift they had brought in their hands. The present, offered with the brothers' own hands, completes the reconciliation's outward form. — Matthew Henry

57 COMMENTARY: They ate before Joseph, and he gave them all a portion, but Benjamin's portion was seven times larger than any of theirs. Henry notes that Joseph showed special regard for Benjamin to try whether his brethren would envy him, and that it must be our rule to be content with what we have and not grieve at what others have. — Matthew Henry

58 COMMENTARY: The portions are described: all received a portion, but Benjamin's was seven times larger. The sevenfold portion is the visible test of envy, the same envy that once sold Joseph. — Matthew Henry

59 COMMENTARY: Benjamin's portion, seven times larger than any brother's, is named again as the mark of Joseph's special regard. The brothers' reaction, or lack of it, is the evidence Joseph seeks. — Matthew Henry

60 COMMENTARY: They ate and drank and arose and remained with their asses. The feast ended in peace, and the brothers stayed with their animals, unaware that the cup was already in Benjamin's sack. — Matthew Henry

61 COMMENTARY: The feasting is completed: they ate and drank and arose, and remained with their asses, the company at rest before the final test. The calm before the pursuit is part of Joseph's design. — Matthew Henry

62 COMMENTARY: Joseph devised a plan to learn their thoughts, whether thoughts of peace prevailed among them. Henry's reading of Joseph's hardness applies: he was hard upon them not from revenge but to bring them to repentance, and the cup would prove whether repentance had come. — Matthew Henry

63 COMMENTARY: The plan's purpose is stated: to learn whether thoughts of peace prevailed among the brothers. Peace among themselves, not merely toward Joseph, is what the test must reveal. — Matthew Henry

64 COMMENTARY: The purpose is completed: Joseph would learn whether peace prevailed among them, the inward state that no feast or portion could prove. Only a crisis over Benjamin would show it. — Matthew Henry

65 COMMENTARY: Joseph told the steward over his house to fill all their sacks with food and return their money into their vessels. The generosity repeats the first journey's gift, so that the brothers leave laden, not wanting. — Matthew Henry

66 COMMENTARY: The steward was told to fill the sacks with food and return the money into their vessels. The returned money, which had frightened Jacob before, is given again, now as part of the test. — Matthew Henry

67 COMMENTARY: The steward was told to put Joseph's silver cup, the cup out of which he drank, into the sack of the youngest, and send them away. The cup in Benjamin's sack is the trap: the youngest, the father's favourite, will seem to have stolen from the lord who feasted him. — Matthew Henry

68 COMMENTARY: The cup is named as the silver cup out of which Joseph drank, the lord's own drinking vessel. Its value and its personal use make the charge of theft grave, and the test severe. — Matthew Henry
