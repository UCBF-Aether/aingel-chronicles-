Study Notes — Jubilees

Chapter 39

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Jacob dwelt in the land of his father's sojournings in Canaan. The verse sets Joseph's story in the land of promise, where his father still sojourned rather than possessed, and Henry notes that Joseph's history displays the singular providence of God making use of good and bad workings alike. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: These are the generations of Jacob, the heading of the Joseph history. Henry observes that in Joseph's history we see something of Christ, first humbled and then exalted, and the lot of Christians who through many tribulations enter into the kingdom. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Joseph was seventeen years old when they took him down into Egypt, and Potiphar, Pharaoh's eunuch, bought him. Henry notes that Joseph's enemies could strip him of outward distinctions and separate him from friends and country, but could not take from him the presence of the Lord. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Potiphar, the chief cook, bought Joseph, and the purchase made him a slave in a foreign house. The low beginning is the point: God's presence, not rank, would make Joseph prosper. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Potiphar set Joseph over all his house, and the blessing of the Lord came upon the Egyptian's house on Joseph's account, prospering him in all he did. Henry notes that good men are the blessings of the place where they live, and that a wicked family was blessed for the sake of one good servant in it. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: The Egyptian committed everything into Joseph's hands, for he saw the Lord was with him and prospered all he did. Henry stresses that God's presence with us makes all we do prosperous, and that wisdom and grace cannot be taken from us even in slavery. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Joseph's appearance was comely and very beautiful, and his master's wife lifted up her eyes, saw him, loved him, and besought him to lie with her. Henry warns that beauty often proves a snare to themselves and others, forbidding pride in it and requiring constant watchfulness. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: Joseph did not surrender his soul, but remembered the Lord and the words his father used to read from Abraham, that no man should commit fornication with another man's wife, for death has been ordained in heaven for it, and the sin is recorded in the eternal books before the Lord. Henry gives Joseph's own three arguments: who he was, a man in covenant with God; what the sin was, great wickedness, not a small matter; and against whom, God himself. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Joseph remembered these words and refused to lie with her. The remembrance was his defence: the law read in his father's house stood between him and the temptation in a foreign house. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: She besought him for a year, but he refused and would not listen. Henry notes the frequent, constant urging, and that the tempter was his mistress, whose favour would help him forward, while slighting her made her his enemy at his utmost peril. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: She embraced him and held him fast in the house to force him, closing the doors, but he left his garment in her hands, broke through the door, and fled from her presence. Henry praises the flight: Joseph would not stay to parley with temptation, but fled from it as escaping for his life, and his escape was as great an instance of divine power as deliverance from the fiery furnace. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: The woman saw Joseph would not lie with her and calumniated him before his lord, saying the Hebrew servant had sought to force her, and that when she lifted her voice he fled, leaving his garment and breaking through the door. Henry notes that having tried in vain to make him guilty, she endeavoured to be avenged on him, and that it is no new thing for the best of men to be falsely accused of the worst crimes by the worst of criminals. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: The Egyptian saw Joseph's garment and the broken door, heard his wife's words, and cast Joseph into prison where the king's prisoners were kept. Henry notes that Potiphar likely chose the worst prison, but God designed it to open the way to Joseph's honour. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Joseph was in the prison, and the Lord gave him favour and compassion in the sight of the chief of the prison guards, who saw the Lord was with him and made all he did prosper. Henry observes that those with a good conscience in a prison have a good God there, and that a good man will do good wherever he is, even in bonds. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: The favour Joseph found with the prison chief is the same favour he found with Potiphar: the Lord was with him, and men saw it. Henry notes that Joseph was owned and righted by his God, away from all friends and relations, with none to help or comfort him but the Lord. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: The prison chief committed all things into Joseph's hands and knew nothing of what was with him, for Joseph did everything and the Lord perfected it. The pattern repeats from Potiphar's house: trust earned by faithfulness, and prosperity given by God. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Joseph remained in prison two years, the long waiting between the false charge and the dreams that would free him. The two years are the hidden part of the providence Henry traces through the whole Joseph history. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: In those days Pharaoh was wroth against his two eunuchs, the chief butler and the chief baker, and put them in ward in the house of the chief cook, in the prison where Joseph was kept. Henry notes that it was not so much the prison that saddened them as their dreams, and that Joseph had compassion on their sadness. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: The chief of the prison guards appointed Joseph to serve the butler and baker, and he served before them. The service placed Joseph where their dreams would be told to him, the next link in the chain toward Pharaoh's court. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Both men dreamed a dream, the butler and the baker, and they told their dreams to Joseph. Henry notes that Joseph was careful to ascribe the glory to God, and that the butler's dream foretold advancement while the baker's foretold death. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: As Joseph interpreted to them, so it befell them: Pharaoh restored the chief butler to his office and slew the chief baker, as Joseph had interpreted. Henry observes that ministers are but interpreters who cannot make the thing otherwise than it is, and that Joseph's interpretations came to pass on the very day fixed. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: The chief butler forgot Joseph in the prison, although Joseph had told him what would befall him, and did not remember to inform Pharaoh. Henry warns that those at ease are apt to forget others in distress, and that Joseph learned by disappointment to trust in God only, since we cannot expect too little from man nor too much from God. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 24 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
