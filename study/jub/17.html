Study Notes — Jubilees

Chapter 17

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: In the first year of the fifth week Isaac was weaned in this jubilee, and Abraham made a great banquet in the third month, on the day his son Isaac was weaned. JFB on Genesis 21:8 notes children are suckled longer in the East, boys usually for two or three years, and weaning is always a season of domestic festivity, the newly weaned child being formally brought before assembled relatives and friends, Isaac attired in the symbolic robe, the badge of birthright, admitted heir of the tribe. — R. H. Charles; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Ishmael, son of Hagar the Egyptian, was before the face of Abraham his father in his place, and Abraham rejoiced and blessed God because he had seen his sons and had not died childless. Both sons are present at the banquet, and Abraham's joy answers his old complaint of going childless in chapter 14. — R. H. Charles

3 COMMENTARY: He remembered the words spoken to him on the day Lot had parted from him, and rejoiced because the Lord had given him seed upon the earth to inherit the earth, and blessed with all his mouth the Creator of all things. And Sarah saw Ishmael playing and dancing and Abraham rejoicing with great joy, and she became jealous of Ishmael and said to Abraham: 'Cast out this bondwoman and her son; for the son of this bondwoman will not be heir with my son, Isaac.' JFB on Genesis 21:9 notes Ishmael, aware of the great change in his prospects, under irritated and resentful feelings, joined probably by his mother, treated the young heir with derision and probably some violence; Sarah's demand follows the play and the joy, with heirship as the stated reason. — R. H. Charles; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The thing was grievous in Abraham's sight, because of his maidservant and because of his son, that he should drive them from him. Expulsion grieves Abraham doubly: for the maidservant and for the son. — R. H. Charles

5 COMMENTARY: God said to Abraham: 'Let it not be grievous in thy sight, because of the child and because of the bondwoman; in all that Sarah hath said unto thee, hearken to her words and do them; for in Isaac shall thy name and seed be called.' JFB on Genesis 21:10, 12 notes nothing but the expulsion of both could now preserve harmony in the household, and Abraham's perplexity was relieved by an announcement of the divine will, which all who fear God will promptly obey, however painful to flesh and blood. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: 'But as for the son of this bondwoman I will make him a great nation, because he is of thy seed.' JFB on Genesis 21:13 notes Providence overruled a family brawl to give rise to two great and extraordinary peoples; Ishmael's nationhood rests on Abraham's seed, not on the covenant. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: This verse opens the expulsion: Abraham rose up early in the morning. The sentence is completed by the bread and water of the next verse. — R. H. Charles

8 COMMENTARY: He took bread and a bottle of water, and placed them on the shoulders of Hagar and the child, and sent her away. JFB on Genesis 21:14 notes the early rising, that the wanderers might reach an asylum before noon; bread includes all sorts of victuals, and the bottle is a leathern vessel of a whole lamb or kid skin, carried over the shoulder, while Ishmael, a lad of seventeen, was sent out at the age when Arab chiefs customarily send sons to do for themselves with a few days' provisions. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: She departed and wandered in the wilderness of Beersheba, and the water in the bottle was spent, and the child thirsted, and was not able to go on, and fell down. JFB on Genesis 21:14-15 notes the wandering in the southern border's wide desert, out of the common direction, where they lost their way, and Ishmael sank exhausted from fatigue and thirst. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: His mother took him and cast him under an olive tree and went and sat down over against him, at the distance of a bow-shot, saying, 'Let me not see the death of my child,' and as she sat she wept. JFB on Genesis 21:15 notes the mother laid his head under a bush to smell the damp, sitting a little distance away in hopeless sorrow, unable to witness his distress. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: An angel of God, one of the holy ones, said unto her: 'Why weepest thou, Hagar?' The angel's question opens the deliverance, addressing Hagar's weeping directly. — R. H. Charles

12 COMMENTARY: 'Arise, take the child, and hold him in thine hand; for God hath heard thy voice, and hath seen the child.' God has heard Hagar's voice and seen the child, fulfilling Ishmael's name, 'God hears,' as JFB notes on Genesis 16:11. — R. H. Charles; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: She opened her eyes and saw a well of water, and went and filled her bottle with water, and gave her child to drink, and arose and went towards the wilderness of Paran. JFB on Genesis 21:19 notes God opened her eyes, and whether she looked to God or not, he regarded her and directed her to a fountain close beside her, probably hidden amid brushwood, by whose waters her almost expiring son was revived. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: The child grew and became an archer, and God was with him, and his mother took him a wife from among the daughters of Egypt. JFB on Genesis 21:20-21 notes God was with the lad in Paran, Arabia, where his posterity ever dwelt, and his mother, on his virtual deprivation of his father, arranged his marriage among her own relatives in Egypt. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: She bare him a son, and he called his name Nebaioth, for she said: 'The Lord was nigh to me when I called upon him.' Nebaioth's name is explained by his mother's confession: the Lord was near when she called. — R. H. Charles

16 COMMENTARY: This unit is the heading for the narrative that follows: Mastema proposes to God that Abraham shall be put to the proof. It divides the expulsion from the heavenly proposal that leads to the Akedah in chapter 18. — R. H. Charles

17 COMMENTARY: In the seventh week, in the first year thereof, in the first month in this jubilee, on the twelfth of this month, there were voices in heaven regarding Abraham, that he was faithful in all that God told him, and that he loved the Lord, and that in every affliction he was faithful. Heavenly voices testify to Abraham's faithfulness in every affliction, the premise on which the coming test will turn. — R. H. Charles

18 COMMENTARY: This verse opens Mastema's approach: and the prince Mastema came. The sentence is completed by his proposal in verse 20. — R. H. Charles

20 COMMENTARY: The prince Mastema came and said before God: 'Behold, Abraham loveth Isaac his son, and he delighteth in him above all things else; bid him offer him as a burnt-offering on the altar, and thou wilt see if he will do this command, and thou wilt know if he is faithful in everything wherein thou dost try him.' Mastema proposes the Akedah as proof: command the offering of the beloved son, and faithfulness will be known by doing. — R. H. Charles

21 COMMENTARY: The Lord knew that Abraham was faithful in all his afflictions, for he had tried him through his country and with famine, and had tried him with the wealth of kings. The trials begin with country and famine, then the wealth of kings, the long schooling of Abraham's faith. — R. H. Charles

22 COMMENTARY: He had tried him again through his wife, when she was torn from him, and with circumcision, and had tried him through Ishmael and Hagar his maid-servant, when he sent them away. The trials continue: the wife torn away, circumcision, and the expulsion of Ishmael and Hagar. — R. H. Charles

23 COMMENTARY: In everything wherein he had tried him, Abraham was found faithful, and his soul was not impatient, and he was not slow to act, for he was faithful and a lover of the Lord. The verdict on all the trials: faithful, not impatient, not slow to act, a lover of the Lord, ready for the greatest trial of chapter 18. — R. H. Charles

No note for verse(s) 19 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
