Study Notes — Jubilees

Chapter 45

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Israel went into Egypt, into the land of Goshen, on the new moon of the fourth month, in the second year of the third week of the forty-fifth jubilee. Henry notes that Joseph contrived to settle his brethren in Goshen, nearest to Canaan, where they might live by themselves and not forget the promise. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Joseph went to meet his father Jacob in Goshen, fell on his father's neck, and wept. Henry notes that Joseph owned his brethren though he was great in Egypt, and that the meeting in Goshen was the reunion the wagons had promised. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Israel said to Joseph, now let me die since I have seen thee, and blessed the Lord God of Israel, the God of Abraham and Isaac, who had not withheld mercy and grace from his servant Jacob. Henry notes that Jacob, old and not expecting to live long, wished his eyes refreshed with this sight before they closed, and then needed no more to be happy in this world. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Israel said it was enough to have seen Joseph's face while yet alive, and that the vision he saw at Bethel was true. The Bethel promise, that God would bring him back, is fulfilled in the son found alive in Egypt. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: The blessing continues: blessed be the Lord his God for ever and ever, and blessed be his name. The father's praise turns the reunion into worship, the God of Bethel blessed in Goshen. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: The blessing is completed: blessed be the Lord his God for ever, and blessed be his name. The double blessing seals Jacob's testimony that mercy had not been withheld through all his pilgrimage. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Joseph and his brothers ate bread before their father and drank wine, and Jacob rejoiced with exceeding great joy to see Joseph eating with his brothers and drinking before him, blessing the Creator who had preserved him and preserved his twelve sons. Henry notes that after tokens of true reconciliation, sweet communion follows, and the family table in Goshen is that communion. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: Joseph had given his father and brothers as a gift the right of dwelling in Goshen and Rameses and all the region round about, which he ruled over before Pharaoh. Henry notes that Pharaoh offered them Goshen and that Joseph owned his brethren, settling them to their advantage and rendering good for evil. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Israel and his sons dwelt in Goshen, the best part of Egypt, and Israel was one hundred and thirty years old when he came into Egypt. Henry notes Jacob's answer to Pharaoh: his days were few and evil, a pilgrimage reckoned by days, though he had lived one hundred and thirty years. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Joseph nourished his father, his brethren, and their possessions with bread as much as sufficed them for the seven years of the famine. Henry notes that care of Jacob and his family was especially designed by providence in Joseph's advancement, and that Joseph supplied his father with bread so that he would not die by famine. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Egypt suffered by reason of the famine, and Joseph acquired all the land of Egypt for Pharaoh in return for food, getting possession of the people, their cattle, and everything for Pharaoh. Henry notes that the Egyptians parted with all their property and even liberty for the saving of their lives, and regarded Joseph as a public benefactor who had saved the kingdom from ruin. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: When the famine years were accomplished, Joseph gave the people seed and food to sow in the eighth year, for the river had overflowed all Egypt. The return of the Nile's overflow ended the famine's grip, and the stored wisdom of the plenty years gave way to sowing again. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: In the seven famine years the river had not overflowed, irrigating only a few places on its banks, but now it overflowed and the Egyptians sowed and reaped much corn that year. Henry notes Egypt's dependence on the river's overflow, and that providence dispenses its gifts in many ways, yet dependence on the First Cause remains the same. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: This was the first year of the fourth week of the forty-fifth jubilee, the year the famine broke. Jubilees dates the renewal of sowing as precisely as it dated the famine's beginning. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Joseph took a fifth part of the harvest corn for the king and left four parts for the people for food and seed, and made it an ordinance for Egypt until this day. Henry notes the settlement: the Egyptians confessed Joseph had saved their lives, and the fifth part became the standing law of the land. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: Israel lived in Egypt seventeen years, and all his days were three jubilees, one hundred and forty-seven years, and he died in the fourth year of the fifth week of the forty-fifth jubilee. Henry notes that Israel, a prince with God, must die, and that his candle gradually burnt down to the socket, so that he saw the time drawing nigh. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: The account of Jacob's life is completed: seventeen years in Egypt, one hundred and forty-seven years in all, and death in the fourth year of the fifth week. The long pilgrimage Henry calls few and evil days ends in Egypt, awaiting burial in Canaan. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Before he died Israel blessed his sons, told them everything that would befall them in Egypt, made known what would come upon them in the last days, blessed them, and gave Joseph two portions in the land. Henry notes that Jacob blessed every son according to the blessings God intended to bestow, speaking by the Spirit of prophecy of the tribes' character and circumstances. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Israel slept with his fathers and was buried in the double cave in Canaan, near Abraham his father, in the grave he dug for himself in the double cave in Hebron. Henry notes that Jacob spoke of his burial from faith in the promise that Canaan should be his seed's inheritance, and that burial in Canaan was a type of the better country he expected. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 20 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
