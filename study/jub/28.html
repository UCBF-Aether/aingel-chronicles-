Study Notes — Jubilees

Chapter 28

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Jacob came to Laban and served him one week of years for Rachel, then asked for his wife when the seven years were done. Henry notes that love makes long and hard services short and easy, and that Jacob's labour of love is the pattern of service for heaven's happiness. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Laban answered that he would give Jacob his wife, agreeing to the bargain. The ready answer hid the deceit that the wedding feast would reveal. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Laban made a feast but gave Jacob his elder daughter Leah, with Zilpah as her handmaid, and Jacob did not know, thinking she was Rachel. Henry observes that Jacob, who had imposed upon his father, was now imposed upon by Laban in like manner, and that the Lord was righteous in the recompense. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Jacob went in to Leah and discovered the deception, then angrily asked Laban why he had dealt so with him. The anger was just, though Henry notes that even the righteous, if they take a false step, are sometimes thus recompensed in the earth. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Jacob reminded Laban that he had served for Rachel, not for Leah. The service had a named price, and Laban had taken the labour while withholding the promised wife. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Jacob asked why Laban had wronged him, pressing the plain injustice of the exchange. Henry adds that there is reason to believe Laban's excuse was not true, and that his way of settling the matter made bad worse. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Jacob told Laban to take his daughter back and let him go, since Laban had done him evil. The offer to walk away showed how completely the bargain had been broken, though Jacob would in the end stay for Rachel. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: Jacob loved Rachel more than Leah, for Leah's eyes were weak though her form was very handsome, while Rachel was beautiful in eyes and form. Henry notes that the sisters' contest over children and favour grew from the desire for the promised Seed and the reproach of barrenness. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Laban excused himself by the custom of his country, that the younger must not be given before the elder. Henry observes that Laban's excuse was probably untrue, a cover for a deceit already carried out. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Laban proposed that the seven days of Leah's feast should pass, and then Rachel would be given if Jacob served another seven years pasturing his sheep. Henry notes that Jacob was thus drawn into the disquiet of multiplying wives, and that Laban's settlement made bad worse. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: When Leah's feast days had passed, Laban gave Rachel to Jacob for another seven years' service, with Bilhah as her handmaid. Jacob could not refuse Rachel, for he had espoused her, nor Leah, who was now his wife. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Jacob served yet another seven years for Rachel, for Leah had been given to him for nothing. Henry's remark stands: love makes long service seem short, and an age of work is but as a few days to those who love God. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: The Lord opened Leah's womb, and she conceived and bore Jacob a son. Henry notes that the names Leah gave her children expressed her regard both to God and to her husband, and that God's providence in hearing her must be owned. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Leah called her first son Reuben, born on the fourteenth day of the ninth month in the first year of the third week. Henry explains the name as See a son, with Leah's thought, now will my husband love me. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: Rachel's womb was closed, for the Lord saw that Leah was hated and Rachel loved. Henry stresses that God made the difference between the sisters, and that Rachel had the advantage in love while Leah had it in children. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Jacob went in to Leah again, and she bore a second son. The births follow one another as the record of a household divided by favour, where each child was named for the mother's hope. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Leah called her second son Simeon, born on the twenty-first of the tenth month in the third year of the week. Henry gives the name as joined, with Leah's expectation, now will my husband be joined unto me. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Leah bore a third son and called his name Levi, in the new moon of the first month in the sixth year of the week. Henry notes Levi as joined, the name that would later belong to the tribe set apart for God's service. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: The birth of Levi is dated precisely, marking the third son in the sequence of Leah's children. In the larger story Levi's line would become the priestly tribe, though here he is simply Leah's third-born. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Leah bore a fourth son and called him Judah, on the fifteenth of the third month in the first year of the fourth week. Henry notes that Judah means praise, from Leah's words, now will I praise the Lord, and that Christ descended after the flesh from him whose name was Praise. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: Rachel envied Leah because she did not bear, and said to Jacob, give me children. Henry warns that envy is grieving at the good of another, a sin hateful to God and hurtful to neighbours and to ourselves. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: Jacob answered Rachel sharply, asking whether he had withheld from her the fruits of the womb. Henry notes that Jacob loved Rachel and therefore reproved her for what she said amiss, since faithful reproofs show true affection. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Rachel, seeing Leah's four sons, told Jacob to go in to her handmaid Bilhah, that she might bear a son for Rachel. Henry explains that by the usage of those times, the handmaid's children would be owned as her mistress's children. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: Bilhah was given to Jacob, conceived, and bore a son whom Rachel called Dan, on the ninth of the sixth month in the sixth year of the third week. Henry notes that Rachel took pleasure in giving names carrying marks of rivalry with her sister, and warns what roots of bitterness envy and strife are among relations. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: Jacob went in to Bilhah a second time, and she bore another son, whom Rachel called Naphtali, on the fifth of the seventh month in the second year of the fourth week. The second son completed Rachel's pair through her handmaid, matching Leah's growing household. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: When Leah saw she had become sterile, she envied Rachel in turn and gave her handmaid Zilpah to Jacob, who bore Gad on the twelfth of the eighth month in the third year of the fourth week. Henry observes the power of jealousy and rivalship in the household, and admires the divine appointment joining one man and one woman only. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: Jacob went in to Zilpah again, and she bore a second son, whom Leah called Asher, on the second of the eleventh month in the fifth year of the fourth week. The contest of the sisters now ran through their handmaids, child against child. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Jacob went in to Leah again, and she bore a son called Issachar, on the fourth of the fifth month in the fourth year of the fourth week, and gave him to a nurse. The births through Leah resumed, and the household's tally of sons kept growing. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: Leah bore twins, a son called Zebulon and a daughter called Dinah, on the seventh of the seventh month in the sixth year of the fourth week. Dinah's birth here is the beginning of the story that will darken the family's return to Canaan. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: The Lord was gracious to Rachel and opened her womb, ending her long barrenness. Henry notes that the sisters' contest was driven by the desire for the promised Seed, and that Rachel's joy came at last by God's gift, not by rivalry. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: Rachel conceived and bore a son called Joseph, on the new moon of the fourth month in the sixth year of the fourth week. Joseph's birth closes the list of Jacob's sons born in Haran and opens the next great movement of the story. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: When Joseph was born, Jacob asked Laban to give him his wives and sons and let him go to his father Isaac, to make a house of his own. Henry notes that the fourteen years were gone, and Jacob was willing to depart with no provision except God's promise. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: Laban asked Jacob to tarry with him for wages and pasture his flock again. Henry observes Laban's great selfishness: he had been greatly benefited by Jacob's services and wished to keep the benefit without paying for it. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: They agreed that Jacob's wages would be the lambs and kids born black and spotted and white. Henry notes that Jacob referred his cause to God rather than agreeing for stated wages with Laban, and that he acted honestly, taking only what was freely agreed. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: All the sheep brought forth spotted and speckled and black, variously marked, and the spotted ones were Jacob's while the unspotted were Laban's. Henry explains that Jacob's course was done by intimation from God, as a token of his power, not by mere policy. — Matthew Henry

37 COMMENTARY: Jacob's possessions multiplied exceedingly, with oxen, sheep, asses, camels, and menservants and maidservants. Henry concludes that the Lord will one way or another plead the cause of the oppressed and honour those who simply trust his providence. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: Laban and his sons envied Jacob, Laban took back his sheep, and observed him with evil intent. Henry notes that Laban could not complain, for Jacob had nothing more than was freely agreed, yet selfishness turned his gratitude into envy. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 10 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
