Study Notes — Jubilees

Chapter 36

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: In the sixth year of this week Isaac called his two sons, Esau and Jacob, and told them he was going the way of his fathers, to the eternal house where his fathers are. Henry notes that a dying patriarch's charge and blessing finish his testimony, and that Isaac's death, though recorded out of strict order, closes his long life of faith. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Isaac charged them to bury him near Abraham in the double cave in Ephron's field, in the sepulchre he had dug for himself. Henry notes that Jacob spoke of his burial-place from faith in the promise that Canaan should be his seed's inheritance in due time, and Isaac's charge breathes the same faith. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Isaac commanded his sons to practise righteousness and uprightness on the earth, so that the Lord might bring upon them all he had promised to Abraham and his seed. The promise is tied to conduct: the covenant blessings follow a righteous walk. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Isaac charged them to love one another as a man loves his own soul, each seeking his brother's benefit, acting together, and loving each other as their own souls. The charge answers the brothers' whole history: the rivalry that began in the womb must end in mutual love. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Isaac commanded them concerning idols, to reject, hate, and not love them, for they are full of deception for worshippers and for those who bow down to them. Henry's account of Jacob's household agrees: strange gods must be put away and buried out of sight before the family can worship purely. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Isaac bade his sons remember the Lord God of Abraham, whom he too had worshipped and served in righteousness and joy, that God might multiply them as the stars of heaven and establish them as a plant of righteousness never rooted out. The memory of the fathers' worship is the ground of the sons' hope. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Isaac said he would make his sons swear a great oath, for there is no greater oath, by the glorious, honoured, great, splendid, wonderful, and mighty name. The oath's greatness matches the charge it seals: brotherly peace for life. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: The oath was sworn by the name that created the heavens and the earth and all things together, that the brothers would fear and worship God. Creation is invoked as the witness of the covenant between the brothers. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: The oath bound each brother to love the other with affection and righteousness, and never to desire evil against his brother all the days of his life, so that they might prosper and not be destroyed. Prosperity and peace are joined: the brothers stand or fall together. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Isaac warned that if either brother devised evil against the other, he would fall into his brother's hand, be rooted out of the land of the living, and his seed destroyed from under heaven. The warning is the sanction of the oath, and it foreshadows the war that Esau's sons will force upon Jacob. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Isaac said and testified to his sons the judgment that would come upon the man who wished to injure his brother. The testimony closes the charge: the oath is witnessed, and its breach carries a named judgment. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Isaac divided all his possessions between the two that day, giving the larger portion to the first-born, with the tower and all about it, and all that Abraham possessed at the Well of the Oath. Henry's account of Esau's portion notes the same pattern: the children of this world have their possession in hand, while the children of God have theirs in promise. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Isaac said the larger portion would go to the first-born, the outward honour of primogeniture, even though the covenant blessing had gone to Jacob. The division separates the father's goods from the promise, giving Esau the goods and Jacob the blessing. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Esau answered that he had sold his birthright to Jacob and given it to him, that it was Jacob's, and that he had not a single word to say regarding it. The acknowledgment settles the old grievance at last: the birthright was sold, not stolen, and Esau owns the sale. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: Isaac blessed his sons, saying a blessing should rest on them and their seed that day, for they had given him rest, and his heart was not pained concerning the birthright, lest Esau should work wickedness on account of it. The father's rest is the peace of brothers reconciled before his death. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Isaac prayed that the Most High God would bless the man who works righteousness, him and his seed for ever. The blessing is conditional in form and universal in scope: righteousness, wherever it is worked, carries the blessing with it. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Isaac ended his commanding and blessing, and the brothers ate and drank together before him, rejoicing because there was one mind between them, then went forth and rested that day and slept. Henry notes the same agreement at the father's funeral: God had wonderfully changed Esau's mind, and the brothers stood together. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Isaac slept on his bed that day rejoicing, slept the eternal sleep, and died one hundred and eighty years old. Henry records Isaac's death with the same quiet: he had set his house in order, blessed his sons, and died in peace. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Isaac completed twenty-five weeks and five years, and his two sons Esau and Jacob buried him. Henry takes particular notice of the agreement of Esau and Jacob at their father's funeral, to show how God had changed Esau's mind. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: Esau went to the land of Edom, to the mountains of Seir, and dwelt there. Henry notes that Mount Seir is called the land of Edom's possession, while Canaan was still only the land of promise for Jacob's seed. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: Jacob dwelt in the mountains of Hebron, in the tower of his father Abraham's sojournings, worshipping the Lord with all his heart according to the visible commands, as God had divided the days of his generations. The tower at Hebron becomes Jacob's seat, the place of the fathers' worship continued by the son. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Leah died in the fourth year of the second week of the forty-fifth jubilee, and Jacob buried her in the double cave near Rebecca his mother, to the left of Sarah's grave. Henry's account of the burial cave notes that Leah was buried there with the patriarchs, the family grave gathering its members across generations. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: All Leah's sons and Jacob's sons came to mourn over Leah with him and to comfort him, for he was lamenting her. The gathered sons mourn the mother whose children had built the house of Israel. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: Jacob loved Leah exceedingly after Rachel her sister died, for she was perfect and upright in all her ways, honoured Jacob, and never spoke a harsh word to him all their life, being gentle, peaceable, upright, and honourable. The eulogy reverses Leah's early story: the unloved wife became the honoured wife, and Henry's account of the sisters agrees that God's providence in hearing Leah must be owned. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: Jacob remembered all Leah's deeds during her life and lamented her exceedingly, loving her with all his heart and soul. The lament is the husband's testimony to a life of quiet faithfulness, remembered deed by deed. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 11, 17, 28 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
