Study Notes — Jubilees

Chapter 46

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: After Jacob died, the children of Israel multiplied in Egypt, became a great nation, and were of one accord in heart, brother loving brother and every man helping his brother, increasing abundantly and multiplying exceedingly for ten weeks of years, all the days of Joseph's life. Henry notes that only about seventy persons went down into Egypt, and there, in about the same number of years, though under cruel bondage, they became a large nation, according to the promise long before made to the fathers. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: There was no Satan nor any evil all the days of Joseph's life after his father Jacob, for all the Egyptians honoured the children of Israel all Joseph's days. Henry notes that all who knew Joseph loved him and were kind to his brethren for his sake, though the best services a man does are soon forgotten after his death. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Joseph died at one hundred and ten years old: seventeen years he lived in Canaan, ten years he was a servant, three years in prison, and eighty years under the king, ruling all the land of Egypt. The sum of his life is the sum of Henry's Joseph history: humbled in Canaan, service, and prison, then exalted to rule, and Henry calls both his death and his life truly excellent. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: The account of Joseph's rule is completed: eighty years under the king, ruling all the land of Egypt. The long rule, longer than the slavery and prison together, is the exaltation that followed the humiliation. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Joseph died, and all his brethren and all that generation died. Henry notes that the best and most useful services are soon forgotten after death, and the generation that knew Joseph passes with him, opening the house of bondage. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Before he died Joseph commanded the children of Israel to carry his bones with them when they went forth from Egypt. Henry explains the commandment: by faith in the resurrection and the promise of Canaan, Joseph charged them to keep his remains unburied till the day they were settled in the promised land, keeping Canaan continually in their minds. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Joseph made them swear regarding his bones, for he knew the Egyptians would not again bring forth and bury him in Canaan, since Makamaron, king of Canaan, dwelling in Assyria, had fought the king of Egypt in the valley, slain him, and pursued the Egyptians to the gates of Ermon. The oath answers the closed gates: only Israel itself, going forth, could carry Joseph's bones home, and Henry notes the commandment was meant to keep up their expectation of a speedy departure. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Joseph died in the forty-sixth jubilee, in the sixth week, in the second year. The date fixes the end of the age of favour, after which the Egyptians who knew Joseph were gone. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Joseph died in the forty-sixth jubilee, was buried in Egypt, and all his brethren died after him. Henry notes that Joseph's death, like his life, furnishes strong encouragement to persevere in God's service, and that his bones waited in Egypt for the exodus he had foreseen. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: The children of Israel brought forth all the bones of Jacob's children except Joseph's, and buried them in the field in the double cave in the mountain. The patriarchs' bones, carried to Hebron even while Egypt's gates were closing, show the same faith as Joseph's commandment: the dead belong to Canaan, not to Egypt. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: The king of Canaan devised an evil device against Israel, afflicting them, and said to the Egyptians that the children of Israel had increased and multiplied more than they. Henry notes that the offence of Israel was that he prospered, and that there is no sight more hateful to a wicked man than the prosperity of the righteous. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: The Egyptians said to deal wisely with Israel before they became too many, afflicting them with slavery before war came, lest they join their enemies and go up out of the land, for their hearts and faces were toward Canaan. Henry notes that the Egyptians feared Israel would join their enemies and get up out of the land, and that wickedness is ever cowardly, fearing where no fear is. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: The Egyptians set taskmasters over Israel to afflict them with slavery, and they built strong cities for Pharaoh, Pithom and Raamses, and built all the walls and fortifications that had fallen in Egypt's cities. Henry notes that taskmasters were set not only to burden Israel but to afflict them with their burdens, so that their lives became bitter. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: The Egyptians made Israel serve with rigour, and the more they dealt evilly with them, the more Israel increased and multiplied. Henry observes that Israel wonderfully increased, and that Christianity spread most when persecuted: the blood of the martyrs was the seed of the church, and those who take counsel against the Lord imagine a vain thing. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: The people of Egypt abominated the children of Israel. The abomination is the hatred Henry names as the offence of prosperity: the righteous prospering under affliction is hateful to the wicked, and the hatred only fed the increase. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 8, 11, 13, 14, 15, 21 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
