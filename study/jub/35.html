Study Notes — Jubilees

Chapter 35

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: In the first year of the first week of the forty-fifth jubilee Rebecca called Jacob and commanded him regarding his father and his brother, that he should honour them all the days of his life. The mother's last charge is honour: the household that began in deception is to end in honour kept for life. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Jacob answered that he would do everything as his mother commanded him. The ready promise answers Rebecca's charge with obedience, the same readiness Henry finds in Jacob's service to his father. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Jacob said that honouring his father and brother would be honour and greatness to him, and righteousness before the Lord. Honour is not servility here but the righteousness of a son who keeps his place in the house. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Jacob told his mother that she knew, from his birth until that day, all his deeds and all that was in his heart, and that he always thought good concerning all. The appeal to a lifetime known by his mother answers any doubt about his intentions toward his brother. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Jacob asked how he could fail to do what she commanded, to honour his father and his brother. The question turns the charge into a certainty: honour is what he already intends. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Jacob asked his mother to tell him what perversity she had seen in him, so that he might turn away from it and mercy might be upon him. The offer of correction shows a conscience willing to be searched, even by the mother who once led him into deceit. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Rebecca answered that she had seen in Jacob all her days no perverse deeds, but only upright ones. The testimony clears the son whom she had once tempted: the years since the blessing have proved his uprightness. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Rebecca went in to Isaac with one petition: that he make Esau swear not to injure Jacob nor pursue him with enmity. Henry's account of Esau's malice explains the fear: Esau bore malice on account of the blessing and aimed to prevent Jacob's seed from having the dominion by taking his life. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Rebecca reminded Isaac that Esau's thoughts had been perverse from his youth, with no goodness in him, and that he desired after Isaac's death to kill Jacob. The petition looks past Isaac's lifetime to the danger that would open at his death. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Rebecca recalled all Esau had done since Jacob went to Haran: he had forsaken his parents with his whole heart, done them evil, taken Isaac's flocks, and carried off all his possessions. The record of wrongs is the ground for asking an oath. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: When his parents implored and besought Esau for what was their own, he acted as a man taking pity on them. The pity of a son toward his own parents' property shows how far the house had been divided. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Esau was bitter against Isaac because he had blessed Jacob, his perfect and upright son, and Jacob since his return from Haran had robbed them of nothing, bringing everything in its season, rejoicing when they took from his hands, blessing them, and remaining at home honouring them. The contrast between the brothers is drawn in deeds: one took, the other gave. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: Isaac answered that he too knew Jacob's deeds, how with all his heart he honoured his parents, but that he had loved Esau formerly more than Jacob because he was first-born, and now loved Jacob more than Esau, for Esau had done manifold evil deeds with no righteousness in him. Henry notes the same change in Isaac: the father who once set his affection on Esau against God's word now sees the sons as their deeds have shown them. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Isaac warned that even if Esau swore, he would not abide by his oath and would do evil only, not good. The warning is realistic about Esau's character: an oath from a man who keeps no faith binds nothing. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Isaac told Rebecca not to fear for Jacob, for Jacob's guardian is great, powerful, honoured, and praised more than Esau's guardian. Henry's account of Jacob's life agrees: angels guarded him, God kept him wherever he went, and his enemies were made to be at peace with him. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Rebecca sent and called Esau, and he came to her. The mother who once sent Jacob away in secret now summons Esau openly, to settle the brothers' peace before her death. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: Rebecca told Esau she had a petition and asked him to promise to do it. The petition is the same in substance as the one laid on Isaac: peace between the brothers, secured before the parents die. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: Esau answered that he would do everything his mother said and would not refuse her petition. The ready promise, whatever its later worth, is the son's honour to his mother in her last days. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Rebecca asked that on the day of her death Esau take her in and bury her near Sarah, his father's mother, and that he and Jacob love each other, neither desiring evil against the other, but mutual love only, so that they would prosper and be honoured, with no enemy rejoicing over them. The burial beside Sarah joins the mother to the matriarchs, and the love between brothers is the condition of the family's honour. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: Esau promised to bury his mother near Sarah on the day of her death, as she desired, so that her bones might lie near Sarah's bones. The promise of burial is the son's last service, and it binds him to act with Jacob when the day comes. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: Esau promised to love Jacob his brother above all flesh, for he had no other brother in all the earth, and they were sown together in their mother's body and came forth together from her womb. The twin bond is pleaded as the reason love is not a favour but a debt. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: Esau begged Rebecca to exhort Jacob concerning him and his sons, for he knew Jacob would assuredly be king over him and his sons, since on the day Isaac blessed him he made Jacob the higher and himself the lower. The acknowledgment accepts the blessing's order at last: Jacob is lord, and Esau knows it. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: Esau swore that he would love Jacob and desire no evil against him all the days of his life, but good only. The oath is the counterpart of the one Rebecca sought from Isaac's side, a sworn peace between the brothers. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Esau swore to his mother regarding all this matter, sealing the promise. The oath stands as the last formal act between mother and elder son. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: Rebecca called Jacob before Esau's eyes and gave him commandment according to the words she had spoken to Esau. The command was given openly, so that both brothers heard the same charge and neither could claim ignorance. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: Jacob answered that he would do his mother's pleasure, that no evil would proceed from him or his sons against Esau, and that he would be first in nothing save in love only. The pledge answers Esau's oath with its mirror: supremacy in love alone. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: They ate and drank together, Rebecca and her sons, that night, and she died, three jubilees and one week and one year old, on that night. The shared meal is the last fellowship of mother and sons, and the death follows it at once, as if the peace were completed just in time. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: Esau and Jacob buried Rebecca in the double cave near Sarah, their father's mother. Henry notes the same agreement of the brothers at a parent's grave in the case of Isaac: God had wonderfully changed Esau's mind, and the brothers who once stood divided buried their parents together. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 8, 9, 10, 17, 19, 34, 35 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
