Study Notes — Jubilees

Chapter 27

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Esau's murderous words were told to Rebecca, and she sent for Jacob to warn him. Henry notes that Rebekah warned Jacob of his danger and advised him to withdraw for his safety, rather than presuming on his wisdom or resolution. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Rebecca told Jacob to flee to her brother Laban in Haran and stay only a few days, until Esau's anger turned away and he forgot what Jacob had done. Henry stresses that care must be taken to keep even hopeful children out of the way of evil. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Rebecca answered that she must not be bereft of both her sons on one day. The danger was double: Esau might kill Jacob, or Jacob might kill Esau in return, and either loss would leave her childless. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Jacob objected that his aged, dim-eyed father would take it badly if he left, fearing his father's anger and curse; Rebecca then said she loathed her life because of the daughters of Heth whom Esau had married. Henry observes that Jacob, though blessed, would still smart for the indirect course taken to obtain the blessing, and that the Heth marriages gave a lawful reason to send him away. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Rebecca closed her plea by saying the daughters of Canaan are evil, and that Jacob must not take such a wife either. Henry's rule is plain: those who profess religion should not marry with those that care not for religion. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Isaac now enters the scene, called in by Rebecca's plea. What follows is his deliberate blessing and charge, which Henry contrasts with the former blessing given unwittingly. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: Isaac called Jacob and blessed him, this time designedly. Henry notes that this blessing is more full than the former, a gospel blessing looking as high as heaven, of which Canaan was a type. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Isaac admonished Jacob with a solemn charge before blessing him. Henry notes that Jacob was dismissed by his father with a solemn charge and a solemn blessing together, discipline and promise in one farewell. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Jacob was charged not to take a wife from the daughters of Canaan. Henry's rule is plain: those who profess religion should not marry with those that care not for religion. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Jacob was told to arise and go to Mesopotamia, to the house of Bethuel his mother's father. The journey was both a flight from Esau and an obedient errand to find a wife among his own kin. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: He was to take a wife from the house of Bethuel, his mother's father, the kin from which Abraham's servant had once brought Rebecca herself. The errand kept the covenant line within the family of faith. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: The wife was to come from the daughters of Laban, his mother's brother. Laban's household in Haran was the place appointed for Jacob's exile and his marriages. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Isaac's blessing began with the name God Almighty, praying that he would bless Jacob and increase him. Henry notes that Jacob had blessings promised both as to this world and that which is to come, yet went out to a hard service. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: The blessing prayed that Jacob might be multiplied until he became a company of nations. One man was to become a multitude, the covenant promise of Abraham now deliberately laid on Jacob. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: Isaac prayed that God would give Jacob the blessings of his father Abraham. Henry calls this blessing more full than the former one, because it names the covenant itself, not only earthly plenty. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: The blessings of Abraham were given to Jacob and to his seed after him, passing the covenant to the next generation. The promise was not for Jacob alone but for the nation that would come from him. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: The blessing was given so that Jacob might inherit the land of his sojournings. The land was still only a promise, but Henry notes it was the better country the patriarchs had in view. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Isaac named the whole land which God gave to Abraham as Jacob's inheritance, and sent him on his way. The gift of the land and the command to go belonged together: the heir must leave home to claim it. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Isaac's charge closed with the words, go, my son, in peace. The peace wished for him was the protection of God on a road that would otherwise be full of danger. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Isaac sent Jacob away, an orderly and blessed dismissal, unlike the secret flight Rebecca had first planned. The father's sending made Jacob's exile an act of obedience, not of mere escape. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: Jacob went to Mesopotamia, to Laban the son of Bethuel. The long road to Haran began here, and with it the hard service that would correct Jacob for the fraud on his father. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: Laban is named as the Syrian, the brother of Rebecca, Jacob's mother. The kinship mattered: Jacob travelled to his mother's family, where he would find both wives and affliction. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: Isaac now speaks to comfort Rebecca, turning from the son who has gone to the mother who grieves. His comfort rests on God's promise, which Henry states plainly: God promises to keep Jacob and bring him back again to this land. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: Isaac told Rebecca not to weep on account of Jacob, calling her his sister in the old familiar style. The comfort began by naming the grief before answering it. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Isaac assured her that Jacob went in peace, and in peace he would return. Henry's comment on God's promise to Jacob fits Isaac's confidence: God had promised to keep him wherever he went and to bring him back again. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: The promise of return turns the farewell from a final parting into a sojourn. Jacob's exile had an appointed end, fixed by God's word rather than by Esau's anger. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: Isaac appealed to the Most High God, who would preserve Jacob. The title stresses the power that guards the traveller, higher than any brother's hatred or any foreign danger. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: God would preserve Jacob from all evil and be with him, the same promise God himself would give at Bethel. Henry notes that God visits his people with comfort precisely when they are most destitute of other comforts. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: God would not forsake Jacob all his days, and Isaac said he knew Jacob's ways would be prospered. Henry stresses that whom God loves, he never leaves, until his last promise is accomplished. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: Isaac's confidence extended to all Jacob's ways, prospered in all things wherever he went. The blessing was not limited to the road to Haran but covered the whole of Jacob's life abroad. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: Jacob would return in peace, and his parents would see him in peace. The return was pictured as a reunion, not merely a safe arrival in the land. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: The vision of seeing Jacob again in peace closes Isaac's assurance. Peace at departure and peace at return frame the whole exile under God's keeping. — Matthew Henry

37 COMMENTARY: Isaac told Rebecca not to fear on Jacob's account, addressing her again as his sister. Fear was answered not by denying the danger but by pointing to the God who kept Jacob. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: Isaac described Jacob as a man on the upright path, a perfect man. The description looks to Jacob's standing before God rather than to his past craft, which Henry says the hard service ahead would correct. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: Jacob was faithful and would not perish, Isaac said. Faithfulness here is trust in God's promise, the same faith Henry finds in Jacob's later prayer and wrestling. — Matthew Henry

40 COMMENTARY: Isaac's comfort closed with the words, weep not. The repetition of the comfort shows a father's care to leave the mother at peace when the son had gone. — Matthew Henry

41 COMMENTARY: Isaac comforted Rebecca concerning her son Jacob, turning her grief into hope grounded on God's promise. The comfort was not empty cheer but a rehearsal of what God had pledged. — Matthew Henry

42 COMMENTARY: Isaac comforted Rebecca and blessed Jacob once more. The second blessing sealed the first: Jacob left not as a fugitive only, but as a son sent with his father's blessing. — Matthew Henry

43 COMMENTARY: Jacob went on his way, the journey itself now beginning after the farewells. From this point the narrative follows the traveller, not the household he left. — Matthew Henry

44 COMMENTARY: Jacob went from the Well of the Oath to go to Haran, leaving the home of his father for his mother's kin. The Well of the Oath was the home he left behind, and Haran the place of his exile and marriages. — Matthew Henry

45 COMMENTARY: The journey is dated to the first year of the second week of the forty-fourth jubilee, in Jubilees' chronology of weeks and jubilees. The careful dating marks Jacob's departure as a fixed point in the book's ordered history. — Matthew Henry

46 COMMENTARY: Jacob came to Luz on the mountains, the place later called Bethel. Henry notes that Jacob, obliged to flee and in trouble, looked only to God to make him dwell in safety, and could sleep in the open air with his head on a stone. — Matthew Henry

47 COMMENTARY: The place was Bethel, reached on the new moon of the first month of that week. Jubilees dates the arrival precisely, while Henry stresses what happened there: God manifested himself to Jacob when he was asleep. — Matthew Henry

48 COMMENTARY: Jacob came to the place at evening, turned from the way, and slept there because the sun had set. Henry observes that any true believer would be willing to take up with Jacob's pillow, provided he might have Jacob's vision. — Matthew Henry

49 COMMENTARY: Jacob took a stone of that place, laid it at his head under the tree, and slept while journeying alone. His destitution was complete, which Henry says is God's time to visit his people with comforts. — Matthew Henry

50 COMMENTARY: Jacob dreamt of a ladder set up on the earth reaching to heaven, with the angels of God ascending and descending on it, and the Lord standing upon it. Henry reads the ladder as God's providence keeping constant intercourse between heaven and earth, and as the mediation of Christ, the Way by which all God's favours come to us. — Matthew Henry

51 COMMENTARY: The Lord spoke to Jacob as the God of Abraham and Isaac, giving him the land where he slept, to him and his seed. Henry notes that God spake comfortably to Jacob from the head of the ladder, and that the Messiah should come from Jacob, the great blessing of the world. — Matthew Henry

52 COMMENTARY: Jacob's seed would be as the dust of the earth, spreading west, east, north, and south, and all families of nations would be blessed in him and his seed. Henry stresses that none of any family are shut out from blessedness in Christ except those who shut out themselves. — Matthew Henry

53 COMMENTARY: God promised to be with Jacob, keep him wherever he went, and bring him back into this land in peace, never leaving him until all was done. Henry observes that Jacob had a long journey to an unknown country, but God promised to keep him and bring him back again. — Matthew Henry

54 COMMENTARY: Jacob awoke and said truly this place was the house of the Lord, though he had not known it. Henry notes that God manifested himself to Jacob while he slept, and that grace, like the dew, tarries not for the sons of men. — Matthew Henry

55 COMMENTARY: Jacob was afraid and said the place was dreadful, none other than the house of God and the gate of heaven. Henry remarks that the more we see of God, the more cause we see for holy trembling before him. — Matthew Henry

56 COMMENTARY: Jacob rose early, took the stone from under his head, set it up as a pillar for a sign, and poured oil on top of it. The pillar marked the place of the vision, so that the spot would be remembered as holy ground. — Matthew Henry

57 COMMENTARY: Jacob called the place Bethel, though its first name was Luz. The new name, house of God, recorded what had happened there rather than what the town had been called before. — Matthew Henry

58 COMMENTARY: Jacob vowed that if the Lord would be with him, keep him on his way, and give him bread and clothing, so that he returned in peace, then the Lord would be his God, the stone would be God's house, and he would give a tenth of all. Henry praises Jacob's moderation in his desires and his piety: he asked not for soft clothing and dainty meat, but for God's presence, and the tenth is a fit proportion to devote to God. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 3, 24, 25, 26 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
