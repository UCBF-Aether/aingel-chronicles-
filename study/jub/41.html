Study Notes — Jubilees

Chapter 41

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: In the forty-fifth jubilee, in the second week, in the second year, Judah took for his first-born Er a wife from the daughters of Aram named Tamar. Henry introduces the whole family account with wonder: of all Jacob's sons, the Lord should spring out of Judah, showing God's choice is of grace and not of merit. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Er hated Tamar and did not lie with her, because her mother was of the daughters of Canaan, and he wished to take a wife from his mother's kin, but Judah his father would not permit him. The hatred began in the same Canaanite question that runs through Jubilees: the house must not be mixed with the daughters of the land. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: This Er, the first-born of Judah, was wicked, and the Lord slew him. Henry's warning over the chapter stands: what awful examples the Lord proclaims in his punishments, of his utter displeasure at sin. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: The sentence on Er is completed: the Lord slew him for his wickedness. The first-born's death opens the levirate duty that drives the rest of the chapter. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Judah said to Onan his brother that he should go in to his brother's wife, perform the duty of a husband's brother, and raise up seed to his brother. The duty was to preserve the dead brother's name and line, the obligation that Tamar would later claim for herself. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Judah's charge to Onan continued: he must go in to his brother's wife and perform the husband's brother's duty. The command is plain, and Onan's evasion of it is the next sin of the house. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: The duty was defined: to raise up seed to his brother, not to himself. The seed would bear Er's name, and that self-denial is exactly what Onan refused. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Onan knew the seed would not be his but his brother's only. Knowledge makes the sin deliberate: he understood the duty and chose to frustrate it. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Onan went into his brother's wife's house but spilt the seed on the ground, defeating the duty while seeming to perform it. Henry's warning fits: seek grace to avoid every appearance of sin, for God sees the deed behind the show. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: The spilling of the seed is named for what it was: a refusal of the brother's duty, not an accident. The act is the centre of Onan's guilt, a deliberate frustration of the command. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Onan was wicked in the eyes of the Lord, and the Lord slew him. The second son dies as the first did, and Henry's point stands: the Lord proclaims his utter displeasure at sin in awful examples, even within the chosen house. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: The sentence is completed: the Lord slew Onan as he had slain Er. Two sons dead, and the line of Judah now hangs on the youngest son and the widowed Tamar. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Judah said to Tamar his daughter-in-law that she should remain in her father's house as a widow till Shelah his son was grown up, and then he would give her to him as wife. The promise kept Tamar waiting for the levirate duty, and her later action answers Judah's failure to keep it. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Tamar was to remain as a widow in her father's house, the waiting place of a woman whose husband's line was not yet settled. The widowhood was meant to be temporary, until Shelah grew up. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: The promise was repeated in full: when Shelah was grown, Judah would give him to Tamar as wife. The repetition binds Judah to the promise his later conduct would break. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: The promise's condition is stated once more: till Shelah his son was grown up, and then he would give Tamar to him. The condition was fulfilled in time, but the promise was not. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Shelah grew up, and the time for Judah's promise arrived. The growth of the boy is the quiet turning point: the excuse of youth was gone, and Tamar's claim stood due. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Bedsu'el, Judah's wife, did not permit her son Shelah to marry Tamar. The mother's refusal blocked the promise, and the house's failure to do right passed from father to mother. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: The refusal is completed: Shelah was not permitted to marry, and Tamar remained a widow beyond the promised time. The broken promise is what drives Tamar to the road to Timnah. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Bedsu'el, Judah's wife, died in the fifth year of this week. The death removes the mother who blocked the marriage, but Judah still does not give Shelah to Tamar, and the widow's wait continues. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: The death is dated to the fifth year of the week, fixing it in Jubilees' chronology between Shelah's growing up and Judah's journey to Timnah. The dates mark how long Tamar waited. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: In the sixth year Judah went up to shear his sheep at Timnah. The shearing journey is the occasion for the encounter: Judah travels, and Tamar, hearing of it, takes her place on his road. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: The destination is named: Timnah, where Judah went to shear his sheep. The place-name matters, for Tamar will sit in the gate adjoining the way to Timnah. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: Tamar was told that her father-in-law was going up to Timnah to shear his sheep. The news reached the widow who had waited for a husband, and she acted on it. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: The message is completed: Judah goes up to Timnah to shear his sheep, and Tamar knows his road and his time. Knowledge becomes her opportunity to claim the right denied her. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: Tamar put off her widow's clothes, put on a veil, adorned herself, and sat in the gate adjoining the way to Timnah. Henry's warning about avoiding every appearance of sin cuts both ways here: Tamar's disguise wore the appearance of harlotry, though her aim was the levirate seed that was her due. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: The veil and adornment completed the disguise, and Tamar sat where Judah must pass. The gate by the way to Timnah was her chosen place, public enough to be found, veiled enough to be mistaken. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: The gate adjoining the way to Timnah is named as her seat, the threshold between the town and the road. There she waited for the father-in-law who owed her a husband. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: As Judah was going along he found her and thought her to be a harlot. Henry calls the chapter's account profligate conduct, and Judah's mistaking of his daughter-in-law for a harlot is its darkest turn. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: Judah found Tamar on his way and took her for a harlot, the disguise working exactly as she intended. The mistake is Judah's, but the design is Tamar's, and both will be judged in the chapter's end. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: Judah said to her, let me come in to thee, and she said come in, and he went in. The encounter is stated plainly, without excuse, as Henry states the whole chapter: an account of profligate conduct in the house from which Christ would come. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: The meeting is completed: he went in to her, and the act that would produce Perez was done in ignorance on his side and in design on hers. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: Tamar asked for her hire, and Judah said he had nothing in his hand save his ring, his necklace, and his staff. The pledges she demanded would become the proof of paternity, and Henry's theme of grace runs through it: God would bring the promised Seed from this very union. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: Judah named his pledges one by one: the ring on his finger, the necklace, and the staff in his hand. Each token was personal and unmistakable, the seal of a man who could later be known by them. — Matthew Henry

37 COMMENTARY: The staff in Judah's hand completes the list of pledges, with the ring and necklace. Three tokens, each bearing its owner's mark, would speak for Tamar when Judah spoke against her. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: The staff is named again as the pledge is handed over, the last of the three tokens passing into Tamar's keeping. The exchange is businesslike, and its business would later become judgment. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: Tamar said to give the tokens to her until he sent her hire, and Judah said he would send a kid of the goats, and gave them to her, and went in to her, and she conceived by him. The conception is the point of the whole design: the seed denied through Shelah came through Judah himself. — Matthew Henry

40 COMMENTARY: The agreement is sealed: the tokens stay with Tamar until the kid arrives, and the union that produced the promised seed is accomplished. — Matthew Henry

41 COMMENTARY: Judah promised to send a kid of the goats as her hire, the payment that would redeem his pledges. The kid would never reach her, because the messenger would not find her. — Matthew Henry

42 COMMENTARY: The kid is named again as the promised hire, and the tokens were given in its place. The pledge-and-payment bargain is the legal frame Tamar will later use to prove her case. — Matthew Henry

43 COMMENTARY: Judah gave the tokens to Tamar, went in to her, and she conceived by him. The conception closes the encounter: the seed of Judah was secured, though by a road of sin and disguise. — Matthew Henry

44 COMMENTARY: The conception by Judah is stated plainly, the fact on which the rest of the chapter turns. From this union would come Perez, ancestor of the Messiah, the grace Henry finds wonderful in this chapter. — Matthew Henry

45 COMMENTARY: Judah went to his sheep, and Tamar went to her father's house. Each returned to their place, Judah to his shearing and Tamar to her widowhood, with the tokens hidden and the child growing. — Matthew Henry

46 COMMENTARY: The parting is complete: he to his flocks, she to her father's house, and the secret between them carried in her keeping and in her womb. — Matthew Henry

47 COMMENTARY: Judah sent a kid of the goats by his shepherd, an Adullamite, to redeem his pledges, and the shepherd found her not. The honest attempt to pay the hire failed, because Tamar had vanished from the roadside. — Matthew Henry

48 COMMENTARY: The shepherd asked the people of the place where the harlot who was there had gone, and they said there was no harlot with them. The place denied what the roadside had shown, and the tokens stayed with Tamar. — Matthew Henry

49 COMMENTARY: The shepherd's question is repeated: where is the harlot who was here? The question itself shows Judah's view of the encounter, a hired woman, not the daughter-in-law claiming her right. — Matthew Henry

50 COMMENTARY: The people answered that there was no harlot there with them, and the search ended. The denial protected Tamar's secret until the pregnancy revealed it. — Matthew Henry

51 COMMENTARY: The people of the place said there was no harlot there, closing the search. Judah's pledges remained unredeemed, waiting for the day they would be produced. — Matthew Henry

52 COMMENTARY: The shepherd returned and told Judah he had not found her, and that the people said there was no harlot there. The report left Judah without his tokens and without knowledge of the child. — Matthew Henry

53 COMMENTARY: Judah answered that she might keep the tokens, lest they become a cause of derision. The fear of derision, not justice, settled the matter for Judah, and the tokens stayed where they would later condemn him. — Matthew Henry

54 COMMENTARY: When three months had completed, it was manifest that Tamar was with child, and they told Judah that his daughter-in-law was with child by whoredom. The pregnancy exposed what the veil had hidden, and the charge of whoredom fell on the woman whose design had been the levirate right. — Matthew Henry

55 COMMENTARY: Judah went to her father and brothers and said to bring her forth and burn her, for she had wrought uncleanness in Israel. The judge who had lain with her now sentenced her to fire, and Henry's warning about God's displeasure at sin falls first on the judge himself. — Matthew Henry

56 COMMENTARY: When they brought Tamar forth to burn her, she sent to her father-in-law the ring, the necklace, and the staff, saying discern whose these are, for by him she was with child. The tokens, kept since Timnah, now spoke: the judge was the father, and the sentence collapsed. — Matthew Henry

57 COMMENTARY: Judah acknowledged the tokens and said Tamar was more righteous than he was. The confession is the chapter's turning point: the sinner judges himself, and Henry's grace shines here, for Christ came to save sinners, even the chief, and sprang from this very line. — Matthew Henry

58 COMMENTARY: Judah ordered that they should not burn her, reversing his own sentence. The reversal is justice done late: the woman condemned by the guilty judge is freed by his confession. — Matthew Henry

59 COMMENTARY: For that reason Tamar was not given to Shelah, and Judah did not again approach her. The separation closed the matter: the levirate claim was satisfied through Judah himself, and no further union followed. — Matthew Henry

60 COMMENTARY: Afterwards Tamar bore two sons, Perez and Zerah, in the seventh year of this second week. Henry's wonder crowns the chapter: from Perez, born of this union, the Lord Jesus would spring, showing God's choice is of grace and not of merit. — Matthew Henry

61 COMMENTARY: Thereupon the seven years of fruitfulness were accomplished, of which Joseph spoke to Pharaoh. The chronology joins Judah's story to Joseph's: the plenty years end as the family story turns toward famine. — Matthew Henry

62 COMMENTARY: Judah acknowledged that the deed he had done was evil, for he had lain with his daughter-in-law, and esteemed it hateful in his eyes, and confessed he had transgressed and gone astray, for he had uncovered his son's skirt, and began to lament and supplicate before the Lord. Henry's counsel fits the repentance: seek grace from God to avoid every appearance of sin, and let the Redeemer's humiliation, in taking such ancestors, endear him to our hearts. — Matthew Henry

63 COMMENTARY: Judah was told in a dream that he was forgiven, because he supplicated earnestly, lamented, and did not again commit the sin. The forgiveness follows the pattern Henry celebrates in this chapter: Christ came into the world to save sinners, and his worthiness is of himself, not of his ancestors. — Matthew Henry

64 COMMENTARY: Judah received forgiveness because he turned from his sin and his ignorance, though he had transgressed greatly before God. Repentance, not merit, is the ground of the pardon, the grace that makes Judah's line the line of the Messiah. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 5, 23, 65, 66, 67, 68 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
