Study Notes — Jubilees

Chapter 19

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: In the first year of the first week in the forty-second jubilee, Abraham returned and dwelt opposite Hebron, that is Kirjath Arba, two weeks of years. Abraham returns to dwell opposite Hebron, Kirjath Arba, for two weeks of years. — R. H. Charles

2 COMMENTARY: In the first year of the third week of this jubilee the days of the life of Sarah were accomplished, and she died in Hebron. Sarah's life is accomplished and she dies in Hebron, in the first year of the third week. — R. H. Charles

3 COMMENTARY: Abraham went to mourn over her and bury her, and we tried him to see if his spirit were patient and he were not indignant in the words of his mouth; and he was found patient in this, and was not disturbed. JFB on Genesis 23:2 notes the 'mourning' describes conformity to the customary usage of sitting on the ground for a time, while the 'weeping' indicates the natural outburst of sorrow; here the mourning and burial negotiation itself is a trial of patience. — R. H. Charles; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: For in patience of spirit he conversed with the children of Heth, to the intent that they should give him a place in which to bury his dead. JFB on Genesis 23:3 notes he stood up and bespoke their kind offices to aid him in obtaining possession of a cave belonging to Ephron, a wealthy neighbor; patience of spirit governs the negotiation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: The Lord gave him grace before all who saw him, and he besought in gentleness the sons of Heth, and they gave him the land of the double cave over against Mamre, that is Hebron, for four hundred pieces of silver. JFB on Genesis 23:9 notes Machpelah is the 'double cave,' and on 23:15 that the land is worth four hundred shekels; grace before all who saw him and gentleness in beseeching win the place. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: They besought him, saying, 'We shall give it to thee for nothing,' but he would not take it from their hands for nothing, for he gave the price of the place, the money in full, and he bowed down before them twice; and after this he buried his dead in the double cave. JFB on Genesis 23:11-16 notes the great show of generosity was only a show, Ephron joining field and cave and expecting costly presents in return, while the patriarch wished to make a purchase, asked the terms, and paid the money in presence of assembled witnesses, weighed; here he pays in full, bows twice, then buries his dead. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: All the days of the life of Sarah were one hundred and twenty-seven years, that is, two jubilees and four weeks and one year: these are the days of the years of the life of Sarah. JFB on Genesis 23:1 notes Sarah is the only woman in Scripture whose age, death, and burial are mentioned, probably to do honor to the venerable mother of the Hebrew people; here her 127 years are reckoned as two jubilees, four weeks and one year. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: This is the tenth trial wherewith Abraham was tried, and he was found faithful, patient in spirit. The burial negotiation is the tenth trial, and its verdict is faithfulness and patience in spirit. — R. H. Charles

9 COMMENTARY: He said not a single word regarding the rumour in the land how that God had said that he would give it to him and to his seed after him, and he begged a place there to bury his dead. JFB on Genesis 23:3 notes Abraham's life of faith, his pilgrim state, had prevented him acquiring even so small a possession; he does not invoke the promise to claim the land, but begs a burial place. — R. H. Charles; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: This verse completes the verdict: he was found faithful, and was recorded on the heavenly tables as the friend of God. JFB on Genesis 25:7 calls Abraham 'the father of the faithful,' 'the friend of God'; here the tables record him as the friend of God. — R. H. Charles; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: In the fourth year thereof he took a wife for his son Isaac, and her name was Rebecca, the daughter of Bethuel, the son of Nahor, the brother of Abraham, the sister of Laban and daughter of Bethuel; and Bethuel was the son of Melca, who was the wife of Nahor, the brother of Abraham. JFB on Genesis 24:1-3 notes Abraham's anxiety to see his son married was natural to a pastoral chief preserving the honor of his tribe, and still more to a patriarch with regard to the divine promise of a numerous posterity, with matrimonial arrangements made by parents within the tribe, from a higher motive: fear lest a Canaanitish marriage lead the son away from the true God; Rebekah's genealogy places her within Abraham's own kin. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Abraham took to himself a third wife, and her name was Keturah, from among the daughters of his household servants, for Hagar had died before Sarah. JFB on Genesis 25:1 notes 'Abraham took a wife' is rather 'had taken,' for Keturah is called Abraham's concubine or secondary wife, and it is improbable he married after Sarah's death; Jubilees makes her a third wife from his household servants' daughters, after Hagar's death before Sarah's. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: She bare him six sons, Zimram, and Jokshan, and Medan, and Midian, and Ishbak, and Shuah, in the two weeks of years. The six sons of Keturah are named, borne within two weeks of years, matching the six more sons promised in chapter 16. — R. H. Charles

14 COMMENTARY: In the sixth week, in the second year thereof, Rebecca bare to Isaac two sons, Jacob and Esau, and Jacob was a smooth and upright man, and Esau was fierce, a man of the field, and hairy, and Jacob dwelt in tents. The twins are contrasted from birth: Jacob smooth and upright, dwelling in tents, against Esau fierce, hairy, a man of the field. — R. H. Charles

15 COMMENTARY: The youths grew, and Jacob learned to write, but Esau did not learn, for he was a man of the field and a hunter, and he learnt war, and all his deeds were fierce. Jacob's learning is writing; Esau's learning is war, and all his deeds are fierce. — R. H. Charles

16 COMMENTARY: This unit is the heading for the narrative that follows: Abraham loves Jacob and blesses him. It divides the twins' growth from Abraham's blessing of Jacob. — R. H. Charles

17 COMMENTARY: Abraham loved Jacob, but Isaac loved Esau. The parental loves divide: Abraham to Jacob, Isaac to Esau. — R. H. Charles

18 COMMENTARY: Abraham saw the deeds of Esau, and he knew that in Jacob should his name and seed be called; and he called Rebecca and gave commandment regarding Jacob, for he knew that she too loved Jacob much more than Esau. Abraham reads Esau's deeds, knows the calling is in Jacob, and charges Rebekah, who loves Jacob more. — R. H. Charles

19 COMMENTARY: He said unto her: 'My daughter, watch over my son Jacob, for he shall be in my stead on the earth, and for a blessing in the midst of the children of men.' The charge opens: watch over Jacob, who shall stand in Abraham's stead on earth, a blessing among the children of men. — R. H. Charles

20 COMMENTARY: This verse completes the charge's opening: 'and for the glory of the whole seed of Shem.' Jacob is a blessing among men and the glory of Shem's whole seed. — R. H. Charles

21 COMMENTARY: 'For I know that the Lord will choose him to be a people for possession unto himself, above all peoples that are upon the face of the earth.' The Lord will choose Jacob as a people for his own possession above all peoples on earth. — R. H. Charles

22 COMMENTARY: 'And behold, Isaac my son loveth Esau more than Jacob, but I see that thou truly lovest Jacob.' Abraham names the divided loves plainly: Isaac loves Esau more, Rebekah truly loves Jacob. — R. H. Charles

23 COMMENTARY: 'Add still further to thy kindness to him, and let thine eyes be upon him in love.' Rebekah is charged to add kindness to Jacob and keep her eyes on him in love. — R. H. Charles

24 COMMENTARY: 'For he will be a blessing unto us on the earth from henceforth unto all generations of the earth.' Jacob will be a blessing on earth henceforth unto all generations. — R. H. Charles

25 COMMENTARY: 'Let thy hands be strong.' Rebekah's hands are to be strong in the charge over Jacob. — R. H. Charles

26 COMMENTARY: 'And let thy heart rejoice in thy son Jacob; for I have loved him far beyond all my sons.' Her heart is to rejoice in Jacob, whom Abraham has loved far beyond all his sons. — R. H. Charles

27 COMMENTARY: 'He will be blessed for ever.' Jacob's blessing is forever. — R. H. Charles

28 COMMENTARY: 'And his seed will fill the whole earth.' Jacob's seed will fill the whole earth. — R. H. Charles

29 COMMENTARY: 'If a man can number the sand of the earth, his seed also will be numbered.' The sand of the earth gives the measure: if it can be numbered, so can Jacob's seed. — R. H. Charles

30 COMMENTARY: 'And all the blessings wherewith the Lord hath blessed me and my seed shall belong to Jacob and his seed alway.' All Abraham's blessings pass to Jacob and his seed always. — R. H. Charles

31 COMMENTARY: 'And in his seed shall my name be blessed, and the name of my fathers, Shem, and.' The names blessed in Jacob's seed begin: Abraham's own name, then his fathers, beginning with Shem. — R. H. Charles

32 COMMENTARY: This verse completes the fathers' names: 'Noah, and Enoch, and Mahalalel, and Enos, and Seth, and Adam.' The line runs from Shem through Noah, Enoch, Mahalalel, Enos and Seth back to Adam. — R. H. Charles

33 COMMENTARY: 'And these shall serve.' The fathers named shall serve, in the work the next verses unfold. — R. H. Charles

34 COMMENTARY: 'To lay the foundations of the heaven.' Their service is to lay the foundations of heaven. — R. H. Charles

35 COMMENTARY: 'And to strengthen the earth.' Their service is to strengthen the earth. — R. H. Charles

36 COMMENTARY: 'And to renew all the luminaries which are in the firmament.' Their service is to renew all the luminaries in the firmament. — R. H. Charles

37 COMMENTARY: He called Jacob before the eyes of Rebecca his mother, and kissed him, and blessed him, and said: 'Jacob, my beloved son, whom my soul loveth, may God bless thee from above the firmament, and may he give thee all the blessings wherewith he blessed Adam, and Enoch, and Noah, and Shem; and all the things of which he told me, and all the things which he promised to give me, may he cause to cleave to thee and to thy seed for ever, according to the days of heaven above the earth.' Before Rebekah's eyes, with a kiss, Abraham blesses Jacob with all the blessings of Adam, Enoch, Noah and Shem, and all God told and promised to Abraham, cleaving to Jacob and his seed forever, according to the days of heaven above earth. — R. H. Charles

38 COMMENTARY: 'And the spirits of Mastema shall not rule over thee or over thy seed to turn thee from the Lord, who is thy God from henceforth for ever.' The spirits of Mastema shall not rule over Jacob or his seed to turn them from the Lord, their God henceforth forever. — R. H. Charles

39 COMMENTARY: 'And may the Lord God be a father to thee and thou the first-born son, and to the people alway.' The Lord God is to be a father to Jacob, and Jacob the first-born son, to the people always. — R. H. Charles

40 COMMENTARY: 'Go in peace, my son.' The blessing closes with dismissal in peace. — R. H. Charles

41 COMMENTARY: And they both went forth together from Abraham. Rebekah and Jacob go forth together from Abraham's presence. — R. H. Charles

42 COMMENTARY: And Rebecca loved Jacob, with all her heart and with all her soul, very much more than Esau; but Isaac loved Esau much more than Jacob. The divided loves are restated with full force: Rebekah loves Jacob with all heart and soul, Isaac loves Esau much more. — R. H. Charles

43 COMMENTARY: This unit is the heading for the narrative that follows: Abraham's last words to his children and grandchildren. It divides the blessing of Jacob from the farewell charge of chapter 20. — R. H. Charles
