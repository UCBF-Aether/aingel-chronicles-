Study Notes — Jubilees

Chapter 29

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: When Rachel had borne Joseph, Laban went to shear his sheep three days' journey away. The shearing gave Jacob the opening to depart, since Laban's attention and household were elsewhere. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Jacob saw that Laban was going to shear his sheep, and took the moment to act. Henry notes that in all our removals we should have respect to the command and promise of God, and Jacob's departure followed the dream-command to return. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Jacob called Leah and Rachel and spoke kindly to them, asking them to come with him to Canaan. Henry stresses that Jacob's wives were consulted and won over, not carried off without a word, and that the family's affairs are related minutely because the Bible teaches the common duties of life. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Jacob told his wives how he had seen in a dream all that God had spoken to him about returning to his father's house, and they answered that wherever he went they would go with him. Henry notes that remembering favoured seasons of communion with God refreshes us in difficulties, and that Jacob's household followed the command and promise together. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Jacob blessed the God of Isaac and Abraham, mounted his wives and children, took all his possessions, crossed the river, and came to Gilead, hiding his intention from Laban. Henry observes that selfish men like Laban consider themselves robbed of all that goes past them, and that covetousness swallows up natural affection. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: In the seventh year of the fourth week Jacob turned his face toward Gilead on the twenty-first of the first month. Jubilees dates the departure exactly, marking it as a fixed point in its chronology of weeks. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Laban pursued Jacob and overtook him in the mountain of Gilead on the thirteenth of the third month. Henry notes that God can put a bridle in the mouth of wicked men to restrain their malice, though he does not change their hearts. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: The Lord did not suffer Laban to injure Jacob, for he appeared to Laban in a dream by night. Henry's point stands: God restrained Laban's malice, and Jacob's cause was committed to God rather than to his own strength. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Laban spoke to Jacob, beginning the parley in which he charged Jacob with things he knew not. Henry notes that those who commit their cause to God are not forbidden to plead it themselves with meekness and fear, as Jacob did. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: On the fifteenth of those days Jacob made a feast for Laban and all who came with him, and they swore to each other that neither would cross the mountain of Gilead against the other with evil purpose. Henry notes that covenants of friendship in ancient times were ratified by the parties eating and drinking together, and that peace with God puts true comfort into peace with friends. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: A heap was made for a witness, and the place was therefore called the Heap of Witness. Henry notes that a heap of stones was raised to keep up the memory of the event, writing being then not known or little used, and that God is judge between contending parties. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: Jacob sent Laban away, and Laban departed into Mesopotamia while Jacob returned to the land of Gilead. Henry observes that after the angry parley they parted friends, and that God is often better to us than our fears. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Jacob passed over the Jabbok on the eleventh of the ninth month. Henry's account of this crossing sets the scene for what followed: Jacob, having committed his case to God by prayer, went on his way, and the angels of God appeared to encourage him with the assurance of divine protection. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: On that day Esau came to Jacob, was reconciled to him, and departed to Seir, while Jacob dwelt in tents. Henry notes that Esau embraced Jacob, that God has the hearts of all men in his hands and can turn them, and that when a man's ways please the Lord he makes even his enemies to be at peace with him. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: In the first year of the fifth week of this jubilee Jacob crossed the Jordan, dwelt beyond it, and pastured his sheep from the sea of the heap to Bethshan, Dothan, and the forest of Akrabbim. Henry notes that Jacob did not content himself with words of thanks for God's favour but gave real thanks, keeping up religion and the worship of God in his family wherever he pitched. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: In the days when Jacob went to Mesopotamia, Esau took Mahalath, the daughter of Ishmael, as his wife, gathered his father's flocks and his wives, and went to dwell on Mount Seir, leaving Isaac alone at the Well of the Oath. Henry notes that good examples impress even the profane, but Esau thought by pleasing his parents in one thing to atone for other wrongdoings. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: Isaac went up from the Well of the Oath and dwelt in the tower of Abraham his father on the mountains of Hebron. The move placed Isaac in the seat of his father's sojourn, apart from Esau, awaiting the gifts his son would send. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: Jacob sent to his father and mother from time to time all they needed, and they blessed Jacob with all their heart and soul. The gifts were the fruit of Jacob's prosperity in Haran, returned in honour to the parents who had sent him away blessed. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 12, 13, 14, 15, 20, 21, 22, 23 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
