Study Notes — Jubilees

Chapter 26

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Isaac, now old and dim of sight, set out to bless his elder son before he died. Henry notes that Isaac meant to put the honour of the promise on Esau, without duly weighing the divine word that the elder should serve the younger. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Isaac sent Esau hunting for venison to make the savoury meat he loved, so that he might eat and bless him before death. The blessing Isaac planned was the solemn passing on of the covenant promise to the next head of the family. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Rebecca overheard Isaac speaking to Esau. Henry stresses that she knew the blessing was intended for Jacob, yet chose a crooked way to secure it rather than leaving the matter to God. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Esau went out early to hunt and bring home what his father had asked. His prompt obedience makes the deception that follows all the sharper, since the blessing was sought behind his back. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Rebecca told Jacob what she had heard and ordered him to fetch two good kids so she could prepare the savoury meat Isaac loved. Henry warns that she wronged Isaac by the cheat, wronged Jacob by tempting him, and put a stumbling-block in Esau's way. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Jacob hesitated, fearing his father would recognise his voice and wish to touch him. His fear shows he knew the plan depended on deceiving a blind old man, not on any honest claim. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Jacob pointed out the plain difference between himself and his hairy brother, fearing he would seem an evildoer and bring a curse instead of a blessing. Henry's warning applies: we must not do evil that good may come, even to further a divine promise. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: Rebecca answered Jacob's fear by taking the risk on herself. Henry calls her words a very rash speech, too daring for any creature to say of the curse of God. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Rebecca's words, that any curse should fall on her, were meant to quiet Jacob's conscience. Henry notes that Christ alone has borne the curse of the law for those who take up his yoke; no creature may claim that place. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Jacob obeyed his mother, fetched two good fat kids, and brought them to her to prepare as Isaac loved. Obedience to a parent did not make the deceit right, and Henry observes that all parties in the affair were to be blamed. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Rebecca dressed Jacob in Esau's goodly raiment and covered his hands and neck with kid skins, so that touch and smell would both point to Esau. Every sense was enlisted in the disguise, which is why Henry calls the whole device a cheat. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Rebecca put the prepared meat and bread into Jacob's hands to carry to his father. The meal was a mother's cooking passed off as a hunter's catch, the centrepiece of the deception. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Jacob went in claiming to be Esau and to have done as his father bade him, asking Isaac to eat so that he might bless him. Henry notes that Jacob gained his point only with difficulty, and by falsehood. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Isaac wondered how his son had found game so quickly. The question was a natural one, and it gave Jacob the chance to turn back from the lie, which he did not take. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Jacob answered that the Lord Isaac's God had caused him to find the game. Henry's account of this chapter warns against using God's name to cover wrong means, since the end never justifies them. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: Isaac proposed the test of touch, to learn whether the man before him was Esau or not. His blindness forced him to judge by hands rather than eyes, and the disguise was made to meet exactly that test. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Isaac felt Jacob and said the voice was Jacob's voice but the hands were Esau's hands, yet he did not discern him. Henry observes that Isaac's attachment to Esau, set against the mind of God, had weakened his own faith and perception in the matter. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Isaac asked directly whether the man before him was his son Esau. The direct question pressed Jacob to confirm the falsehood in plain words. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Jacob answered that he was Isaac's son, and Isaac asked him to bring the food near so he might eat and bless him. The blessing was now within reach of the deception, though not yet given. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Isaac's request to eat of what his son had caught set the meal before the blessing, as he had planned with Esau. In the patriarchal order, the father's eating and blessing went together as one solemn act. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Jacob brought the food near, Isaac ate, and Jacob brought wine which he drank. The shared meal sealed the scene: Isaac believed he was eating Esau's venison and was ready to bless. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: Isaac called Jacob near to kiss him, adding the test of smell to the tests of voice and touch. Each test was met by the disguise, and Henry notes how God overruled even these bad actions to fulfil his purposes. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: Jacob came near and kissed his father. The kiss of a son, given under a false name, completed the deception that the blessing would then ratify. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Isaac smelled the raiment and blessed Jacob, saying his smell was as the smell of a full field which the Lord had blessed. Henry notes the blessing was given in very general terms, with no mention of the distinguishing mercies of the covenant with Abraham. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: The blessing promised the dew of heaven and of earth, with plenty of corn and oil. These are the fruits of a favoured land, the outward plenty that attends God's blessing on the heir of the promise. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: The blessing continued that nations should serve Jacob and peoples bow down to him. Henry reads this dominion as the lot appointed to Jacob's seed, a sovereignty that would reach beyond his own household. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: Jacob was made lord over his brethren, reversing the natural order of elder and younger. The words confirmed what God had said before the twins were born, that the elder should serve the younger. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: His mother's sons were to bow down to him, binding the whole family under Jacob's headship. The blessing thus settled the leadership of the house, not merely a share of its goods. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Isaac prayed that all the blessings given to him and to Abraham his father might be imparted to Jacob and his seed for ever. This clause tied Jacob to the covenant line, which is why Henry stresses that Jacob's privilege was to convey spiritual blessings to all nations. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: The blessing closed with the ancient formula, cursed be he that curseth thee and blessed be he that blesseth thee. The same words guarded Abraham, and now they guarded Jacob as Abraham's heir. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: As soon as Isaac had finished blessing Jacob, Jacob went out and hid himself, and Esau came in from his hunting. The timing is the hinge of the story: the blessing was given and could not be taken back. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: Esau also made savoury meat and brought it to his father, asking him to arise, eat, and bless him. He came in full confidence that the blessing was still waiting for him. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: Isaac asked who Esau was, and Esau answered that he was the first-born, who had done as commanded. Henry notes that Isaac, made sensible of the deceit practised on him, trembled exceedingly. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: Isaac, greatly astonished, asked who had hunted and brought food before Esau came, and confirmed that the one he had blessed would indeed be blessed, with all his seed for ever. Henry stresses that Isaac soon recovered and confirmed the blessing, saying he had blessed him and he shall be blessed. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: When Esau heard his father's words he cried with an exceeding great and bitter cry, begging a blessing for himself also. Henry warns that those who sell their title to spiritual blessings for what is of no value will one day ask urgently for them in vain. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: Isaac told Esau that his brother had come with guile and taken away the blessing. The word guile names the means plainly: the blessing was gained by deceit, even though God overruled it. — Matthew Henry

37 COMMENTARY: Esau said he now knew why his brother was named Jacob, for he had supplanted him twice, taking first the birthright and now the blessing. The name Jacob, supplanter, had proved painfully true in Esau's experience. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: Esau asked whether his father had reserved no blessing for him. The question shows he still hoped some part of the covenant blessing might remain to be given. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: Isaac answered Esau, beginning the lesser blessing that remained. What followed was a common blessing of earthly good, not the covenant blessing already given to Jacob. — Matthew Henry

40 COMMENTARY: Isaac told Esau that he had made Jacob his lord, a settled fact that could not be undone. The headship of the family had passed, and Esau's blessing had to be framed within it. — Matthew Henry

41 COMMENTARY: All Jacob's brethren had been given to him for servants, completing the transfer of dominion. Henry notes that Esau's blessing, unlike Jacob's, contained nothing pointing to Christ, and earthly fatness alone would stand him in little stead. — Matthew Henry

42 COMMENTARY: Isaac added that Jacob had been strengthened with plenty of corn, wine, and oil, the same plenty named in Jacob's blessing. The store of the blessing was already spent on Jacob. — Matthew Henry

43 COMMENTARY: Isaac asked what he could now do for Esau, his son. The question is not unkindness but the plain arithmetic of a blessing already given away entire. — Matthew Henry

44 COMMENTARY: Esau spoke again to his father, pressing his plea. His persistence shows how deeply he felt the loss once it was irreversible. — Matthew Henry

45 COMMENTARY: Esau asked whether his father had but one blessing, and begged to be blessed also. Henry observes that faint desires of happiness, without right choice of the end and right use of the means, deceive many to their ruin. — Matthew Henry

46 COMMENTARY: Esau lifted up his voice and wept. His tears were real grief for a lost inheritance, though Henry warns they came after he had himself despised the best things. — Matthew Henry

47 COMMENTARY: Isaac answered Esau with the blessing that remained to him. What follows is a genuine blessing of a lesser kind, not a curse, though it lacks the covenant promise. — Matthew Henry

48 COMMENTARY: Esau's dwelling was to be far from the dew of the earth, the reverse of Jacob's fruitful portion. His lot would be a harder land, away from the fatness promised to his brother. — Matthew Henry

49 COMMENTARY: Esau was also to be far from the dew of heaven from above, completing the picture of a drier, less favoured dwelling. The blessing measured his portion against Jacob's at every point. — Matthew Henry

50 COMMENTARY: Esau would live by his sword, a life of arms and roaming rather than settled plenty. Henry notes this as the common blessing Esau desired, earthly strength without the covenant's spiritual promise. — Matthew Henry

51 COMMENTARY: Esau would serve his brother, confirming the elder's subjection to the younger. The service was the appointed order, however unwelcome, and it matched the word spoken before their birth. — Matthew Henry

52 COMMENTARY: Isaac added that when Esau became great he would shake his brother's yoke from off his neck. The yoke would not last for ever: Esau's descendants would in time break free of Jacob's dominion. — Matthew Henry

53 COMMENTARY: The shaking off of the yoke marked the limit of Jacob's dominion over Esau in Isaac's blessing. Henry reads Esau's blessing as a whole as a common, earthly portion, without the promise of Christ that made Jacob's blessing great. — Matthew Henry

56 COMMENTARY: Esau kept threatening Jacob because of the blessing, and said in his heart that he would slay his brother once the days of mourning for his father had come. Henry compares Esau here to Cain, who slew his brother because he had gained the acceptance of which he had rendered himself unworthy. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 54, 55 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
