Study Notes — Jubilees

Chapter 48

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: In the sixth year of the third week of the forty-ninth jubilee Moses departed and dwelt in Midian five weeks and one year. Henry notes that Moses was obliged to flee into Midian, that God ordered this for wise and holy ends, and that his life falls into three forties: prince in Egypt, shepherd in Midian, and king in Jeshurun. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Moses returned into Egypt in the second week, in the second year, in the fiftieth jubilee. Henry notes that after God appeared in the bush, he often spoke to Moses, and that Moses left Midian with God's message to Pharaoh, to demand Israel's discharge. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Moses himself knew what God spoke to him on Mount Sinai, and what prince Mastema desired to do with him when returning into Egypt, meeting him at the lodging-place. Henry notes that God met Moses in anger at the lodging-place, threatening him with death for neglecting to circumcise his son, and that when God shows us what is amiss, we must amend it speedily. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Mastema sought with all his power to slay Moses and deliver the Egyptians out of his hand, when he saw Moses was sent to execute judgment and vengeance on the Egyptians. Henry's account of the lodging-place anger fits Jubilees' reading: a spiritual adversary sought Moses' death on the very road of his mission. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: The angel delivered Moses out of Mastema's hand, and Moses performed the signs and wonders he was sent to perform in Egypt against Pharaoh, his house, his servants, and his people. Henry notes that God gave Moses power to work miracles, so that the people would take his word, and that Moses, strengthened in faith, went about his work with courage and perseverance. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: The Lord executed great vengeance on Egypt for Israel's sake, smiting them through blood, frogs, lice, dog-flies, malignant boils breaking forth in blains, cattle by death, hail-stones destroying all that grew, locusts devouring the residue left by hail, darkness, the death of the first-born of men and animals, and on all their idols he took vengeance and burned them with fire. Henry calls the first plague, the river turned to blood, a righteous plague, since the Egyptians had stained the river with Hebrew children's blood, and notes that the last plague slew the first-born from throne to dungeon, because they had slain the Hebrews' children. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Everything was sent through Moses' hand, that he should declare these things before they were done. Henry notes that Moses, as Jehovah's ambassador, laid commands upon Pharaoh, denounced threatenings, and called for judgments upon him, declaring God's word before God's hand struck. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: Moses was to declare the judgments before they were done, and he spoke with the king of Egypt before all his servants and before his people. The public declaration is the point: God's true miracles were not performed in corners, but in the sight of Pharaoh and his attendants. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Everything took place according to Moses' words, and ten great and terrible judgments came on Egypt, that he might execute vengeance on it for Israel. Henry counts the plagues as ten, beginning with blood and ending with the first-born, each a righteous judgment on the oppressors. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: The Lord did everything for Israel's sake, according to his covenant with Abraham, that he would take vengeance on Egypt as they had brought Israel by force into bondage. Henry notes that God remembered his covenant, of which he is ever mindful, and looked upon Israel to help them, and that Exodus plainly fulfils the promises and prophecies made to Abraham respecting his seed. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: The prince of Mastema stood up against Moses, sought to cast him into Pharaoh's hands, and helped the Egyptian sorcerers, who stood up and wrought before him. Henry notes that the magicians were cheats trying to copy Moses' real miracles by secret sleights, succeeding to a small extent so as to deceive bystanders, and that God often permits a snare wherein the disobedient are entangled. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: The evils indeed were permitted to the sorcerers to work, but the remedies were not allowed to be wrought by their hands. Henry's account agrees: the magicians could imitate to a point, but were at length obliged to confess they could no longer imitate the effects of divine power. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: The Lord smote the sorcerers with malignant ulcers, and they were not able to stand, for they were destroyed so that they could not perform a single sign. Henry notes the same defeat: the counterfeit wonders failed, and the magicians confessed they could not imitate divine power, for Satan is most to be dreaded when transformed into an angel of light, yet his imitations always fail at last. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Notwithstanding all these signs and wonders, the prince of Mastema was not put to shame, but took courage and cried to the Egyptians to pursue Israel with all Egypt's powers, chariots, horses, and hosts of the peoples of Egypt. Henry notes that Pharaoh, having let Israel go, was angry with himself for it, and that God would be honoured upon Pharaoh, whose envy and rage became a torment to himself. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: The angel stood between the Egyptians and Israel, and delivered Israel out of Mastema's hand and his people's hand, and the Lord brought them through the midst of the sea as if it were dry land. Henry notes that the pillar of cloud and fire came behind Israel where they needed a guard, a wall between them and their enemies, and that they walked upon dry land in the midst of the sea. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: All the peoples brought to pursue Israel, the Lord cast into the midst of the sea, into the depths of the abyss beneath Israel, even as the Egyptians had cast Israel's children into the river. Henry notes the just and righteous wrath of God upon his people's enemies: the waters returned and overwhelmed all Pharaoh's host, not one escaping, and the ruin of sinners is brought on by their own rage and presumption. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Mastema hardened the Egyptians' hearts and made them stubborn, and the device was devised by the Lord God that he might smite the Egyptians and cast them into the sea. Henry notes that Pharaoh had hardened his own heart against the oppressed, and now God, in righteous judgment, hardened his heart against the teaching of the miracles, so that he would not let Israel alone and fell with his host. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: On the fourteenth the angel bound Mastema, that he might not accuse Israel on the day when they asked the Egyptians for vessels and garments, silver, gold, and bronze, in order to despoil the Egyptians in return for the bondage they had forced them to serve. Henry notes that the Egyptians, in terror, would purchase Israel's favour and speedy departure, and that the Lord took care that their hard-earned wages should be paid and the people provided for their journey. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: Israel was not led forth from Egypt empty-handed. Henry notes the same provision: the Egyptians furnished Israel with riches at their departure, and the people who had served in bondage left with the wages of their years. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 17, 18, 19, 20 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
