Study Notes — Jubilees

Chapter 43

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The steward did as Joseph had told him, filling all their sacks with food, putting their money in their sacks, and putting the cup in Benjamin's sack. The gifts and the trap travel together: generosity on the surface, testing underneath. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Early in the morning the brothers departed, and Joseph told the steward to pursue them, seize them, and charge them with requiting good with evil by stealing the silver cup out of which his lord drank, and to bring back the youngest brother quickly before Joseph went forth to his seat of judgment. Henry notes that Joseph tried how his brethren felt towards Benjamin, to see whether they would leave him to be a slave as they had once left Joseph. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: The steward ran after them and spoke to them according to Joseph's words. The pursuit was swift and the charge was grave, the steward acting as Joseph's voice before the brothers knew Joseph's face. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: The brothers answered that God forbid they should steal any utensil from their lord's house, and that the money found in their sacks the first time they had brought back from Canaan. Henry notes that they threw themselves on Joseph's mercy while acknowledging the righteousness of God, perhaps thinking of the injury done to Joseph for which God was now reckoning with them. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: They asked how they could steal any utensil, having returned the money before. The argument is from the lesser to the greater: men who returned found money would not steal a lord's cup. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: They offered their sacks to be searched, saying wherever the cup was found, let that man be slain, and they and their asses would serve Joseph's lord. The rash offer, made in innocence of the trap, would have condemned Benjamin to death if Joseph had held them to it. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: The steward answered that it would not be so: only the man with whom the cup was found would be taken as a servant, and the rest would return in peace to their house. The milder term is itself part of the test: Benjamin alone would be kept, and the brothers could walk away free. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: As the steward searched their vessels, beginning with the eldest and ending with the youngest, the cup was found in Benjamin's sack. Henry notes the design: when the cup was found on Benjamin, the brothers would have a pretext for leaving him to be a slave, if their hearts were still what they had been. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: The brothers rent their garments, laded their asses, and returned to the city, coming to Joseph's house and bowing on their faces to the ground before him. The rent garments are grief, not for the cup, but for Benjamin and for their father, and the return to the city proves they would not abandon the youngest. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Joseph said to them, ye have done evil. The short charge, spoken by the lord of Egypt, is the judgment the brothers feared, and it opens their confession. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: The brothers spoke, asking what they should say and how they should defend themselves. The question is the voice of men who know they cannot prove a negative, and who throw themselves on mercy. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: They said their lord had discovered the transgression of his servants, and that they and their asses were his servants. Henry notes that even in afflictions where we believe ourselves wronged by men, we must own that God is righteous and finds out our sin, and the brothers' words own a transgression deeper than the cup. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: The confession is completed: they and their asses are their lord's servants, surrendering all rather than leaving Benjamin. The surrender is the opposite of their old bargain over Joseph, and it is the evidence Joseph sought. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Joseph answered that he too feared the Lord, and that they should go to their homes while their brother remained his servant, for they had done evil. The offer repeats the test's terms: freedom for ten, slavery for one, and the brothers' answer will decide everything. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Joseph asked whether they did not know that a man delights in his cup as he delighted in this cup. Henry notes the steward's charge that the cup was of daily use and would soon be missed, and that diligent search would be made for it. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: Joseph charged them again with stealing the cup from him. The repetition presses the brothers: the evidence is against Benjamin, the terms allow their escape, and only Judah's plea stands between Benjamin and slavery. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Judah said, let his servant speak a word in his lord's ear: his mother bore two brothers to their father, one went away and was lost and not found, and Benjamin alone was left of his mother, and their father loved him, and his father's life was bound up with the lad's life. Henry calls Judah's reasoning powerful: even a stranger could not but be wrought upon by it, and Judah's faithful cleaving to Benjamin in distress would later be recompensed when Benjamin's tribe kept with Judah's. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Judah warned that when they returned to their father without the lad, he would die, and they would bring down their father with sorrow to death. The father's death is the stake Judah lays before the lord: Benjamin's slavery would kill Jacob. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Judah offered himself instead: let him abide as bondsman to his lord, and let the lad go with his brethren, for he became surety for Benjamin to his father, and if he did not bring him back he would bear the blame for ever. Henry notes the parallel Henry draws to Christ: Judah not only interceded for the transgressors but became a surety for them, testifying tender concern both for his father and his brethren. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Joseph saw that they were all accordant in goodness one with another, and he could not refrain himself, and told them he was Joseph. Henry notes that Joseph found his brethren humbled for their sins, mindful of himself, respectful to their father, and tender of Benjamin, and now they were ripe for the comfort he designed by making himself known. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Joseph conversed with them in the Hebrew tongue, fell on their neck, and wept. Henry notes that Joseph shed tears of tenderness and strong affection, throwing off the austerity with which he had behaved toward them, a picture of divine compassion toward returning penitents. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: The brothers knew him not at first and began to weep. Their weeping is the shock of recognition deferred: the lord of Egypt weeps over them in their own tongue, and they do not yet know why. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: Joseph told them not to weep over him, but to hasten and bring his father to him, for they saw it was his mouth speaking and Benjamin's eyes saw it. Henry notes the words, I am Joseph, your brother, which humbled them yet more for selling him but encouraged them to hope for kind treatment. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Joseph told them this was the second year of the famine, with five years still to come without harvest, fruit of trees, or ploughing. The count of years is the practical ground of the invitation: the famine would outlast any store they could carry home. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: Joseph urged them to come down quickly with their households, so that they would not perish through the famine. Henry notes that Joseph promised to take care of his father and all the family, and that it is children's duty to support their parents to the utmost of their ability. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: Joseph told them not to be grieved for their possessions, for the Lord had sent him before them to set things in order that many people might live. Henry notes the heart of Joseph's comfort: whatever they thought to do against him, God had brought good out of it, and sinners must still grieve for their sins, though God brings good from them. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: Joseph told them to tell his father he was still alive, and that the Lord had made him as a father to Pharaoh and ruler over his house and all Egypt. The message reverses twenty years of mourning with a single report: the lost son rules the land of their hunger. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: Joseph told them to tell his father of all his glory and riches that the Lord had given him. The glory is reported not as boast but as proof: the father must believe the messenger, and the riches are the evidence. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: By Pharaoh's command Joseph gave them chariots and provisions for the way, and gave them all many-coloured raiment and silver. Henry notes that Pharaoh was kind to Joseph and his relations for his sake, and that Egypt would make up the losses of their removal. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: Joseph sent to his father raiment and silver and ten asses carrying corn, and sent his brothers away. The gifts to the father are the earnest of the provision to come, corn for the journey and wealth for the house. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: The brothers went up and told their father that Joseph was alive, measuring out corn to all nations, and ruling over all Egypt. Henry notes that to hear Joseph was alive was too good news to be true, and that Jacob fainted, for he believed it not. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: Their father did not believe it, for he was beside himself in his mind, but when he saw the wagons Joseph had sent, his spirit revived, and he said it was enough if Joseph lived, and he would go down and see him before he died. Henry notes that Jacob was old and did not expect to live long, but wished his eyes refreshed with this sight before they closed, and then he needed no more to be happy in this world. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 33, 34 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
