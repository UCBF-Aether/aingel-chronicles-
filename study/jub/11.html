Study Notes — Jubilees

Chapter 11

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: In the thirty-fifth jubilee, in the third week, in the first year thereof, Reu took 'Ora, daughter of 'Ur, son of Kesed, to wife, and she bare him a son. The genealogy continues from Peleg to Reu, married into the Kesed line of the Chaldees. — R. H. Charles

2 COMMENTARY: He called his name Seroh in the seventh year of this week in this jubilee. The naming is dated to the seventh year, and verse 8 will explain the name as Serug. — R. H. Charles

3 COMMENTARY: The sons of Noah began to war on each other, to take captive and slay each other, shed blood, eat blood, build strong cities, walls and towers, and individuals began to exalt themselves above the nation, found the beginnings of kingdoms, go to war people against people, nation against nation, city against city, all doing evil, acquiring arms, teaching their sons war, capturing cities, and selling male and female slaves. The post-Babel world is war, kingdoms and slavery: the errors from which Abram will separate himself. — R. H. Charles

4 COMMENTARY: 'Or, son of Kesed, built the city of 'Ara of the Chaldees, and called its name after his own name and the name of his father. City-founding continues the pattern of Enoch, Ham, Japheth and Shem: the city bears its founder's and his father's names. — R. H. Charles

5 COMMENTARY: They made for themselves molten images, and worshipped each the idol, the molten image they had made for themselves, and began to make graven images and unclean simulacra, and malignant spirits assisted and seduced them into transgression and uncleanness. Idolatry is not mere craft: malignant spirits assist and seduce the makers into transgression. — R. H. Charles

6 COMMENTARY: This verse completes the idolatry notice: malignant spirits seduced them into committing transgression and uncleanness. The spirits behind the images are the remaining tenth of chapter 10, at work through worship. — R. H. Charles

7 COMMENTARY: The prince Mastema exerted himself to do all this, and sent forth other spirits, those put under his hand, to do all manner of wrong and sin, all manner of transgression, to corrupt and destroy, and to shed blood upon the earth. Mastema directs the spirits under his hand into universal wrong, corruption, destruction and bloodshed. — R. H. Charles

8 COMMENTARY: For this reason he called the name of Seroh, Serug, for everyone turned to do all manner of sin and transgression. Serug's name marks the universal turning: in his days everyone turned to sin and transgression. — R. H. Charles

9 COMMENTARY: He grew up and dwelt in Ur of the Chaldees, near to the father of his wife's mother, and he worshipped idols, and he took to himself a wife in the thirty-sixth jubilee, in the fifth week, in the first year thereof, Melka, daughter of Kaber, daughter of his father's brother. JFB on Genesis 11:28 notes Ur, now Orfa, meaning 'light' or 'fire,' was devoted to fire-worship rites, and Terah's family was equally infected with that idolatry as the rest of the inhabitants; Serug worships idols in that city. — R. H. Charles; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: She bare him Nahor in the first year of this week, and he grew and dwelt in Ur of the Chaldees, and his father taught him the researches of the Chaldees to divine and augur according to the signs of heaven. Nahor is taught Chaldean divination and augury by heavenly signs, the Watcher astrology of chapter 8 now as a learned science. — R. H. Charles

11 COMMENTARY: In the thirty-seventh jubilee, in the sixth week, in the first year thereof, he took to himself a wife, 'Ijaska, daughter of Nestag of the Chaldees. Nahor's marriage continues the Chaldean line toward Terah. — R. H. Charles

12 COMMENTARY: She bare him Terah in the seventh year of this week. Terah's birth is dated to the seventh year, and the next verses will name him from the raven plague. — R. H. Charles

13 COMMENTARY: The prince Mastema sent ravens and birds to devour the seed sown in the land, in order to destroy the land and rob the children of men of their labours. Mastema's plague strikes at labour itself: ravens devour the sown seed to destroy the land's yield. — R. H. Charles

14 COMMENTARY: Before they could plough in the seed, the ravens picked it from the surface of the ground. The seed is taken before ploughing can hide it, so the loss is total. — R. H. Charles

15 COMMENTARY: For this reason he called his name Terah, because the ravens and the birds reduced them to destitution and devoured their seed. Terah's name comes from destitution by birds: the plague names the child born into it. — R. H. Charles

16 COMMENTARY: This verse opens the barren years: the years began to be barren owing to the birds, and they devoured all the fruit of the trees. The sentence is completed by verses 19-20's little saved fruit. — R. H. Charles

19 COMMENTARY: This verse continues the barren years: the birds devoured the fruit from the trees, and it was only with great effort that a little of all the fruit of the earth could be saved in their days. The plague leaves only a little, saved with great effort. — R. H. Charles

20 COMMENTARY: This verse completes the barren years: only a little of the fruit of the earth was saved in their days. The destitution of Terah's name is the condition of his generation. — R. H. Charles

21 COMMENTARY: In this thirty-ninth jubilee, in the second week in the first year, Terah took to himself a wife, 'Edna, daughter of 'Abram, daughter of his father's sister. Terah marries his father's sister's daughter, 'Edna, daughter of 'Abram. — R. H. Charles

22 COMMENTARY: This verse completes the marriage notice: 'Edna is the daughter of 'Abram, the daughter of Terah's father's sister. The marriage stays within the close kin of the family. — R. H. Charles

23 COMMENTARY: In the seventh year of this week she bare him a son, and he called his name Abram, by the name of the father of his mother, for he had died before his daughter had conceived a son. Abram is named for his maternal grandfather, who died before his daughter conceived. — R. H. Charles

24 COMMENTARY: This unit is the heading for the narrative that follows: Abram's knowledge of God and wonderful deeds. It divides the genealogy from Abram's youth, the turning point of the book. — R. H. Charles

25 COMMENTARY: The child began to understand the errors of the earth, that all went astray after graven images and after uncleanness, and his father taught him writing, and he was two weeks of years old, and he separated himself from his father that he might not worship idols with him. JFB on Genesis 12:1 calls Abraham's conversion one of the most remarkable in Bible history, God revealing himself to one who sought him not; Jubilees shows it beginning at fourteen, when Abram discerns the error and separates from his father's idol worship. — R. H. Charles; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: He began to pray to the Creator of all things that he might save him from the errors of the children of men, and that his portion should not fall into error after uncleanness and vileness. Abram's first prayer is to the Creator, asking deliverance from the universal error and a portion kept from uncleanness. — R. H. Charles

27 COMMENTARY: The seed time came for sowing seed upon the land, and they all went forth together to protect their seed against the ravens, and Abram went forth with those that went, and the child was a lad of fourteen years. At fourteen Abram joins the communal seed-guard against the raven plague of his father's generation. — R. H. Charles

28 COMMENTARY: A cloud of ravens came to devour the seed, and Abram ran to meet them before they settled on the ground, and cried to them before they settled on the ground to devour the seed, saying, 'Descend not: return to the place whence ye came.' Abram meets the cloud before it settles and commands its return, confronting Mastema's plague directly. — R. H. Charles

29 COMMENTARY: This verse completes Abram's command: the ravens proceeded to turn back. The commanded return is the first of the seventy turnings in the next verse. — R. H. Charles

30 COMMENTARY: He caused the clouds of ravens to turn back that day seventy times, and of all the ravens throughout all the land where Abram was there settled not so much as one. Seventy turnings-back leave not one raven settled where Abram stands. — R. H. Charles

31 COMMENTARY: All who were with him throughout all the land saw him cry out, and all the ravens turn back, and his name became great in all the land of the Chaldees. The public sign makes Abram's name great throughout Chaldea, before any call to leave it. — R. H. Charles

32 COMMENTARY: There came to him this year all those that wished to sow, and he went with them until the time of sowing ceased, and they sowed their land, and that year they brought enough grain home and ate and were satisfied. Abram's protection brings a satisfying harvest to all who come to him, reversing the destitution of Terah's years. — R. H. Charles

33 COMMENTARY: In the first year of the fifth week Abram taught those who made implements for oxen, the artificers in wood, and they made a vessel above the ground, facing the frame of the plough, in order to put the seed thereon, and the seed fell down therefrom upon the share of the plough, and was hidden in the earth, and they no longer feared the ravens. Abram's seed-box invention hides the seed in the earth at ploughing, so the ravens can no longer take it. — R. H. Charles

34 COMMENTARY: After this manner they made vessels above the ground on all the frames of the ploughs, and they sowed and tilled all the land according as Abram commanded them, and they no longer feared the birds. The invention spreads to all ploughs, and the fear of birds ends wherever Abram's command is followed. — R. H. Charles

No note for verse(s) 17, 18 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
