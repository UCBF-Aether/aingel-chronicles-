Study Notes — Jubilees

Chapter 30

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: In the first year of the sixth week Jacob went up to Salem, east of Shechem, in peace, in the fourth month. Henry notes that Jacob came to Shalem and built an altar there, keeping up religion and the worship of God in his family wherever he pitched. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Dinah, Jacob's daughter, was carried off into the house of Shechem, son of Hamor the Hivite, prince of the land, who lay with her and defiled her; she was a little girl of twelve. Henry warns that young persons are never so safe as under the care of pious parents, and that Dinah's gadding abroad among strangers to true religion exposed her to great danger. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Shechem besought his father and her brothers that Dinah might be given to him as wife. The request after the deed could not undo the defilement, and Henry traces the chain that follows: foolish pleasures lead to seduction, seduction to wrath, wrath to revenge. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Jacob and his sons were wroth because of the men of Shechem. The anger was shared by the whole house, since the shame touched the family name, not only the girl herself. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: This unit is not narrative but the translator's footnote: Charles explains that Jubilees omits all reference to the circumcision of the Shechemites because the author approves of Simeon and Levi's conduct and extols it. The note is essential to reading what follows, since it states the book's stance outright. — R. H. Charles

6 COMMENTARY: This unit continues Charles's footnote, contrasting Jubilees with Genesis: the brothers' conduct is severely reprobated in Genesis 49:5-7. The same deed is thus judged in opposite ways by the two books, and the reader should not mistake Jubilees' praise for the canonical verdict. — R. H. Charles

7 COMMENTARY: This unit closes Charles's footnote, suggesting the author's view reflects a bitter feeling against the people of Shechem prevailing in his time. The praise of the vengeance is therefore historically situated, not a timeless moral rule. — R. H. Charles

8 COMMENTARY: The sons spoke to the Shechemites with evil intent, dealing deceitfully and beguiling them. Henry's account of the chapter stresses the treachery: the Shechemites submitted to the sacred rite only to serve a turn, while Simeon and Levi answered deceit with a worse deceit ending in murder. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Simeon and Levi came unexpectedly to Shechem, executed judgment on all its men, and left not one remaining, slaying them in torments because they had dishonoured Dinah. Henry judges the brothers most unrighteous, warning that those who act wickedly under pretext of religion are the worst enemies of the truth; Jubilees, by contrast, records the slaughter as judgment, and the note must hold both verdicts in view. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Jubilees turns the event into a standing ordinance: let it not again be done that a daughter of Israel be defiled, for judgment is ordained in heaven against such men. Henry's warning underlies the ordinance's concern, that acquaintance with the Canaanites and their ways ends in blood, though Henry himself condemns the brothers' revenge. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: The Lord is said to have delivered the Shechemites into the sons of Jacob, that they might exterminate them and execute judgment, so that no virgin of Israel should again be defiled. The verse states Jubilees' doctrine plainly: the vengeance was God's judgment delivered by human hands, a claim Henry's commentary on Genesis does not endorse. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Let no adulteress and no uncleanness be found in Israel through all generations, for Israel is holy to the Lord. Henry's warning against needless exposure to danger and against learning Canaanite ways grounds the call to holiness, even where the penalties Jubilees attaches are its own law. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Moses is commanded to exhort Israel not to give their daughters to the Gentiles nor take Gentile daughters for their sons, for this is abominable before the Lord. Henry's rule from the Jacob stories points the same way: those who profess religion should not marry with those that care not for religion. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: The Law is said to record all the deeds of the Shechemites against Dinah, and the sons' words, that they would not give their daughter to an uncircumcised man, since that would be a reproach. The verse anchors Jubilees' marriage law in the Shechem story itself, as a warning written into the Law. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: It is a reproach to Israel, to givers and takers alike, to exchange daughters with the Gentiles, for this is unclean and abominable to Israel. Henry's account of Dinah's gadding shows the same fear: acquaintance with the daughters of the land led to defilement and then to blood. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Israel will not be free from this uncleanness if it has a Gentile wife or has given a daughter to a Gentile. Henry's chain applies: one sin leads on to another, and unlawful commerce between the sexes ends, more than any other sin, in blood. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Moses is told to testify to Israel how the Shechemites fared: they were delivered into the hands of two sons of Jacob, slain under tortures, and it was reckoned to them for righteousness. The verse is Jubilees' own verdict, opposite to Henry's judgment that Simeon and Levi were most unrighteous, and the study note records the disagreement rather than hiding it. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: All this account is written and commanded to the children of Israel, that they should not sin, transgress the ordinances, or break the covenant, but fulfil it and be recorded as friends. Henry's general warning fits the exhortation: carefully avoid all occasions of sin and approaches to it, for the beginning of sin is as the letting forth of water. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: On the day the sons of Jacob slew Shechem, a writing was recorded in their favour in heaven, that they had executed righteousness, uprightness, and vengeance on the sinners, and it was written for a blessing. Jubilees thus closes the episode with a heavenly record of approval, where Genesis closes it with Jacob's rebuke and a dying father's curse on their anger. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: The brothers brought Dinah out of Shechem's house and took captive everything in the city, sheep, oxen, asses, wealth, and flocks, bringing them all to Jacob. Henry notes that murder in this chapter was followed by other lawless actions, the plunder completing the desolation that began with seduction. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: Jacob reproached his sons because they had put the city to the sword, fearing the Canaanites and Perizzites who dwelt in the land. Henry's account of the next chapter explains the sequel: though the Canaanites were angry, they were kept back by divine power, and the way of duty proved the way of safety. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: The dread of the Lord fell on all the cities around Shechem, and they did not rise to pursue Jacob's sons, for terror had fallen on them. Henry notes that God governs the world more by secret terrors on men's minds than we are aware of, and that while Jacob was about God's work he was under special protection. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 12, 14, 15, 16, 21, 22, 23, 25, 26, 28, 29, 30 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
