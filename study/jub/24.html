Study Notes — Jubilees

Chapter 24

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The blessing after Abraham's death marks Isaac as heir: JFB's reading of the Genesis parallel is a general statement of the prosperity attending the father's heir, and the move to the Well of the Vision opens that heirship. — R. H. Charles; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: In the first year of the fourth week a famine began in the land, besides the first famine, which had been in the days of Abraham. A second famine begins, distinct from the first famine in Abraham's days. JFB on Genesis 26:1 notes the pressure of famine in Canaan forced Isaac with family and flocks to migrate into the land of the Philistines. — R. H. Charles; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Jacob sod lentil pottage, and Esau came from the field hungry. JFB on Genesis 25:29 notes the pottage was made of lentils or small beans, common in Egypt and Syria, probably Egyptian beans procured as a dainty, very palatable, and to the weary hunter, faint with hunger, its odor irresistibly tempting. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: He said to Jacob his brother: 'Give me of this red pottage.' And Jacob said to him: 'Sell to me thy primogeniture, this birthright, and I will give thee bread, and also some of this lentil pottage.' JFB on Genesis 25:31 notes the birthright is the rights and privileges of the first-born, very important, the chief being that they were the family priests and had a double portion of the inheritance. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Esau said in his heart: 'I shall die; of what profit to me is this birthright?' JFB on Genesis 25:32 notes 'I am at the point to die' means running daily risk of life, and asks of what use the birthright will be to him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Esau's spoken gift is only the first step: it still needs Jacob's oath in the next verse to seal the transfer before any bread changes hands. — R. H. Charles

7 COMMENTARY: Jacob insists on a sworn oath the same day, turning a hungry bargain into a binding transfer before the pottage is even given. — R. H. Charles

8 COMMENTARY: Jacob gave his brother Esau bread and pottage, and he ate till he was satisfied, and Esau despised his birthright; for this reason was Esau's name called Edom, on account of the red pottage which Jacob gave him for his birthright. JFB on Genesis 25:32 notes he despised or cared little about it, in comparison with gratifying his appetite, throwing away his religious privileges for a trifle, and thence styled 'a profane person'; here the red pottage gives Esau the name Edom. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: The birth order itself is overturned here: Jacob stands as elder in right, Esau is lowered from his dignity, the chapter's reversal in one line. — R. H. Charles

10 COMMENTARY: The famine was over the land, and Isaac departed to go down into Egypt in the second year of this week, and went to the king of the Philistines to Gerar, unto Abimelech. And the Lord appeared unto him and said unto him: 'Go not down into Egypt; dwell in the land that I shall tell thee of, and sojourn in this land, and I shall be.' JFB on Genesis 26:1-2 notes Gerar lay on the great central road from Canaan into Egypt and was the usual stage for travelling caravans, so Isaac, arriving as a great emir with large flocks, had to seek the pastoral king's permission or a lease to pasture nearby; and this appearance was the first divine communication made to Isaac personally, after he had succeeded to his father's inheritance. — R. H. Charles; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: 'With thee and bless thee.' The Lord will be with Isaac and bless him. — R. H. Charles

12 COMMENTARY: 'For to thee and to thy seed shall I give all this land, and I shall establish my oath which I sware unto Abraham thy father, and I shall multiply thy seed as the stars of heaven, and shall give unto thy seed all this land.' JFB on Genesis 26:2-5 notes this is a renewal, not a first grant: the covenant originated entirely in divine grace, was suspended on Abraham walking before God and being perfect, and Abraham having fully met that condition, Isaac is now assured it will progressively take effect, the oath sworn to Abraham making the assurance doubly sure; the first instalment is possession of Canaan, 'all these countries' from its many petty tribal subdivisions. — R. H. Charles; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: 'And in thy seed will all the nations of the earth be blessed; because thy father obeyed my voice, and kept my charge and my commandments, and my laws, and my ordinances, and my covenant; and now obey my voice and dwell in this land.' JFB on Genesis 26:2-5: the blessing to the nations reaches Isaac as an inherited assurance, not a reward for his own obedience, because Abraham had already met the covenant's condition through grace-enabled faith honoured with the strongest expression of divine approval; Isaac's own charge is simply to obey and dwell. — R. H. Charles; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: The stay in Gerar lasts three weeks of years, twenty-one years of dwelling among the Philistines before the expulsion that follows. — R. H. Charles

15 COMMENTARY: Abimelech charged concerning him, and concerning all that was his, saying: 'Any man that shall touch him or aught that is his shall surely die.' Abimelech's charge protects Isaac and all that is his under pain of death. — R. H. Charles

16 COMMENTARY: Prosperity itself becomes the provocation: Isaac's wealth among the Philistines sets up Abimelech's expulsion order in the verses that follow. — R. H. Charles

17 COMMENTARY: He sowed in the land of the Philistines and brought in a hundred-fold, and Isaac became exceedingly great, and the Philistines envied him. JFB on Genesis 26:12 notes during his sojourn he farmed a piece of land, which by God's blessing on his skill and industry was very productive, and by his plentiful returns he increased so rapidly in wealth and influence that the Philistines, afraid or envious of his prosperity, obliged him to leave. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Now all the wells which the servants of Abraham had dug during the life of Abraham, the Philistines had stopped them after the death of Abraham, and filled them with earth. JFB on Genesis 26:15 notes the same base stratagem for annoying those against whom they have taken an umbrage is practiced still by choking the wells with sand or stones, or defiling them with putrid carcases; here the Philistines stop and earth-fill Abraham's wells after his death. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Abimelech said unto Isaac: 'Go from us, for thou art much mightier than we'; and Isaac departed thence in the first year of the seventh week, and sojourned in the valleys of Gerar. Expelled for mightiness, Isaac departs in the first year of the seventh week and sojourns in the valleys of Gerar. — R. H. Charles

20 COMMENTARY: They digged again the wells of water which the servants of Abraham his father had digged, and which the Philistines had closed after the death of Abraham his father, and he called their names as Abraham his father had named them. JFB on Genesis 26:18-22 notes the naming of wells by Abraham, the hereditary right of his family, the change of names by the Philistines to obliterate traces of origin, the restoration of names by Isaac, and the contests between shepherds; here Isaac redigs Abraham's closed wells and restores Abraham's names. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: The servants of Isaac dug a well in the valley, and found living water, and the shepherds of Gerar strove with the shepherds of Isaac, saying: 'The water is ours'; and Isaac called the name of the well 'Perversity,' because. A valley well yields living water, Gerar's shepherds claim 'The water is ours,' and Isaac names the well Perversity, because they had been perverse, completed by the next verse. — R. H. Charles

22 COMMENTARY: 'They had been perverse with us.' The well is named Perversity because they had been perverse with Isaac. — R. H. Charles

23 COMMENTARY: They dug a second well, and they strove for that also, and he called its name 'Enmity.' A second well brings strife again, and is named Enmity. — R. H. Charles

24 COMMENTARY: He arose from thence and they digged another well, and for that they strove not, and he called the name of it 'Room,' and Isaac said: 'Now the Lord hath made room for us, and we have increased in the land.' Matthew Henry on Genesis 26:22 notes Isaac cleaved to his peaceable principle, rather to fly than fight, preferring quietness to victory: those that follow peace, sooner or later, shall find peace; the two former wells show what the earth is, straitness and strife, while this well shows what heaven is, enlargement and peace, room enough there. — R. H. Charles; Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: Isaac returns to the Well of the Oath, the place of oath-taking, at the opening of the forty-fourth jubilee, closing the Gerar wanderings. — R. H. Charles

26 COMMENTARY: The Lord appeared to him that night, on the new moon of the first month, and said unto him: 'I am the God of Abraham thy father; fear not, for I am with thee, and shall bless thee and shall surely multiply thy seed as the sand of the earth, for the sake of Abraham my servant.' JFB on Genesis 26:24-25: 'Man's extremity is God's opportunity' - Isaac arrived at Beersheba disappointed, wearied and troubled by unceasing molestation, and that same night God delighted his drooping spirit with the comforts of grace, raising him above the fear of man; Jewish writers remark the altar he then builds is the only one he is recorded to have raised. — R. H. Charles; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: He built an altar there, which Abraham his father had first built, and he called upon the name of the Lord, and he offered sacrifice to the God of Abraham his father. Isaac rebuilds the altar Abraham first built there, calls on the Lord's name, and offers sacrifice to the God of Abraham his father. — R. H. Charles

28 COMMENTARY: At the Well of the Oath the digging succeeds at once: living water where the Gerar wells brought strife, and where the next well finds none. — R. H. Charles

29 COMMENTARY: The servants of Isaac digged another well and did not find water, and they went and told Isaac that they had not found water, and Isaac said: 'I have sworn this day to the Philistines and this thing hath been announced to us.' A second well finds no water, and Isaac links the report to the day's oath to the Philistines, announced to them. — R. H. Charles

30 COMMENTARY: He called the name of that place the 'Well of the Oath'; for there he had sworn to Abimelech and Ahuzzath his friend and Phicol the prefect of his host. JFB on Genesis 26:26-33 notes the king and two of his courtiers paid a visit to Isaac's tent, an overture of peace fulfilling the promise, and Isaac, of pacific disposition, forgave, accepted their proposals, and treated them to the banquet by which a covenant's ratification was usually crowned; here the place is named Well of the Oath for the oath to Abimelech, Ahuzzath and Phicol. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: Isaac himself names the pressure: the peace oath was sworn under constraint, not freely, and the chapter is careful to record that it was forced. — R. H. Charles

32 COMMENTARY: This unit is the heading for the curse that follows: Isaac curses the Philistines. It divides the oath at the Well of the Oath from the curse. — R. H. Charles

33 COMMENTARY: The curse is dated, not vague: it runs unto the day of wrath, so the Philistines' judgment is reserved for a set day rather than spent at once. — R. H. Charles

35 COMMENTARY: No remnant is left: the total verdict whose scope the following verses unfold, from heaven to Sheol to captivity. — R. H. Charles

36 COMMENTARY: 'Nor one that will be saved on the day of the.' Nor one saved on the day of the wrath of judgment, completed by the next verse. — R. H. Charles

38 COMMENTARY: Even flight fails in the curse: captives are cut down on the road itself by those hunting their lives, no escape by captivity either. — R. H. Charles

40 COMMENTARY: The sentence is engraved, not merely spoken: written on the heavenly tables for the day of judgment, so the rooting-out is decreed in advance. — R. H. Charles

COMMENTARY: This unit is the heading for the admonition that follows: Rebekah admonishes Jacob not to marry a Canaanitish woman. It divides the curse of the Philistines from Rebekah's admonition in chapter 25. — R. H. Charles

COMMENTARY: This unit is the heading for the blessing that follows: Rebekah's blessing. It marks the blessing embedded in chapter 25. — R. H. Charles

No note for verse(s) 34, 37, 39 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
