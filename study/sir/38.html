Study Notes — Sirach

Chapter 38

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Honour a physician with the honour due to him for the uses you may have of him, for the Lord has created him. Physicians deserve honour for their uses, since God created them. — W. O. E. Oesterley

2 COMMENTARY: For of the Most High comes healing, and he will receive honour of the king. Healing comes from God, and the physician receives honour even from kings. — W. O. E. Oesterley

3 COMMENTARY: The skill of the physician will lift up his head, and in the sight of great men he will be in admiration. A physician's skill lifts his head and wins admiration among great men. — W. O. E. Oesterley

4 COMMENTARY: The Lord has created medicines out of the earth, and he who is wise will not abhor them. Medicines are God's creation from the earth, and the wise do not abhor them. — W. O. E. Oesterley

5 COMMENTARY: Was not the water made sweet with wood, that the virtue thereof might be known? Water sweetened by wood, as at Marah, shows the virtue God puts in natural things. — W. O. E. Oesterley

6 COMMENTARY: He has given men skill, that he might be honoured in his marvellous works. Human skill in healing exists so that God may be honoured in his marvellous works. — W. O. E. Oesterley

7 COMMENTARY: With such he heals men, and takes away their pains. With medicines and skill God heals and removes pains. — W. O. E. Oesterley

8 COMMENTARY: Of such the apothecary makes a confection, and of his works there is no end, and from him is peace over all the earth. The apothecary compounds medicines, God's works in healing have no end, and from him comes peace over the earth. — W. O. E. Oesterley

9 COMMENTARY: My son, in your sickness do not be negligent, but pray to the Lord, and he will make you whole. Sickness calls first for prayer to God, who makes whole, not negligence. — W. O. E. Oesterley

10 COMMENTARY: Leave off from sin, order your hands aright, and cleanse your heart from all wickedness. Sickness should prompt leaving sin, ordering hands aright, and cleansing the heart. — W. O. E. Oesterley

11 COMMENTARY: Give a sweet savour, and a memorial of fine flour, and make a fat offering, as not being. Offerings of sweet savour, fine flour, and fat offerings belong with repentance, as if the offerer were already gone, giving all. — W. O. E. Oesterley

12 COMMENTARY: Then give place to the physician, for the Lord has created him; let him not go from you, for you have need of him. After prayer and offering, the physician should be given place, since God created him and you need him. — W. O. E. Oesterley

13 COMMENTARY: There is a time when in their hands there is good success. There are times when physicians' hands meet with good success. — W. O. E. Oesterley

14 COMMENTARY: For they will also pray to the Lord, that he would prosper what they give for ease and remedy to prolong life. Physicians themselves pray that God prosper their remedies for ease and prolonged life. — W. O. E. Oesterley

15 COMMENTARY: He who sins before his Maker, let him fall into the hand of the physician. Sinning before the Maker leads to falling into the physician's hands, sickness as consequence. — W. O. E. Oesterley

16 COMMENTARY: My son, let tears fall down over the dead, and begin to lament, as if you had suffered great harm yourself; and then cover his body according to custom, and do not neglect his burial. Tears and lamentation over the dead, as for one's own great harm, should be followed by customary covering and burial, not neglected. — W. O. E. Oesterley

17 COMMENTARY: Weep bitterly, make great moan, and use lamentation, as he is worthy, and that a day or two, lest you be evil spoken of; and then comfort yourself for your heaviness. Bitter weeping and lamentation, worthy of the dead, should last a day or two, lest evil be spoken, and then heaviness should be comforted. — W. O. E. Oesterley

18 COMMENTARY: For of heaviness comes death, and the heaviness of the heart breaks strength. Heaviness breeds death and breaks the heart's strength. — W. O. E. Oesterley

19 COMMENTARY: In affliction also sorrow remains, and the life of the poor is the curse of the heart. In affliction sorrow persists, and a poor life is a curse to the heart. — W. O. E. Oesterley

20 COMMENTARY: Take no heaviness to heart; drive it away, and remember the last end. Heaviness must not be taken to heart but driven away, remembering the last end. — W. O. E. Oesterley

21 COMMENTARY: Forget it not, for there is no turning again; you will not do him good, but hurt yourself. The dead do not return, so endless grief does the dead no good and hurts the mourner. — W. O. E. Oesterley

22 COMMENTARY: Remember my judgment, for yours also will be so; yesterday for me, and today for you. Judgment is shared: yesterday for the dead, today for you. — W. O. E. Oesterley

23 COMMENTARY: When the dead is at rest, let his remembrance rest, and be comforted for him, when his spirit has departed from him. Once the dead rests, remembrance should rest too, and comfort should follow the departed spirit. — W. O. E. Oesterley

24 COMMENTARY: The wisdom of a learned man comes by opportunity of leisure, and he who has little business will become wise. Learned wisdom requires leisure, and the man of little business can become wise. — W. O. E. Oesterley

25 COMMENTARY: How can he get wisdom who holds the plough, glories in the goad, drives oxen, is occupied in their labours, and whose talk is of bullocks? The ploughman, glorifying in goad and oxen, talking of bullocks, has no room to get wisdom. — W. O. E. Oesterley

26 COMMENTARY: He gives his mind to make furrows, and is diligent to give the cattle fodder. The ploughman's mind is on furrows and fodder, not on wisdom. — W. O. E. Oesterley

27 COMMENTARY: So every carpenter and workmaster who labours night and day, and those who cut and grave seals, diligent to make great variety, giving themselves to counterfeit imagery, and watching to finish a work. Carpenters, workmasters, and seal-cutters labour night and day at their craft, absorbed in finishing work. — W. O. E. Oesterley

28 COMMENTARY: The smith also sitting by the anvil, considering the iron work, the vapour of the fire wasting his flesh, fighting with the heat of the furnace, the noise of hammer and anvil ever in his ears, eyes still upon the pattern of what he makes; he sets his mind to finish his work, and watches to polish it perfectly. The smith at the anvil, wasted by fire vapour, deafened by hammer and anvil, eyes on the pattern, minds only finishing and polishing. — W. O. E. Oesterley

29 COMMENTARY: So does the potter sitting at his work, turning the wheel with his feet, always carefully set at his work, making all his work by number. The potter at his wheel, feet turning, careful and counting, is likewise absorbed in craft. — W. O. E. Oesterley

30 COMMENTARY: He fashions the clay with his arm, bows down his strength before his feet, applies himself to lead it over, and is diligent to make clean the furnace. The potter fashions clay with arm and bowed strength, leading it over, diligently cleaning the furnace. — W. O. E. Oesterley

31 COMMENTARY: All these trust to their hands, and every one is wise in his work. Craftsmen trust their hands, and each is wise in his own work. — W. O. E. Oesterley

32 COMMENTARY: Without these a city cannot be inhabited, and they will not dwell where they will, nor go up and down. Cities cannot be inhabited without craftsmen, though craftsmen themselves do not dwell freely or go where they will. — W. O. E. Oesterley

33 COMMENTARY: They will not be sought for in public counsel, nor sit high in the congregation; they will not sit on the judges' seat, nor understand the sentence of judgment; they cannot declare justice and judgment, and will not be found where parables are spoken. Craftsmen are not sought for public counsel, high seats, judges' seats, or parables; their wisdom is in craft, not in judgment. — W. O. E. Oesterley

34 COMMENTARY: But they will maintain the state of the world, and all their desire is in the work of their craft. Yet craftsmen maintain the world's state, and their whole desire lies in their craft. — W. O. E. Oesterley
