Study Notes — Sirach

Chapter 19

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: A labouring man given to drunkenness will not be rich, and he who contemns small things will fall by little and little. Drunken labour cannot grow rich, and despising small things leads to falling gradually, little by little. — W. O. E. Oesterley

2 COMMENTARY: Wine and women will make men of understanding fall away, and he who cleaves to harlots will become impudent. Wine and women cause even understanding men to fall, and clinging to harlots breeds impudence. — W. O. E. Oesterley

3 COMMENTARY: Moths and worms will have him for heritage, and a bold man will be taken away. The end of such a man is moths and worms for heritage, and boldness itself is taken away. — W. O. E. Oesterley

4 COMMENTARY: He who is hasty to give credit is lightminded, and he who sins will offend against his own soul. Hasty belief is lightmindedness, and sin offends first against the sinner's own soul. — W. O. E. Oesterley

5 COMMENTARY: Whoever takes pleasure in wickedness will be condemned, but he who resists pleasures crowns his life. Pleasure in wickedness brings condemnation, while resisting pleasures crowns a life. — W. O. E. Oesterley

6 COMMENTARY: He who can rule his tongue will live without strife, and he who hates babbling will have less evil. A ruled tongue means life without strife, and hatred of babbling means less evil. — W. O. E. Oesterley

7 COMMENTARY: Do not rehearse to another what is told to you, and you will fare never the worse. Keeping told things unrepeated is the way to fare no worse. — W. O. E. Oesterley

8 COMMENTARY: Whether to friend or foe, do not talk of other men's lives, and if you can without offence, reveal them not. Other men's lives should not be talked of to friend or foe, and where silence is possible without offence, they should not be revealed. — W. O. E. Oesterley

9 COMMENTARY: For he heard and observed you, and when time comes he will hate you. The tale-bearer is heard and watched, and in time will be hated for it. — W. O. E. Oesterley

10 COMMENTARY: If you have heard a word, let it die with you, and be bold, it will not burst you. A heard word should die with its hearer; kept silence will not burst a man. — W. O. E. Oesterley

11 COMMENTARY: A fool travails with a word, as a woman in labour of a child. A fool labours over a word as a woman labours to bring forth a child, unable to keep it in. — W. O. E. Oesterley

12 COMMENTARY: As an arrow sticking in a man's thigh, so is a word within a fool's belly. A word in a fool's belly pains like an arrow in the thigh until it is out. — W. O. E. Oesterley

13 COMMENTARY: Admonish a friend; it may be he has not done it, and if he has, that he do it no more. Admonishing a friend rests on uncertainty: perhaps he did not do it, and if he did, admonition aims at stopping it. — W. O. E. Oesterley

14 COMMENTARY: Admonish your friend; it may be he has not said it, and if he has, that he speak it not again. The same rule holds for speech: admonish in case he did not say it, or to stop its repetition if he did. — W. O. E. Oesterley

15 COMMENTARY: Admonish a friend, for many times it is a slander, and do not believe every tale. Admonition is needed because reports are often slander, and not every tale should be believed. — W. O. E. Oesterley

16 COMMENTARY: There is one who slips in his speech, but not from his heart, and who is he who has not offended with his tongue? A slip of speech may not come from the heart, and no one has never offended with the tongue. — W. O. E. Oesterley

17 COMMENTARY: Admonish your neighbour before you threaten him, and not being angry, give place to the law of the Most High. Admonition should come before threat, without anger, leaving room for God's law. — W. O. E. Oesterley

18 COMMENTARY: The fear of the Lord is the first step to be accepted by him, and wisdom obtains his love. Fear of God is the first step toward acceptance, and wisdom obtains his love. — W. O. E. Oesterley

19 COMMENTARY: The knowledge of the commandments of the Lord is the doctrine of life, and those who do things that please him will receive the fruit of the tree of immortality. Knowledge of God's commandments is life's doctrine, and doing what pleases him brings the fruit of immortality's tree. — W. O. E. Oesterley

20 COMMENTARY: The fear of the Lord is all wisdom, and in all wisdom is the performance of the law and the knowledge of his omnipotency. Fear of God is the whole of wisdom, and wisdom itself is law-keeping and knowledge of God's almighty power. — W. O. E. Oesterley

21 COMMENTARY: If a servant says to his master, I will not do as it pleases you, yet afterward he does it, he angers him who nourishes him. Even a servant who finally obeys angers his master by first refusing, since the master nourishes him. — W. O. E. Oesterley

22 COMMENTARY: The knowledge of wickedness is not wisdom, nor at any time is the counsel of sinners prudence. Knowing wickedness is not wisdom, and sinners' counsel is never prudence. — W. O. E. Oesterley

23 COMMENTARY: There is a wickedness, and the same is an abomination, and there is a fool wanting in wisdom. Some wickedness is outright abomination, and some folly is mere want of wisdom; the two must be distinguished. — W. O. E. Oesterley

24 COMMENTARY: He who has small understanding and fears God is better than one who has much wisdom and transgresses the law of the Most High. A little understanding with fear of God outvalues much wisdom joined to transgression. — W. O. E. Oesterley

25 COMMENTARY: There is an exquisite subtlety, and the same is unjust, and there is one who turns aside to make judgment appear, and there is a wise man who justifies in judgment. Subtlety can be unjust, twisting judgment's appearance, while the truly wise man justifies rightly in judgment. — W. O. E. Oesterley

26 COMMENTARY: There is a wicked man who hangs down his head sadly, but inwardly he is full of deceit. A sad, bowed head can hide inward deceit in a wicked man. — W. O. E. Oesterley

27 COMMENTARY: Casting down his countenance and making as if he heard not, where he is not known he will do you a mischief before you are aware. The deceiver's downcast, deaf-seeming manner covers mischief done unawares where he is unknown. — W. O. E. Oesterley

28 COMMENTARY: If for want of power he is hindered from sinning, yet when he finds opportunity he will do evil. Hindrance by lack of power is not virtue; given opportunity, such a man will do evil. — W. O. E. Oesterley

29 COMMENTARY: A man may be known by his look, and one who has understanding by his countenance, when you meet him. A man's look reveals him, and an understanding man's countenance shows itself at meeting. — W. O. E. Oesterley

30 COMMENTARY: A man's attire, excessive laughter, and gait show what he is. Dress, laughter, and walk together reveal a man's character. — W. O. E. Oesterley
