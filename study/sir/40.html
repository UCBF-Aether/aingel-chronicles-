Study Notes — Sirach

Chapter 40

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Great travail is created for every man, and a heavy yoke is upon the sons of Adam, from the day they go out of their mother's womb till the day they return to the mother of all things. From birth to return to earth, every man bears created travail and a heavy yoke. — W. O. E. Oesterley

2 COMMENTARY: Their imagination of things to come, and the day of death, trouble their thoughts, and cause fear of heart. Foreboding imagination and the thought of death trouble minds and cause fearful hearts. — W. O. E. Oesterley

3 COMMENTARY: From him who sits on a throne of glory, to him who is humbled in earth and ashes. This fear reaches from the enthroned glorious to the humbled in earth and ashes. — W. O. E. Oesterley

4 COMMENTARY: From him who wears purple and a crown, to him who is clothed with a linen frock. From purple and crown to linen frock, all stations share the same dread. — W. O. E. Oesterley

5 COMMENTARY: Wrath, envy, trouble, unquietness, fear of death, anger, and strife, and in the time of rest upon his bed his night sleep, change his knowledge. Passions and death-fear, even in bedtime rest, change a man's knowledge and unsettle him. — W. O. E. Oesterley

6 COMMENTARY: A little or nothing is his rest, and afterward he is in his sleep, as in a day of keeping watch, troubled in the vision of his heart, as if he had escaped out of a battle. Rest is little or nothing, and sleep is like a watch-day, troubled by heart-visions as if escaped from battle. — W. O. E. Oesterley

7 COMMENTARY: When all is safe, he awakes, and marvels that the fear was nothing. Waking to safety, he marvels that the fear was nothing after all. — W. O. E. Oesterley

8 COMMENTARY: Such things happen to all flesh, both man and beast, and that is sevenfold more upon sinners. These troubles befall all flesh, man and beast, and sevenfold more upon sinners. — W. O. E. Oesterley

9 COMMENTARY: Death, bloodshed, strife, sword, calamities, famine, tribulation, and the scourge. The catalogue of woes runs from death and bloodshed to famine, tribulation, and scourge. — W. O. E. Oesterley

10 COMMENTARY: These things are created for the wicked, and for their sakes came the flood. These woes were created for the wicked, and for their sake the flood came. — W. O. E. Oesterley

11 COMMENTARY: All things of the earth will turn to the earth again, and that which is of the waters returns into the sea. Earth returns to earth and waters to sea, each to its origin. — W. O. E. Oesterley

12 COMMENTARY: All bribery and injustice will be blotted out, but true dealing will endure for ever. Bribery and injustice are blotted out, while true dealing endures for ever. — W. O. E. Oesterley

13 COMMENTARY: The goods of the unjust will be dried up like a river, and vanish with noise, like a great thunder in rain. Unjust goods dry up like a river and vanish noisily like thunder in rain. — W. O. E. Oesterley

14 COMMENTARY: While he opens his hand he will rejoice, so will transgressors come to nought. The unjust rejoices while his hand opens, yet transgressors come to nothing. — W. O. E. Oesterley

15 COMMENTARY: The children of the ungodly will not bring forth many branches, but are as unclean roots upon a hard rock. Ungodly children branch little, like unclean roots on hard rock. — W. O. E. Oesterley

16 COMMENTARY: The weed growing upon every water and bank of a river will be pulled up before all grass. Riverbank weeds are pulled up before all grass, early and easily removed. — W. O. E. Oesterley

17 COMMENTARY: Bountifulness is as a most fruitful garden, and mercifulness endures for ever. Bountifulness is a most fruitful garden, and mercifulness endures for ever. — W. O. E. Oesterley

18 COMMENTARY: To labour, and to be content with what a man has, is a sweet life, but he who finds a treasure is above them both. Labour with contentment is sweet life, yet finding a treasure is above both. — W. O. E. Oesterley

19 COMMENTARY: Children and the building of a city continue a man's name, but a blameless wife is counted above them both. Children and city-building continue a name, but a blameless wife counts above both. — W. O. E. Oesterley

20 COMMENTARY: Wine and music rejoice the heart, but the love of wisdom is above them both. Wine and music gladden the heart, but love of wisdom is above both. — W. O. E. Oesterley

21 COMMENTARY: The pipe and psaltery make sweet melody, but a pleasant tongue is above them both. Pipe and psaltery make sweet melody, but a pleasant tongue is above both. — W. O. E. Oesterley

22 COMMENTARY: Your eye desires favour and beauty, but more than both, corn while it is green. The eye desires favour and beauty, but green corn is desired more than both. — W. O. E. Oesterley

23 COMMENTARY: A friend and companion never meet amiss, but above both is a wife with her husband. Friend and companion never meet amiss, but a wife with her husband is above both. — W. O. E. Oesterley

24 COMMENTARY: Brethren and help are against time of trouble, but alms will deliver more than them both. Brethren and help stand against trouble, but alms deliver more than both. — W. O. E. Oesterley

25 COMMENTARY: Gold and silver make the foot stand sure, but counsel is esteemed above them both. Gold and silver steady the foot, but counsel is esteemed above both. — W. O. E. Oesterley

26 COMMENTARY: Riches and strength lift up the heart, but the fear of the Lord is above them both; there is no want in the fear of the Lord, and it needs not to seek help. Riches and strength lift the heart, but fear of God is above both, lacking nothing and needing no help. — W. O. E. Oesterley

27 COMMENTARY: The fear of the Lord is a fruitful garden, and covers him above all glory. Fear of God is a fruitful garden, covering its owner above all glory. — W. O. E. Oesterley

28 COMMENTARY: My son, do not lead a beggar's life, for it is better to die than to beg. A beggar's life must not be led, since death is better than begging. — W. O. E. Oesterley

29 COMMENTARY: The life of him who depends on another man's table is not to be counted for a life, for he pollutes himself with other men's meat; but a wise man well nurtured will beware thereof. Dependence on another's table is scarcely life, polluting oneself with others' meat, and the well-nurtured wise man beware of it. — W. O. E. Oesterley

30 COMMENTARY: Begging is sweet in the mouth of the shameless, but in his belly there will burn a fire. Begging tastes sweet to the shameless mouth, but burns like fire in the belly. — W. O. E. Oesterley
