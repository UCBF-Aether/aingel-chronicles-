Study Notes — Sirach

Chapter 13

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: He who touches pitch will be defiled with it, and he who has fellowship with a proud man will be like him. Contact defiles: pitch stains the toucher, and fellowship with the proud makes a man proud. — W. O. E. Oesterley

2 COMMENTARY: Do not burden yourself above your power while you live, and have no fellowship with one mightier and richer than yourself, for how do the kettle and the earthen pot agree together? If one is smitten against the other, it will be broken. Unequal fellowship with the mightier and richer breaks the weaker vessel, as an earthen pot breaks against a kettle. — W. O. E. Oesterley

3 COMMENTARY: The rich man has done wrong, and yet he threatens as well; the poor man is wronged, and must entreat also. Wrongdoing by the rich is answered with threats, while the wronged poor man must still beg. — W. O. E. Oesterley

4 COMMENTARY: If you are for his profit, he will use you, but if you have nothing, he will forsake you. The rich man's friendship lasts only while you profit him; emptiness ends it. — W. O. E. Oesterley

5 COMMENTARY: If you have anything, he will live with you; indeed he will make you bare, and will not be sorry for it. While you have something, the rich man lives off you until you are bare, without sorrow for it. — W. O. E. Oesterley

6 COMMENTARY: If he has need of you, he will deceive you, smile upon you, put you in hope, speak you fair, and say, what do you want? Need makes the rich man a smiling deceiver, raising hopes with fair speech to get what he wants. — W. O. E. Oesterley

7 COMMENTARY: He will shame you by his meats until he has drawn you dry twice or thrice, and at last he will laugh you to scorn; afterward, when he sees you, he will forsake you and shake his head at you. Feasts at his table shame and drain you repeatedly, ending in scorn, forsaking, and a shaken head. — W. O. E. Oesterley

8 COMMENTARY: Beware that you are not deceived and brought down in your jollity. Jollity itself is a danger: deception in merriment brings a man down. — W. O. E. Oesterley

9 COMMENTARY: If you are invited by a mighty man, withdraw yourself, and so much the more will he invite you. Modest withdrawal from a mighty man's invitation draws a warmer second invitation. — W. O. E. Oesterley

10 COMMENTARY: Do not press upon him, lest you be put back; do not stand far off, lest you be forgotten. Court manners require a middle course: pressing earns rebuff, distance earns forgetfulness. — W. O. E. Oesterley

11 COMMENTARY: Do not affect to be made equal to him in talk, and do not believe his many words, for with much communication he will tempt you, and smiling upon you will get out your secrets. Claiming equality in talk and trusting many words lets the mighty man tempt you and draw out your secrets with a smile. — W. O. E. Oesterley

12 COMMENTARY: But cruelly he will lay up your words, and will not spare to do you hurt and put you in prison. Words once drawn out are stored cruelly and used to hurt, even to imprisonment. — W. O. E. Oesterley

13 COMMENTARY: Observe and take good heed, for you walk in peril of your overthrowing; when you hear these things, awake in your sleep. This whole warning is a call to wakefulness, because the path described is perilous to the point of overthrow. — W. O. E. Oesterley

14 COMMENTARY: Love the Lord all your life, and call upon him for your salvation. Against all the snares of the mighty, the steady answer is lifelong love of the Lord and prayer for salvation. — W. O. E. Oesterley

15 COMMENTARY: Every beast loves his like, and every man loves his neighbour. Creatures keep to their kind, and men love those near them; fellowship follows likeness. — W. O. E. Oesterley

16 COMMENTARY: All flesh consorts according to kind, and a man will cleave to his like. All living things associate by kind, and a man clings to those like himself. — W. O. E. Oesterley

17 COMMENTARY: What fellowship has the wolf with the lamb? So the sinner with the godly. Wolf and lamb have no fellowship, and neither do sinner and godly man. — W. O. E. Oesterley

18 COMMENTARY: What agreement is there between the hyena and a dog, and what peace between the rich and the poor? Hyena and dog cannot agree, and rich and poor have no peace between them, for their interests clash. — W. O. E. Oesterley

19 COMMENTARY: As the wild ass is the lion's prey in the wilderness, so the rich eat up the poor. The poor are prey to the rich as the wild ass is prey to the lion. — W. O. E. Oesterley

20 COMMENTARY: As the proud hate humility, so does the rich abhor the poor. The rich man's abhorrence of the poor is like the proud man's hatred of humility. — W. O. E. Oesterley

21 COMMENTARY: A rich man beginning to fall is held up by his friends, but a poor man being down is thrust away by his friends. Falling rich men are propped by friends, while fallen poor men are pushed away by theirs. — W. O. E. Oesterley

22 COMMENTARY: When a rich man is fallen, he has many helpers; he speaks things not to be spoken, and yet men justify him; the poor man slipped, and yet they rebuked him too; he spoke wisely, and could have no place. Fallen rich men find helpers and justification for unspeakable speech, while a poor man's slip is rebuked and his wise speech finds no place. — W. O. E. Oesterley

23 COMMENTARY: When a rich man speaks, every man holds his tongue, and look, what he says they extol to the clouds; but if the poor man speaks, they say, what fellow is this? And if he stumbles, they will help to overthrow him. Rich speech silences rooms and is extolled to the clouds, while poor speech is mocked as a fellow's chatter and a stumble is helped into a fall. — W. O. E. Oesterley

24 COMMENTARY: Riches are good to him who has no sin, and poverty is evil in the mouth of the ungodly. Riches are good when sinless, but poverty in the mouth of the ungodly is spoken of as evil. — W. O. E. Oesterley

25 COMMENTARY: The heart of a man changes his countenance, whether for good or evil, and a merry heart makes a cheerful countenance. The heart shows in the face, for good or evil, and a merry heart makes the face cheerful. — W. O. E. Oesterley

26 COMMENTARY: A cheerful countenance is a token of a heart in prosperity, and the finding out of parables is a wearisome labour of the mind. A cheerful face signals a prosperous heart, while working out parables is wearying labour for the mind. — W. O. E. Oesterley
