Study Notes — Sirach

Chapter 22

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: A slothful man is compared to a filthy stone, and everyone will hiss him out to his disgrace. Sloth is likened to a filthy stone, and all hiss the slothful man out in disgrace. — W. O. E. Oesterley

2 COMMENTARY: A slothful man is compared to the filth of a dunghill; every man who takes it up will shake his hand. The slothful man is dunghill filth, and whoever handles it shakes his hand to be rid of it. — W. O. E. Oesterley

3 COMMENTARY: An evil-nurtured man is the dishonour of his father who begat him, and a foolish daughter is born to his loss. Bad upbringing dishonours the father, and a foolish daughter is born to her father's loss. — W. O. E. Oesterley

4 COMMENTARY: A wise daughter will bring an inheritance to her husband, but she who lives dishonestly is her father's heaviness. A wise daughter enriches her husband by inheritance, while a dishonest life makes her a heaviness to her father. — W. O. E. Oesterley

5 COMMENTARY: She who is bold dishonours both her father and her husband, but they both will despise her. A bold woman dishonours father and husband alike, and both will despise her. — W. O. E. Oesterley

6 COMMENTARY: A tale out of season is as music in mourning, but stripes and correction of wisdom are never out of time. An ill-timed tale jars like music at a mourning, while wisdom's correction is always timely. — W. O. E. Oesterley

7 COMMENTARY: Whoever teaches a fool is as one who glues a potsherd together, and as he who wakes one from a sound sleep. Teaching a fool is like gluing a broken pot or waking a deep sleeper: fruitless and resented. — W. O. E. Oesterley

8 COMMENTARY: He who tells a tale to a fool speaks to one in a slumber; when he has told his tale, he will say, what is the matter? Telling a tale to a fool is speaking to a sleeper, who wakes only to ask what is the matter. — W. O. E. Oesterley

9 COMMENTARY: If children live honestly and have wherewithal, they will cover the baseness of their parents. Honest, provided-for children cover their parents' baseness. — W. O. E. Oesterley

10 COMMENTARY: But children, being haughty, through disdain and want of nurture stain the nobility of their kindred. Haughty children, through disdain and lack of nurture, stain their kindred's nobility. — W. O. E. Oesterley

11 COMMENTARY: Weep for the dead, for he has lost the light; and weep for the fool, for he wants understanding. Make little weeping for the dead, for he is at rest; but the life of the fool is worse than death. The dead have lost light and deserve weeping, yet little, since they rest; the fool, lacking understanding, lives a life worse than death and deserves more tears. — W. O. E. Oesterley

12 COMMENTARY: Seven days do men mourn for him who is dead, but for a fool and an ungodly man all the days of his life. Mourning for the dead lasts seven days, but a fool and an ungodly man are mourned all their lives. — W. O. E. Oesterley

13 COMMENTARY: Talk not much with a fool, and go not to him who has no understanding; beware of him, lest you have trouble, and you will never be defiled with his fooleries. Depart from him, and you will find rest, and never be disquieted with madness. Much talk with fools brings trouble and defilement by their fooleries, while departure brings rest from madness. — W. O. E. Oesterley

14 COMMENTARY: What is heavier than lead, and what is its name, but a fool? A fool is heavier than lead; that is his name and his weight. — W. O. E. Oesterley

15 COMMENTARY: Sand, salt, and a mass of iron are easier to bear than a man without understanding. Sand, salt, and iron are lighter burdens than an uncomprehending man. — W. O. E. Oesterley

16 COMMENTARY: As timber girt and bound together in a building cannot be loosed with shaking, so the heart established by advised counsel will fear at no time. A heart bound by advised counsel is like bound timber in a building: shaking cannot loosen it, and it fears at no time. — W. O. E. Oesterley

17 COMMENTARY: A heart settled upon a thought of understanding is as a fair plastering on the wall of a gallery. A heart settled on understanding is like fine plaster on a gallery wall, smooth and firm. — W. O. E. Oesterley

18 COMMENTARY: Pales set on a high place will never stand against the wind, so a fearful heart in the imagination of a fool cannot stand against any fear. Pales on high ground fall before wind, and a fool's fearful, imagining heart falls before any fear. — W. O. E. Oesterley

19 COMMENTARY: He who pricks the eye will make tears fall, and he who pricks the heart makes it show her knowledge. Pricking the eye brings tears, and pricking the heart brings out what it knows. — W. O. E. Oesterley

20 COMMENTARY: Whoever casts a stone at the birds frightens them away, and he who upbraids his friend breaks friendship. Stones scatter birds, and upbraiding scatters friendship. — W. O. E. Oesterley

21 COMMENTARY: Though you drew a sword at your friend, yet despair not, for there may be a returning to favour. Even drawing a sword against a friend need not end hope, since favour may return. — W. O. E. Oesterley

22 COMMENTARY: If you have opened your mouth against your friend, fear not, for there may be a reconciliation, except for upbraiding, pride, disclosing of secrets, or a treacherous wound, for for these things every friend will depart. Open speech against a friend can be reconciled, but upbraiding, pride, disclosed secrets, and treacherous wounds make every friend depart. — W. O. E. Oesterley

23 COMMENTARY: Be faithful to your neighbour in his poverty, that you may rejoice in his prosperity; abide steadfast to him in the time of his trouble, that you may be heir with him in his heritage; for a mean estate is not always to be contemned, nor the rich who is foolish to be had in admiration. Faithfulness in a neighbour's poverty and trouble leads to shared joy and heritage, since low estate is not always contemptible, nor foolish riches admirable. — W. O. E. Oesterley

24 COMMENTARY: As the vapour and smoke of a furnace go before the fire, so reviling before blood. Reviling goes before bloodshed as furnace smoke goes before fire. — W. O. E. Oesterley

25 COMMENTARY: I will not be ashamed to defend a friend, neither will I hide myself from him. Defending a friend openly, without hiding, is a point of honour. — W. O. E. Oesterley

26 COMMENTARY: And if any evil happens to me by him, everyone who hears it will beware of him. If evil comes to me through a friend, hearers will learn to beware of him. — W. O. E. Oesterley

27 COMMENTARY: Who will set a watch before my mouth, and a seal of wisdom upon my lips, that I fall not suddenly by them, and that my tongue destroy me not? The prayer is for a guard on the mouth and wisdom's seal on the lips, lest a sudden fall come by the tongue. — W. O. E. Oesterley
