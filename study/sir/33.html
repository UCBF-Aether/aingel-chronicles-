Study Notes — Sirach

Chapter 33

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: No evil will happen to him who fears the Lord, but in temptation even again he will deliver him. The God-fearing are kept from evil, and in repeated temptation God delivers again and again. — W. O. E. Oesterley

2 COMMENTARY: A wise man does not hate the law, but he who is a hypocrite therein is as a ship in a storm. The wise do not hate the law, while the hypocrite in it is tossed like a ship in a storm. — W. O. E. Oesterley

3 COMMENTARY: A man of understanding trusts in the law, and the law is faithful to him as an oracle. Understanding trusts the law, and the law proves faithful like an oracle. — W. O. E. Oesterley

4 COMMENTARY: Prepare what to say, and so you will be heard; bind up instruction, and then make answer. Prepared speech is heard, and instruction bound up beforehand makes the answer ready. — W. O. E. Oesterley

5 COMMENTARY: The heart of the foolish is like a cartwheel, and his thoughts are like a rolling axletree. A fool's heart turns like a cartwheel and his thoughts roll like an axle, never settling. — W. O. E. Oesterley

6 COMMENTARY: A stallion horse is as a mocking friend; he neighs under everyone who sits upon him. A mocking friend is like a stallion that neighs under every rider, faithless to all. — W. O. E. Oesterley

7 COMMENTARY: Why does one day excel another, when all the light of every day in the year is of the sun? Days differ though all their light comes from the same sun; the question opens the doctrine of God's distinctions. — W. O. E. Oesterley

8 COMMENTARY: By the knowledge of the Lord they were distinguished, and he altered seasons and feasts. Days were distinguished by God's knowledge, and he appointed differing seasons and feasts. — W. O. E. Oesterley

9 COMMENTARY: Some of them he has made high days and hallowed them, and some he has made ordinary days. Some days God made holy high days and others ordinary days. — W. O. E. Oesterley

10 COMMENTARY: All men are from the ground, and Adam was created of earth. All men share earthly origin, since Adam was created of earth. — W. O. E. Oesterley

11 COMMENTARY: In much knowledge the Lord has divided them, and made their ways diverse. In great knowledge God divided men and made their ways diverse. — W. O. E. Oesterley

12 COMMENTARY: Some of them he has blessed and exalted, some he has sanctified and set near himself, but some he has cursed and brought low, and turned out of their places. God's division blesses and exalts some, sanctifies and nears others, and curses, lowers, and displaces the rest. — W. O. E. Oesterley

13 COMMENTARY: As the clay is in the potter's hand, to fashion it at his pleasure, so man is in the hand of him who made him, to render to them as it likes him best. As clay in the potter's hand, man lies in his Maker's hand, to be dealt with as pleases him best. — W. O. E. Oesterley

14 COMMENTARY: Good is set against evil, and life against death; so is the godly against the sinner, and the sinner against the godly. Creation is paired in opposites: good against evil, life against death, godly against sinner. — W. O. E. Oesterley

15 COMMENTARY: So look upon all the works of the Most High, and there are two and two, one against another. All God's works come in pairs, two and two, one against another. — W. O. E. Oesterley

16 COMMENTARY: I awoke up last of all, as one who gathers after the grapegatherers; by the blessing of the Lord I profited, and trod my winepress like a gatherer of grapes. The writer, last like a gleaner after grape-gatherers, still profited by God's blessing and trod his winepress. — W. O. E. Oesterley

17 COMMENTARY: Consider that I laboured not for myself only, but for all who seek learning. The writer's labour is for all learners, not for himself alone. — W. O. E. Oesterley

18 COMMENTARY: Hear me, you great men of the people, and hearken with your ears, you rulers of the congregation. Great men and rulers of the congregation are called to hear. — W. O. E. Oesterley

19 COMMENTARY: Do not give your son and wife, your brother and friend, power over you while you live, and do not give your goods to another, lest it repent you, and you entreat for the same again. While living, power over yourself and your goods must not be surrendered to son, wife, brother, or friend, lest repentance follow and you beg back your own. — W. O. E. Oesterley

20 COMMENTARY: As long as you live and have breath in you, give not yourself over to any. While breath lasts, self-surrender to any other is forbidden. — W. O. E. Oesterley

21 COMMENTARY: It is better that your children should seek to you, than that you should stand to their courtesy. It is better for children to seek to the parent than for the parent to depend on their courtesy. — W. O. E. Oesterley

22 COMMENTARY: In all your works keep to yourself the preeminence; leave not a stain in your honour. In all works, keep preeminence for yourself and leave no stain on your honour. — W. O. E. Oesterley

23 COMMENTARY: At the time when you end your days and finish your life, distribute your inheritance. Inheritance should be distributed only at life's end, not before. — W. O. E. Oesterley

24 COMMENTARY: Fodder, a wand, and burdens are for the ass, and bread, correction, and work for a servant. Asses need fodder, wand, and burdens, and servants need bread, correction, and work. — W. O. E. Oesterley

25 COMMENTARY: If you set your servant to labour, you will find rest, but if you let him go idle, he will seek liberty. A labouring servant gives his master rest, while an idle servant seeks liberty. — W. O. E. Oesterley

26 COMMENTARY: A yoke and a collar bow the neck, so are tortures and torments for an evil servant. As yoke and collar bow the neck, tortures and torments bow an evil servant. — W. O. E. Oesterley

27 COMMENTARY: Send him to labour, that he be not idle, for idleness teaches much evil. Labour prevents idleness, and idleness teaches much evil. — W. O. E. Oesterley

28 COMMENTARY: Set him to work, as is fit for him; if he is not obedient, put on more heavy fetters. Work should fit the servant, and disobedience should be met with heavier fetters. — W. O. E. Oesterley

29 COMMENTARY: But do not be excessive toward any, and without discretion do nothing. Excess toward anyone is forbidden, and nothing should be done without discretion. — W. O. E. Oesterley

30 COMMENTARY: If you have a servant, let him be to you as yourself, because you have bought him with a price. A bought servant should be treated as oneself, since a price was paid for him. — W. O. E. Oesterley

31 COMMENTARY: If you have a servant, entreat him as a brother, for you have need of him, as of your own soul; if you entreat him evil, and he runs from you, which way will you go to seek him? A servant must be treated as a brother, needed as one's own soul, for an ill-treated runaway servant leaves the master with nowhere to seek him. — W. O. E. Oesterley
