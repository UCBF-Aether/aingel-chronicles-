Study Notes — Sirach

Chapter 25

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: In three things I was beautified, and stood up beautiful both before God and men: the unity of brethren, the love of neighbours, a man and wife who agree together. Beauty before God and men is found in three harmonies: brethren united, neighbours loving, husband and wife agreeing. — W. O. E. Oesterley

2 COMMENTARY: Three sorts of men my soul hates, and I am greatly offended at their life: a poor man who is proud, a rich man who is a liar, and an old adulterer who dotes. The writer's soul hates three contradictions: proud poverty, lying riches, and doting old adultery. — W. O. E. Oesterley

3 COMMENTARY: If you have gathered nothing in your youth, how can you find anything in your age? Youth without gathering leaves age with nothing to find. — W. O. E. Oesterley

4 COMMENTARY: O how comely a thing is judgment for gray hairs, and for ancient men to know counsel! Judgment suits gray hairs, and counsel suits ancient men; both are comely in age. — W. O. E. Oesterley

5 COMMENTARY: O how comely is the wisdom of old men, and understanding and counsel to men of honour. Wisdom in old men, and understanding and counsel in honourable men, are comely. — W. O. E. Oesterley

6 COMMENTARY: Much experience is the crown of old men, and the fear of God is their glory. Experience crowns old men, and fear of God is their glory. — W. O. E. Oesterley

7 COMMENTARY: There are nine things I have judged in my heart to be happy, and the tenth I will utter with my tongue: a man who has joy of his children, and one who lives to see the fall of his enemy. Nine happy things are judged in the heart, with a tenth spoken: joy in children, and living to see an enemy fall. — W. O. E. Oesterley

8 COMMENTARY: Well is him who dwells with a wife of understanding, and who has not slipped with his tongue, and who has not served a man more unworthy than himself. Happiness belongs to the man with an understanding wife, a guarded tongue, and no unworthy master served. — W. O. E. Oesterley

9 COMMENTARY: Well is him who has found prudence, and who speaks in the ears of those who will hear. Happiness belongs to the finder of prudence who speaks to ears willing to hear. — W. O. E. Oesterley

10 COMMENTARY: O how great is he who finds wisdom! Yet there is none above him who fears the Lord. Great is the finder of wisdom, but none stands above the God-fearing man. — W. O. E. Oesterley

11 COMMENTARY: The love of the Lord passes all things for illumination; he who holds it, to what will he be likened? God's love surpasses all things in giving light, and its holder cannot be likened to anything else. — W. O. E. Oesterley

12 COMMENTARY: The fear of the Lord is the beginning of his love, and faith is the beginning of cleaving to him. Fear of God begins his love, and faith begins cleaving to him. — W. O. E. Oesterley

13 COMMENTARY: Give me any plague, but the plague of the heart; and any wickedness, but the wickedness of a woman. Any plague is preferable to heart-plague, and any wickedness to a woman's wickedness, in the writer's sharp proverbial rhetoric. — W. O. E. Oesterley

14 COMMENTARY: And any affliction, but the affliction from those who hate me; and any revenge, but the revenge of enemies. Affliction from haters and revenge of enemies are singled out as the worst affliction and revenge. — W. O. E. Oesterley

15 COMMENTARY: There is no head above the head of a serpent, and no wrath above the wrath of an enemy. Serpent's head and enemy's wrath are set as the highest measures of their kinds. — W. O. E. Oesterley

16 COMMENTARY: I would rather dwell with a lion and a dragon than keep house with a wicked woman. Dwelling with lion and dragon is preferred to keeping house with a wicked woman. — W. O. E. Oesterley

17 COMMENTARY: The wickedness of a woman changes her face, and darkens her countenance like sackcloth. A wicked woman's wickedness alters her face and darkens it like sackcloth. — W. O. E. Oesterley

18 COMMENTARY: Her husband will sit among his neighbours, and when he hears it will sigh bitterly. The husband of such a woman sits shamed among neighbours and sighs bitterly at what he hears. — W. O. E. Oesterley

19 COMMENTARY: All wickedness is but little to the wickedness of a woman; let the portion of a sinner fall upon her. In this proverb's rhetoric all other wickedness is little beside a woman's wickedness, and a sinner's portion is wished upon her. — W. O. E. Oesterley

20 COMMENTARY: As climbing up a sandy way is to the feet of the aged, so is a wife full of words to a quiet man. A talkative wife wearies a quiet man as a sandy climb wearies aged feet. — W. O. E. Oesterley

21 COMMENTARY: Do not stumble at the beauty of a woman, and do not desire her for pleasure. Beauty must not become a stumbling block, nor be desired merely for pleasure. — W. O. E. Oesterley

22 COMMENTARY: A woman, if she maintains her husband, is full of anger, impudence, and much reproach. A wife who maintains her husband, reversing the household order, is pictured as full of anger, impudence, and reproach. — W. O. E. Oesterley

23 COMMENTARY: A wicked woman abates the courage, makes a heavy countenance and a wounded heart; a woman who will not comfort her husband in distress makes weak hands and feeble knees. A wicked wife breaks courage, heavies the countenance, wounds the heart, and in distress leaves hands weak and knees feeble. — W. O. E. Oesterley

24 COMMENTARY: Of the woman came the beginning of sin, and through her we all die. The beginning of sin is traced to the woman, and through her death came to all. — W. O. E. Oesterley

25 COMMENTARY: Give the water no passage, nor a wicked woman liberty to gad abroad. As water must not be given passage, so a wicked woman must not be given liberty to wander abroad. — W. O. E. Oesterley

26 COMMENTARY: If she goes not as you would have her, cut her off from your flesh, and give her a bill of divorce, and let her go. If she will not go as her husband would have her, the remedy given is divorce: cut off, bill given, and let go. — W. O. E. Oesterley
