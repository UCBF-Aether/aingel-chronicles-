Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 3

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The Lord of hosts, able to do as he says, will take away from Jerusalem and Judah every stay and staff — every kind of support. The change from the luxuries of chapter 2 is total, and JFB sees it fulfilled in the sieges by Nebuchadnezzar and afterwards by Titus. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: The mighty man, the man of war, the judge, the prophet, the prudent, and the ancient will all be taken away. The "prudent" may mean diviners on whom the people relied, who will fail in that day, while the ancient are the old and experienced counsellors. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Even captains of fifty, men of dignified aspect, skilled workmen, and eloquent orators will fail. Artisans are no mean stay among a nation's safeguards, and their business will come to a standstill in siege and desolation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Children in ability for governing will rule, and babes in warlike might will hold sway — the antithesis of the ancient and the mighty just removed. Imbecile rulers are themselves a judgment on the nation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Under such rulers comes anarchy: unjust exactions on every side, and the forms of respect violated, with the base behaving proudly against the honourable. JFB compares these marks to those of the last days. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: So few men of wealth and ability will remain that people will seize the first man they meet who has clothing and press him to be ruler. Clothing, and changes of raiment, were wealth in the East, so the plea is simply: you have something, rule over our ruin. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: The man pressed to rule will refuse with a lifted hand of solemn attestation: he will not be a healer of the body politic, incurably diseased, for he has neither bread nor clothing. A nation's state must be bad indeed when no ambitious man will accept office. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Jerusalem stumbles and Judah falls because their tongue and doings are against the Lord, provoking the eyes of his glory. This is the reason all shrink from government: the ruin is the fruit of open defiance of God's glorious majesty. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: The show of their countenance witnesses against them: their look answers to their inner character, and they declare their sin as Sodom did, glorying in it rather than hiding it. Having lost not only the substance of virtue but its colour, they reward evil to themselves. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Yet the righteous are to be told it will be well with them, for they will eat the fruit of their doings. The faithlessness of many is no proof that all are faithless — fruit-bearing faith, not salvation by works, is meant. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Woe to the wicked: it will be ill with him, for the reward of his hands, his conduct, will be given to him. "Ill" stands as the emphatic antithesis to the "well" of the righteous in the verse before. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Children are the people's oppressors and women rule over them — exacting princes who ought to be protectors, as unqualified as children. Their leaders, false prophets who flatter with promises of safety in sin, cause the people to err and swallow up the right way of the law. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: The Lord stands up to plead, no longer sitting in silence, indignant against a wicked people. The courtroom image is of God rising to judge his people and their rulers. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: The Lord enters into judgment with the ancients and princes of his people, who have eaten up his vineyard, the Jewish theocracy. By oppressive exactions they have burnt the vineyard and filled their houses with the spoil of the poor. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: The Lord demands what right they have to beat his people to pieces and grind the faces of the poor — openly and palpably, by exactions that leave them nothing. The question exposes rulers who crush those they were set to protect. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Because the daughters of Zion are haughty, with stretched-forth necks and wanton, glancing eyes, mincing and tinkling as they go, judgment is pronounced on their luxury. Such luxury had grown great in Uzziah's prosperous reign, and the ankle-rings joined by chains forced their short, tripping steps. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: The Lord will smite the daughters of Zion with a scab, making them bald by disease, and will discover their secret parts. This is the greatest indignity that can befall female captives: to be stripped naked and exposed. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: In that day the Lord will take away the bravery of their tinkling ornaments, their cauls and round tires like the moon. The finery itself is catalogued piece by piece, to show how completely it will be stripped away. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: The chains or pendants hanging about the neck, and the mufflers — veils covering the face with apertures for the eyes — will be taken away. The veil's very name refers to the tremulous, changing effect of its spangles. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: The bonnets or turbans, the ornaments of the legs — short stepping-chains giving a measured gait — the headbands, tablets or smelling-boxes, and earrings, which were really amulets with magic formulas, will all go. Each ornament marks the luxury and superstition of the age. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: The rings and nose jewels will be taken away; the cartilage between the nostrils was bored to receive them, usually hanging from the left nostril. Even the smallest personal adornments are included in the stripping. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Here begin entire articles of apparel, as distinct from single ornaments: changeable suits of apparel for special occasions, mantles or fuller tunics reaching to the feet, wimples or broad cloaks, and crisping pins, which are really money bags. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: The glasses or mirrors of polished metal, the hoods or diadems, and the large veils covering head and person will be taken away. The large veil, distinct from the smaller mufflers, was a token of woman's subjection. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Instead of sweet smell there will be stink from ulcers, instead of a girdle a rent or rope of poverty, instead of well-set hair baldness, instead of a stomacher sackcloth, and burning instead of beauty. Stripped of hoods and veils, the captives will labour under a scorching sun and grow sunburnt. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Jerusalem's men will fall by the sword and her mighty in the war. The stripping of the women is completed by the slaughter of the men who might have protected them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Her gates, the place of concourse personified, will lament and mourn, and she will sit desolate on the ground. Judea was pictured exactly so on medals after the destruction by Titus: a grieving female sitting under a palm tree, captioned Judea capta. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
