Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 19

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The burden of Egypt: Behold, the Lord rides upon a swift cloud and will come into Egypt, and the idols of Egypt will be moved at his presence, and the heart of Egypt will melt in its midst. Egypt in Hebrew is Misraim, a plural form expressing the two regions, Upper and Lower Egypt, and the idols — bull, crocodile, and the rest — are poetically said to be moved with fear at the presence of one mightier than even they were supposed to be, as at the Exodus. The invasion meant is probably Assyria under Sargon, when Judah was Assyria's ally. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: God will set the Egyptians against the Egyptians, and they will fight everyone against his brother, and everyone against his neighbour, city against city, and kingdom against kingdom. Lower will fight Upper, and Saitic against both, for Egypt was divided into forty-two nomes or districts, and the dissensions of the ruling castes — priests, warriors, and the Saitic dynasty — are certainly referred to. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: The spirit of Egypt will fail in its midst, and God will destroy its counsel, and they will seek to the idols, and to the charmers, and to those with familiar spirits, and to the wizards. The spirit is wisdom, for which Egypt was famed, answering to counsel in the parallel clause, and it will literally be poured out and made void, so that Egypt seeks help from sources that can afford none — charmers making the faint sound attributed to spirits of the dead. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The Lord, the Lord of hosts, will deliver the Egyptians into the hand of a cruel lord, and a fierce king will rule over them. The cruel lord is Sargon in the Hebrew plural of greatness, with king singular proving one is meant, though the general reference also reaches Nebuchadnezzar and the Persian conquerors Cambyses and Ochus, noted for fierce cruelty, and Gesenius refers it to Psammetichus with his foreign mercenaries. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: The waters will fail from the sea, and the river will be wasted and dried up. The sea is the Nile, whose waters at overflow resemble a sea and which Egyptians still call El-Bahr, the sea; physical calamities often accompany political convulsions, and if the Nile fails to rise to its wonted height, or rises too high to drain in time for sowing, barrenness and famine follow. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: The rivers will be turned — rather, the artificial streams made for irrigation will become putrid, stagnant, and offensive when the waters fail — and the brooks of defence will be emptied and dried up, and the reeds and flags will wither. The brooks of defence are the canals of Egypt, literally Niles, and reed and rush will utterly wither. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: The paper reeds by the brooks, by the mouth of the brooks, and everything sown by the brooks will wither, be driven away, and be no more. The paper reeds are rather pastures, places naked of wood and famed for rich herbage on the Nile's banks, and even close to the river's source vegetation will be so withered as to be scattered like powder by the wind. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: The fishers also will mourn, and all who cast angle into the brooks will lament, and those who spread nets upon the waters will languish. The Nile was famed for fish, and many will be thrown out of employment by the failure of fishes, whether they use the hook in the canals or the net in the river itself. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Moreover those who work in fine flax, and those who weave networks, will be confounded. Fine flax, combed linen worn by the rich only, was Egypt's famous manufacture, learned by Israel in Egypt and pictured on Egyptian tombs, and the networks are rather white cloth; both trades collapse with the crops. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: They will be broken in the purposes thereof — rather, the foundations, that is, the nobles will be broken and brought low — and all that make sluices and ponds for fish will be sad. The foundations are the princes, the pillars of the people, while the makers of dams confining Nile overflow in fish-ponds, or makers of gain, the common people earning their livelihood, stand opposed to the nobles, and both are broken. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Surely the princes of Zoan are fools, and the counsel of the wise counsellors of Pharaoh is become brutish: how do you say to Pharaoh, I am the son of the wise, the son of ancient kings? Zoan, Greek Tanis, near Palestine and the scene of Moses' miracles, was the capital under Sethos, and the priests, Pharaoh's usual counsellors, boast descent from wise and royal ancestors yet have no advice for the crisis. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Where are they? Where are your wise men? Let them tell you now, and let them know what the Lord of hosts has purposed upon Egypt. With all their boast of knowing the future, the wise men do not know what Jehovah of hosts has purposed, and the question exposes their pretended foresight. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: The princes of Zoan are become fools, the princes of Noph are deceived, and they have also seduced Egypt, even those who are the stay of the tribes. Noph, Greek Memphis on the Nile's western bank, capital of Lower Egypt second only to Thebes, was ruled by the military caste, deceived in fancying the country secure from Assyrian invasion, and the stay of the tribes is rather the corner-stone of her castes, the priestly and warrior princes on whom the building rests. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: The Lord has mingled a perverse spirit in the midst of Egypt, and they have caused Egypt to err in every work thereof, as a drunken man staggers in his vomit. As one mixes spices with wine to make it intoxicating, so Jehovah has poured among them a spirit of giddiness, so that they are as helpless as a drunken man, erring through the anarchy of their internal feuds and misinterpreting God's dealings at every step. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Neither will there be any work for Egypt which the head or tail, branch or rush, may do. Nothing Egypt can do will extricate it from the difficulty, neither the high nor the low, neither the lofty palm branch nor the humble reed. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: In that day Egypt will be like women, afraid and fearful because of the shaking of the hand of the Lord of hosts, which he shakes over it. Egypt will be timid and helpless as women are pictured in the prophets, fearing God's judgments by means of the invaders. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: The land of Judah will be a terror unto Egypt, and everyone who makes mention of it will be afraid in himself, because of the counsel of the Lord of hosts, which he has determined against it. Judah is a terror not by itself but as Hezekiah was the active subordinate ally of Assyria in Sargon's invasion of Egypt, and also because Egypt in calamity would remember that Judah's prophets had foretold them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: In that day five cities in the land of Egypt will speak the language of Canaan and swear to the Lord of hosts, and one will be called the city of destruction. Suffering will lead to repentance: several cities of Lower Egypt, in close intercourse with neighbouring Jewish cities, will speak the language of revelation — figuratively embracing the Jewish religion — and bind themselves to the Lord by solemn covenant, while one city, whether Leontopolis of Onias's rival temple or a city of deliverance, bears the name of destruction or defence in the manuscripts. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: In that day there will be an altar to the Lord in the midst of the land of Egypt, and a pillar at its border to the Lord. The altar is not for sacrifice but as a pillar for memorial and worship, like the patriarchs' altars, for Isaiah does not contemplate a temple in Egypt, since the only legal temple was at Jerusalem; the pillar at the border of Egypt and Judah proclaims the common faith to both countries, showing the Spirit raising Isaiah above narrow nationality to a charity anticipatory of gospel catholicity. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: It will be for a sign and for a witness to the Lord of hosts in the land of Egypt, for they will cry unto the Lord because of the oppressors, and he will send them a saviour, and a great one, and he will deliver them. The altar and pillar are a sign to contemporaries and a witness to descendants, no longer to idols but to Jehovah, and the saviour is probably Alexander the Great, welcomed as deliverer from the Persians under Cambyses, with Messiah as the antitype ultimately intended. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: The Lord will be known to Egypt, and the Egyptians will know the Lord in that day, and will do sacrifice and oblation, yes, they will vow a vow unto the Lord and perform it. The oblation is unbloody, and Egypt's knowledge of the Lord will express itself in sacrifice and vows kept, not merely promised. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: The Lord will smite Egypt, he will smite and heal it, and they will return even to the Lord, and he will be entreated of them, and will heal them. The return is from heathen sin and idolatry as apostasy from primitive truth, and the healing is as described in the conversion of verses 18–20: smitten and then healed. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: In that day there will be a highway out of Egypt to Assyria, and the Assyrian will come into Egypt, and the Egyptian into Assyria, and the Egyptians will serve with the Assyrians. The highway is free communication resting on the common faith of both, and Assyria and Egypt, joined under Alexander as parts of his empire with Jews and proselytes meeting at Jerusalem's feasts, are a type of gospel times, serving Jehovah together. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: In that day Israel will be the third with Egypt and with Assyria, even a blessing in the midst of the land. The three will be joined as one nation, and Israel will be the source of blessings to other nations and the object of their benedictions, in the midst of the earth, with Judah as the grand centre of the whole earth. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: The Lord of hosts will bless them, saying: Blessed be Egypt my people, and Assyria the work of my hands, and Israel my inheritance. The peculiar designation of Israel, the elect people, is here applied to Egypt to express its entire admission to religious privileges, Assyria is the work of God's hands spiritually, and Israel remains his inheritance. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
