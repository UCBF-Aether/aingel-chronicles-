Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 7

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: In the days of Ahaz son of Jotham, Rezin king of Syria and Pekah son of Remaliah king of Israel went up toward Jerusalem to war, but could not prevail against it. Their design, formed in Jotham's reign, was probably to unite the three kingdoms against Assyria, and an actual siege of Jerusalem took place but was foiled. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: When it was told the house of David that Syria was confederate with Ephraim — encamped upon Ephraim's territory, or supported by Ephraim, whose land lay between Syria and Judah — the heart of Ahaz and his people was moved as trees of the wood are moved by wind. The mention of David contrasts sadly with the time when David made Syria subject to him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: The Lord told Isaiah to go forth to meet Ahaz, with his son Shearjashub, at the end of the conduit of the upper pool in the highway of the fuller's field. Ahaz was there superintending works of defence and securing the water supply, and Shearjashub's very name, "A remnant shall return," was a standing memorial that the nation would not be utterly destroyed. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Isaiah was to say: Take heed and be quiet, fear not, neither be fainthearted for the two tails of smoking firebrands — Rezin and the son of Remaliah. The two kings are mere ends of firebrands, almost consumed themselves and about to go out, therefore harmless, and Ahaz must not seek Assyrian aid in a fit of panic. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Because Syria, Ephraim, and the son of Remaliah have taken evil counsel against Judah, God answers their plot with the assurance that follows. Their counsel is the scheme spelled out in the next verse: to break Judah apart and set up a vassal king of their own. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Their plan was to go up against Judah, throw it into consternation, cleave it asunder, and set a king in its midst, even the son of Tabeal. The scheme was to divide a large portion of the territory between themselves and set up a vassal king over the rest, Tabeal being an unknown Syrian-sounding name perhaps favoured by a party in Jerusalem. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Thus says the Lord God: It shall not stand, neither shall it come to pass. The confederates' counsel against Judah is overruled by God's word, as Proverbs says that no counsel stands against the Lord. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: For the head of Syria is Damascus and the head of Damascus is Rezin, and within sixty-five years Ephraim will be broken so as to be no people. Each capital will remain only its own dominion and not conquer Judah, while Israel's final breaking — under Esarhaddon, with Samaria colonised by foreigners, sixty-five years from the prophecy — assures Judah that God will ultimately destroy his people's enemies. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: The head of Ephraim is Samaria and the head of Samaria is Remaliah's son, and if you will not believe, surely you shall not be established. There is a play on words in Hebrew — if you will not confide, you shall not abide — and Ahaz brought distress on himself by distrust in the Lord and trust in Assyria. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Since Ahaz does not credit the prophet's words, the Lord offers a sign: ask it either in the depth or in the height above. A sign is a miraculous token assuring that God will fulfil his promise of saving Jerusalem, and Ahaz is offered the widest limits — depth of earth or Hades, or height of heaven — within which to choose. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: But Ahaz said, I will not ask, neither will I tempt the Lord, using a hypocritical pretext of keeping the law. To ask a sign when thus offered by God's prophet would not have been tempting God; Ahaz's true reason was his resolve to negotiate with Assyria and persevere in idolatry, excusing distrust in God by professed reverence for him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Isaiah answers: Hear now, O house of David; is it a small thing for you to weary men, but will you weary my God also? The allusion to David contrasts his trust in God with his degenerate descendant's distrust, and since God has now offered a sign which Ahaz publicly rejects, the sin is openly against God, not merely against men. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Therefore the Lord himself will give a sign: Behold, the virgin shall conceive and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel. The sign is given for the sake of the believing house of David, not for unbelieving Ahaz; "virgin" in its simplest sense applies to the Virgin Mary, and "Immanuel," God with us, cannot in a strict sense apply to Isaiah's son, but only to him expressly called the mighty God in chapter 9 — while the language is also partially applicable to the immediate event, as a type foreshadowing the great central end of prophecy, Jesus Christ. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Butter and honey shall he eat, that he may know to refuse the evil and choose the good. Butter is curdled milk, grateful in the heat of the East, and honey was abundant in Palestine; at about three years of age moral consciousness begins, and the child's ordinary infant food also implies the real humanity of Immanuel. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: For before the child shall know to refuse the evil and choose the good, the land that you dread will be forsaken by both her kings. Desolate shall be the land before whose two kings Ahaz is alarmed — Syria and Samaria regarded as one — just two years after this prophecy, as Tiglath-pileser subdued them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: The Lord will bring upon Ahaz, his people, and his father's house days that have not come since Ephraim departed from Judah: the king of Assyria. Though temporary deliverance was given then and final deliverance through Messiah, sore punishment follows Ahaz's Assyrian policy, and after subduing Syria and Israel the Assyrians will make Judah the battlefield against Egypt and tributary to Assyria itself. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: In that day the Lord will hiss — whistle, as to bring bees to settle — for the fly in the uttermost part of the rivers of Egypt and for the bee in the land of Assyria. The fly figures numerous, troublesome foes from the remotest parts of Egypt, such as Pharaoh-necho, and the bee figures Assyria's hosts, fulfilled by Sennacherib, Esarhaddon, and Nebuchadnezzar. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: They will come and rest in the desolate valleys, in the holes of the rocks, upon all thorns, and upon all bushes, the image of flies and bees kept up. The enemy will overspread the land everywhere, even in desolate valleys, among wild thorns as well as valued bushes. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: In the same day the Lord will shave with a razor that is hired, by them beyond the river, by the king of Assyria, the head and the hair of the feet. The Assyrians are God's instrument for devastating Judea, as a razor sweeps away all hair, and "hired" alludes to Ahaz hiring Tiglath-pileser; to cut the beard is the greatest indignity to an Easterner. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: In that day a man will nourish a young cow and two sheep, for agriculture will cease and the land become one great pasturage. With so few inhabitants left, a single heifer and two sheep will be all a man owns. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Because of the abundance of milk they give, he will eat butter — thick milk or cream — for butter and honey will everyone eat who is left in the land. Food of spontaneous growth will be the resource of the few inhabitants left, with honey abundant as bees find wild flowers everywhere. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: In that day every place where there were a thousand vines worth a silverling each will become briers and thorns. Vineyards were estimated by the number and goodness of their vines, and Judea, which admits of a high state of cultivation, will fall barren through neglect. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: With arrows and bows men will come there, because all the land will become briers and thorns. The once-cultivated land will become a vast hunting ground abounding in wild beasts. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: On all hills that were digged with the mattock for planting vines, no one will come for fear of briers and thorns; it will be for the sending forth of oxen and the treading of lesser cattle, sheep and goats. Only cattle will be able to penetrate the briery ground where vine-dressers once worked. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

No note for verse(s) 10 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
