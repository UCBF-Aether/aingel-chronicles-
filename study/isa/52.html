Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 52

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Awake, awake, put on your strength, O Zion; put on your beautiful garments, O Jerusalem, the holy city, for henceforth there will no more come into you the uncircumcised and the unclean. Verses 1 through 13 are connected with chapter 51. Zion long in bondage in chapter 51 verses 17 to 20 is called to put on beautiful garments appropriate to its future prosperity. Strength means as thy adornment, answering to beautiful garments in the parallel clause; arouse thyself from dejection and assume confidence. The holy city recalls Nehemiah and Revelation. No more unclean recalls chapters 35 and 60, Joel, and Revelation: a prophecy never yet fulfilled. Uncircumcised means spiritually, as in Ezekiel and Acts. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Shake yourself from the dust; arise, and sit down, O Jerusalem; loose yourself from the bands of your neck, O captive daughter of Zion. From the dust means the seat of mourners, as in Job. Arise, and sit means namely, in a more dignified place: on a divan or a throne, after having shaken off the dust gathered up by the flowing dress when seated on the ground; or simply, Arise, and sit erect. Bands of neck means the yoke of thy captivity. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: For thus says the Lord: You have sold yourselves for nought, and you will be redeemed without money. As you became your foes' servants, without their paying any price for you, as in Jeremiah, so they shall release you without demanding any price or reward, as in chapter 45, where Cyrus is represented as doing so: a type of their final restoration gratuitously in like manner. So the spiritual Israel, sold under sin, gratuitously, as in Romans, shall be redeemed also gratuitously, as in chapter 55. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: For thus says the Lord God: My people went down aforetime into Egypt to sojourn there, and the Assyrian oppressed them without cause. My people means Jacob and his sons. Went down means Judea was an elevated country compared with Egypt. Sojourn means they went there to stay only till the famine in Canaan should have ceased. Assyrian means Sennacherib. Remember how I delivered you from Egypt and the Assyrian; what, then, is to prevent Me from delivering you out of Babylon, and the mystical Babylon and the Antichrist in the last days? Without cause means answering to for naught in verse 5; it was an act of gratuitous oppression in the present case, as in that case. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Now therefore, what have I here, says the Lord, that my people is taken away for nought? They that rule over them make them to howl, says the Lord, and my name continually every day is blasphemed. What have I here means that is, what am I called on to do? The fact that My people is taken away into captivity, as in chapter 49, for naught, by gratuitous oppression, as in verse 4 and verse 3, demands My interposition. They that rule means or tyrannize, namely, Babylon, literal and mystical. Make to howl means or, raise a cry of exultation over them. Blasphemed means namely, in Babylon: God's reason for delivering His people, not their goodness, but for the sake of His holy name, as in Ezekiel. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Therefore my people will know my name; therefore they will know in that day that I am he that does speak; behold, it is I. Shall know in that day means when Christ shall reveal Himself to Israel sensibly; the only means whereby their obstinate unbelief shall be overcome, as in Psalm 102 and Zechariah. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him that brings good tidings, that publishes peace, that brings good tidings of good, that publishes salvation, that says unto Zion: Your God reigns! Feet means that is, The advent of such a herald seen on the distant mountains, as in chapters 40, 41, 25, and Song of Solomon, running in haste with the long-expected good tidings, is most grateful to the desolated city, as in Nahum. Good tidings means only partially applying to the return from Babylon. Fully, and antitypically, the Gospel, as in Luke, beginning at Jerusalem, the city of the great King, as in Matthew, where Messiah shall, at the final restoration of Israel, reign as peculiarly Zion's God, Thy God reigneth, as in Psalm 2. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Your watchmen will lift up the voice; with the voice together will they sing, for they will see eye to eye, when the Lord will bring again Zion. Watchmen means set on towers separated by intervals to give the earliest notice of the approach of any messenger with tidings, as in chapter 21. The Hebrew is more forcible than the English version, The voice of thy watchmen, exclamatory as in Song of Solomon: They lift up their voice! together they sing. Eye to eye means that is, close at hand, and so clearly, as in Numbers, face to face, mouth to mouth. Compare 1 Corinthians and Revelation, of which Simeon's sight of the Saviour was a prefiguration, as in Luke. The watchmen, spiritually, are ministers and others who pray for the peace of Jerusalem, as in chapter 62. Bring again means that is, restore, or else, return to. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Break forth into joy, sing together, you waste places of Jerusalem, for the Lord has comforted his people, he has redeemed Jerusalem. This recalls chapters 14 and 42. Redeemed means spiritually and nationally, as in chapter 48. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: The Lord has made bare his holy arm in the eyes of all the nations, and all the ends of the earth will see the salvation of our God. Arm is a metaphor from warriors who bare their arm for battle, as in Ezekiel. All earth see salvation of God means the deliverance wrought by God for Israel will cause all nations to acknowledge the Lord, as in chapter 66. The partial fulfilment, as in Luke, is a forerunner of the future complete fulfilment. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Depart, depart, go out from thence, touch no unclean thing; go out of the midst of her; be clean, that bear the vessels of the Lord. This recalls chapters 48 and Zechariah. Long residence in Babylon made many loath to leave it: so as to mystical Babylon, as in Revelation. Ye that bear vessels of the Lord means the priests and Levites, whose office it was to carry the vessels of the temple, as in Jeremiah. Nebuchadnezzar had carried them to Babylon. Cyrus restored them, as in Ezra. Be clean means by separating yourselves wholly from Babylonian idolaters, mystical and literal. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: For you will not go out with haste, nor go by flight, for the Lord will go before you, and the God of Israel will be your rereward. With haste means as when ye left Egypt, as in Exodus and Deuteronomy, compare the note on chapter 28. Ye shall have time to cleanse yourselves and make deliberate preparation for departure. Lord means Jehovah, as your Leader in front, as in chapters 40 and Exodus and Micah. Rereward is literally, gather up, that is, to bring up the rear of your host. The transition is frequent from the glory of Messiah in His advent to reign, to His humiliation in His advent to suffer. Indeed, so are both advents accounted one, that He is not said, in His second coming, to be about to return, but to come. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Behold, my servant will deal prudently, he will be exalted and extolled, and be very high. Here the fifty-third chapter ought to begin, and the fifty-second chapter end with verse 12. This section, from here to end of the fifty-third chapter, settles the controversy with the Jews, if Messiah be the person meant, and with infidels, if written by Isaiah, or at any time before Christ. The correspondence with the life and death of Jesus Christ is so minute, that it could not have resulted from conjecture or accident. An impostor could not have shaped the course of events so as to have made his character and life appear to be a fulfilment of it. The writing is, moreover, declaredly prophetic. The quotations of it in the New Testament show: first, that it was, before the time of Jesus, a recognised part of the Old Testament; second, that it refers to Messiah, as in Matthew, Mark, Luke, John, Acts, Romans, and 1 Peter. The indirect allusions to it still more clearly prove the Messianic interpretation; so universal was that interpretation, that it is simply referred to in connection with the atoning virtue of His death, without being formally quoted, as in Mark, Romans, Corinthians, Peter, and John. The genuineness of the passage is certain; for the Jews would not have forged it, since it is opposed to their notion of Messiah, as a triumphant temporal prince. The Christians could not have forged it; for the Jews, the enemies of Christianity, are our librarians. The Jews try to evade its force by the figment of two Messiahs, one a suffering Messiah, Ben Joseph, the other a triumphant Messiah, Ben David. Hillel maintained that Messiah has already come in the person of Hezekiah. Buxtorf states that many of the modern Rabbins believe that He has been come a good while, but will not manifest Himself because of the sins of the Jews. But the ancient Jews, as the Chaldee paraphrast, Jonathan, refer it to Messiah; so the Medrasch Tauchuma, a commentary on the Pentateuch, also Rabbi Moses Haddarschan. Some explain it of the Jewish people, either in the Babylonish exile, or in their present sufferings and dispersion. Others, the pious portion of the nation taken collectively, whose sufferings made a vicarious satisfaction for the ungodly. Others, Isaiah, or Jeremiah, the prophets collectively. But an individual is plainly described: he suffers voluntarily, innocently, patiently, and as the efficient cause of the righteousness of His people, which holds good of none other but Messiah, as in chapter 53 verses 4 to 6, 9, and 11, contrast Jeremiah and Psalms. Chapter 53 verse 9 can hold good of none other. The objection that the sufferings in chapter 53 verses 1 to 10 referred to are represented as past, the glorification alone as future in verses 13 to 15 and chapter 53 verses 11 and 12, arises from not seeing that the prophet takes his stand in the midst of the scenes which he describes as future. The greater nearness of the first advent, and the interval between it and the second, are implied by the use of the past tense as to the first, the future as to the second. Behold means awakening attention to the striking picture of Messiah that follows, as in John. My servant means Messiah, as in chapter 42. Deal prudently is rather, prosper, as the parallel clause favours, as in chapter 53 verse 10. Or, uniting both meanings, shall reign well. This verse sets forth in the beginning the ultimate issue of His sufferings, the description of which follows: the conclusion in chapter 53 verse 12 corresponds; the section, from verse 13 to chapter 53 verse 12, begins as it ends with His final glory. Extolled means elevated, as in Mark, Ephesians, and 1 Peter. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: As many were astonished at you; his visage was so marred more than any man, and his form more than the sons of men. This, with verse 15, is a summary of Messiah's history, which is set forth more in detail in the fifty-third chapter. Just as many were astonished, accompanied with aversion, as in Jeremiah, etc.; his visage, etc.; so shall He sprinkle, etc.; Israel in this answers to its antitype Messiah, now an astonishment and byword, as in Deuteronomy, hereafter about to be a blessing and means of salvation to many nations, as in chapters 2 and Micah. Thee, his means such changes of persons are common in Hebrew poetry. Marred is Hebrew, disfigurement, abstract for concrete; not only disfigured, but disfigurement itself. More than man means, some translate, so that it was no longer that of a man, as in Psalm 22. The more perfect we may suppose the body prepared, as in Hebrews, for Him by God, the sadder by contrast was the marring of His visage and form. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: So will he sprinkle many nations; the kings will shut their mouths at him, for that which had not been told them will they see, and that which they had not heard will they consider. To exult is not the sense; but the word universally in the Old Testament means either to sprinkle with blood, as the high priest makes an expiation, as in Leviticus, or with water, to purify, as in Ezekiel, compare as to the Spirit, Acts, both appropriate to Messiah, as in John, Hebrews, and 1 Peter. The antithesis is sufficient without any forced rendering. Many were astonished; so many, not merely men, but nations shall be sprinkled. They were amazed at such an abject person claiming to be Messiah; yet it is He who shall justify and purify. Men were dumb with the amazement of scorn at one marred more than the lowest of men, yet the highest: even kings, as in chapter 49, shall be dumb with awe and veneration, shut mouths, as in Job and Micah. That not told them means the reason why kings shall so venerate them; the wonders of redemption, which had not been before told them, shall then be announced to them, wonders such as they had never heard or seen paralleled, as in chapters 55 and Romans. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
