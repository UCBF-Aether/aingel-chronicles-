Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 33

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Woe to you that spoil, and you were not spoiled, and deal treacherously, and they dealt not treacherously with you! When you will cease to spoil, you will be spoiled, and when you make an end to deal treacherously, they will deal treacherously with you. This is the last of Isaiah's prophecies as to Sennacherib's overthrow, in the autumn of 713 B.C., describing the Assyrian spoiler who, strong as he is, will fall before Jehovah who is stronger. And you means: though you were not spoiled, though you were not dealt treacherously with, your spoiling and treachery are therefore without excuse, being unprovoked; and cease means when God has let you do your worst in execution of His plans, your own turn will come. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: O Lord, be gracious unto us; we have waited for you; be their arm every morning, and our salvation also in the time of trouble. The prophet speaks interceding for his people, separating himself in thought for a moment from them, and immediately returning to his natural identification with them in "our"; every morning means each day as it dawns, especially during danger, as the parallel time of trouble shows. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: At the noise of the tumult the people fled; at the lifting up of yourself the nations were scattered. The tumult is the approach of Jehovah likened to an advancing thunderstorm, which is His voice, causing the people to flee, and the nations are the Assyrian levies scattered at His lifting up. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Your spoil will be gathered like the gathering of the caterpillar, and as the running to and fro of locusts he will run upon them. The invaders' spoil will be left behind by them in their flight, and the Jews will gather it; the caterpillar is rather the wingless locust, and as it gathers — the Hebrew word for gathers is properly used of gathering harvest fruits — so the Jews will gather the spoil, running to and fro in gathering, and they, not he, will run upon it, the prey. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Wisdom and knowledge will be the stability of your times, and strength of salvation; the fear of the Lord is his treasure. Wisdom is sacred, that is, piety; thy is Hezekiah's, or rather Judea's, with his referring to the same, such changes of pronoun being common in Hebrew poetry; and the treasure is not so much material wealth as piety, which will constitute the riches of the nation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Behold, their valiant ones will cry without, and the ambassadors of peace will weep bitterly. From the vision of future glory Isaiah returns to the disastrous present: the grief of the valiant ones, parallel to and identical with the ambassadors of peace, men of rank sent with presents to sue for peace but standing without the enemy's camp, their suit being rejected, and the highways deserted through fear, the cities insulted, the lands devastated. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: The highways lie waste, the wayfaring man ceases, he has broken the covenant, he has despised the cities, he regards no man. The covenant is broken when Sennacherib invaded Judea: Hezekiah paid him a large sum to leave the land, yet Sennacherib received the money and still sent his army against Jerusalem; despised means making slight of cities as unable to resist him, easily capturing them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: The earth mourns and languishes, Lebanon is ashamed and hewn down, Sharon is like a wilderness, and Bashan and Carmel shake off their fruits. This recalls chapter 24: Lebanon is personified, with allusion to the Assyrian cutting down its choice trees, Sharon south of Carmel along the Mediterranean is proverbial for fertility, Bashan afterwards called Batanea is as in chapter 2, and fruits are rather leaves, lying as desolate as in winter. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Now will I rise, says the Lord; now will I be exalted; now will I lift up myself. The sight of His people's misery arouses Jehovah, for He has let the enemy go far enough; I is emphatic, for God Himself will do what man could not. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: You will conceive chaff, you will bring forth stubble, and your breath, as fire, will devour you. You are the enemy: conceive chaff recalls chapters 26 and 59, and your breath is rather your own spirit of anger and ambition, which as fire will devour you. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: The people will be as the burnings of lime, as thorns cut up they will be burned in the fire. This recalls chapter 9 and Amos: perhaps alluding to their being about to be burnt on the funeral pyre of chapter 30, and thorns are the wicked. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Hear, you that are far off, what I have done, and you that are near, acknowledge my might. Far off means distant nations, and near means the Jews and adjoining peoples, both called to hear what God has done and acknowledge His might. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: The sinners in Zion are afraid; fearfulness has surprised the hypocrites. Who among us will dwell with the devouring fire? Who among us will dwell with everlasting burnings? The sinners in Zion are false professors of religion among the elect people, and the hypocrites are rather the profane, the abandoned. Who among us asks: if Jehovah's wrath could thus consume such a host in one night, who could abide it if continued forever? Fire is a common image for divine judgments. Among us adds: if such awful judgments have fallen on those who knew not the true God, how infinitely worse will fall on us who, amid religious privileges and profession, sin against God? — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: He that walks righteously, and speaks uprightly; he that despises the gain of oppressions, that shakes his hands from holding of bribes, that stops his ears from hearing of blood, and shuts his eyes from seeing evil. In contrast to the trembling sinners in Zion, the righteous will be secure amid all judgments, described according to the Old Testament standpoint of righteousness; stopping ears and eyes means rejoicing not in iniquity, for the senses are avenues for sin's entrance. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: He will dwell on high, and his place of defence will be the munitions of rocks; bread will be given him, and his waters will be sure. On high means heights inaccessible to the foe, and bread and waters are an image from the expected siege by Sennacherib: however besieged by trials without, the godly will have literal and spiritual food, as God sees good for them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Your eyes will see the king in his beauty, and they will behold the land that is very far off. Thine are the saints': the king in his beauty is not as now Hezekiah in sackcloth, oppressed by the enemy, but King Messiah in His beauty; and the land very far off is rather the land in its remotest extent, no longer pent up as Hezekiah was with the siege — or the far-off land may be heaven, the Jerusalem above, which is to follow Messiah's earthly reign at literal Jerusalem. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Your heart will meditate terror. Where is the scribe? Where is the receiver? Where is he that counted the towers? Meditate means on the terror caused by the enemy, but now past, and where is the language of the Jews exulting over escape from danger. The scribe is he who enrolled the army, or prescribed the tribute, or kept account of the spoil, the principal scribe of the host; the receiver is the weigher, with scales for weighing spoils mentioned in Assyrian inscriptions; and he that counted the towers is he whose duty was to reconnoitre and report the strength of the city to be besieged. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: You will not see a fierce people, a people of a deeper speech than you can perceive, of a stammering tongue, that you cannot understand. The fierce people are the Assyrians, who will not be allowed to enter Jerusalem, or you will no longer see fierce enemies threatening you as previously, such as the Assyrians, Romans, and the last Antichristian host yet to assail Jerusalem; the stammering tongue is barbarous and unintelligible, for the Assyrian tongue differed only in dialect from Hebrew, but in the Assyrian levies were many of non-Semitic race and language, such as Medes and Elamites. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Look upon Zion, the city of our solemnities: your eyes will see Jerusalem a quiet habitation, a tabernacle that will not be taken down, not one of its stakes will ever be removed, neither will any of its cords be broken. The solemnities are solemn assemblies at the great feasts, and the tabernacle not taken down means there will be no more taking away to an enemy's land, or, from nomads in shifting tents, the saints who once sojourned in tabernacles as pilgrims will have a building of God eternal in the heavens. The stakes driven into the ground fastened the cords, and Christ's Church will never fall, so individual believers also stand firm. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: But there the glorious Lord will be unto us a place of broad rivers and streams, wherein no galley with oars will go, neither will a gallant ship pass thereby. There, namely in Jerusalem, Jehovah will be as a broad river surrounding the city, and a river of such a kind as no ship of war can pass; Jerusalem had not the advantage of a river, and Jehovah will be as one to it, affording all its advantages without any disadvantages. A galley with oars is a war vessel of long shape propelled by oars, while merchant vessels were broader and carried sail, and a gallant ship is a mighty vessel that will not dare to pass where the mighty Lord stands as defence. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: For the Lord is our judge, the Lord is our lawgiver, the Lord is our king; he will save us. The Lord thrice repeated suggests the Trinity, and judge, lawgiver, and king are the perfect ideal of the theocracy, to be realised under Messiah alone, with judicial, legislative, and administrative functions exercised by Him in person. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Your tacklings are loosed; they could not well strengthen their mast, they could not spread the sail: then is the prey of a great spoil divided, and the lame take the prey. Continuing the allegory of verse 21, the enemies' host is compared to a war galley deprived of the tacklings or cords by which the mast is sustained and the sail spread, and therefore sure to be wrecked on the broad river and become Israel's prey; the tacklings hold not firm the base of the mast. Then, when the Assyrian host is discomfited, the prey of a great spoil is divided — Hezekiah had given Sennacherib three hundred talents of silver and thirty of gold and stripped the temple's gold, and this treasure was probably part of the prey found in the foe's camp, so that after the invasion Hezekiah had much wealth — and even the lame will spoil the Assyrian camp. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: The inhabitant will not say: I am sick; the people that dwell therein will be forgiven their iniquity. Sick may allude to the beginning of the pestilence by which the Assyrians were destroyed, which while sparing the righteous affected some within the city, the sinners in Zion, or to the sickness that visited Hezekiah; in the Jerusalem to come there will be no sickness because there will be no iniquity, it being forgiven, and the latter clause contains the cause of the former. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
