Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 21

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The burden of the desert of the sea: As whirlwinds in the south pass through, so it comes from the desert, from a terrible land. This repeats the assurance of chapters 13–14 to the Jews about to be captives in Babylon, that their enemy will be destroyed and they delivered; the prophet supposes himself a watchman in Babylon beholding events as they pass. The desert is the plain between Babylon and Persia, once desert and to become so again, the sea is the plain covered with Euphrates water until Semiramis raised dams which Cyrus removed, and the terrible land is Media, whose south whirlwinds come unbroken across the plain. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: A grievous vision is declared to Isaiah: the treacherous dealer deals treacherously, and the spoiler spoils. Go up, O Elam; besiege, O Media; all the sighing thereof God has made to cease. The treachery refers to Cyrus's military stratagem in taking Babylon, or Babylon repaid with treachery in her own coin, and Isaiah abruptly recites God's order to the Persians, his instruments of vengeance; Elam is a province of Persia east of the Euphrates, and the sighing made to cease is the sighing caused by Babylon. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Therefore Isaiah's loins are filled with pain, pangs have taken hold of him as the pangs of a woman that travails, and he is bowed down at the hearing of it and dismayed at the seeing of it. Imagining himself among the exiles in Babylon, the prophet cannot help feeling moved by the calamities coming on it, as he was moved for Moab; the Hebrew may mean he was so bowed down he could not hear, and so dismayed he could not see. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: His heart panted, fearfulness affrighted him, and the night of his pleasure he has turned into fear unto him. His heart is bewildered, and he supposes himself one of the banqueters at Belshazzar's feast on the night Babylon was taken by surprise, hence "my pleasure" turned to fear. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Prepare the table, watch in the watchtower, eat, drink: arise, you princes, and anoint the shield. Isaiah first represents the king ordering the feast ready, during which Cyrus opened the dykes confining the Euphrates so he could enter Babylon by the river channel; then the language turns suddenly to alarm for the Babylonian princes, who had set the watch and thought they might feast in security, and are now called to anoint their shields — oiling the leather lest it crack — and make ready for instant self-defence. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: For thus has the Lord said to Isaiah: Go, set a watchman, let him declare what he sees. This is God's direction to set a watchman to declare what he sees, and Isaiah himself is represented as the one who declares, or as the watchman on the tower reporting what he sees. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: When the watchman sees a chariot with a couple of horsemen, a chariot of asses, and a chariot of camels, he will hearken diligently with much heed. The chariots are rather a body of riders, some riding in pairs on horses two abreast, others on asses, others on camels, for the Persians used asses and camels for war, and the joint army of Medes and Persians appears under their respective leaders. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: And he cried, A lion: My lord, I stand continually upon the watchtower in the daytime, and I am set in my ward whole nights. The watchman cries, I am as a lion: the point of comparison is vigilance, for the lion's eyelids are short so that even asleep he seems on watch, and hence lions were painted on temple doors as symbols of watchfulness guarding the place. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Behold, here comes a chariot of men, with a couple of horsemen, and he answered and said: Babylon is fallen, is fallen, and all the graven images of her gods he has broken unto the ground. The first half describes what the watchman sees — the same body of riders two abreast — and the second what he says, for in the interval the overthrow is accomplished, needing announcement because of the city's great extent; the repetition, fallen, fallen, expresses emphasis and certainty, and the Persians, who had no images or temples, dashed Babylon's images of Bel, Merodach, and the rest to the ground. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: O my threshing, and the corn of my floor: that which I have heard of the Lord of hosts, the God of Israel, have I declared unto you. My threshing is God's people, the Jews trodden down by Babylon, and the corn of the floor is his people treated as corn laid on the floor for threshing, implying that by affliction a remnant, grain, would be separated from the ungodly, chaff; Isaiah addresses his countrymen, not the Babylonians. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: The burden of Dumah: He calls to Isaiah out of Seir, Watchman, what of the night? Watchman, what of the night? This is a prophecy to the Idumeans who taunted the afflicted Jews in the Babylonian captivity: one out of Seir asks what of the night, whether there is hope of dawn deliverance, and the repetition marks the Idumeans' anxiety. Dumah is a tribe and region of Ishmael in Arabia, a part put for the whole of Edom, and its name in Hebrew means silence, implying Edom will soon be reduced to silence; Seir is the principal mountain in Idumea, and the watchman is the prophet announcing future events from his tower. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: The watchman said: The morning comes, and also the night; if you will inquire, inquire, and return, come. The morning of prosperity comes, and soon after follows the night of adversity, for though the Idumeans may have a gleam of prosperity it will soon be followed by adversity again — or, prosperity comes to the Jews to be quickly followed by adversity to the Idumeans who exulted in Jerusalem's fall; and if they wish to ask again they may, but if they want an answer of peace they must return, be converted to God, and come. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: The burden upon Arabia: In the forest in Arabia you will lodge, O you travelling companies of Dedanim. This prophecy that Arabia would be overrun by a foreign foe within a year, probably in the wars between Assyria and Egypt, has the caravans driven through fear to unfrequented routes; the forest is not a grove of trees but a region of thick underwood, rugged and inaccessible, for Arabia has no forest of trees, and Dedanim is in North Arabia. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: The inhabitants of the land of Tema brought water to him that was thirsty, and they prevented with their bread him that fled. Tema is a kindred tribe, an oasis in that region, and the Temeans give water to the faint and thirsting Dedanites — the greatest act of hospitality in the burning East where water is scarce — and anticipate the fugitives' wants by supplying the bread due to them for support. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: For they fled from the swords, from the drawn sword, and from the bent bow, and from the grievousness of war. The fugitive Dedanites and other Arabs flee from the drawn sword, the bent bow, and the grievousness of war brought by the invading foe. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: For thus has the Lord said to Isaiah: Within a year, according to the years of a hireling, all the glory of Kedar will fail. As a hireling's fixed term can neither be added to nor taken from, so Kedar's fall is fixed within a year; Kedar is a wandering tribe north of Arabia-Petraea, put for Arabia in general. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: The residue of the number of archers, the mighty men of the children of Kedar, will be diminished, for the Lord God of Israel has spoken it. The remnant of Arab warriors, famous in the bow, left after the invasion will be small, and the sentence stands because the Lord God of Israel has spoken it. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
