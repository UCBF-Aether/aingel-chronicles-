Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 2

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: This is the inscription of a new section: the word, the revelation, that Isaiah saw concerning Judah and Jerusalem. JFB treats the whole chapter, verses 1–22, as one prophecy under this heading. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: In the last days, Messiah's days, the mountain of the Lord's house will be established on the highest hill, and all nations will flow to it as a broad stream. The temple on Mount Moriah is a type of the gospel beginning at Jerusalem, made so conspicuous that the nations are attracted to it. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Many peoples will come up to the mountain of the Lord to learn his ways, and the law will go forth from Jerusalem. JFB reads this as a literal promised blessing matching the literal fulfilment of the curse, with the Holy Spirit poured out for a general conversion. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The Lord will judge as sovereign umpire, settling controversies between nations, and swords will be beaten into plowshares and spears into pruninghooks. Eastern plowshares resembled short swords, so the image is of weapons turned to farming tools when war is learned no more. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: In view of Israel's high destiny to be a blessing to all nations, the house of Jacob is urged to walk in the light of the Lord. The exhortation follows from the glorious future just described: Israel should live worthy of it now. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: The Lord has forsaken his people because they are filled with the superstitions of the East, with soothsayers forbidden by the law, and with alliances with strangers. Joining hands with the Philistines and others, in marriage and in national league, was forbidden. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: The land is full of silver and gold, horses and chariots, heaped together against the law's forbidding. God meant his people to be wholly dependent on him rather than on the ordinary means of warfare, and horses were also connected with idolatry. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: The land is also full of idols, the work of men's hands, worshipped in private even when public idolatry was not sanctioned under Uzziah and Jotham. The turn from military trust to idolatry is natural, since horses themselves were tied to idol worship. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Both the mean man and the great man bow down to idols, so all ranks are idolaters together. The words "forgive them not" are a threat expressed as an imperative: Isaiah so identifies with God's will that he prays for what he knows God purposes. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Sinners will be driven to hide in the rock and in the dust from the terror of the Lord and the glory of his majesty. This is a poetical way of saying that God's judgments will force them to seek a hiding-place from his wrath. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: The lofty looks of man will be humbled and his haughtiness bowed down, and the Lord alone will be exalted in that day. God will so vindicate his honour in the day of judgments that no one else shall be exalted. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: The day of the Lord will come at last upon every proud and lofty thing on which the nation prided itself, not merely upon proud persons. Man has had many days; this is God's day, beginning with judgment. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: The day of the Lord falls on the cedars of Lebanon and the oaks of Bashan, images for haughty nobles and princes. Bashan, east of Jordan, was famous for fine oaks, and the oaks may also recall the idolatry of chapter 1. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: The day falls on all high mountains and hills, the high places where sacrifices were unlawfully offered, and also the strong fastnesses in which men trusted rather than in God. Both idolatrous worship and military confidence are struck together. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Every high tower and fenced wall will be brought low in that day, the fortifications on which cities relied for safety. Towers were often built upon city walls, and "fenced" means strongly fortified. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Judgment will fall on the ships of Tarshish, richly laden far-voyaging vessels, and on all pleasant pictures and luxurious ornaments. The judgment reaches all that ministers to man's luxury, and the Vulgate's wider reading covers everything beautiful to the sight. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: The loftiness of man will be bowed down and the haughtiness of men made low, and the Lord alone will be exalted in that day. The verse repeats verse 11 for emphatic confirmation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: The idols, literally "vain things" and "nothings," will utterly pass away. JFB notes this was fulfilled to the letter: before the Babylonian captivity the Jews were most prone to idolatry, and in no instance ever since. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Men will go into the holes of the rocks and caves of the earth, abounding in hilly Judea, to hide from the terror of the Lord when he arises to shake the earth. The fulfilment answers exactly to the threat of verse 10, as a figure for severe and universal judgments. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: In that day men will cast their idols of silver and gold to the moles and to the bats, creatures of darkness and of tenantless ruins. Idols made for worship will be thrown away to unclean, underground dwellers when their worshippers flee. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Therefore cease from man, whose breath is in his nostrils, for the high ones on whom the people trusted will themselves be brought low. Dependence belongs on the Lord, not on princes who must themselves fall. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

No note for verse(s) 21 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
