Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 37

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: When king Hezekiah heard it, he rent his clothes, and covered himself with sackcloth, and went into the house of the Lord. This chapter continues the narrative of chapter 36. Sackcloth is as in chapter 20, and the house of the Lord is the sure resort of God's people in distress. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: He sent Eliakim, who was over the household, and Shebna the scribe, and the elders of the priests, covered with sackcloth, unto Isaiah the prophet, the son of Amoz. Sending unto Isaiah implies the importance of the prophet's position at the time: the chief officers of the court are deputed to wait on him, as in 2 Kings 22. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: They said unto him: Thus says Hezekiah: This day is a day of trouble, and of rebuke, and of blasphemy, for the children are come to the birth, and there is not strength to bring forth. Rebuke is the Lord's rebuke for His people's sins, blasphemy is the blasphemous railing of Rab-shakeh, and the children come to the birth is a proverbial expression for being in the most extreme danger with no power to avert it, as in Hosea 13. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: It may be the Lord your God will hear the words of Rabshakeh, whom the king of Assyria his master has sent to reproach the living God, and will reprove the words which the Lord your God has heard: wherefore lift up your prayer for the remnant that is left. Hear means take cognizance of, reprove means will punish him for the words, and the remnant is the two tribes of the kingdom of Judah, Israel being already captive; Isaiah is entreated to act as intercessor with God. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Isaiah said unto them: Thus will you say unto your master: Thus says the Lord: Be not afraid of the words that you have heard, wherewith the servants of the king of Assyria have blasphemed me. Servants is literally youths, mere lads, implying disparagement, not an embassy of venerable elders, and the Hebrew is different from that for servants in verse 5; blasphemed me recalls chapter 36 verse 20. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Behold, I will send a blast upon him, and he will hear a rumour, and return to his own land, and I will cause him to fall by the sword in his own land. Blast is rather, I will put a spirit into him, so influencing his judgment that when he hears the report concerning Tirhakah he will return; the report also of the destruction of his army at Jerusalem, reaching Sennacherib while he was in the southwest of Palestine on the borders of Egypt, led him to retreat. By the sword recalls verse 38. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: So Rabshakeh returned, and found the king of Assyria warring against Libnah, for he had heard that he was departed from Lachish. Returned means to the camp of his master, and Libnah means whiteness, the Blanche-garde of the Crusaders; Eusebius and Jerome place it more south, in the district of Eleutheropolis, ten miles northwest of Lachish, which Sennacherib had captured. Libnah was in Judea and given to the priests. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: He heard say concerning Tirhakah king of Ethiopia: He is come forth to make war with you. And when he heard it, he sent messengers to Hezekiah, saying. Tirhakah is as in chapters 17 and 18: Egypt was in part governed by three successive Ethiopian monarchs for forty or fifty years, Sabacho, Sevechus, and Tirhakah. Sevechus retired from Lower Egypt owing to the resistance of the priests, whereupon Sethos, a prince-priest, obtained supreme power with Tanis, Zoan in Scripture, or Memphis as capital; the Ethiopians retained Upper Egypt under Tirhakah, with Thebes as capital. Tirhakah's fame as a conqueror rivalled that of Sesostris, and he, with at least one Pharaoh of Lower Egypt, was Hezekiah's ally against Assyria; the tidings of his approach made Sennacherib the more anxious to get possession of Jerusalem before his arrival. Sent in Kings more fully expresses Sennacherib's eagerness by adding again. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Thus will you speak to Hezekiah king of Judah, saying: Let not your God, in whom you trust, deceive you, saying: Jerusalem will not be given into the hand of the king of Assyria. He tries to influence Hezekiah himself, as Rab-shakeh had addressed the people; God deceiving is as in Numbers 23, for God cannot deceive. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Behold, you have heard what the kings of Assyria have done to all lands by destroying them utterly, and will you be delivered? All lands recalls chapter 14, and he does not dare to enumerate Egypt in the list. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Have the gods of the nations delivered them which my fathers have destroyed, as Gozan, and Haran, and Rezeph, and the children of Eden which were in Telassar? Gozan is in Mesopotamia, on the Chabour, the district's name, Chabour the river's. Haran is more to the west, whither Abraham removed from Ur, the Carroe of the Romans. Rezeph is farther west in Syria. Eden is an ancient village, Adna, north of Baghdad; some think Eden the name of a region of Mesopotamia or its vicinity in which was Paradise, for Paradise was not Eden itself, but a garden in Eden. Telassar is now Tel-afer, west of Mosul; Tel means a hill in Arabic and Assyrian names. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Where is the king of Hamath, and the king of Arphad, and the king of the city of Sepharvaim, Hena, and Ivah? Ivah is in Babylonia; from Ava colonists had been brought to Samaria. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Hezekiah received the letter from the hand of the messengers, and read it, and Hezekiah went up unto the house of the Lord, and spread it before the Lord. Spread means unrolled the scroll of writing; God knows our necessities before we ask Him, but He delights in our unfolding them to Him with filial confidence. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: O Lord of hosts, God of Israel, that dwells between the cherubim, you are the God, even you alone, of all the kingdoms of the earth; you have made heaven and earth. Dwellest refers to the Shekinah, the fiery symbol of God's presence dwelling in the temple with His people, from shachan, to dwell. Cherubim are derived by transposition from a Hebrew root, rachab, to ride, or rather barach, to bless; they were formed out of the same mass of pure gold as the mercy seat itself. The phrase dwellest between the cherubim arose from their position at each end of the mercy seat, while the Shekinah and the awful name Jehovah in written letters were in the intervening space. They are so inseparably associated with the manifestation of God's glory that whether the Lord is at rest or in motion, they are always mentioned with Him. They are first mentioned on the edge of Eden, where the Hebrew for placed is properly to place in a tabernacle, implying a local tabernacle in which the symbols of God's presence were manifested suitably to man's altered circumstances after the fall; it was here Cain and Abel and the patriarchs down to the flood presented their offerings, and it is called the presence of the Lord. When those symbols were removed at the close of that early patriarchal dispensation, small models were made for domestic use, called in Chaldee seraphim or teraphim. In the Mosaic tabernacle and Solomon's temple the cherubim were the same in form as at the outskirts of Eden: compound figures combining the distinguishing properties of several creatures, the ox chief among tame and useful animals, the lion among wild ones, the eagle among birds, and man the head of all, with the original headship of man over the animal kingdom, about to be restored in Jesus Christ, implied in the combination. They are throughout Scripture represented as distinct from God, for they could not be likenesses of Him which He forbade in any shape. They are introduced in the third or gospel dispensation as living creatures, not beasts, not angels, but beings closely connected with the redeemed church, as also in Ezekiel. Thus throughout the three dispensations they seem to be symbols of those who in every age should officially study and proclaim the manifold wisdom of God. Thou alone is literally, Thou art He who alone art God of all the kingdoms, whereas Sennacherib had classed Jehovah with the heathen gods; Hezekiah asserts the nothingness of the latter and the sole lordship of the former. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Incline your ear, O Lord, and hear; open your eyes, O Lord, and see, and hear all the words of Sennacherib, which he has sent to reproach the living God. Ear and eyes are singular and plural: when we wish to hear a thing we lend one ear, when we wish to see a thing we open both eyes. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Of a truth, Lord, the kings of Assyria have laid waste all the nations, and their countries. Have laid waste concedes the truth of the Assyrian's allegation in chapter 36, but adds the reason in the next verse, for they were no gods. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: And have cast their gods into the fire, for they were no gods, but the work of men's hands, wood and stone, therefore they have destroyed them. Cast their gods into the fire reflects the Assyrian policy, in order to alienate conquered peoples from their own countries, both to deport them elsewhere and to destroy the tutelary idols of their nation, the strongest tie binding them to their native land; the Roman policy was just the reverse. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Now therefore, O Lord our God, save us from his hand, that all the kingdoms of the earth may know that you are the Lord, even you only. The strongest argument to plead before God in prayer is the honour of God. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Then Isaiah the son of Amoz sent unto Hezekiah, saying: Thus says the Lord God of Israel: Whereas you have prayed to me against Sennacherib king of Assyria. Whereas thou hast prayed to me means thou hast not relied on thy own strength but on Me; that which thou hast prayed to Me against Sennacherib, I have heard. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: This is the word which the Lord has spoken concerning him: The virgin, the daughter of Zion, has despised you, and laughed you to scorn; the daughter of Jerusalem has shaken her head at you. This is a transition to poetry, in parallelism. Virgin and daughter are honourable terms: virgin implies the city is as yet inviolate, and daughter is an abstract collective feminine personification of the population, the child of the place denoted — Zion and her inhabitants. Shaken her head is in scorn, though with us to shake the head is a sign of denial or displeasure, gestures have different meanings in different countries. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Whom have you reproached and blasphemed? And against whom have you exalted your voice, and lifted up your eyes on high? Even against the Holy One of Israel. Whom is not an idol, but the Holy One of Israel, against whom Sennacherib has exalted his voice and lifted his eyes on high. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: By your servants you have reproached the Lord, and have said: By the multitude of my chariots am I come up to the height of the mountains, to the sides of Lebanon, and I will cut down its tall cedars, and its choice fir trees, and I will enter into the height of its border, and the forest of its Carmel. Said is virtually, hast thou within thyself? Height is imagery from the Assyrian felling of trees in Lebanon, figuratively for carrying a victorious army through regions most difficult of access to the most remote lands. Sides are rather recesses, fir trees are not cypresses as some translate, for pine foliage and cedars are still found on the northwest side of Lebanon. Height of its border in Kings is the lodgings of his borders, perhaps a place of repose or caravansary on the ascent bounding usual attempts to ascend, but here simply its extreme height. Forest of its Carmel is rather its thickest forest, for Carmel expresses thick luxuriance. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: I have digged, and drunk water, and with the sole of my feet have I dried up all the rivers of the besieged places. Digged and drunk water in Kings is strange waters: Sennacherib marched into foreign lands where he had to dig wells for his armies' supply, and even natural destitution of water did not impede his march. Rivers of the besieged places are rather the streams, artificial canals from the Nile, of Egypt; with the sole of my foot expresses that as soon as his vast armies marched into a region the streams were drunk up by them, or rather that the rivers proved no obstruction to the onward march of his armies, as in chapter 19, where the river, brooks of defence, will be dried up. Some translate the Hebrew for besieged places as rocks. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Have you not heard long ago, how I have done it, and of ancient times, that I have formed it? Now have I brought it to pass, that you should be to lay waste defenced cities into ruinous heaps. This is the reply of God to Sennacherib. Long ago joins rather with I have done it: thou boastest that it is all by thy counsel and might, but it is I who, long ago, have ordered it so, and thou wert but the instrument in My hands. This was the reason the inhabitants were of small power before thee, namely that I ordered it so; yet thou art in My hands, and I know thy ways, and I will check thee. Connect also, I from ancient times have arranged, formed, it; however, the English version is supported by chapters 33, 45, and 48. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Therefore their inhabitants were of small power, they were dismayed and confounded; they were as the grass of the field, and as the green herb, as the grass on the housetops, and as corn blasted before it be grown up. Therefore means not because of thy power, but because I made them unable to withstand thee. Grass is that which easily withers, on the housetops having little earth to nourish it fades soonest, and corn blasted before it be grown up is, as Smith translates, the cornfield frail and tender before the corn is grown. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: But I know your abode, and your going out, and your coming in, and your rage against me. Abode is rather sitting down, and the expressions describe a man's whole course of life; there is also a special reference to Sennacherib's first being at home, then going forth against Judah and Egypt, and raging against Jehovah. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Because your rage against me, and your tumult, is come up into my ears, therefore will I put my hook in your nose, and my bridle in your lips, and I will turn you back by the way by which you came. Tumult is insolence, and the hook in the nose means like a wild beast led by a ring through the nose he will be forced back to his own country; in a bas-relief of Khorsabad captives are led before the king by a cord attached to a hook or ring passing through the under lip or upper lip and nose. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: This will be a sign unto you: You will eat this year such as grows of itself, and the second year that which springs of the same, and in the third year sow, and reap, and plant vineyards, and eat the fruit thereof. This is addressed to Hezekiah. The sign is a token which, when fulfilled, would assure him of the truth of the whole prophecy as to the enemy's overthrow. The two years in which they were sustained by the spontaneous growth of the earth were the two in which Judea had already been ravaged by Sennacherib, as in chapter 32; thus translate, Ye did eat the first year such as groweth of itself, and in the second year that which springs of the same, but in this third year sow ye, for in this year the land will be delivered from the foe. The fact that Sennacherib moved his camp away immediately after shows the first two years refer to the past, not the future; others, referring the first two years to the future, get over the difficulty of Sennacherib's speedy departure by supposing that year to have been the sabbatical year and the second the jubilee, but no indication of this appears in the context. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: The remnant that is escaped of the house of Judah will again take root downward, and bear fruit upward. The remnant is Judah remained after the ten tribes were carried away, and also those of Judah who should survive Sennacherib's invasion. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: Therefore thus says the Lord concerning the king of Assyria: He will not come into this city, nor shoot an arrow there, nor come before it with shields, nor cast a bank against it. With shields means he did come near it but was not allowed to conduct a proper siege, and bank is a mound to defend the assailants in attacking the walls. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: By the way that he came, by the same will he return, and will not come into this city, says the Lord. This recalls verses 29 and 37 and chapters 29 and 5 to 8: by the way he came he will return, and he will not come into this city. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: For I will defend this city to save it for my own sake, and for my servant David's sake. I will defend, notwithstanding Hezekiah's measures of defence in 2 Chronicles, for Jehovah was its true defender. Mine own sake is since Jehovah's name was blasphemed by Sennacherib, and David's sake is on account of His promise to David, and to Messiah, the heir of David's throne. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: Then the angel of the Lord went forth, and smote in the camp of the Assyrians a hundred and fourscore and five thousand, and when they arose early in the morning, behold, they were all dead corpses. Some attribute the destruction to the agency of the plague, which may have caused Hezekiah's sickness narrated immediately after, but chapter 33 proves the Jews spoiled the corpses, which they would not have dared to do had there been infection of a plague. The secondary agency seems, from chapters 29 and 30, to have been a storm of hail, thunder, and lightning; the simoon belongs rather to Africa and Arabia than Palestine and ordinarily could not produce such a destructive effect. Some few of the army, as 2 Chronicles seems to imply, survived and accompanied Sennacherib home. Herodotus gives an account confirming Scripture so far as the sudden discomfiture of the Assyrian army is concerned: the Egyptian priests told him Sennacherib was forced to retreat from Pelusium owing to a multitude of field mice, sent by one of their gods, having gnawed the Assyrians' bow-strings and shield-straps, comparing with the language of verse 33, he shall not shoot an arrow there nor come before it with shields, which the Egyptians corrupted into their version of the story. Sennacherib was at the time with part of his army, not at Jerusalem, but on the Egyptian frontier southwest of Palestine; the sudden destruction of the host near Jerusalem, a considerable part of his whole army, as well as the advance of the Ethiopian Tirhakah, induced him to retreat, which the Egyptians accounted for in a way honouring their own gods. The mouse was the Egyptian emblem of destruction, and the Greek Apollo was called Sminthian, from a Cretan word for mouse, as tutelary god of agriculture, represented with one foot upon a mouse, since field mice hurt corn. The Assyrian inscriptions suppress their own defeat but nowhere boast of having taken Jerusalem, and the only reason to be given for Sennacherib not having, amidst his many subsequent expeditions recorded in the monuments, returned to Judah is the terrible calamity he sustained there, which convinced him Hezekiah was under divine protection. Rawlinson says that in Sennacherib's account of his wars with Hezekiah, inscribed in the hall of the palace of Koyunjik, built by him, wherein even the Jewish physiognomy of the captives is portrayed, there occurs a remarkable passage: after mentioning his taking two hundred thousand captive Jews, he adds, Then I prayed unto God, the only instance of an inscription wherein the name of God occurs without a heathen adjunct. The forty-sixth Psalm probably commemorates Judah's deliverance; it occurred in one night, according to Kings, with which Isaiah's words, when they arose early in the morning, are in undesigned coincidence. They and they mean the Jews and the Assyrians. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

37 COMMENTARY: So Sennacherib king of Assyria departed, and went and returned, and dwelt at Nineveh. Dwelt at Nineveh means for about twenty years after his disaster, according to the inscriptions; the word dwelt is consistent with any indefinite length of time. Nineveh, so called from Ninus, that is, Nimrod, its founder, whose name means exceedingly impious rebel; he subverted the existing patriarchal order of society by setting up a system of chieftainship founded on conquest, the hunting field was his training school for war, he was of the race of Ham and transgressed the limits marked by God, encroaching on Shem's portion, he abandoned Babel for a time after the miraculous confusion of tongues and went and founded Nineveh, and he was after death worshipped as Orion, the constellation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

38 COMMENTARY: It came to pass, as he was worshipping in the house of Nisroch his god, that Adrammelech and Sharezer his sons smote him with the sword, and they escaped into the land of Armenia, and Esarhaddon his son reigned in his stead. Nisroch in Semitic, Nisr, means eagle, and the termination och means great; the eagle-headed human figure in Assyrian sculptures is no doubt Nisroch, the same as Asshur, the chief Assyrian god, with the corresponding goddess Asheera or Astarte, meaning a grove or sacred tree, often found as the symbol of the heavenly hosts in the sculptures, as Asshur the Eponymus hero of Assyria answered to the sun or Baal, Belus, the title of office, Lord. This explains image of the grove in Kings. The eagle was worshipped by the ancient Persians and Arabs. Esar-haddon in Ezra is mentioned as having brought colonists into Samaria, and is thought to have been the king who carried Manasseh captive to Babylon; he built the palace on the mound Nebbiyunus and that called the southwest palace of Nimroud, the latter destroyed by fire, but his name and wars are recorded on the great bulls taken from the building, and he obtained his building materials from the northwest palaces of the ancient dynasty ending in Pul. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

No note for verse(s) 5, 15, 32 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
