Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 40

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Comfort, comfort my people, says your God. This chapter begins the second part of the prophecies of Isaiah: the former were local and temporary in their reference, these belong to the distant future and are world-wide in their interest, with the deliverance from Babylon under Cyrus, foretold by prophetic suggestion, carrying him on to the greater deliverance under Messiah, the Saviour of Jews and Gentiles in the present eclectic church, and the restorer of Israel and Head of the world-wide kingdom, literal and spiritual, ultimately. As Assyria was the hostile world power in the former part, referring to Isaiah's own time, so Babylon is so in the latter part, referring to a period long subsequent, with the connecting link furnished at the close of the former part in chapter 39 verse 6. The latter part was written in Isaiah's old age, as appears from the greater mellowness of style and tone pervading it, less fiery and more tender and gentle than the former part. Comfort ye, comfort ye is twice repeated to give double assurance; having announced the coming captivity of the Jews in Babylon, God now desires His servants, the prophets, to comfort them. The scene is laid in Babylon, the time near the close of the captivity, and the ground of comfort is the speedy ending of the captivity, the Lord Himself being their leader. My people and your God are correlatives: it is God's covenant relation with His people, and His word of promise to their forefathers, which is the ground of His interposition in their behalf, after having for a time chastised them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Speak comfortably to Jerusalem, and cry unto her, that her warfare is accomplished, that her iniquity is pardoned, for she has received of the Lord's hand double for all her sins. Comfortably is literally to the heart, not merely to the intellect. Jerusalem, though then in ruins, is regarded by God as about to be rebuilt; her people are chiefly meant, but the city is personified. Cry means publicly and emphatically as a herald cries aloud. Warfare is or the appointed time of her misery; the ulterior and Messianic reference probably is the definite time when the legal economy of burdensome rites is at an end. Pardoned means in Hebrew that her iniquity is so expiated that God now delights in restoring her. Double for all her sins can only in a very restricted sense hold good of Judah's restoration after the first captivity, for how can it be said her warfare was accomplished when as yet the galling yoke of Antiochus and also of Rome was before them? The double for her sins must refer to the twofold captivity, the Assyrian and the Roman; at the coming close of this latter dispersion, and then only, can her iniquity be said to be pardoned, or fully expiated. It does not mean double as much as she deserved, but ample punishment in her twofold captivity. Messiah is the antitypical Israel, and He indeed has received of sufferings amply more than enough to expiate for our sins. Otherwise, cry unto her that she shall receive blessings of the Lord's hand double to the punishment of all her sins; the English version is simpler. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: The voice of him that cries in the wilderness: Prepare the way of the Lord, make straight in the desert a highway for our God. Crieth in the wilderness is so connected by the Septuagint and Matthew; the Hebrew accents, however, connect them thus: In the wilderness prepare ye, and the parallelism also requires this, Prepare ye in the wilderness, answering to make straight in the desert. Matthew was entitled, as under inspiration, to vary the connection, so as to bring out another sense included in the Holy Spirit's intention; in Matthew, John the Baptist, preaching in the wilderness, answers thus to the voice of one crying in the wilderness. The clause, in the wilderness, alludes to Israel's passage through it from Egypt to Canaan, Jehovah being their leader; so it shall be at the coming restoration of Israel, of which the restoration from Babylon was but a type, not the full realisation, for their way from it was not through the wilderness. Where John preached, namely in the wilderness, the type of this earth, a moral wilderness, there were the hearers ordered to prepare the way of the Lord, and there was to be the coming of the Lord. John, though immediately followed by the suffering Messiah, is rather the herald of the coming reigning Messiah, as Malachi proves; Matthew implies John is not exclusively meant, and that though in one sense Elias has come, in another he is yet to come. John was the figurative Elias, coming in the spirit and power of Elias; John the Baptist denies he was the actual Elias, which accords with this view. Malachi cannot have received its exhaustive fulfilment in John; the Jews always understood it of the literal Elijah. As there is another consummating advent of Messiah Himself, so perhaps there is to be of his forerunner Elias, who also was present at the transfiguration. The Lord in Hebrew is Jehovah, and as this is applied to Jesus, He must be Jehovah. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Every valley will be exalted, and every mountain and hill will be made low, and the crooked will be made straight, and the rough places plain. Eastern monarchs send heralds before them in a journey to clear away obstacles, make causeways over valleys, and level hills; so John's duty was to bring back the people to obedience to the law and to remove all self-confidence, pride in national privileges, hypocrisy, and irreligion, so that they should be ready for His coming. Crooked means declivities. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: The glory of the Lord will be revealed, and all flesh will see it together, for the mouth of the Lord has spoken it. See it: the Septuagint for it has the salvation of God, so Luke, that is, Messiah, but the Evangelist probably took these words from chapter 52; for means rather, All flesh shall see that the mouth of Jehovah hath spoken it. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: The voice said: Cry. And he said: What will I cry? All flesh is grass, and all the goodliness thereof is as the flower of the field. The voice is the same divine herald as in verse 3. He is one of those ministers or prophets whose duty it was, by direction of the voice, to comfort the Lord's afflicted people with the promises of brighter days. All flesh is grass means the connection is, All human things, however goodly, are transitory, and God's promises alone steadfast; this contrast was already suggested in verse 5, All flesh and the mouth of the Lord. Peter applies this passage distinctly to the gospel word of Messiah. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: The grass withers, the flower fades, because the spirit of the Lord blows upon it; surely the people is grass. Spirit of the Lord is rather wind of Jehovah, the withering east wind of those countries sent by Jehovah. The people is rather this people, which may refer to the Babylonians, but better, mankind in general, as in chapter 42, so verse 6, all flesh, this whole race, that is, man. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: O Zion, that brings good tidings, get up into the high mountain; O Jerusalem, that brings good tidings, lift up your voice with strength; lift it up, be not afraid; say unto the cities of Judah: Behold your God! Rather, O thou that bringest good things to Zion, thou that bringest good tidings to Jerusalem; thou is thus the collective personification of the messengers who announce God's gracious purpose to Zion, as chapter 52 confirms. If the English version be retained, the sense will be the glad message was first to be proclaimed to Jerusalem, and then from it as the centre to all Judea, Samaria, and the uttermost parts of the earth. Mountain means it was customary for those about to promulgate any great thing to ascend a hill from which they could be seen and heard by all. Be not afraid means to announce to the exiles that their coming return home is attended with danger in the midst of the Babylonians; the gospel minister must open his mouth boldly. Behold is especially at His second coming. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Behold, the Lord God will come with strong hand, and his arm will rule for him; behold, his reward is with him, and his work before him. With strong hand is or against the strong, rather as a strong one, or against the strong one, namely Satan. Arm means power. For him means He needs not to seek help for Himself from any external source, but by His own inherent power He gains rule for Himself. Work is or recompense for his work, rather recompense which He gives for work. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: He will feed his flock like a shepherd; he will gather the lambs with his arm, and carry them in his bosom, and will gently lead those that are with young. Feed includes all a shepherd's care, tend. Carry is applicable to Messiah's restoration of Israel, as sheep scattered in all lands and unable to move of themselves to their own land; as Israel was carried from the womb, that is, in its earliest days, so it shall be in old age, that is, its latter days. Gently lead is as a thoughtful shepherd does the ewes giving suck. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Who has measured the waters in the hollow of his hand, and meted out heaven with the span, and comprehended the dust of the earth in a measure, and weighed the mountains in scales, and the hills in a balance? Lest the Jews should suppose that He who was just before described as a shepherd is a mere man, He is now described as God. Who means who else but God could do so? Therefore, though the redemption and restoration of His people, foretold here, was a work beyond man's power, they should not doubt its fulfilment since all things are possible to Him who can accurately regulate the proportion of the waters as if He had measured them with His hand. But some translate, Who can measure, etc., that is, How immeasurable are the works of God? The former is a better explanation. Span is the space from the end of the thumb to the end of the middle finger extended; God measures the vast heavens as one would measure a small object with his span. Dust of the earth means all the earth is to Him but as a few grains of dust contained in a small measure, literally the third part of a larger measure. Hills in a balance means adjusted in their right proportions and places, as exactly as if He had weighed them out. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Who has directed the Spirit of the Lord, or being his counsellor has taught him? This is quoted in Romans and 1 Corinthians. The Hebrew here for directed is the same as in verse 12 for meted out; thus the sense is, Jehovah measures out heaven with His span, but who can measure Him? That is, who can search out His Spirit, mind, wherewith He searches out and accurately adjusts all things? The Hebrew is rightly taken in the same sense as in verse 12, weigh, ponder; direct, as in the English version, answers, however, better to taught in the parallel clause. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: With whom took he counsel, and who instructed him, and taught him in the path of judgment, and taught him knowledge, and showed to him the way of understanding? Path of judgment means His wisdom, whereby He so beautifully adjusts the places and proportions of all created things. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Behold, the nations are as a drop of a bucket, and are counted as the small dust of the balance; behold, he takes up the isles as a very little thing. Of is rather, hanging from a bucket. He taketh up as a very little thing is rather, are as a mere grain of dust which is taken up, namely by the wind, literally one taketh up, impersonally. Isles is rather lands in general, answering to the nations in the parallel clause, perhaps lands like Mesopotamia enclosed by rivers; however, the English version, isles, answers well to mountains in verse 12, both alike being lifted up by the power of God, in fact isles are mountains upheaved from the bed of the sea by volcanic agency, only that he seems here to have passed from unintelligent creatures in verse 12 to intelligent, as nations and lands, that is, their inhabitants. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Lebanon is not sufficient to burn, nor the beasts thereof sufficient for a burnt offering. All Lebanon's forest would not supply fuel enough to burn sacrifices worthy of the glory of God, and the beasts are those which abounded in Lebanon. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: All nations before him are as nothing, and they are counted to him less than nothing, and vanity. This recalls Psalm 62 and Daniel 4; less than nothing is, as in chapter 41, of nothing, partitively, or expressive of the nature of a thing, a mere nothing; vanity is emptiness. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: To whom then will you liken God? Or what likeness will you compare unto him? Which of the heathen idols, then, is to be compared to this Almighty God? This passage, if not written so late as the idolatrous times of Manasseh, has at least a prospective warning reference to them and subsequent reigns; the result of the chastisement of Jewish idolatry in the Babylonish captivity was that thenceforth after the restoration the Jews never fell into it. Perhaps these prophecies here may have tended to that result. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: The workman melts a graven image, and the goldsmith spreads it over with gold, and casts silver chains. Graven is rather an image in general, for it is incongruous to say melteth, that is, casts out of metal, a graven image, that is, one of carved wood, so Jeremiah, molten image. Spreadeth it over is as in chapter 30. Chains are an ornament lavishly worn by rich Orientals, and so transferred to their idols; Egyptian relics show idols were suspended in houses by chains. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: He that is so impoverished that he has no oblation chooses a tree that will not rot; he seeks unto him a cunning workman to prepare a graven image, that will not be moved. Impoverished is literally sunk in circumstances. No oblation means he who cannot afford to overlay his idol with gold and silver, as in verse 19. Tree that will not rot is the cedar, cypress, oak, or ash, as in chapter 44. Graven is of wood, not a molten one of metal. Not be moved means that shall be durable. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Have you not known? Have you not heard? Has it not been told you from the beginning? Have you not understood from the foundations of the earth? Ye means who worship idols; the question emphatically implies, they had known. From the beginning recalls chapters 41 and 48: God is the beginning. The tradition handed down from the very first, of the creation of all things by God at the beginning, ought to convince you of His omnipotence and of the folly of idolatry. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: It is he that sits upon the circle of the earth, and the inhabitants thereof are as grasshoppers; that stretches out the heavens as a curtain, and spreads them out as a tent to dwell in. It is he is rather, connected with the last verse, Have ye not known? — have ye not understood Him that sitteth? Circle is applicable to the globular form of the earth, above which, and the vault of sky around it, He sits; for upon translate above. As grasshoppers means or locusts in His sight, as He looks down from on high. Curtain refers to the awning which the Orientals draw over the open court in the centre of their houses as a shelter in rain or hot weather. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: That brings the princes to nothing; he makes the judges of the earth as vanity. This recalls Psalm 107 and Daniel 2. Judges means rulers, for these exercised judicial authority; the Hebrew, shophtee, answers to the Carthaginian chief magistrates, suffetes. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Yes, they will not be planted, yes, they will not be sown, yes, their stock will not take root in the earth, and he will also blow upon them, and they will wither, and the whirlwind will take them away as stubble. They are the princes and judges of verse 23 who oppose God's purposes and God's people, often compared to tall trees. Not sown means the seed, that is, race, shall become extinct. Stock means not even shall any shoots spring up from the stump when the tree has been cut down: no descendants whatever. And also is so in the Septuagint, but some translate, They are hardly, literally not yet, planted when He, God, blows upon them. Blow is the image from the hot east wind, simoon, that withers vegetation. Whirlwind as stubble recalls Psalm 83, where like a wheel refers to the rotatory action of the whirlwind on the stubble. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: To whom then will you liken me, or will I be equal? says the Holy One. Compare verse 18: to whom will you liken God, or what likeness will you compare unto Him? — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Lift up your eyes on high, and behold who has created these things, that brings out their host by number; he calls them all by names by the greatness of his might, for that he is strong in power, not one fails. Host is an image from a general reviewing his army: He is Lord of Sabaoth, the heavenly hosts. Calleth by names means numerous as the stars are, God knows each in all its distinguishing characteristics, a sense which name often bears in Scripture, so in Genesis Adam, as God's vicegerent, called the beasts by name, that is, characterised them by their several qualities, which indeed He has imparted. By the greatness faileth is rather, by reason of abundance of their inner essential force and firmness of strength, not one of them is driven astray, referring to the sufficiency of the physical forces with which He has endowed the heavenly bodies, to prevent all disorder in their motions. In the English version the sense is, He has endowed them with their peculiar attributes, names, by the greatness of His might, and the power of His strength, the better rendering, instead of, for that He is strong. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Why say you, O Jacob, and speak, O Israel: My way is hid from the Lord, and my judgment is passed over from my God? Since these things are so, thou hast no reason to think that thine interest, way, that is, condition, is disregarded by God. Judgment is passed over from means rather, My cause is neglected by my God; He passes by my case in my bondage and distress without noticing it. My God means who especially might be expected to care for me. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Have you not known? Have you not heard, that the everlasting God, the Lord, the Creator of the ends of the earth, faints not, neither is weary? There is no searching of his understanding. Known is by thine own observation and reading of Scripture. Heard is from tradition of the fathers. Everlasting, etc., means these attributes of Jehovah ought to inspire His afflicted people with confidence. No searching of his understanding means therefore thy cause cannot, as thou sayest, escape His notice; though much in His ways is unsearchable, He cannot err. He is never faint or weary with having the countless wants of His people ever before Him to attend to. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: He gives power to the faint, and to them that have no might he increases strength. Not only does He not faint in verse 28, but He gives power to them who do faint. No might increaseth strength is a seeming paradox: they have no might in themselves, but in Him they have strength, and He increases that strength. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: Even the youths will faint and be weary, and the young men will utterly fall. Young men are literally those selected, men picked out on account of their youthful vigour for an enterprise, yet even they faint and fall. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: But they that wait upon the Lord will renew their strength; they will mount up with wings as eagles; they will run, and not be weary; and they will walk, and not faint. Mount up is, rather, They shall put forth fresh feathers as eagles are said to renovate themselves; the parallel clause, renew their strength, confirms this. The eagle was thought to moult and renew his feathers, and with them his strength, in old age, so the Septuagint, Vulgate, and Psalm 103. However, the English version is favoured by the descending climax, mount up, run, walk; in every attitude the praying, waiting child of God is strong in the Lord. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
