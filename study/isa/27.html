Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 27

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: In that day the Lord with his sore and great and strong sword will punish leviathan the piercing serpent, even leviathan that crooked serpent, and he will slay the dragon that is in the sea. This continues chapters 24–26: when Israel is delivered and the ungodly nations punished, God will punish also the great enemy of the church. Sore is rather hard, well-tempered; leviathan is literally in Arabic the twisted animal, applicable to every great tenant of the waters, sea-serpents and crocodiles, and potentates hostile to Israel are similarly described in Ezekiel and Daniel, with Satan antitypically and ultimately intended. Piercing is rigid or flying, long and extended like the crocodile which cannot readily bend its body, crooked is winding, the dragon is the crocodile, and the sea is the Euphrates or its expansion near Babylon. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: In that day sing unto her: A vineyard of red wine. In the day when leviathan is destroyed, the vineyard, the church of God purged of its blemishes, will be lovely in God's eyes — Lowth reads pleasant, lovely, for red wine — and the song is a responsive song concerning her, the Jewish state. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: I the Lord do keep it; I will water it every moment, lest any hurt it, I will keep it night and day. Lest any hurt it means lest any attack it, or lest anything be wanting in her, and the Lord's constant watering and keeping night and day guard the vineyard against every assault. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Fury is not in me: who would set the briers and thorns against me in battle? I would go through them, I would burn them together. God no longer entertains anger toward his vine, and he wishes, as it were, that he had the briers and thorns, the wicked foe, before him, to go against them and burn them together. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Or let him take hold of my strength, that he may make peace with me, and he shall make peace with me. Or — else — is the only alternative if Israel's enemies wish to escape being burnt together: let him take hold of God's strength, rather the refuge God affords, as fugitives laid hold of the horns of the altar as an asylum, for Jesus is God's strength and refuge which sinners must take hold of to have peace with God. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: He will cause them that come of Jacob to take root: Israel will blossom and bud, and fill the face of the world with fruit. Here the Lord's song as to his vineyard ends, and the prophet confirms its sentiment under the same vine image: Israel, rooted, blossoming, and budding, will fill the world with fruit, as Paul says Israel's fullness will be the riches of the world. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Has he smitten him, as he smote those that smote him? Or is he slain according to the slaughter of them that are slain by him? Has God punished his people as severely as he has punished those enemies whom he employed to chastise Israel? No, far from it: Israel, after trials, he will restore, while Israel's enemies he will utterly destroy at last, for Israel is not slain according to the slaughter wherewith the enemy is slain. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: In measure, when it shoots forth, you will debate with it: he stays his rough wind in the day of the east wind. In measure means not beyond measure, in moderation, and when it shoots forth passes from the vine image to the thing itself: when sending Israel away to exile — God only putting the adulteress away when he might justly have put her to death — he punished her, and he stays, or removes by, his rough wind in the day of the east wind, especially violent in the East. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: By this therefore will the iniquity of Jacob be purged, and this is all the fruit to take away his sin: when he makes all the stones of the altar as chalkstones that are beaten in sunder, the groves and images will not stand up. By this means — the exile of verse 8 — Jacob's iniquity will be expiated, and the whole benefit designed by the chastisement is the removal of his sin, namely idolatry, when Jehovah, at Jerusalem's destruction by Nebuchadnezzar his instrument, makes the altar stones like beaten chalkstones, so that groves and images rise no more; the Jews ever since have abhorred idolatry. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Yet the defenced city will be desolate, and the habitation forsaken, and left like a wilderness: there the calf will feed, and there it will lie down, and consume its branches. The city is Jerusalem, whose altars and images were beaten asunder in verse 9, and it will become a vast wild pasture where the calf feeds and lies down and consumes the vine's branches, resuming the vineyard image. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: When its boughs are withered, they will be broken off, and the women will come and set them on fire, for it is a people of no understanding; therefore he that made them will not have mercy on them, and he that formed them will show them no favour. The withered boughs are broken off as the Jews are called broken off in Romans, and women, whose office it was to collect fuel and kindle cooking fires, will set them on fire; the people have no understanding as to God's ways, and therefore their Maker will show them no mercy. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: In that day the Lord will beat off from the channel of the river unto the stream of Egypt, and you will be gathered one by one, O you children of Israel. This is the restoration of the Jews from dispersion, described as fruits shaken from trees and collected: beaten off as fruit beaten from a tree with a stick and then gathered, from the Euphrates to the stream of Egypt on Palestine's confines, now Wady-el-Arish, so that Jehovah's vineyard Israel extends from Nile to Euphrates as purposed, and they are gathered most carefully, not merely as a nation but as individuals, one by one. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: In that day the great trumpet will be blown, and those who were ready to perish in the land of Assyria, and the outcasts in the land of Egypt, will come and worship the Lord in the holy mount at Jerusalem. The great trumpet is an image from the trumpets blown on the first day of the seventh month to summon a holy convocation, and antitypically the gospel trumpet which the Jews will hearken to in the last days; Assyria, whither the ten tribes were carried, is used to point ultimately to the future full restoration of the ten, never yet accomplished, and Egypt is whither many fled at the Babylonian captivity. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
