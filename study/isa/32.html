Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 32

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Behold, a king will reign in righteousness, and princes will rule in judgment. This chapter treats Messiah's kingdom, desolations succeeded by lasting peace when the Spirit is poured out: first the times of purity and happiness after the defeat of Jehovah's people's enemies, then the period of wrath before that happy state, then the assurance of the church's final prosperity repeated. The king is not Hezekiah, already on the throne, whereas a future time is contemplated; if he is meant at all it is only as a type of Messiah the King, to whom alone the language fully applies, for the kingdom will be transferred from world kings who exercised power against God to the rightful King of kings. The princes are subordinate, all in authority under Christ in the coming kingdom on earth, such as the apostles. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: A man will be as a hiding place from the wind, and a covert from the tempest, as rivers of water in a dry place, and as the shadow of a great rock in a weary land. A man is rather the man Christ, for as Son of man He is to reign as as Son of man He suffered; He will be a hiding place and covert, and rivers as refreshing as water and cool shade are to the heated traveller, and the shadow of a great rock in a weary land. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: The eyes of them that see will not be dim, and the ears of them that hear will hearken. Them that see are the seers or prophets, and them that hear are the people under instruction, whose eyes and ears will no longer be dim or dull. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The heart also of the rash will understand knowledge, and the tongue of the stammerers will be ready to speak plainly. Rash is rather the hasty, in contrast to not making haste in chapter 28, the reckless who will not take time to weigh religious truth aright, or else the well-instructed; and the stammerers are those who speak confusedly on divine things, or rather those drunken scorners who in stammering style imitated Isaiah's warnings to mock them, who will then speak uprightly, agreeably to the divine law, not merely distinctly. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: The vile person will be no more called liberal, nor the churl said to be bountiful. Vile is rather fool, that is, ungodly, liberal is rather noble-minded, churl is rather fraudulent, and bountiful is religiously: the atheistic churl who envies the believer his hope full of immortality will no longer be held as a patriot struggling for mankind's emancipation from superstition. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: For the vile person will speak villany, and his heart will work iniquity, to practise hypocrisy, and to utter error against the Lord, to make empty the soul of the hungry, and he will cause the drink of the thirsty to fail. Villainy is rather the irreligious fool's folly, and he will speak — rather present — folly and work iniquity, for so far is the fool from deserving the epithet noble-minded; hypocrisy is rather profligacy, error is impiety and perverse arguments, and the hungry are spiritually hungry, whom the fool would leave empty. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: The instruments also of the churl are evil: he devises wicked devices to destroy the poor with lying words, even when the needy speaks right. The churl is the fraudulent, and this verse refers to the last clause of verse 5, as verse 6 referred to its first clause; to speak right is to plead a just cause, and spiritually the poor man's cause is the divine doctrine, his rule of faith and practice, which the churl would destroy with lying words. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: But the liberal devises liberal things, and by liberal things he will stand. Liberal is rather noble-minded, and he will stand, approved under the government of the righteous King, by his noble-minded devices. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Rise up, you women that are at ease; hear my voice, you careless daughters; give ear unto my speech. This begins an address to the women of Jerusalem who troubled themselves little about the political signs of the times but lived a life of self-indulgence, as in chapter 3; the failure of food through the enemy's devastations is foretold, being what would most affect them as mothers of families heretofore accustomed to every luxury. Women and daughters may also be understood as the cities and villages of Judea. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Many days and years you will be troubled, you careless women, for the vintage will fail, and the gathering will not come. Many days and years is rather in little more than a year, literally days upon a year, as in chapter 29; the vintage will fail through the Assyrian invader's arrival, and since the wheat harvest is omitted, Isaiah must look for the invasion in the summer or autumn of 714 B.C., when wheat would already be secured and the later gathering and vintage still in danger. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Tremble, you women that are at ease; be troubled, you careless ones; strip yourselves, and make yourselves bare, and gird sackcloth upon your loins. Strip yourselves of gay clothing, and gird sackcloth in mourning, for the ease and carelessness are over. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: They will lament for the teats, for the pleasant fields, for the fruitful vine. Teats is rather, they will smite on their breasts in lamentation for the pleasant fields, for teats in the English version stands for fertile lands which, like breasts, nourish life; the transition from "ye" to "they" is frequent in prophecy. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Upon the land of my people will come up thorns and briers, yes, upon all the houses of joy in the joyous city. This recalls chapters 5 and 7: the houses of joy are pleasure-houses outside Jerusalem, not Jerusalem itself, but other cities destroyed by Sennacherib in his march, though in full accomplishment the prophecy refers to the utter desolation of Judea and its capital by Rome, and subsequently before the second coming of the King, with the joyous city as Jerusalem. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Because the palaces will be forsaken, the multitude of the city left, and the forts and towers will be for dens for ever, a joy of wild asses, and a pasture of flocks. The palaces are most applicable to Jerusalem, the multitude left is the noisy din of the city, the city with its noisy multitude lying forsaken, and the forts are rather Ophel, the mound on the east declivity of Zion surrounded with its own wall and furnished with watchtowers; for ever is limited by until in verse 15, for a long time. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Until the spirit be poured upon us from on high, and the wilderness be a fruitful field, and the fruitful field be counted for a forest. This can only partially apply to the spiritual revival in Hezekiah's time; its full accomplishment belongs to the Christian dispensation, first at Pentecost, perfectly in coming times, when the Spirit will be poured on Israel and through it on the Gentiles. The wilderness becoming a fruitful field, and the fruitful field counted for a forest, mean that Judea, so long waste, will be populous and fruitful while the enemies' land becomes desolate — or the field now fruitful will seem but a barren forest compared with what it will then be, for the barren will become fruitful by regeneration and the regenerate will bear fruit so abundantly their former life seems a wilderness without fruit. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Then judgment will dwell in the wilderness, and righteousness remain in the fruitful field. Judgment is justice, the wilderness is then reclaimed, and the fruitful field then becomes more fruitful, so wilderness and fruitful field together include the whole land of Judea. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: The work of righteousness will be peace, and the effect of righteousness quietness and assurance for ever. The work is the effect of righteousness, and the peace is internal and external, with quietness and assurance forever. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: My people will dwell in a peaceable habitation, and in sure dwellings, and in quiet resting places. The dwellings are sure and quiet, free from fear of invasion, under the righteous King's peace. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: When it will hail, coming down on the forest, and the city will be low in a low place. Literally, it will hail with the coming down of the forest, and in lowness the city, Nineveh, will be brought low and humbled; the hail is Jehovah's wrathful visitation, and the forest is the Assyrian host, dense as trees of a forest. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Blessed are you that sow beside all waters, that send forth thither the feet of the ox and the ass. While the enemy is brought low, the Jews will cultivate their land in undisturbed prosperity: all waters are well-watered places, and beside ought rather to be upon, as in casting seed upon the waters when the river overflows its banks, where the seed sinks into mud and springs up in a rich harvest after many days. Before sowing they send oxen and asses into the water to tread the ground, and the sense is, blessed are you that sow the gospel seed without distinction of race in teachers or taught, though the ox and ass here are probably sent forth separately, as in chapter 30. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
