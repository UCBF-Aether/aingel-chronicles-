Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 8

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The Lord told Isaiah to take a great roll — a tablet of wood, metal, or stone — and write on it with a man's pen, in ordinary characters the humblest can read, concerning Maher-shalal-hash-baz. The large, plain letters ensured that after the event all might see it had been predicted, and the name means that the Assyrians hasten to the spoil and speed to the prey. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Isaiah was to take faithful witnesses, Uriah the priest and Zechariah son of Jeberechiah, to attest the tablet. Uriah was an accomplice of Ahaz in idolatry and therefore a witness not likely to help the prophet fabricate a prophecy after the event; the witnesses existed so that when the event came they could testify the tablet was inscribed beforehand. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Isaiah went to the prophetess — perhaps the same as the virgin of chapter 7, now married as his second wife — and she conceived and bore a son, whose name the Lord gave as Maher-shalal-hash-baz. Immanuel remains distinct from this child even in the temporary sense, with about three years in all from the chapter 7 prophecy to this child crying "Father," agreeing with chapters 7:15–16. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: For before the child knows how to cry, My father and my mother, the riches of Damascus and the spoil of Samaria will be taken away before the king of Assyria. JFB notes this is within a year, a shorter interval than in chapter 7, since the accomplishment is now nearer. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: The Lord spoke again to Isaiah, saying: Forasmuch as this people refuses the waters of Shiloah that go softly, and rejoice in Rezin and Remaliah's son. Shiloah, meaning "sent," its water sent through an aqueduct, figures the mild though now weak sway of the house of David, and in the highest sense the benignant sway of Jehovah in the theocracy administered through David. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: This people — both Israel, which preferred alliance with Rezin to the kings of Judah, and Judah, a party of which favoured the son of Tabeal and sought an Assyrian alliance — has refused Shiloah and rejoiced in Rezin and Remaliah's son. Both nations alike rejected the divine Shiloah, and God pointedly calls them "this people," not "my people" as when he expresses favour. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Therefore the Lord will bring upon them the waters of the river, strong and many, even the king of Assyria and all his glory, and it will come up over all its channels and go over all its banks. The Assyrian flood, first to overflow Syria and Samaria, will rise high enough to reach rebel Judah also, like the Euphrates swollen in spring by melting Armenian snow, with Eastern kings travelling in gorgeous retinue. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: The flood will pass through Judah, reaching even to the neck, and the stretching out of its wings will fill the breadth of thy land, O Immanuel. When waters reach the neck a man is near drowning, yet the head is not overflowed: Jerusalem on her hills is the head, and though danger will come near at Sennacherib's invasion, she will be spared — a pledge that Immanuel's land, however overwhelmed, will be saved at last. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Associate yourselves, O peoples — rather, raise tumults, do your worst — and you will be thrown into consternation; gird yourselves, and you will be thrown into consternation. The name Immanuel suggests the ultimate safety of Immanuel's land from its present invaders and even from the Assyrians, since the succession of David's house cannot be set aside, for Messiah is to be born in it. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Take counsel together and it will come to nothing; speak the word and it will not stand, for God is with us. The word of command for the assault of Jerusalem fails because "Immanuel" implies God is with his people. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: For the Lord spoke to Isaiah with a strong hand — the felt impulse of inspiration grasping him — instructing him not to walk in the way of this people. Their way was distrust of Jehovah and the panic that led them and Ahaz to seek Assyrian aid. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Do not say, A confederacy, to all those to whom this people says, A confederacy; neither fear their fear, nor be afraid. The confederacy is rather a conspiracy — the unnatural combination of Israel with Syrian foreigners against Judah and the theocracy — and the fear meant is the hostile conspiracy as an object of fear, which believers must not share or spread. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Sanctify the Lord of hosts himself, and let him be your fear and your dread. To sanctify him is to honour his holy name by regarding him as the only hope of safety, fearing lest you provoke his wrath by fear of man and distrust of him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: He will be for a sanctuary — an inviolable asylum like the altar of the temple — to those who fear and trust in him, but for a stone of stumbling and a rock of offence to both houses of Israel, and for a gin and a snare to the inhabitants of Jerusalem. The very stone Immanuel, a sanctuary on belief, becomes a fatal stumbling-block through unbelief, as Jesus himself applies it. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Many among them will stumble and fall, and be broken, snared, and taken, images drawn from the means used in taking wild animals. The gin of verse 14 closes on those who reject the sanctuary. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Bind up the testimony and seal the law among my disciples. What Isaiah had briefly inscribed as Maher-shalal-hash-baz he later wrote out more fully and now seals, implying it relates to distant events and is a sealed testimony understood only in part among God's disciples, those who sanctify the Lord by obedient trust, until later revelations clear it up. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Whatever the rest of the nation does, Isaiah says, I will wait upon the Lord, who hides his face from the house of Jacob, and I will look for him. Though God seems to withdraw his countenance from Judah, the prophet will wait and trust in him though he cannot see him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Behold, I and the children whom the Lord has given me are for signs and wonders in Israel from the Lord of hosts, who dwells in Mount Zion. Isaiah's own name means salvation of Jehovah, and his children's names were signs of coming and final deliverance; Hebrews quotes this to prove the manhood of Messiah, whose believers are his children. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: When people say, Seek unto those with familiar spirits and wizards that peep and mutter, the answer is: should not a people seek unto their God, and for the living seek to the dead? Necromancers and spirit-charmers, chirping faintly like young birds as if from the grave, followed in the wake of idolatry under Ahaz, though the law forbids consulting them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: To the law and to the testimony: if they speak not according to this word, it is because there is no light in them. The revelation of God by his prophet is the standard to which necromancy's advisers must be referred, and those in the dark night of trial without a dawn of hope will themselves say: seek not to necromancy but to the law. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: They will pass through the land hardly bestead and hungry, and when they are hungry they will fret themselves and curse their king and their God, and look upward. This describes the despair of those who sought necromancy instead of God: given over to despair, they curse Jehovah their King and God, foreshadowing those under the beast's kingdom who curse God under his plagues. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: They will look unto the earth, and behold trouble and darkness, dimness of anguish, and they will be driven to darkness. Whether they look up to heaven or down to the land of Judea, nothing but despair presents itself, and they are driven onward as by a sweeping storm into thick darkness. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
