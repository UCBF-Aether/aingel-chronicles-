Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 6

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: In the year that King Uzziah died, Isaiah saw the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up, with his train filling the temple. This is not the first beginning of Isaiah's prophecies but his inauguration to a higher degree of the prophetic office, and he saw the Son, not the divine essence itself, with a flowing robe and train as the badge of an Eastern monarch. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Above the throne stood the seraphim in attendance, each with six wings: with two they covered their faces as unworthy to look on the holy God, with two they covered their feet in reverence, and with two they flew, ready for instant flight in God's service. "Seraph" means to burn, implying burning zeal, dazzling brightness, and serpent-like rapidity in God's service. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: One seraph cried to another, Holy, holy, holy is the Lord of hosts, and the whole earth is full of his glory — the fulness of the whole earth is his glory. The thrice-repeated Holy implies the Trinity in unity, and God's holiness is the keynote of Isaiah's whole prophecy. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: At the voice of him that cried, the foundations of the thresholds moved and the house was filled with smoke. The posts are the foundations of the thresholds, and the smoke is the Shekinah cloud that filled the temple. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Then Isaiah said, Woe is me, for I am undone, because I am a man of unclean lips dwelling among a people of unclean lips, yet my eyes have seen the King, the Lord of hosts. The lips are named because the seraphim's praises are sung by lips, and because Isaiah is about to be commissioned to speak as God's prophet. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: One of the seraphim flew to Isaiah with a live coal — literally a hot stone such as was used to roast the meat of the sacrifices — taken with tongs from the altar of burnt offering. The fire on that altar was first kindled by God and kept continually burning, and fire is a symbol of purification, taking dross out of metals. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: The seraph laid the coal on Isaiah's mouth and said, this has touched your lips, your iniquity is taken away and your sin purged — literally covered, expiated. The purging is not by any physical effect of fire but in relation to the altar sacrifices, of which Messiah, who here commissions Isaiah, was to be the antitype in his death. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Then Isaiah heard the voice of the Lord saying, Whom shall I send, and who will go for us? The change from "I" to "us" indicates the Trinity, and the question implies that few would be willing to bear the self-denial of delivering so unwelcome a message. Isaiah answered with prompt zeal, now specially qualified: Here am I, send me. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: God's message is: Go and tell this people, Hear indeed but understand not, and see indeed but perceive not. Though they hear the warnings again and again, they are doomed by their perverse will not to understand, while light enough is given to guide those sincerely seeking to do God's will. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Isaiah is told to make the heart of this people fat, their ears heavy, and their eyes shut, lest they see, hear, understand, convert, and be healed of their spiritual malady, sin. This hardening is the fruit not of truth in itself but of the corrupt state of their hearts, to which God judicially gives them over; corruption flows from the heart into ears and eyes, while healing reaches the heart through eyes and ears. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Isaiah asks, Lord, how long? — how long will this hardening to destruction continue? The answer runs until the cities are wasted without inhabitant, the houses without man, and the land utterly desolate, fulfilled primarily at the Babylonian captivity and more fully at the dispersion under Titus. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: The Lord will have removed men far away, and there will be a great forsaking — abandonment of dwellings by their inhabitants — in the midst of the land. The verse points to the deportations that emptied Judah. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Yet if a tenth remains in the land, it will again be given over to be consumed, as a terebinth or oak cast down still keeps its stock or trunk. That stock is the holy seed, the elect remnant alone destined to salvation, with seeds of vitality waiting for the returning spring of God's favour; God shows unchangeable severity toward sin but covenant faithfulness in preserving a remnant. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
