Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 47

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Come down, and sit in the dust, O virgin daughter of Babylon, sit on the ground; there is no throne, O daughter of the Chaldeans, for you will no more be called tender and delicate. This chapter represents the destruction of Babylon under the image of a royal virgin brought down in a moment from her magnificent throne to the extreme of degradation. In the dust recalls chapters 3 and Job and Lamentations. Virgin means that is, heretofore uncaptured, as in Herodotus. Daughter of Babylon means Babylon and its inhabitants, as in chapters 1 and 37. No throne means the seat of empire was transferred to Shushan; Alexander intended to have made Babylon his seat of empire, but Providence defeated his design. He soon died, and Seleucia, being built near, robbed it of its inhabitants, and even of its name, which was applied to Seleucia. Delicate alludes to the effeminate debauchery and prostitution of all classes at banquets and religious rites. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Take the millstones, and grind meal; uncover your locks, make bare the leg, uncover the thigh, pass over the rivers. Millstones are like the querns or hand-mills, found in this country, before the invention of water mills and windmills: a convex stone, made by the hand to turn in a concave stone, fitted to receive it, the corn being ground between them, the office of a female slave in the East, most degrading. Uncover thy locks is rather, take off thy veil, perhaps the removal of the plaited hair worn round the women's temples is included; it, too, is a covering. To remove it and the veil is the badge of the lowest female degradation; in the East the head is the seat of female modesty, the face of a woman is seldom, the whole head almost never, seen bare. Make bare the leg is rather lift up, literally uncover, as in lifting up the train the leg is uncovered, thy flowing train. In Mesopotamia, women of low rank, as occasion requires, wade across the rivers with stript legs, or else entirely put off their garments and swim across. Exchange thy rich, loose, queenly robe, for the most abject condition, that of one going to and fro through rivers as a slave, to draw water, etc. Uncover the thigh means gather up the robe, so as to wade across. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Your nakedness will be uncovered, yes, your shame will be seen; I will take vengeance, and I will not meet you as a man. As a man is rather, I will not meet a man, that is, suffer man to intercede with me, give man an audience. Or, I will not make peace with any man, before all are destroyed. Literally, strike a league with, a phrase arising from the custom of striking hands together in making a compact, or else from striking the victims sacrificed in making treaties. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: As for our redeemer, the Lord of hosts is his name, the Holy One of Israel. As for is rather supply, Thus saith our Redeemer. Some suppose this verse to be the exclamation of a chorus breaking in with praises, Our Redeemer! Jehovah of hosts, etc., as in Jeremiah. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Sit silent, and get into darkness, O daughter of the Chaldeans, for you will no more be called: The lady of kingdoms. Sit is the posture of mourning. Darkness means mourning and misery. Lady of kingdoms means mistress of the world, as in chapter 13. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: I was angry with my people, I have polluted my inheritance, and given them into your hand; you did show them no mercy; upon the ancient you have very heavily laid your yoke. This is the reason for God's vengeance on Babylon: in executing God's will against His people, she had done so with wanton cruelty. Polluted my inheritance recalls chapter 43 verse 28. The ancient means even old age was disregarded by the Chaldeans, who treated all alike with cruelty; or, the ancient means Israel, worn out with calamities in the latter period of its history, as in chapter 46, as its earlier stage of history is called its youth. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: You said: I will be a lady for ever, so that you did not lay these things to your heart, neither did you remember the latter end of it. So that means through thy vain expectation of being a queen for ever, thou didst advance to such a pitch of insolence as not to believe these things, namely, as to thy overthrow in verses 1 to 5, possible. End of it means namely, of thy insolence, implied in her words, I shall be a lady for ever. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Therefore hear now this, you that are given to pleasures, that dwell carelessly, that say in your heart: I am, and none else beside me; I will not sit as a widow, neither will I know the loss of children. Given to pleasures recalls verse 1; in no city were there so many incentives to licentiousness. I am, none beside me recalls verse 10; language of arrogance in man's mouth, fitting for God alone, as in chapter 45. Widow and loss of children means a state, represented as a female, when it has fallen is called a widow, because its king is no more, and childless, because it has no inhabitants, they having been carried off as captives. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: But these two things will come to you in a moment in one day, the loss of children, and widowhood; they will come upon you in their perfection for the multitude of your sorceries, and for the great abundance of your enchantments. In a moment means it should not decay slowly, but be suddenly and unexpectedly destroyed; in a single night it was taken by Cyrus. The prophecy was again literally fulfilled when Babylon revolted against Darius, and, in order to hold out to the last, each man chose one woman of his family, and strangled the rest, to save provisions; Darius impaled three thousand of the revolters. In perfection means that is, in full measure. For, for is rather, notwithstanding the, notwithstanding, in spite of, so for in Numbers. Babylon was famous for expiations or sacrifices, and other incantations, whereby they tried to avert evil and obtain good. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: For you have trusted in your wickedness; you have said: None sees me. Your wisdom and your knowledge, it has perverted you, and you have said in your heart: I am, and none else beside me. Wickedness means as in chapter 13, the cruelty with which Babylon treated its subject states. None seeth me recalls Psalms 10 and 94: There is none to exact punishment from me. Sinners are not safe, though seeming secret. Thy wisdom means astrological and political, as to Egypt in chapter 19. Perverted means turns thee aside from the right and safe path. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Therefore evil will come upon you; you will not know from whence it rises; and mischief will fall upon you; you will not be able to put it off; and desolation will come upon you suddenly, which you will not know. From whence it riseth is Hebrew, the dawn thereof, that is, its first rising; evil shall come on thee without the least previous intimation. But dawn is not applied to evil, but to prosperity shining out after misery, as in chapter 21; translate, Thou shalt not see any dawn, of alleviation. Put off is rather, as in the margin, remove by expiation; it shall be never ending. Not know means unawares, which thou dost not apprehend, proving the fallacy of thy divinations and astrology. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Stand now with your enchantments, and with the multitude of your sorceries, wherein you have laboured from your youth; if so be you will be able to profit, if so be you may prevail. Stand means forth: a scornful challenge to Babylon's magicians to show whether they can defend their city. Laboured means the devil's service is a laborious yet fruitless one, as in chapter 55. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: You are wearied in the multitude of your counsels. Let now the astrologers, the stargazers, the monthly prognosticators, stand up, and save you from these things that will come upon you. Wearied recalls chapters 57 and Ezekiel. Astrologers are literally, those who form combinations of the heavens, who watch conjunctions and oppositions of the stars, casters of the configurations of the sky; some explain it, the dividers of the heavens. In casting a nativity they observed four signs: the horoscope, or sign which arose at the time one was born, the mid-heaven, the sign opposite the horoscope towards the west, and the hypogee. Monthly prognosticators are those who at each new moon profess to tell thereby what is about to happen. Join, not as in the English version, save from those things, etc., but, They that at new moons make known from, by means of, them the things that shall come upon thee. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Behold, they will be as stubble; the fire will burn them; they will not deliver themselves from the power of the flame; there will not be a coal to warm at, nor fire to sit before it. This recalls chapters 29 and 30. Not a coal means like stubble, they shall burn to a dead ash, without leaving a live coal or cinder, as in chapter 30, so utterly shall they be destroyed. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Thus will they be unto you with whom you have laboured, even your merchants, from your youth; they will wander every one to his quarter; none will save you. Thus, etc., means such shall be the fate of those astrologers who cost thee such an amount of trouble and money. Thy merchants, from thy youth means that is, with whom thou hast trafficked from thy earliest history, the foreigners sojourning in Babylon for the sake of commerce, as in chapters 13 and Jeremiah and Nahum; rather, the astrologers, with whom Babylon had so many dealings in verses 12 to 14. To his quarter is literally, straight before him, as in Ezekiel; the foreigners, whether soothsayers or merchants, shall flee home out of Babylon, as in Jeremiah. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
