Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 5

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Isaiah sings a song concerning his Beloved's vineyard, planted on a very fruitful hill, isolated, secure, and sunny in aspect. The Beloved is Jehovah as God of the Jews, and the vineyard is the covenant people separated from the nations for his glory. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: The owner digged and trenched the ground, gathered out the stones, planted the choicest vine — the sorek, whose grapes had scarcely perceptible seeds — and built a watchtower and a wine-press hewn from rock. Yet it brought forth wild grapes, fetid fruit expressing offensive putrefaction, answering to the corrupt state of the Jews. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: God now appeals to the inhabitants of Jerusalem and men of Judah to judge between him and his vineyard, as Jesus later makes his hearers pass sentence on themselves. God condemns sinners out of their own mouth. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: What more could have been done for the vineyard that was not done in it, God asks, amazed as it were at the unnatural fruit of so well-cared a vineyard. He has done all that could be done for sinners consistently with his justice and goodness. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Now God will show what he will do to his vineyard: he will take away its hedge and break down its wall, so that it will be trodden down by wild beasts, his enemies. The vineyard had both hedge and wall, proof of the owner's care, and both protections will be removed. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: He will lay it waste, unpruned and undigged, overgrown with briers and thorns, and command the clouds to rain no rain upon it. The withheld rain is antitypically the heaven-sent teachings of the prophets, withheld in gospel times. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Here Isaiah applies the parable: the vineyard of the Lord of hosts is the house of Israel, and the men of Judah his pleasant plant, the plant of his delight chosen as carefully as the sorek. He looked for judgment but behold oppression — in Hebrew, mishpat but mispat, bloodshed — and for righteousness but behold a cry, the cry of anarchy, covetousness, and dissipation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: The first woe falls on those who join house to house and lay field to field until there is no place left for anyone else. The jubilee restoration of possessions was intended as a guard against exactly this avarice. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: The Lord of hosts has revealed in Isaiah's ears that many houses, great and fair, will be desolate without inhabitant. The desolation comes on account of the national sins, turning seized estates into emptiness. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Ten acres of vineyard will yield only one bath of wine, and a homer of seed only an ephah — eight bushels of seed yielding barely three pecks of produce. The land seized by greed will answer with meagre harvests. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: The second woe falls on those who rise early to follow strong drink and continue until night till wine inflames them. Banquets for revelry began earlier than usual, and drinking all day was regarded as especially shameful. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: At their feasts are harp, viol of twelve strings, tabret, pipe, and wine, but they do not regard the work of the Lord or consider the operation of his hands. Feasting commonly produces this blindness, and God's work here is his punishing of the guilty. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Therefore the people will go into captivity because they have no knowledge, their honourable men famished and their multitude dried up with thirst. The prophet sees the future as present, and the luxurious feasters will hunger and thirst in deportation and exile. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Therefore hell, the grave and unseen world of spirits, enlarges herself and opens her mouth without measure to receive the countless hosts that will perish. Their glory, multitude, pomp, and even the drunken reveller of Jerusalem will descend into it. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: The mean man will be brought down, the mighty man humbled, and the eyes of the lofty humbled, as in chapter 2. All ranks alike — honourable and multitude — share the humiliation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: But the Lord of hosts will be exalted in judgment, and God who is holy will be sanctified in righteousness. God is exalted in men's view by his manifestation of justice in punishing the guilty, and regarded as holy by reason of his righteous dealings. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Then lambs will feed after their manner and strangers will eat the waste places of the fat ones. The desolate land will become a vast pasturage for nomad shepherds, and figuratively the pious lambs will enjoy what the impious rich have lost, with Gentile strangers brought into the privileges the Jews fell from. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: The third woe falls on those who draw iniquity with cords of vanity and sin as with a cart rope, straining every nerve in obstinate perseverance in sin. An evil inclination begins like a fine hair-string and finishes like a cart-rope, as one sin draws on another until they bind themselves with great guilt. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: In defiance they say: let God make speed and hasten his work that they may see it, and let the counsel of the Holy One draw near that they may know it. Their language is defiance of God's threatened purpose to punish, like Lamech's boast of impunity. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: The fourth woe falls on those who call evil good and good evil, putting darkness for light and bitter for sweet. Their moral perception is darkened and undiscriminating, though sin is bitter even when it seems sweet for a time, while religion is sweet. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: The fifth woe falls on those wise in their own eyes and prudent in their own sight, who thought they knew better than the prophet and therefore rejected his warnings. Self-wise contempt for prophecy is itself the sin condemned. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: The sixth woe falls on corrupt judges, mighty to drink wine — a boast still not uncommon — and men of strength to mingle strong drink, spiced to make it intoxicating rather than mixed with water. Their drinking is joined to bribery, the means of their self-indulgence. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: These judges justify the wicked for reward and take away the righteousness of the righteous, setting aside the just claims of those with a righteous cause. The two verses are closely joined, describing one corrupt bench. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Therefore, as fire devours stubble and flame consumes chaff, their root will be rottenness and their blossom dust. The hidden source and the outward manifestation of prosperity perish together, because they have cast away the law of the Lord in its spirit while retaining its letter. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Therefore the anger of the Lord is kindled against his people, his hand is stretched out, the hills tremble, and their carcasses are torn like dung in the streets. The trembling hills probably recall the earthquake in Uzziah's days, and yet for all this his anger is not turned away but his hand is stretched out still — a refrain repeated through chapters 9 and 10. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: He will lift up an ensign to the nations from far and hiss to them from the end of the earth, and they will come with speed swiftly. As bees are drawn from their hives by hissing, God will collect the nations round Judea to execute his judgments, chiefly the distant subject races of the Assyrian army. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: None of the invaders will be weary or stumble, none will slumber or sleep, neither will the girdle of their loins be loosed nor the latchet of their shoes broken. Ever ready for march or battle, they need no rest, and not even a sandal strap gives way to impede them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Their arrows are sharp and all their bows bent, their horses' hoofs counted like flint — the ancients did not shoe horses — and their wheels like a whirlwind. The Assyrian army abounded in cavalry and chariots. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Their roaring will be like a lion, like young lions roaring and laying hold of prey and carrying it away safe, with none to deliver it. The roaring is their battle cry. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: In that day they will roar against Judah like the roaring of the sea, and if one looks to the land, behold darkness and sorrow, and the light darkened in its heavens. Distress and hope may alternately succeed in an unsettled state, but here darkness and distress alone are visible, like mariners in a storm who cannot see the land. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
