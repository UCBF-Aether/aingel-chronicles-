Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 13

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The burden of Babylon, which Isaiah son of Amoz saw, opens the prophecies concerning foreign nations in chapters 13–23. "Burden" means a weighty or mournful prophecy, and these foreign prophecies are given for the covenant people's sake, to preserve them from despair or reliance on human confederacies and to strengthen their faith in God. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Lift up a banner upon the high mountain — rather, a bare, bald mountain without trees, from which the banner can be seen afar — exalt the voice, shake the hand, that they may go into the gates of the nobles. The banner rallies the peoples against Babylon, the hand beckons the Medes to march, and the nobles are Babylon's tyrant rulers. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: God has commanded his sanctified ones and called his mighty ones for his anger, even those who rejoice in his highness. The Median and Persian soldiers are solemnly set apart by God for Babylon's destruction, not inwardly sanctified but designated to fulfil his holy purpose, and they rejoice in their own highness while unconsciously glorifying his. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The noise of a multitude in the mountains, like as of a great people, a tumultuous noise of the kingdoms of nations gathered together: the Lord of hosts musters the host of the battle. The Babylonians hear an unwonted din like a host but can only perceive a tumultuous noise, for Medes, Persians, and Armenians compose Cyrus's army. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: They come from a far country, from the end of heaven, even the Lord and the weapons of his indignation, to destroy the whole land. The far country is Media and Persia stretching to the far north and east, and the end of heaven is the far east; the armies are the weapons of God's indignation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Howl, for the day of the Lord is at hand; it will come as a destruction from the Almighty. The day of the Lord is the day of his vengeance on Babylon, a type of the future day of wrath, and the destruction is literally a devastating tempest from Shaddai, not from mere man, and therefore irresistible. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Therefore all hands will be faint, and every man's heart will melt. Babylon was taken by surprise on the night of Belshazzar's impious feast, hence the sudden fainting and melting of hearts. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: They will be afraid, pangs and sorrows will take hold of them, they will be in pain as a woman that travails, amazed one at another, their faces as flames. The Hebrew for pangs can also mean messengers, so the heralds bringing word of the unexpected invasion are themselves terrified, and the faces bear the livid hue of flame with anguish and indignation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Behold, the day of the Lord comes, cruel both with wrath and fierce anger, to lay the land desolate and destroy its sinners out of it. Cruel here means unsparingly just, opposed to mercy, answering Babylon's own cruelty now visited on itself; the language applies primarily and partially to Babylon, but fully and exhaustively to the judgments to come on the whole earth, since Babylon's arrogancy, cruelty, false worship, and persecution of God's people mark the Antichristian world. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: For the stars of heaven and the constellations will not give their light, the sun will be darkened in his going forth, and the moon will not cause her light to shine. The darkened heavens figure anarchy, distress, and revolutions of kingdoms, with a possible literal fulfilment finally, shadowed under this imagery; the constellations are in Hebrew the impious one, Orion, the deified Nimrod, Babylon's founder, chained to the sky. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: God will punish the world for their evil and the wicked for their iniquity, lay low the arrogancy of the proud, and bring down the haughtiness of the terrible. The world here means the impious of the world, arrogancy is Babylon's besetting sin, and the terrible are tyrants whose haughtiness will fall. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: God will make a man more precious than fine gold, even a man than the golden wedge of Ophir. He will so cut off Babylon's defenders that a single man will be as rare and precious as the finest gold. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Therefore God will shake the heavens, and the earth will remove out of her place, in the wrath of the Lord of hosts and in the day of his fierce anger. The shaking is an image for mighty revolutions, as in the prophets' visions of the final judgment. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: It will be as the chased roe and as a sheep that no man takes up: every man will turn to his own people and flee everyone into his own land. Babylon's mingled peoples of foreign lands will flee out of her like a timid gazelle and like defenceless sheep without a shepherd. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Everyone found in the city will be thrust through, and everyone joined to them — intercepted, or withdrawn to hide in the houses — will fall by the sword. None found in Babylon will escape the invaders' sword. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Their children will be dashed to pieces before their eyes, their houses spoiled, and their wives ravished. JFB links this verse to Psalm 137, where the same vengeance is called down on Babylon, completing the picture of the city's unsparing destruction. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Behold, God will stir up the Medes against them, who will not regard silver, and as for gold, they will not delight in it. At that time the Medes were subject to Assyria, but they would rise and destroy Babylon, and in vain would anyone try to buy his life from them for ransom, since disregard of riches was characteristic of the Medes. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Their bows will dash the young men to pieces, and they will have no pity on the fruit of the womb; their eye will not spare children. The Medes and Persians were particularly skilled with the bow, and their conquest will be pitiless toward every age. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Babylon, the glory of kingdoms, the beauty of the Chaldees' excellency, will be as when God overthrew Sodom and Gomorrah. Babylon was the glory and boast of the Chaldees, yet it will fall as utterly as Sodom, taken by Cyrus when he diverted the Euphrates into a new channel and entered the city by the old riverbed in the night. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: It will never be inhabited, neither dwelt in from generation to generation, neither will the Arabian pitch tent there, neither will shepherds make their fold there. The prophecy was literally fulfilled: not even a temporary resting-place remains, for Arabs will not pass the night there through dread of evil spirits, and the once-fertile region has become stagnant marsh where no grass grows on the wastes of brick and cement. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: But wild beasts of the desert will lie there, and their houses will be full of doleful creatures, and owls will dwell there, and satyrs will dance there. The wild beasts are animals of arid wastes, the doleful creatures howling beasts, the owls rather ostriches delighting in solitary deserts, and the satyrs goat-like creatures believed to haunt the ruins. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: The wild beasts of the islands will cry in their desolate houses, and dragons in their pleasant palaces, and her time is near to come, and her days will not be prolonged. Jackals, called sons of howling, will answer each other at night in the desolate houses, and serpents hiss in the pleasant palaces, though the time was 174 years distant — near to Isaiah, who speaks to the Jews as if already captives in Babylon. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
