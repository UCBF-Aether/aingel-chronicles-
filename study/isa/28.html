Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 28

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Woe to the crown of pride, to the drunkards of Ephraim, whose glorious beauty is a fading flower, which are on the head of the fat valleys of them that are overcome with wine! Chapters 28–33 form almost one continuous prophecy on Ephraim's destruction, Judah's impiety and folly, the Egyptian league, Assyrian straits, and Jehovah's deliverance, with chapter 28 referring to just before Hezekiah's sixth year. The proud crown of the drunkards is Samaria, capital of Ephraim, drunkards literally and metaphorically, like drunkards rushing to their own destruction, and its glorious beauty is a fading flower, for at feasts brows were wreathed with flowers, and Samaria on a hill surrounded by rich valleys as a garland is fading because Ephraim is close to ruin, fulfilled in 721 B.C. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Behold, the Lord has a mighty and strong one, which as a tempest of hail and a destroying storm, as a flood of mighty waters overflowing, will cast down to the earth with the hand. The strong one is the Assyrian, and he will cast down Ephraim and Samaria its crown with violence. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: The crown of pride, the drunkards of Ephraim, will be trodden under feet. The crown of the drunkards will be trodden underfoot by the Assyrian flood. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The glorious beauty, which is on the head of the fat valley, will be a fading flower, and as the hasty fruit before the summer, which when he that looks upon it sees it, while it is yet in his hand he eats it up. The fading flower, their glorious beauty on the head of the fertile valley, will be as the early fig, ripening in June as a delicacy while common figs ripen in August; the Assyrian Shalmaneser will seize it immediately, without delay, describing his eagerness not merely to conquer but utterly to destroy Samaria, whereas other conquered cities were often spared. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: In that day the Lord of hosts will be for a crown of glory, and for a diadem of beauty, unto the residue of his people. The prophet now turns to Judah: a gracious promise to the remnant, the residue, with a warning lest through like sins Judah share Samaria's fate. The crown here stands against Ephraim's fading crown, and the residue is primarily Judah in Hezekiah's prosperous reign, antitypically the elect of God; as God is their crown and diadem, so they are called his, a beautiful reciprocity. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: And for a spirit of judgment to him that sits in judgment, and for strength to them that turn the battle to the gate. Jehovah will inspire Judah's magistrates with justice and her soldiers with strength of spirit, so that her defenders not only repel the foe but drive him to the gates of his own cities. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: But they also have erred through wine, and through strong drink are out of the way; the priest and the prophet have erred through strong drink, they are swallowed up of wine, they are out of the way through strong drink, they err in vision, they stumble in judgment. Though Judah is to survive Ephraim's fall, yet they also have perpetrated like sins, which must be chastised: erred and out of the way mean stagger and reel, repeated to express the frequency of the vice, and if ministers of religion sin so grievously, how much more the other rulers. The prophet errs even in the sacred function of declaring God's revealed will, and the priest stumbles in judgment, though priests administered the law and were forbidden wine before entering the tabernacle. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Whom will he teach knowledge, and whom will he make to understand doctrine? Them that are weaned from the milk, and drawn from the breasts. Here the drunkards scoffingly comment on Isaiah's warnings: whom does Isaiah presume to teach, and whom will he make to understand instruction — does he take us to be just weaned children? — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: For precept must be upon precept, precept upon precept, line upon line, line upon line, here a little, and there a little. The scoffers mimic Isaiah's constant repetition as if to little children: precept upon precept, line — a rule or law — upon line, with the Hebrew repetition of sounds expressing their scorn of his stammering speech. God's mode of teaching offends sinners' pride by its simplicity, but as stammerers by drunkenness and children in knowledge of God, they needed to be spoken to in children's language and with stammering lips, a just and merciful retribution. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: For with stammering lips and another tongue he will speak to this people. This is Isaiah's reply to the scoffers: their drunken questions will be answered by severe lessons from God conveyed through the Assyrians and Babylonians, whose dialect, though Semitic like Hebrew, sounded to the Jews like the speech of stammerers; to those who will not understand, God will speak still more unintelligibly. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: To whom he said: This is the rest wherewith you may cause the weary to rest, and this is the refreshing, yet they would not hear. Rather, He, Jehovah, who has said to them: this is the rest — primarily rest from national warlike preparations, the Jews being weary through preceding calamities such as the Syro-Israelite invasion, but spiritually the rest found in obeying those very precepts they jeered at — yet they would not hear. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: But the word of the Lord was unto them precept upon precept, precept upon precept, line upon line, line upon line, here a little, and there a little, that they might go, and fall backward, and be broken, and snared, and taken. Therefore, because they would not hear, God's word became to them precept upon precept as a stumbling-block, by defect of will, and they go and fall — an image apt for drunkards who in trying to go forward fall backward — and are broken, snared, and taken. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Therefore hear the word of the Lord, you scornful men, that rule this people which is in Jerusalem. The scornful men are the scoffers of verses 9–10, now addressed as rulers of Jerusalem's people. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Because you have said: We have made a covenant with death, and with hell are we at agreement; when the overflowing scourge passes through, it will not come unto us, for we have made lies our refuge, and under falsehood have we hid ourselves. They said this virtually in conduct if not in words: there may be a tacit reference to confidence in their covenant with the Assyrians in Hezekiah's early prosperous reign, as if it ensured Judah from evil whatever befell Ephraim, but the full meaning applies to all lulled in false security spiritually, for only the godly are in covenant with death. The overflowing scourge is the hostile Assyrian armies like an overwhelming flood passing through Judea on their way to Egypt, and lies are what Isaiah calls their sentiments by their true name, though they did not use the words. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Therefore thus says the Lord God: Behold, I lay in Zion for a foundation a stone, a tried stone, a precious corner stone, a sure foundation; he that believes will not make haste. Literally, Behold Me as Him who has laid the stone in His divine counsel, for none save God could lay it; the stone is Jesus Christ, for Hezekiah or the temple do not realise the full significance, but only in type point to Him, in whom the prophecy receives its exhaustive accomplishment. The stone is tried by the devil, by men, and even by God, a stone of tested solidity to bear the vast superstructure of man's redemption, and its tested righteousness gives its peculiar merit to His vicarious sacrifice. Though a scourge visits Judea, God's gracious purpose as to the elect remnant and His kingdom centred on Zion will not fail, because it rests on Messiah. Precious means literally of preciousness — He is preciousness — the corner-stone is laid where two walls meet and connects them, often costly, and he that believes will not make haste in fleeing alarm, or be ashamed, for he who rests on Him will not have the shame of disappointment nor flee in sudden panic. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Judgment also will I lay to the line, and righteousness to the plummet, and the hail will sweep away the refuge of lies, and the waters will overflow the hiding place. The line is the measuring-line of the plummet, and God will appoint judgment for the rule and justice for the plummet: as the corner-stone stands most perpendicular and exactly proportioned, so Jehovah, while holding out grace to believers in the Foundation-stone, will judge the scoffers according to the exact justice of the law, and hail, divine judgment, will sweep away their refuge of lies while waters overflow their hiding place. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Your covenant with death will be disannulled, and your agreement with hell will not stand; when the overflowing scourge passes through, then you will be trodden down by it. The covenant will be disannulled, obliterated as letters traced on a waxen tablet are obliterated by passing the stylus over it, and trodden down passes from the scourge metaphor to the thing meant, the army which treads down its enemies. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: From the time that it goes forth it will take you, for morning by morning it will pass over, by day and by night, and it will be a vexation only to understand the report. As often as it comes over, it will overtake them, like a flood returning from time to time, for frequent hostile invasions will assail Judah after the ten tribes' deportation, and it will be a terror even to hear the mere report of it — or hard treatment only will make them understand instruction, since they scorned the prophet's simple way and must be taught by severe adversity. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: For the bed is shorter than that a man can stretch himself on it, and the covering narrower than that he can wrap himself in it. This is proverbial: they will find all their sources of confidence fail them, and all will be hopeless perplexity in their affairs. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: For the Lord will rise up as in Mount Perazim, and be wroth as in the valley of Gibeon, that he may do his work, his strange work, and bring to pass his act, his strange act. At Perazim in the valley of Rephaim, Jehovah by David broke forth as waters do and made a breach among the Philistines, as Perazim means, expressing sudden and complete overthrow, and at Gibeon he likewise overthrew David's enemies; his work is strange as being against his own people, for judgment is not what God delights in, though necessary, and his work is punishing the guilty. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Now therefore be not mockers, lest your bands be made strong, for I have heard from the Lord God of hosts a consumption, even determined upon the whole earth. The mockers are guilty of the sin of verses 9–10, and their bands are their Assyrian bondage, Judah then being tributary to Assyria, or lest their punishment be made still more severe; the consumption is the determined destruction of chapters 10 and 24. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Give ear, and hear my voice; hearken, and hear my speech. The prophet calls attention to the following illustration from husbandry: as the husbandman does his different kinds of work each in its right time and due proportion, so God adapts His measures to varying exigencies — now mercy, now judgments, now punishing sooner, now later — answering the scoff that His judgments, being put off so long, would never come; His object is not to destroy His people any more than the farmer's object in threshing is to destroy his crop, vindicating God's strange work in punishing His people. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Does the plowman plow all day to sow? Does he open and break the clods of his ground? The plowman is not always ploughing, emphatically not all day: he also sows, and that in accordance with sure rules. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: When he has made plain the face thereof, does he not cast abroad the fitches, and scatter the cummin, and cast in the principal wheat and the appointed barley and the rye in their place? When the surface of the ground is made plain and level by harrowing, he casts abroad the fitches — rather dill or fennel, Nigella with black seed easily beaten out, used as condiment and medicine — and scatters cummin used the same way, and plants the wheat in rows, for wheat was thought to yield the largest crop planted sparingly, and barley in its appointed place, and rye in the field's border. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: For his God does instruct him to discretion, and does teach him. God first taught man the due rules of husbandry, instructing him to discretion. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: For the fitches are not threshed with a threshing instrument, neither is a cart wheel turned about upon the cummin, but the fitches are beaten out with a staff, and the cummin with a rod. The husbandman uses the same discretion in threshing: dill and cummin, leguminous and tender grains, are beaten out not with the heavy corn-drag but with a staff, for heavy instruments would crush and injure the seed; the cart wheel is two iron wheels armed with iron teeth like a saw joined by a wooden axle, and the corn-drag is three or four wooden cylinders armed with iron teeth or flint stones underneath, joined like a sledge, cutting straw for fodder as well as separating corn. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Bread corn is bruised, because he will not ever be threshing it, nor break it with the wheel of his cart, nor bruise it with his horsemen. Bread corn is threshed with the corn-drag, as contrasted with dill and cummin beaten with the staff, or trodden out by cattle hoofs on the threshing-floor; but though threshed with the heavy instrument, yet he will not always be thus threshing it, nor drive over it continually with the wheel, nor bruise it with his horses. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: This also comes forth from the Lord of hosts, who is wonderful in counsel, and excellent in working. The skill wherewith the husbandman duly adjusts his modes of threshing is given by God, as well as the skill wherewith he tills and sows; therefore God must also be able to adapt His modes of treatment to the several moral needs of His creatures. His object in sending tribulation — from the Latin tribulum, a threshing instrument — is to sever the moral chaff from the wheat, not to crush utterly, and His judgments are usually in the line of our offences, so by the nature of the judgments we may usually ascertain the nature of the sin. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
