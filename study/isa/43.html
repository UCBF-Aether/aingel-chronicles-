Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 43

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: But now thus says the Lord that created you, O Jacob, and he that formed you, O Israel: Fear not, for I have redeemed you, I have called you by your name; you are mine. This chapter is a succession of arguments wherein Israel may be assured that, notwithstanding their perversity towards God in chapter 42 verse 25, He will deliver and restore them. But now means notwithstanding God's past just judgments for Israel's sins. Created means not only in the general sense, but specially created as a peculiar people unto Himself, as in verses 7, 15, and 21, and chapters 44. So believers, created in Christ Jesus, a peculiar people. Redeemed is a second argument why they should trust Him besides creation; the Hebrew means to ransom by a price paid in lieu of the captives, as in verse 3. Babylon was to be the ransom in this case, that is, was to be destroyed, in order that they might be delivered; so Christ became a curse, doomed to death, that we might be redeemed. Called by name means not merely called in general, as in chapters 42, 48, and 51, but designated as His own peculiar people. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: When you pass through the waters, I will be with you, and through the rivers, they will not overflow you; when you walk through the fire, you will not be burned, neither will the flame kindle upon you. Not overflow thee is so in passing Jordan, though at its overflow, when its swellings were especially dangerous. Waters and fire are a proverbial phrase for the extremest perils; literally fulfilled at the Red Sea, and in the case of the three youths cast into the fiery furnace for conscience' sake. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: For I am the Lord your God, the Holy One of Israel, your Saviour; I gave Egypt for your ransom, Ethiopia and Seba for you. Egypt for thy ransom means either Egypt or Israel must perish; God chose that Egypt, though so much more mighty, should be destroyed, in order that His people might be delivered; thus Egypt stood, instead of Israel, as a kind of ransom. The Hebrew, kopher, means properly that with which anything is overlaid, as the pitch with which the ark was overlaid, hence that which covers over sins, an atonement. Nebuchadnezzar had subdued Egypt, Ethiopia, Hebrew Cush, and Saba, descended from Cush, probably Meroe of Ethiopia, a great island formed by the Astaboras and the Nile, conquered by Cambyses, successor of Cyrus. Cyrus received these from God with the rest of the Babylonian dominions, in consideration of his being about to deliver Israel. However, the reference may be to the three years' war in which Sargon overcame these countries, and so had his attention diverted from Israel, as in chapter 20. But the reference is probably more general, namely, to all the instances in which Jehovah sacrificed mighty heathen nations, when the safety of Israel required it. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Since you were precious in my sight, you have been honourable, and I have loved you; therefore will I give men for you, and people for your life. Since means all along from the beginning, for there was never a time when Israel was not Jehovah's people; the apodosis should be at, I will give: Since ever thou wast precious in My sight, honourable, and that I loved thee, I will give, etc. Gesenius, as the English version, takes Since to mean, Inasmuch as. If the apodosis be as in the English version, Since thou wast precious will refer to the time when God called His people out of Egypt, manifesting then first the love which He had from everlasting towards them; honourable and loved refer to outward marks of honour and love from God. Men and people mean other nations for thee, as in verse 3. Thy life means thy person. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Fear not, for I am with you; I will bring your seed from the east, and gather you from the west. This recalls Deuteronomy 30. Seed means descendants scattered in all lands; some understand it of the spiritual seed of the church produced by mystical regeneration, for the expression is, bring, not bring back. This sense is perhaps included, but not to the exclusion of the literal Israel's restoration. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: I will say to the north: Give up, and to the south: Keep not back; bring my sons from far, and my daughters from the ends of the earth. Give up means namely, My people. Sons and daughters means the feminine joined to the masculine expresses the complete totality of anything. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Even every one that is called by my name, for I have created him for my glory, I have formed him, yes, I have made him. Called by my name means belong to Israel, whose people, as sons of God, bear the name of their Father. For my glory recalls verses 21 and chapter 29. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Bring forth the blind people that have eyes, and the deaf that have ears. This is a solemn challenge given by God to the nations to argue with Him the question of His superiority to their idols, and His power to deliver Israel, as in chapter 41 verse 1. Blind people are the Gentiles, who also, like Israel in chapter 42 verse 19, are blind spiritually, though having eyes, that is, natural faculties, whereby they might know God; or else, the Jews. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Let all the nations be gathered together, and let the people be assembled; who among them can declare this, and show us former things? Let them bring forth their witnesses, that they may be justified, or let them hear, and say: It is truth. Can declare this means who among the idolatrous soothsayers hath predicted this, that is, as to Cyrus being the deliverer of Israel? Former means predictions, as in chapter 42 verse 9, or things that shall first come to pass, as in chapter 41. Let them bring forth their witnesses means as I do mine in verse 10. Justified means declared veracious in their pretended prophecies. Or means rather, and; let men hear their prediction and say, from the event, It is verified, as in chapter 41 verse 26. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: You are my witnesses, says the Lord, and my servant whom I have chosen, that you may know and believe me, and understand that I am he; before me there was no God formed, neither will there be after me. Ye are the Jews, to whom I have given predictions, verified by the event, and in delivering whom I have so often manifested My power, as in verses 3 and 4 and chapter 44. And my servant means that is, the whole Jewish people, as in chapter 41 verse 8. Believe means trust in. Formed means before I existed none of the false gods were formed; formed applies to the idols, not to God. Revelation uses the same language to prove the Godhead of Jesus, as Isaiah here to prove the Godhead of Jehovah. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: I, even I, am the Lord, and beside me there is no saviour. Lord is Jehovah. Saviour is temporally, from Babylon, and eternally, from sin and hell; the same titles as are applied to God are applied to Jesus. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: I have declared, and have saved, and I have showed, when there was no strange god among you; therefore you are my witnesses, says the Lord, that I am God. Declared means predicted the future, as in chapter 41. Saved means the nation, in past times of danger. Showed means namely, that I was God. When no strange god, etc., means to whom the predictions uttered by Me could be assigned; strange means foreign, introduced from abroad. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Yes, before the day was I am he, and there is none that can deliver out of my hand; I will work, and who will let it? Before means literally, from the time of the first existence of day. Let is Old English for hinder, as in chapter 14; rather, translate, undo it. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Thus says the Lord, your redeemer, the Holy One of Israel: For your sake I have sent to Babylon, and have brought down all their nobles, and the Chaldeans, whose cry is in the ships. Sent means namely, the Medes and Persians. Brought down means made to go down to the sea, as in chapter 42 verse 10, in order to escape the impending destruction of Babylon. Nobles are rather fugitives, namely, the foreigners who sojourned in populous Babylon, distinct from the Chaldeans. Whose cry is in the ships means exulting in their ships with the joyous sailors, cry, boastingly; their joy heretofore in their ships contrasts sadly with their present panic in fleeing to them. Babylon was on the Euphrates, which was joined to the Tigris by a canal, and flowed into the Persian Gulf; thus it was famed for ships and commerce until the Persian monarchs, to prevent revolt or invasion, obstructed navigation by dams across the Tigris and Euphrates. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: I am the Lord, your Holy One, the creator of Israel, your King. Creator of Israel recalls verse 1. Your means proved to be specially yours by delivering you. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Thus says the Lord, which makes a way in the sea, and a path in the mighty waters. This, with verse 17, alludes to the deliverance of Israel and overthrow of Pharaoh in the Red Sea, the standing illustration of God's unchanging character towards His people. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Which brings forth the chariot and horse, the army and the power; they will lie down together, they will not rise; they are extinct, they are quenched as tow. This, with verse 16, alludes to the deliverance of Israel and overthrow of Pharaoh in the Red Sea, the standing illustration of God's unchanging character towards His people. The power means the might of the enemies' host, every mighty warrior. They shall lie down together means as Pharaoh's army sank together in a watery grave. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Remember not the former things, neither consider the things of old. So wonderful shall be God's future interpositions in your behalf, that all past ones shall be forgotten in comparison; plainly the future restoration of Israel is the event ultimately meant. Thus the former things are such events as the destruction of Sennacherib and the return from Babylon. Things of old are events still more ancient, the deliverance from Egypt and at the Red Sea, and entry into Canaan. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Behold, I will do a new thing; now it will spring forth; will you not know it? I will even make a way in the wilderness, and rivers in the desert. New means unprecedented in its wonderful character, as in chapter 42 verse 9. Spring forth means as a germinating herb: a beautiful image of the silent but certain gradual growth of events in God's providence. Way in the wilderness means just as Israel in the wilderness, between the Red Sea and Canaan, was guided, and supplied with water by Jehovah; but the new deliverance shall be attended with manifestations of God's power and love, eclipsing the old, as in chapter 41. I will open a way, not merely in the Red Sea, but in the wilderness of the whole world; and not merely one river shall gush out of the rock, but many, which shall refresh, not the bodies as formerly, but the souls of the thirsty, so that the prophecy shall be fulfilled: With joy shall ye draw water out of the wells of salvation. A way often stands for the true religion. Rivers express the influences of the Holy Spirit. Israel's literal restoration hereafter is included, as appears by comparing chapters 11. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: The beast of the field will honour me, the dragons and the owls, because I give waters in the wilderness, and rivers in the desert, to give drink to my people, my chosen. Beast is an image of idolaters, defiled with blood and pollutions, dwelling like dragons, etc., in the wastes of Gentile ignorance: even they shall be converted. Or else, literally, such copious floods of water shall be given by God in the desert, that the very beasts shall, in poetic language, praise the Lord. Dragons are serpents, or else jackals, as in chapter 13. Owls are rather ostriches. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: This people have I formed for myself; they will show forth my praise. This people means namely, the same as My people, My chosen, as in verses 1 and 7. My praise means on account of the many and great benefits conferred on them, especially their restoration. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: But you have not called upon me, O Jacob, but you have been weary of me, O Israel. But means Israel, however, is not to think that these divine favours are due to their own piety towards God; so the believer. But is rather, for. Weary of me recalls Amos and Malachi, though I have not wearied thee in verse 23, yet thou hast been weary of Me. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: You have not brought me the small cattle of your burnt offerings, neither have you honoured me with your sacrifices. I have not caused you to serve with an offering, nor wearied you with incense. Small cattle are rather the lamb or kid, required by the law to be daily offered to God. Sacrifices means offered any way, whereas the Hebrew for holocaust, or burnt offering, denotes that which ascends as an offering consumed by fire. I have not caused thee to serve means that is, to render the service of a slave. Offering is bloodless. Wearied is antithetical to verse 22, Thou hast been weary of Me. Though God in the law required such offerings, yet not so as to weary the worshipper, or to exact them in cases where, as in the Babylonish captivity, they were physically unable to render them; God did not require them, save in subordination to the higher moral duties. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: You have bought me no sweet cane with money, neither have you filled me with the fat of your sacrifices, but you have made me to serve with your sins, you have wearied me with your iniquities. Bought means for sweet cane, aromatic calamus, was not indigenous to Palestine, but had to be bought from foreign countries; it was used among the Hebrews to make the sacred ointment. It is often offered as a mark of hospitality. Filled means satiated; God deigns to use human language to adapt Himself to human modes of thought. Made me to serve means though I have not caused thee to serve in verse 23; our sin made the Son of God to become a servant. He served to save us from servile bondage. Wearied me means though I have not wearied thee, as in verse 23 and chapter 1. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: I, even I, am he that blots out your transgressions for my own sake, and will not remember your sins. I, even I means the God against whom your sin is committed, and who alone can and will pardon, as in chapter 44. For mine own sake recalls chapter 48. How abominable a thing sin is, since it is against such a God of grace! Blotted out is an image from an account-book, in which, when a debt is paid, the charge is cancelled or blotted out. Not remember sins means, as in Jeremiah, when God forgives, He forgets; that is, treats the sinner as if He had forgotten his sins. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Put me in remembrance; let us plead together; declare you, that you may be justified. Put me in remembrance means remind Me of every plea which thou hast to urge before Me in thy defence; image from a trial, as in chapters 1 and 41. Our strongest plea is to remind God of His own promises, so Jacob did at Mahanaim and Peniel. God, then, instead of pleading against us with His great power, will put His strength in us; we thus become the Lord's remembrancers. Declare God's righteousness vindicated in Jesus Christ that thou mayest be justified. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Your first father has sinned, and your teachers have transgressed against me. First father is collectively for most ancient ancestors, as the parallelism, teachers, proves; or, thy chief religious ministers or priests. Adam, the common father of all nations, can hardly be meant here, as it would have been irrelevant to mention his sin in an address to the Jews specially; Abraham is equally out of place here, as he is everywhere cited as an example of faithfulness, not of sin. However, taking the passage in its ultimate application to the church at large, Adam may be meant. Teachers are literally interpreters between God and man, the priests. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Therefore I have profaned the princes of the sanctuary, and have given Jacob to the curse, and Israel to reproaches. Profaned the princes recalls Psalm 89 and Lamentations: I have esteemed, or treated, them as persons not sacred. I have left them to suffer the same treatment as the common people, stripped of their holy office and in captivity. Princes of the sanctuary are governors of it, directing its holy services, priests. Curse is Hebrew, cherim, a solemn anathema, or excommunication. Reproaches recalls Psalm 123. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
