Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 1

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The heading names Isaiah, whose name means "The Lord shall save," and sets his vision in Judah and Jerusalem under Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah. JFB treats this title as the program of the whole book, with Jerusalem named as the temple city and centre of the theocracy. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Heaven and earth are called as witnesses, as in Moses' song, because prophecy rests on the law as its charter. God's complaint is that children he raised to peculiar privilege have rebelled against him as sons and as subjects. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Even an ox knows its owner and an ass its master's crib, yet Israel does not know or consider the Lord who feeds it. JFB reads the crib as God's word and ordinances, which his people ignore despite the provision made for them. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Sin is described as a load, and the nation as a seed meant to be holy that has become evildoers and corrupters of others. Their offence is sharpened because it is against the Holy One of Israel, and they have gone backward, estranged from him. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: The question is on what part they can still be struck, since the whole body already bears the marks of blows. JFB notes the image covers the universality of their punishment, while sin itself, the disease of head and heart, becomes its own punishment. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: From the lowest to the highest there is no soundness, only wounds that have never been closed, bound up, or softened with ointment. Henry compares the nation to a sick body from sole to head, with no good principle or religion left as the health of the soul. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: The land lies desolate and its cities burned, with strangers devouring it before their eyes while they cannot prevent it. This desolation recalls earlier ravages by Syria and Israel, and matches the fire-judgments Amos describes. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Jerusalem alone is left, like a cottage in a vineyard or a lodge in a cucumber field — a temporary shelter, not a secure home. JFB reads "left" as a remnant preserved from the desolation all around, rather than a city still besieged. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Only the Lord of hosts, God of the angelic and starry hosts, has preserved a very small remnant; without him Jerusalem would have become like Sodom and Gomorrah. Henry stresses that it is of the Lord's mercies alone that they are not consumed. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: The rulers and people are addressed as rulers of Sodom and people of Gomorrah, and told to hear the word and law of the Lord. The spiritual likeness to Sodom marks how far judgment by fire has already pointed to their condition. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: God does not disparage sacrifice in itself, but sacrifice without obedience of heart and life; he is full to satiety of burnt offerings brought while sin continues. JFB sees here a foreshadowing of the one real sacrifice that would supersede all the shadowy ones. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Coming to appear before God in his courts is no service to him when the worshipper comes in hypocrisy. God did require attendance at the temple, but not in this spirit, as if the worshipper were doing God a favour. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Vain oblations, incense, new moons, and sabbaths are an abomination when joined to iniquity; God says he cannot bear the solemn meeting that is yoked to sin. Henry warns that the costliest devotions, without thorough reformation of heart and life, cannot be acceptable. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: God is weary of the appointed feasts — sabbath, passover, pentecost, atonement, and tabernacles — because they are kept while the heart stays corrupt. The feasts were fixed by God himself, yet their observance had become a trouble to him. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: When hands spread in prayer are full of blood, God will hide his eyes and not hear, for all heinous sins and especially persecution of his servants stain the prayer. The prophets' task was to dispel the delusion that outward ritual alone could satisfy God. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: The command to wash and make clean is given so that the sinner, finding he cannot cleanse himself, cries to God to wash him. The cleansing must be before God's eyes, not mere outward reformation before men who cannot see the heart. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: True religion is active: learn to do well, seek justice, relieve the oppressed, and vindicate the fatherless and the widow. JFB notes that magistrates in particular are called to seek justice instead of seeking bribes. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: God deigns to reason the case with his people so that all may see the justice and love of his dealings. Though sins are scarlet, double-dyed and deep-fixed, pardoning mercy can make them white as snow and as wool, restored to original whiteness. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Willingness and obedience are the condition of eating the good of the land, with temporal blessings prominent under the old covenant as suited to the childhood of the church. Repentance is presupposed before the promised whitening of verse 18 can be enjoyed. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Refusal and rebellion bring devouring by the sword, and the sentence stands because the mouth of the Lord has spoken it. Isaiah's prophecies rest on the law, and God does not alter his word. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: The faithful city has become a harlot: righteousness once lodged in her, but now murderers — murderous oppressors — dwell there. Henry notes that neither holy nor royal cities are faithful to their trust if religion does not dwell in them. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Jerusalem's silver has become dross and its wine mixed with water: solid worth is debased and the living word diluted. The princes and people alike are degenerate, shining like silver while worthless within. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: The princes are rebellious companions of thieves, loving gifts and following rewards, judging neither the fatherless nor the widow's cause. JFB observes that a nation's corruption begins with its rulers. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: The Lord, the mighty One of Israel, declares he will ease himself of his adversaries and avenge himself of his enemies. God's language condescends to human conceptions; his long-tried patience finds relief in at last punishing the guilty. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: God will turn his hand upon Zion not in wrath but in grace, purging away dross purely, as alkali purifies silver. The dross is not sins in the abstract but the sinful persons — the degenerate princes — intermingled with the elect remnant. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Judges and counsellors will be restored as at the first, and the city will again be called the city of righteousness, the faithful city. The restoration was partly typified after Babylon, but fully and antitypically under Messiah. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Zion shall be redeemed with judgment and her converts with righteousness, a redemption that is civil and moral in type and spiritual in its fulfilment by the price of Christ's blood. God's judgment and righteousness come first, and then become man's when converted. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: The destruction of transgressors and sinners together will be a breaking into shivers, with those who forsake the Lord consumed. The prophets hasten forward to the final extinction of the ungodly, of which earlier judgments are types. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Those who desired the oaks and chose the gardens of idolatry will be ashamed of them, for groves were dedicated to idols and planted enclosures served idol worship. Henry warns that trusting in idols or in an arm of flesh ends in shame. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: The idolaters will become like an oak whose leaf fades and a garden without water, withering by a law of necessary consequence. People become like the gods they worship and never rise above their level, and their sins become their own scourges. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: The strong man will be as tow and his work as a spark, and both will burn together with none to quench them. The wicked ruler is at once the fuel and the cause of the fire, kindling the first spark himself. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
