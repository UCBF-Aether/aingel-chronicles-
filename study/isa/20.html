Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 20

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: In the year that Tartan came to Ashdod, when Sargon king of Assyria sent him, and he fought against Ashdod and took it. In Sargon's reign, 722–715 B.C., an Assyrian invasion of Egypt took place, and its success is here foretold to warn the party among the Jews who expected aid from Egypt or Ethiopia; Tartan was probably the general later sent by Sennacherib against Hezekiah, and Ashdod, Greek Azotus on the Mediterranean and one of the five Philistine cities, was the key to Egypt in that quarter, so its taking was a necessary preliminary to invading Egypt, while Sargon himself remained with the Phoenician cities or led the main force into Egypt. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: At the same time the Lord spoke by Isaiah son of Amoz, saying: Go and loose the sackcloth from off your loins and put off your shoe from your foot, and he did so, walking naked and barefoot. The sackcloth is the loose outer garment of coarse dark hair-cloth worn by mourners and prophets, fastened at the waist by a girdle, and naked here means uncovered: Isaiah merely put off the outer sackcloth, retaining the tunic or inner vest, as an emblem that Egypt would be stripped of its possessions, while his very dress was a silent exhortation to repentance. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: The Lord said: Like as my servant Isaiah has walked naked and barefoot three years for a sign and wonder upon Egypt and upon Ethiopia. The symbolical action did not continue all this time but at intervals, to keep it before the people's minds during that period — or rather, a three years' sign, a sign that a three years' calamity would come on Egypt and Ethiopia — and this is the only instance of a strictly symbolical act performed by Isaiah, whereas with later prophets such acts were common. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: So will the king of Assyria lead away the Egyptians prisoners and the Ethiopians captives, young and old, naked and barefoot, even with their buttocks uncovered, to the shame of Egypt. Captives are represented thus on Egyptian monuments, where Egypt and Ethiopia appear in alliance as here, and the shameful exposure matches the prophet's own uncovered state. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: They will be afraid and ashamed of Ethiopia their expectation, and of Egypt their glory. They are the Philistine allies of Egypt who trusted in it against Assyria, and a warning to the party among the Jews who, though Judah was then Assyria's subordinate ally, looked to Egypt as preferable: Ethiopia was their expectation, an alliance hoped for but not yet obtained, and Egypt their glory and boast, since that alliance was completed. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: The inhabitant of this isle — that is, coast on the Mediterranean, Philistia and perhaps Phoenicia — will say in that day: Behold, such is our expectation, whither we flee for help to be delivered from the king of Assyria, and how will we escape? If Egypt, in which they trusted, was overcome, how will they, a small weak state, escape? The emphatic "we" drives the warning home to Judah's own party of Egyptian trust. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
