Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 46

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Bel bows down, Nebo stoops, their idols were upon the beasts, and upon the cattle; your carriages were heavy loaden; they are a burden to the weary beast. This chapter treats how Babylon's idols could not save themselves, much less her, but God can and will save Israel, with Cyrus as His instrument. Bel is the same as the Phoenician Baal, that is, lord, the chief god of Babylon, to whom was dedicated the celebrated tower of Babylon, in the centre of one of the two parts into which the city was divided, the palace being in the centre of the other. Bel is identical with the sun, worshipped on turrets, housetops, and other high places, so as to be nearer the heavenly hosts; some identify Bel with the planet Jupiter, which, with the planet Venus under the name Astarte or Astaroth, was worshipped in the East as the god of fortune, the most propitious star to be born under. According to the Apocryphal book, Bel and the Dragon, Bel was cast down by Cyrus. Boweth and stoopeth mean falleth prostrate. Nebo is the planet Mercury or Hermes, in astrology, the scribe of heaven, answering to the Egyptian Anubis; the extensive worship of it is shown by the many proper names compounded of it: Nebuchadnezzar, Nebuzar-adan, Nabonassar, etc. Were upon means that is, were a burden, supplied from the following clause, upon; it was customary to transport the gods of the vanquished to the land of the conquerors, who thought thereby the more effectually to keep down the subject people. Carriages are in the Old English sense of the things carried, the images borne by you: the lading, carriages, not the vehicles, but the baggage; or, the images which used to be carried by you formerly in your solemn processions. Were heavy loaden is rather, are put as a load on the beasts of burden; some translate, They who should have been your carriers, as Jehovah is to His people in verses 3 and 4, are become burdens. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: They stoop, they bow down together; they could not deliver the burden, but themselves are gone into captivity. Deliver means from the enemies' hands. Burden means their images laid on the beasts in verse 1. Themselves means the gods, here also distinguished from their images. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Hearken unto me, O house of Jacob, and all the remnant of the house of Israel, which are borne by me from the belly, which are carried from the womb. This is in contrast to what precedes: Babylon's idols, so far from bearing its people safely, are themselves borne off, a burden to the laden beast; but Jehovah bears His people in safety even from the womb to old age. God compares Himself to a nurse tenderly carrying a child, in contrast to Moses' language in Numbers. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: And even to your old age I am he, and even to hoar hairs will I carry you; I have made, and I will bear, even I will carry, and will deliver you. Old age means as your, you, you, are not in the Hebrew, the sentiment is more general than the English version, though of course it includes the Jews from the infancy to the more advanced age of their history, as in chapter 47. I am he means that is, the same. I will bear and carry means not only do I not need to be borne and carried Myself, as the idols in verse 1. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: To whom will you liken me, and make me equal, and compare me, that we may be like? This recalls chapters 40 verses 18 and 25. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: They lavish gold out of the bag, and weigh silver in the balance, and hire a goldsmith, and he makes it a god; they fall down, yes, they worship. This recalls chapters 40 verses 19 and 20 and 41 verse 7. They lavish gold out of their purses and spare no expense for their idol; their profuseness shames the niggardliness of professors who worship God with what cost them nothing. Sin is always a costly service. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: They bear him upon the shoulder, they carry him, and set him in his place, and he stands; from his place he will not remove; yes, one will cry unto him, yet can he not answer, nor save him out of his trouble. Can not save recalls chapter 45 verse 20, with which contrast chapter 45 verse 19. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Remember this, and show yourselves men; bring it again to mind, O you transgressors. Show yourselves men means renounce the childishness of idolatry as shown in what precedes; in order to be manly we must be godly, for man was made in the image of God, and only rises to his true dignity when joined to God; virtue is derived from the Latin vir, a man. Bring to mind is rather, lay it to heart. Transgressors are addressed to the idolaters among the Jews. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Remember the former things of old, for I am God, and there is none else; I am God, and there is none like me. Former means namely, proofs of the sole Godship of Jehovah, from predictions fulfilled, and interpositions of God in behalf of Israel, as in chapter 45 verse 5. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Declaring the end from the beginning, and from ancient times the things that are not yet done, saying: My counsel will stand, and I will do all my pleasure. This recalls chapters 45 verse 21, 41 verses 22 and 23, and 44 verse 26. Yet is not in the Hebrew; translate, What had not been done. Do all my pleasure recalls chapters 53 and Romans 9. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Calling a ravenous bird from the east, the man that executes my counsel from a far country; yes, I have spoken it, I will also bring it to pass; I have purposed it, I will also do it. Ravenous bird is Cyrus, so called on account of the rapidity of his marches from the distant regions of Persia to pounce on his prey, as in chapters 41 and Jeremiah and Ezekiel; the standard of Cyrus, too, was a golden eagle on a spear, as in Xenophon, where almost the same word is used, aetos, as here, ayit. Executeth my counsel recalls chapters 44 verse 28 and 45 verse 13. Babylon represents, mystically, the apostate faction: the destruction of its idols symbolises the future general extirpation of all idolatry and unbelief. Purposed, also do it recalls chapter 43 verse 13. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Hearken unto me, you stouthearted, that are far from righteousness. Stout-hearted means stubborn in resisting God. Far from righteousness recalls chapters 59 and Habakkuk. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: I bring near my righteousness; it will not be far off, and my salvation will not tarry, and I will place salvation in Zion for Israel my glory. Near is antithetical to far in verse 12, as in chapters 51, 56, and 61 and Romans. Righteousness, answering to salvation in the parallel clause, therefore means here, my righteous deliverance, righteous, because proving the truth of God's promises, and so contrived as to not compromise, but vindicate, His righteousness, as in chapter 42 and Romans. Zion, my glory is rather, I will give salvation in Zion; to Israel I will give my glory. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
