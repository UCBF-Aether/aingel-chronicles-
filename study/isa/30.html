Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 30

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Woe to the rebellious children, says the Lord, who take counsel, but not of me, and who cover with a covering, but not of my spirit, that they may add sin to sin. Chapters 30–32 refer probably to the summer of 714 B.C., with Jewish ambassadors now on their way to Egypt to seek aid against Assyria, and Isaiah denounces reliance on Egypt rather than Jehovah, for God had prohibited such alliances and it was a leading part of Jewish polity that they be a separate people. Take counsel is rather execute counsels, and cover with a covering is to wrap themselves in reliances disloyal toward Jehovah, answering to seeking to hide deeply their counsel from the Lord — or literally, to pour out libations, by which leagues were made, so they make a league not suggested by God's Spirit. That they may add sin to sin speaks of consequence as intention, so reckless were they: one sin entails another. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Who walk to go down into Egypt, and have not asked at God's mouth, to strengthen themselves in the strength of Pharaoh, and to trust in the shadow of Egypt! Walk means they are now setting out, namely their ambassadors, and Pharaoh is the generic name of Egypt's kings, as Caesar was at Rome, meaning king, with Phra, the sun, as hieroglyphic symbol and title; the shadow is an image from shelter against heat, meaning protection. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Therefore the strength of Pharaoh will be your shame, and the trust in the shadow of Egypt your confusion. Shame means disappointment, for Egypt, weakened by its internal dissensions, can give no solid help. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: For his princes were at Zoan, and his ambassadors came to Hanes. His is Judah's, and the princes are already arrived at Zoan on their errand to Pharaoh, while the ambassadors have come to Hanes, west of the Nile in central Egypt, Egyptian Hnes, Greek Heracleopolis, the seat of a reigning prince at the time, as was Zoan, hence the ambassadors go to both. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: They were all ashamed of a people that could not profit them, nor be a help nor profit, but a shame, and also a reproach. As Jeremiah says, they were ashamed of a people that could not profit them, for Egypt's help proves shame and reproach rather than profit. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: The burden of the beasts of the south: into the land of trouble and anguish, from whence come the young and old lion, the viper and fiery flying serpent, they will carry their riches upon the shoulders of young asses, and their treasures upon the bunches of camels, to a people that will not profit them. The burden is the prophecy as to the beasts, or Isaiah sees in vision the ambassadors' beasts burdened with rich presents travelling southwards to Egypt and exclaims at the burden of treasure; the land of trouble is the desert between Palestine and Egypt, destitute of water and abounding in dangerous animals, the flying serpent a species springing like a dart from trees upon its prey, and the riches are carried, rather presented, as presents to Egypt on full-grown asses and camels. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: For the Egyptians will help in vain, and to no purpose; therefore I have cried concerning this: Their strength is to sit still. Egypt is vanity, and to no purpose will they help; strength is in Hebrew Rahab, a designation for Egypt implying haughty fierceness, so the phrase is, I call her Arrogance that sits still, for she who boasted of the help she would give, when it came to the test, sat still, as in chapter 36. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Now go, write it before them in a table, and note it in a book, that it may be for the time to come for ever and ever. The table is a tablet set in public containing the prophecy in briefer form, to be read by all, and the book is a parchment roll containing the prophecy in full for distant posterity, whose truth will be seen hereafter when the event comes to pass; for ever and ever is rather, for a testimony forever, for testimony is often joined to perpetuity. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: That this is a rebellious people, lying children, children that will not hear the law of the Lord. Lying means unfaithful to Jehovah, whose covenant they had taken on them as His adopted children. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Who say to the seers: See not; and to the prophets: Prophesy not unto us right things, speak unto us smooth things, prophesy deceits. See not means, as you now do, foretelling misfortune, and prophesy not right things was not avowedly requested, but their conduct virtually expressed it: no man professedly wishes to be deceived, but many seek a kind of teaching which is deceit, and the Jews desired success to be foretold as the issue of their Egyptian league, though ill had been announced by God's prophet — these constituted the deceits. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Get out of the way, turn aside out of the path, cause the Holy One of Israel to cease from before us. Get out of the way means depart from the true way of religion, and cause to cease means let us hear no more of His name, for God's holiness is what troubles sinners most. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Therefore thus says the Holy One of Israel: Because you despise this word, and trust in oppression and perverseness, and stay thereon. Isaiah so little yields to their wicked prejudices that he repeats the very name and truth they disliked; this word is Isaiah's exhortation to reliance on Jehovah, oppression is whereby they levied the treasures sent to conciliate Egypt, and perverseness is relying on Egypt rather than Jehovah. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Therefore this iniquity will be to you as a breach ready to fall, swelling out in a high wall, whose breaking comes suddenly at an instant. The image is from a curve swelling out in a wall: when it gives way, it causes the downfall of the whole wall, so their Egyptian policy will collapse. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: He will break it as the breaking of the potters' vessel that is broken in pieces, and he will not spare, so that there will not be found in the bursting of it a sherd to take fire from the hearth, or to take water withal out of the pit. He is the enemy, or rather God, and it is the Jewish state, fragile as a potter's vessel, whose bursting leaves not even a sherd large enough to take up a live coal or water from a cistern; the swell of the wall is at first imperceptible and gradual, but at last comes to the crisis, so the decay of the Jewish state. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: For thus says the Lord God, the Holy One of Israel: In returning and rest you will be saved; in quietness and in confidence will be your strength, and you would not. Returning and rest mean turning back from the embassy to Egypt and ceasing from warlike preparations, and quietness answers to waiting for God, but they would not. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: But you said: No, for we will flee upon horses, therefore you will flee; and, We will ride upon the swift, therefore will they that pursue you be swift. Flee here means not as fugitives but we will speed our course against the Assyrians by help of cavalry supplied by Egypt, expressly against the Mosaic law; and they will flee before their enemies, for their sin and its punishment correspond. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: One thousand will flee at the rebuke of one; at the rebuke of five you will flee, till you are left as a beacon upon the top of a mountain, and as an ensign on a hill. One thousand at once, or as one man, will flee at the rebuke — the battle cry — of one, and at the rebuke of five all will flee so utterly that even two will not be left together, but each one will be as solitary as a signal staff or a banner on a hill, erected to rally a nation in war; the remnant left will be beacons warning all men of God's justice and the truth of His threatenings. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: And therefore the Lord will wait, that he may be gracious unto you, and therefore he will be exalted, that he may have mercy upon you, for the Lord is a God of judgment; blessed are all they that wait for him. Therefore — on account of their perverseness — Jehovah will delay to be gracious, or wait, delay in punishing, to give time for repentance, or yet therefore, because the distress will lead the Jews to repentance and so Jehovah will pity them; he will be exalted, so men will have more elevated views of God's mercy, or he will rise up to pity them, or be far removed, not immediately descending to punish, in order to have mercy. Judgment means justice and faithfulness to His covenant, and wait compares with verse 15: wait for His times of having mercy. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: For the people will dwell in Zion at Jerusalem, and you will weep no more; he will be very gracious unto you at the voice of your cry, and when he hears it, he will answer you. The restoration from Babylon only typifies the full accomplishment of verses 18–33: they will weep no more, as in chapter 25, and at the voice of their cry, as in chapters 26 and Jeremiah 29, God will answer. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: And though the Lord give you the bread of adversity, and the water of affliction, yet your teachers will not be removed into a corner any more, but your eyes will see your teachers. Rather, The Lord will give the bread of adversity — He will not deny food enough to save them in adversity — and the water of affliction, yet their teachers will not hide themselves any more, but will be openly received with reverence, in contrast to times when teachers were forced to hide from persecution. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Your ears will hear a word behind you, saying: This is the way, walk in it, when you turn to the right hand, and when you turn to the left. The word behind is conscience, guided by the Holy Spirit, directing them to the way whenever they turn aside. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: You will defile also the covering of your graven images of silver, and the ornament of your molten images of gold, and cast them away as a menstruous cloth, and say unto it: Get you hence. The images are formed of wood or potter's clay and covered with silver, and Hezekiah and afterwards Josiah defiled them, casting them away as unclean, as in chapter 2. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Then he will give the rain of your seed, that you will sow the ground withal, and bread of the increase of the earth, and it will be fat and plenteous; in that day your cattle will feed in large pastures. The rain is rather rain for your seed, for physical prosperity accompanies national piety, especially under the Old Testament: the early rain fell soon after sowing in October or November, the latter rain in spring before ripening, and both were needed for a good harvest; the increase is the produce, and fat is bread made of the best wheat flour. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: The oxen likewise and the young asses that ear the ground will eat clean provender, which has been winnowed with the shovel and with the fan. Ear means till, and asses were employed in tillage as well as oxen; clean is rather salted provender, for sweet provender is as bread to camels and salted provender as confectionery, so the very cattle will share the coming felicity, or else well-fermented maslin of grain, beans, vetches, hay, and salt. Winnowed means not as usually given to cattle before separation from chaff, for grain will be so abundant it will be given winnowed, with the shovel throwing grain up in the wind and the fan winnowing it. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: There will be upon every high mountain, and upon every high hill, rivers and streams of waters in the day of the great slaughter, when the towers fall. Even the otherwise barren hills will then be well-watered, and the day is when the disobedient among the Jews are slain, as foretold in verse 16: the towers are mighty men, or else the towers of Sennacherib or of Babylon, types of all God's people's enemies. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Moreover the light of the moon will be as the light of the sun, and the light of the sun will be sevenfold, as the light of seven days, in the day that the Lord binds up the breach of his people, and heals the stroke of their wound. The image from the heavenly bodies expresses the increase of spiritual light and felicity, and sevenfold implies the perfection of that felicity, seven being the sacred number, also to be literally fulfilled hereafter in the heavenly city; the breach is the wound or calamity sent by God on account of their sins. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Behold, the name of the Lord comes from far, burning with his anger, and the burden thereof is heavy; his lips are full of indignation, and his tongue as a devouring fire. The Lord is Jehovah Himself, represented as a storm approaching and ready to burst over the Assyrians; the burden is heavy, literally grievousness is the flame, the flame darting from Him is grievous, or the uprising cloud is grievous, the gathering cloud gradually rising till it bursts. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: His breath, as an overflowing stream, will reach to the midst of the neck, and sift the nations with the sieve of vanity, and there will be a bridle in the jaws of the people, causing them to err. The stream reaches to the neck, the most extreme danger, yet as the head, the capital of Judah, was to be spared, so the head, the sovereign of Assyria, Sennacherib, should escape; the sieve of vanity is rather the winnowing fan of destruction, and the bridle in the jaws is as prisoners are represented in Assyrian inscriptions, causing the peoples, the various races composing the Assyrian armies, to err. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: You will have a song, as in the night when a holy solemnity is kept, and gladness of heart, as when one goes with a pipe to come into the mountain of the Lord, to the mighty One of Israel. The solemnity is as in the passover night when they celebrate deliverance from Egypt, so they will celebrate rescue from Assyrian bondage; going with a pipe, or flute, recalls going up to Jerusalem at the three feasts with music and gladness. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: The Lord will cause his glorious voice to be heard, and will show the lighting down of his arm, with the indignation of his anger, and with the flame of a devouring fire, with scattering, and tempest, and hailstones. Jehovah's glorious voice, raised against the enemy, is mentioned again in contrast to the music with which His people come to worship Him, and the lighting down of His arm is the descent of His arm in striking, with a scattering blast or inundation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: For through the voice of the Lord the Assyrian will be beaten down, who smote with a rod. The Assyrian rod which beat will itself be beaten, and that by the mere voice of the Lord, an unseen divine agency. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: In every place where the grounded staff passes, which the Lord will lay upon him, it will be with tabrets and harps, and in battles of shaking he will fight with it. Grounded is rather decreed, appointed, and the staff is the avenging rod; every passing through, or infliction, of the appointed rod will be with tabrets, accompanied with joy on the part of the rescued peoples, and in battles of shaking, the shock of battles, God will fight with it, namely Assyria. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: For Tophet is ordained of old; yes, for the king it is prepared; he has made it deep and large; the pile thereof is fire and much wood; the breath of the Lord, like a stream of brimstone, does kindle it. Tophet is literally a place of abomination, the valley of the sons of Hinnom southeast of Jerusalem, where Israel offered human sacrifices to Moloch by fire, hence a place of burning, and latterly Gehenna, the receptacle of the city's refuse, consumed by constantly burning fires, hence hell, the place of torment. In the former sense it fitly symbolises the funeral pyre of the Assyrian army, not that it actually perished there, for the Hebrews buried their dead, but the heathen Assyrians are to be burnt as a mark of ignominy; in the latter sense Tophet is the receptacle prepared for the devil, antitype to the king of chapter 14, and his angels, and unbelieving men. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
