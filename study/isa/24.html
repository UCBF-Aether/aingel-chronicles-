Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 24

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Behold, the Lord makes the earth empty, and makes it waste, and turns it upside down, and scatters abroad its inhabitants. Chapters 24–27 form one continuous poetical prophecy: the dispersion and successive calamities of the Jews, the gospel preached by the first Hebrew converts, judgments on the church's adversaries and its final triumph, thanksgiving for the apostate faction's overthrow, the righteous established in peace, and leviathan judged. Having treated the several nations in particular, Isaiah passes to the last times of the world at large and of Judah, representative and future head of the churches. The earth here is rather the land of Judah, and the desolation under Nebuchadnezzar prefigured that under Titus. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: It will be as with the people, so with the priest; as with the servant, so with his master; as with the maid, so with her mistress; as with the buyer, so with the seller; as with the lender, so with the borrower; as with the taker of usury, so with the giver of usury to him. All alike will share the same calamity, and no favoured class will escape. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The earth mourns and fades away, the world languishes and fades away, and the haughty people of the earth do languish. The world is the kingdom of Israel, as in chapter 13 Babylon is, and the haughty are literally the height of the people, the high people, so even the nobles share the general distress. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: The earth also is defiled under its inhabitants, because they have transgressed the laws, changed the ordinance, and broken the everlasting covenant. The land is defiled with innocent blood, and the laws, ordinance, and everlasting covenant are the moral laws, positive statutes, and national covenant designed to be forever between God and them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Therefore the curse has devoured the earth, and those who dwell in it are desolate; therefore the inhabitants of the earth are burned, and few men are left. The land is burned with the consuming wrath of heaven, whether internally or externally, as the people are consumed with the withering dryness of their doomed land. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: The new wine mourns, and the vine languishes, and all the merryhearted do sigh. The new wine mourns because there are none to drink it, or rather it has become vapid, and the vine languishes because there are none to cultivate it now. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: The mirth of tabrets ceases, the noise of those who rejoice ends, and the joy of the harp ceases. The mirth of tabrets and the joy of the harp end as in Revelation's picture of fallen Babylon, with all festive music silenced. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: They will not drink wine with a song, and strong drink will be bitter to them that drink it. Wine will no longer be drunk with a song, the usual accompaniment of feasts, and strong drink — date wine — will be bitter in consequence of the national calamities. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: The city of confusion is broken down, and every house is shut up, that no man may come in. The city of confusion is rather desolation, what Jerusalem would become, called so by anticipation, and the houses are shut up through fear, or rather choked up by ruins. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: There is a crying for wine in the streets, and all joy is darkened, and the mirth of the land is gone. The crying is for wine to drown sorrows in drink, as in chapter 16, and Joel, written about the same time, resembles this. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: In the city is left desolation, and the gate is smitten with destruction. The gate is smitten with a crash, as with a great tumult the gate is battered down, leaving only desolation in the city. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: When thus it will be in the midst of the land among the people, it will be as the shaking of an olive tree, and as the gleaning grapes when the vintage is done. The land is Judea, and among the people a remnant will be left, as the shaking or after-picking of an olive tree, a few remaining on the highest boughs as in chapter 17. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: They will lift up their voice, and sing for the majesty of the Lord, and cry aloud from the sea. They are those who are left, the remnant, singing a thanksgiving for the goodness of the Lord who has so mercifully preserved them, crying from the distant lands beyond the sea whither they have escaped. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Therefore glorify the Lord in the fires, even the name of the Lord God of Israel in the isles of the sea. The fires may be translated caves, or the fires of affliction that loose carnal bonds without injuring a hair, as with Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, or in the regions of morning light, the east, in antithesis to the isles of the sea, the west: wherever the scattered are, east or west, they are still to glorify the Lord. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: From the uttermost part of the earth we have heard songs, even glory to the righteous. But Isaiah says: My leanness, my leanness, woe unto me! The treacherous dealers have dealt treacherously, yes, the treacherous dealers have dealt very treacherously. Songs to God come together to Palestine from distant lands as a grand chorus, with glory to the righteous as their burden; amidst exile, loss of temple, and all dear to man, their confidence in God is unshaken. These songs recall the joy of other times and draw from Jerusalem in her calamities the cry "My leanness" — or glory to the Just One, with leanness expressing man's corruption that led the Jews to crucify the Just One and the prophet's own deficiency of righteousness. The treacherous dealers are the foreign nations oppressing Jerusalem and overcoming it by stratagem. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Fear, and the pit, and the snare, are upon you, O inhabitant of the earth. This verse explains the wretchedness of verse 16, and Jeremiah uses the same proverbial words: the inhabitants are nowhere safe, for if they escape one danger they fall into another and worse on the opposite side, and fear is the term for the cords with coloured feathers which, fluttered in the air, scare beasts into the pitfall or birds into the snare. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: He who flees from the noise of the fear will fall into the pit, and he who comes up out of the midst of the pit will be taken in the snare, for the windows from on high are open, and the foundations of the earth do shake. Fear here is the shout designed to rouse game and drive it into the pitfall, and the windows from on high are taken from the deluge account, the flood-gates, so the final judgments of fire on the apostate world are compared to the deluge. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: The earth is utterly broken down, the earth is clean dissolved, and the earth is moved exceedingly. The land is pictured by the image of an earthquake, utterly broken, dissolved, and moved. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: The earth will reel to and fro like a drunkard, and will be removed like a cottage, and the transgression thereof will be heavy upon it, and it will fall, and not rise again. The earth will wave to and fro like a hammock, a hanging couch suspended from trees by cords as Arab land-keepers use, swung about by the wind, and its transgression will lie heavy upon it like an overwhelming burden; it will not rise again in those convulsions, though not meaning it would never rise, as verse 23 shows. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: In that day the Lord will punish the host of the high ones that are on high, and the kings of the earth upon the earth. The high ones are the heavenly host, either the visible host of heaven, the present economy of nature affected by sun, moon, and stars, objects of idolatry, being abolished, or rather the invisible rulers of the darkness of this world, as the antithesis to kings of the earth shows, for angels preside over kingdoms of the world. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: They will be gathered together, as prisoners are gathered in the pit, and will be shut up in the prison, and after many days they will be visited. The image is of captives thrust together into a dungeon, and the shutting up of the Jews in Jerusalem under Nebuchadnezzar and again under Titus was to be followed by a visitation of mercy after many days — seventy years in the former case, the time not yet elapsed in the latter — though visited may also be taken in wrath, and probably a double visitation is intended, deliverance to the elect and wrath to hardened unbelievers. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Then the moon will be confounded, and the sun ashamed, when the Lord of hosts will reign in Mount Zion, and in Jerusalem, and before his ancients gloriously. Still future, of which Jesus' triumphal entry into Jerusalem amidst hosannas was a pledge, Jehovah's kingdom will be set up with a splendour exceeding the light of sun and moon under the previous order, and his ancients are the elders of his people, or his ancient people the Jews, before whom he reigns gloriously after the overthrow of world kingdoms. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

No note for verse(s) 3 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
