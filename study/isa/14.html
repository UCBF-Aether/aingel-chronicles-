Study Notes — Isaiah

Chapter 14

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: For the Lord will have mercy on Jacob and will yet choose Israel, setting them in their own land, and strangers will be joined with them and cleave to the house of Jacob. Their restoration is grounded on election, a deliberate predilection, and the strangers are proselytes — an earnest of the future effect on the heathen world of the Jews' spiritual restoration. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: The people will take them and bring them to their place, and the house of Israel will possess them in the land of the Lord for servants and handmaids, and they will take captive those whose captives they were and rule over their oppressors. The people are primarily the Babylonians and ultimately the whole Gentile world, their place is Judea, and the captivity is won not by physical but by moral might, the force of love and regard for Israel's God. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: In the day the Lord gives Israel rest from sorrow and fear and the hard bondage in which they were made to serve, this rest will be the promised rest of the prophets. The verse closes the certainty of deliverance from Babylon in verses 1–3. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Then Israel will take up this proverb — a taunting song of triumph, as Orientals embodied thoughts in weighty, figurative gnomes — against the king of Babylon: How has the oppressor ceased, and the golden city ceased! The king is the ideal representative of Babylon, perhaps Belshazzar, with mystical Babylon ultimately meant, and the golden city is rather the exactress of gold. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: The Lord has broken the staff of the wicked and the sceptre of the rulers. The staff is not the sceptre but the staff with which one strikes others, since more tyrants than one are spoken of, and the rulers are tyrants, as the parallelism with the wicked proves. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: He who smote the people in wrath with a continual stroke, and ruled the nations in anger, is persecuted, and none hinders. The Hebrew is rather active: he who persecuted them without any to hinder him is now himself pursued, and the peoples subjected to Babylon are the ones he smote. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: The whole earth is at rest and quiet, and they break forth into singing. The once-subject nations of the whole earth, freed from the oppressor, rejoice in the quiet that follows his fall. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Yes, the fir trees rejoice at you, and the cedars of Lebanon, saying: Since you are laid down, no feller has come up against us. The fir trees, now left undisturbed, rejoice at his fall, for no feller comes as formerly when he was in power and felled Lebanon's cedars. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Hell from beneath is moved for you to meet you at your coming, stirring up the dead for you, even all the chief ones of the earth, and raising all the kings of the nations from their thrones. The scene changes from earth to hell, Hades the unseen abode of the departed, whose tenants, once mighty monarchs, rise in astonishment at the humbled king of Babylon's descent among them; the chief ones are literally goats, rams and leaders of the flock, princes, with gigantic wickedness included. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: All of them will speak and say to you: Are you also become weak as we? Are you become like unto us? They taunt him and derive from his calamity consolation under their own, for he is now weak as a shade bereft of blood and life. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Your pomp is brought down to the grave, and the noise of your viols; the worm is spread under you, and the worms cover you. Pomp and music, the accompaniment of Babylon's former feastings, give place to the corruption and stillness of the grave, and instead of a crimson coverlet over him there are worms, and instead of a gorgeous couch the maggot under him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: How are you fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning! How are you cut down to the ground, who did weaken the nations! The language applies to the Babylonian king primarily and at the same time shadows forth the great final enemy, the man of sin, who alone will exhaustively fulfil all its lineaments; Lucifer, day star, is a title truly belonging to Christ, the bright and morning star, and therefore hereafter to be assumed by Antichrist, and to weaken means to prostrate and discomfit. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: For you have said in your heart: I will ascend into heaven, I will exalt my throne above the stars of God, I will sit also upon the mount of the congregation, in the sides of the north. The stars express earthly potentates and heavenly principalities, and the mount of the congregation is the place of solemn meeting between God and his people in the temple at Jerusalem — or, in the Babylonian king's own terms, the northern mountain fabled as the common meeting-place of his gods, in the extreme parts of the north. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: I will ascend above the heights of the clouds, I will be like the Most High. As the Shekinah cloud was connected with the temple, this claim answers to Antichrist sitting as God in the temple of God, seated in Babylon, literal and spiritual, to which Isaiah refers. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Yet you will be brought down to hell, to the sides of the pit — antithetical to the sides of the north. He who said "I will ascend into heaven" falls to Sheol, to the sides of the sepulchre round which the dead were arranged in niches, or the innermost parts of the sepulchre. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Those who see you will narrowly look upon you and consider you, saying: Is this the man that made the earth to tremble, that did shake kingdoms? Passers-by contemplate with astonishment the body of the king cast out instead of lying in a splendid mausoleum, and can hardly believe their senses that it is he, looking narrowly to be certain they are not mistaken. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Is this he who made the world as a wilderness and destroyed its cities, who opened not the house of his prisoners? He made the world a wilderness and destroyed its cities, and did not let his captives loose homewards, keeping them shut in prison instead of releasing them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: All the kings of the nations, even all of them, lie in glory, every one in his own house. This is the usual practice: kings lie in grand mausoleums, their sepulchres, whereas the Babylonian king will be excluded from the family sepulchre, a mark of infamy. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: But you are cast out of your grave like an abominable branch, and as the raiment of those slain, thrust through with a sword, that go down to the stones of the pit, as a carcass trodden under foot. He is cast out without a grave such as might have been expected, like a useless sucker cut away by the husbandman, covered with the slain as with a garment of gore, unlike those buried in sepulchres excavated amidst stones. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: You will not be joined with them in burial, because you have destroyed your land and slain your people; the seed of evildoers will never be renowned. Whereas the princes slain with him will be buried, he will not, for Belshazzar oppressed his land with wars and tyranny and was much hated, and his seed will not be named forever: the Babylonian dynasty ends with him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Prepare slaughter for his children for the iniquity of their fathers, that they do not rise, nor possess the land, nor fill the face of the world with cities. The charge goes to the Medes and Persians as if they were God's conscious instruments, against Belshazzar's children, lest they occupy their fathers' places and inundate the world with armies, or fill the land with such proud cities as Babylon was. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: For God will rise up against them, says the Lord of hosts, and cut off from Babylon the name, and remnant, and son, and nephew. Against the family of Babylon's king, God will cut off the name — all male representatives so the name becomes extinct — and the remnant, all that is left, so the dynasty ceases. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: God will make Babylon a possession for the bittern, and pools of water, and sweep it with the besom of destruction, says the Lord of hosts. The bittern is rather the hedgehog, found in enormous numbers in the Euphrates islands, the pools come from Cyrus turning the Euphrates over the country, and the besom is a sweep-net that leaves nothing behind. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: The Lord of hosts has sworn, saying: Surely as I have thought, so will it come to pass, and as I have purposed, so will it stand. This fragment on the destruction of the Assyrians under Sennacherib comforts the Jews in Babylon as a pledge: he who with such ease overthrew the Assyrian could likewise effect his purpose as to Babylon, and the Lord's thought stands against the Assyrians' thoughts. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: God will break the Assyrian in his land, and upon his mountains tread him under foot; then his yoke will depart from off them, and his burden depart from off their shoulders. The purpose is that Sennacherib's army, destroyed on the mountains near Jerusalem, will no longer yoke Judah, for God regards Judah as peculiarly his. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: This is the purpose purposed upon the whole earth, and this is the hand stretched out upon all the nations. The purpose embraces the present world of all ages in its scope, of which Babylon and Assyria, the then representatives of world power, are but a part, and the hand is stretched out in punishment. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: For the Lord of hosts has purposed, and who will disannul it? And his hand is stretched out, and who will turn it back? No one can annul God's purpose or turn back his stretched-out hand, as Daniel declares that none can stay his hand. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: In the year that King Ahaz died was this burden. This prophecy against Philistia, given in 726 B.C., comforts the Jews lest they fear that people, for the Philistines, subdued by Uzziah, had taken several towns in south Judea under Ahaz, and Isaiah now denounces their final subjugation by Hezekiah. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Rejoice not, whole Palestina, because the rod of him that smote you is broken; for out of the serpent's root will come forth a cockatrice, and his fruit will be a fiery flying serpent. Palestina is literally the land of sojourners; the broken rod is Uzziah's yoke thrown off under Ahaz, and though Uzziah as a biting serpent is gone, a more deadly viper awaits Philistia in Hezekiah. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: The firstborn of the poor will feed, and the needy will lie down in safety, and God will kill your root with famine, and he will slay your remnant. The firstborn of the poor means the most abject poor, as firstborn of death means the most fatal death; the Jews, heretofore exposed to Philistine invasions, will feed in safety like a flock, while Philistia's root is radically destroyed by famine. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: Howl, O gate; cry, O city; you, whole Palestina, are dissolved, for there will come from the north a smoke, and none will be alone in his appointed times. The gate is those who throng the gate, the chief place of concourse; the smoke is from the north, Judea north and east of Palestine, whether the Jews' signal-fire or the region devastated by fire, and there will not be a straggler among the enemy's levies — the Jewish host will advance in close array, none lagging from weariness. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: What will one then answer the messengers of the nation? That the Lord has founded Zion, and the poor of his people will trust in it. The answer to the nations' messengers is that Zion is founded by the Lord himself, and the poor, the humble remnant, will find refuge and trust there. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
