Study Notes — Luke

Chapter 15

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Then drew near unto him all the publicans and sinners for to hear him. The audience itself is the occasion: outcasts draw near to hear. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: The Pharisees and scribes murmured, This man receives sinners, and eats with them. Murmuring at reception becomes the target of three parables. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: And he spoke this parable unto them, saying. The parables answer the murmur directly, not in general. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: What man of you, having a hundred sheep, if he lose one, does not leave the ninety-nine in the wilderness, and go after that which is lost, until he find it? Henry notes the lost sheep represents the sinner departed from God, exposed to certain ruin if not brought back, yet not desirous to return, and Christ is earnest in bringing sinners home. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: And when he has found it, he lays it on his shoulders, rejoicing. Finding is not scolding; it is carrying, rejoicing. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: When he comes home, he calls friends and neighbours, saying, Rejoice with me, for I have found my sheep which was lost. Joy is shared, not private. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Likewise joy shall be in heaven over one sinner that repents, more than over ninety-nine just persons which need no repentance. Heaven's joy over one repentant outweighs complacency over the ninety-nine. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: Either what woman having ten pieces of silver, if she lose one, does not light a candle, sweep the house, and seek diligently till she find it? Henry notes the lost piece is of small value compared with the rest, yet the woman seeks diligently, representing the various means God uses to bring lost souls home. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: And when she has found it, she calls her friends and neighbours, saying, Rejoice with me, for I have found the piece which I had lost. The second parable repeats the pattern: diligence, finding, shared rejoicing. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Likewise, there is joy in the presence of the angels of God over one sinner that repents. Henry urges care that our repentance be unto salvation, in view of the Saviour's joy on return. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: And he said, A certain man had two sons. The third parable has two sons, because the murmurers are the second son. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: The younger said, Father, give me the portion of goods that falls to me. And he divided unto them his living. Henry notes it is bad, and the beginning of worse, when men look on God's gifts as debts due to them. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Not many days after, the younger gathered all, took his journey into a far country, and there wasted his substance with riotous living. Henry notes a sinful state is departure and distance from God, and a spending state: wilful sinners misemploy thoughts, powers, time, and opportunities. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: When he had spent all, there arose a mighty famine in that land, and he began to be in want. Spending ends in want, and famine arrives after the money is gone. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: He went and joined himself to a citizen of that country, who sent him into his fields to feed swine. Henry notes a sinful state is vile and slavish: the devil's servants make provision for the flesh, no better than feeding swine. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: He would fain have filled his belly with the husks the swine did eat, and no man gave unto him. Henry notes a sinful state is wanting: sinners want necessaries for souls, neither food nor raiment nor provision hereafter, and in vain cry to world and flesh, which poison but cannot feed. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: When he came to himself, he said, How many hired servants of my father's have bread enough and to spare, and I perish with hunger! Henry notes recovery begins by coming to himself, the turning point of conversion, when the Lord opens eyes and convinces of sin, and the meanest servant of God appears happier than he. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: I will arise and go to my father, and will say, Father, I have sinned against heaven, and before thee. The resolve names sin against heaven first, then before the father. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: And am no more worthy to be called thy son: make me as one of thy hired servants. Unworthiness is confessed, and servanthood, not sonship, is requested. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: He arose and came to his father. But when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him, had compassion, ran, fell on his neck, and kissed him. Henry notes the Lord meets him with unexpected tokens of forgiving love, before the speech is finished. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: And the son said, Father, I have sinned against heaven, and in thy sight, and am no more worthy to be called thy son. The prepared speech is spoken, but the hired-servant request is now unnecessary. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: But the father said to his servants, Bring forth the best robe, and put it on him, and put a ring on his hand, and shoes on his feet. Henry notes the humbled sinner is clothed in the robe of the Redeemer's righteousness, made partaker of the Spirit of adoption, prepared by peace and gospel grace to walk in holiness. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: And bring hither the fatted calf, and kill it, and let us eat, and be merry. Feasting replaces husks, and merriment replaces want. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: For this my son was dead, and is alive again; he was lost, and is found. And they began to be merry. Henry notes the parable fully sets forth gospel grace, of unspeakable use to poor sinners to direct and encourage them in repenting and returning. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: Now his elder son was in the field, and as he came near the house, he heard music and dancing. The elder son, who stayed, hears the celebration from outside. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: He called one of the servants, and asked what these things meant. Inquiry, not entry, is his first response. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: And he said, Thy brother is come, and thy father has killed the fatted calf, because he has received him safe and sound. The servant's plain report states the reason: safe and sound return. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: And he was angry, and would not go in; therefore came his father out, and entreated him. Henry notes the latter part sets forth the Pharisees' character, and the Lord's kindness, and the proud manner in which gracious kindness is often received. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Lo, these many years do I serve thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment, and yet thou never gavest me a kid, that I might make merry with my friends. Years of service are tallied against a kid never given. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: But as soon as this thy son was come, which has devoured thy living with harlots, thou hast killed for him the fatted calf. The elder son renames his brother 'this thy son,' distancing himself from both. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: And he said, Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine. The father's answer corrects the accounting: presence and possession were never withheld. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: It was meet that we should make merry, and be glad, for this thy brother was dead, and is alive again, and was lost, and is found. Henry notes God's mercy shines almost as bright in tender bearing with peevish saints as in receiving prodigals, and it is the children's happiness, keeping close to the Father's house, to be ever with him. — Matthew Henry
