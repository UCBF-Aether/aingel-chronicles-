Study Notes — Luke

Chapter 16

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: He said also unto his disciples, There was a certain rich man, which had a steward, accused that he had wasted his goods. Henry notes whatever we have, the property is God's; we have only its use, according to our great Lord's direction and for his honour, and we are all liable to the same charge of not improving what God trusted us with. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: He called him, and said, How is it that I hear this of thee? Give an account of thy stewardship, for thou mayest be no longer steward. The steward cannot deny it; he must make up accounts and be gone, Henry notes, teaching that death will come and deprive us of present opportunities. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Then the steward said within himself, What shall I do? For my lord takes away my stewardship: I cannot dig; to beg I am ashamed. Dismissal forces a plan, and both honest alternatives are closed to him. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: I am resolved what to do, that, when I am put out of the stewardship, they may receive me into their houses. The plan is to buy future welcome with present discounts. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: So he called every one of his lord's debtors, and said to the first, How much owest thou unto my lord? The steward works through the debtors one by one. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: He said, A hundred measures of oil. And he said, Take thy bill, sit down quickly, and write fifty. Half the oil debt is struck off, quickly, before the stewardship ends. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Then said he to another, And how much owest thou? He said, A hundred measures of wheat. And he said, Take thy bill, and write fourscore. A fifth of the wheat debt is struck off, the same policy at a different rate. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: And the lord commended the unjust steward, because he had done wisely, for the children of this world are in their generation wiser than the children of light. Henry stresses the lord commended not the fraud, but the policy; the steward is not an example in cheating, but worldly men in activity and perseverance are often wiser than believers, and children of light should as earnestly pursue their better object. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: And I say unto you, Make to yourselves friends of the mammon of unrighteousness, that, when ye fail, they may receive you into everlasting habitations. Wealth, used for others now, becomes welcome hereafter. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: He that is faithful in that which is least is faithful also in much, and he that is unjust in the least is unjust also in much. Faithfulness is a character, not a scale; it shows in the least. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: If therefore ye have not been faithful in the unrighteous mammon, who will commit to your trust the true riches? Henry notes true riches signify spiritual blessings, and if a man spends on himself or hoards what God trusted him with, what evidence has he of being an heir of God through Christ? — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: And if ye have not been faithful in that which is another man's, who shall give you that which is your own? Stewardship of another's is the test for receiving one's own. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: No servant can serve two masters, for either he will hate the one and love the other, or hold to the one and despise the other. Ye cannot serve God and mammon. Henry adds the solemn warning: ye cannot serve God and the world, so divided are the two interests. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: And the Pharisees also, who were covetous, heard all these things, and they derided him. Covetous hearers answer the mammon teaching with derision. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Ye are they which justify yourselves before men, but God knows your hearts, for that which is highly esteemed among men is abomination in the sight of God. Henry notes many covetous sticklers for forms of godliness are the bitterest enemies to its power, and try to set others against the truth. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: The law and the prophets were until John; since that time the kingdom of God is preached, and every man presses into it. John marks the turn from law's era to the kingdom's preaching. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: And it is easier for heaven and earth to pass, than one tittle of the law to fail. The kingdom's newness does not abolish the law's smallest letter. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Whosoever puts away his wife, and marries another, commits adultery, and whosoever marries her put away from her husband commits adultery. Henry notes the Lord showed, in a case respecting divorce, that what the Pharisees contended for as law was a wresting of its meaning. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: There was a certain rich man, clothed in purple and fine linen, and fared sumptuously every day. Henry notes we are not told he got his estate by fraud or oppression, yet a man may have wealth, pomp, and pleasure, yet perish under God's wrath; his sin was providing for himself only. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: And there was a certain beggar named Lazarus, laid at his gate, full of sores. The named beggar lies at the unnamed rich man's gate, a contrast Henry stresses. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Desiring to be fed with crumbs from the rich man's table; moreover dogs came and licked his sores. Henry notes we are not told the rich man harmed him, but we do not find he had any care for him; dogs show more attention than the rich man. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: It came to pass, the beggar died, and was carried by angels into Abraham's bosom; the rich man also died, and was buried. Henry notes the godly poor man, in depth of adversity here, is carried by angels, while the rich man is merely buried. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: And in hell he lifted up his eyes, being in torments, and sees Abraham afar off, and Lazarus in his bosom. The reversal is now visible across an unbridgeable distance. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: He cried, Father Abraham, have mercy on me, and send Lazarus, that he may dip the tip of his finger in water, and cool my tongue, for I am tormented in this flame. The man who would not give crumbs now begs a fingertip of water. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: But Abraham said, Son, remember that thou in thy lifetime receivedst thy good things, and likewise Lazarus evil things; but now he is comforted, and thou art tormented. Henry notes wicked people have good things only in this life, and at death are for ever separated from all good, while godly people have evil things only in this life, and at death are for ever put from them. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: And beside all this, between us and you there is a great gulf fixed, so that they which would pass from hence to you cannot, neither can they pass to us, that would come from thence. Henry notes in this world there is no gulf between nature and grace, we may pass from sin to God, but if we die in our sins, there is no coming out. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: Then he said, I pray thee therefore, father, that thou wouldest send him to my father's house. The tormented man turns advocate for his family. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: For I have five brethren, that he may testify unto them, lest they also come into this place of torment. Henry notes the rich man would have his brethren stopped in their course, since their coming would make his misery worse, having helped show them the way thither. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Abraham said, They have Moses and the prophets; let them hear them. Scripture already given is the sufficient testimony. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: And he said, Nay, father Abraham, but if one went unto them from the dead, they will repent. A resurrection witness is pleaded as stronger than Scripture. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: And he said, If they hear not Moses and the prophets, neither will they be persuaded, though one rose from the dead. Henry concludes a messenger from the dead could say no more than Scripture says, and the same corruption that breaks through the written word would triumph over a witness from the dead; let us seek the law and testimony, the sure word of prophecy, for no terrors can give true repentance without God's special grace renewing the heart. — Matthew Henry
