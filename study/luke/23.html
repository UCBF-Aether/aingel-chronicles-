Study Notes — Luke

Chapter 23

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: And the whole multitude of them arose, and led him unto Pilate. The council's verdict becomes a procession to Pilate. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: They began to accuse him: We found this fellow perverting the nation, forbidding tribute to Caesar, saying he himself is Christ a King. The religious charge is recast as political perversion and rival kingship. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Pilate asked, Art thou the King of the Jews? And he answered, Thou sayest it. The answer neither denies nor pleads; it returns Pilate's own words. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Then said Pilate to the chief priests and people, I find no fault in this man. Pilate's first finding is innocence, declared to accusers and people alike. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: They were the more fierce, saying, He stirs up the people, teaching throughout all Jewry, beginning from Galilee to this place. Henry notes instead of being softened by Pilate's declaration of innocence, and considering whether they brought innocent blood's guilt on themselves, the Jews were the more angry. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: When Pilate heard of Galilee, he asked whether the man were a Galilaean. Galilee offers Pilate a jurisdictional exit. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: As soon as he knew he belonged unto Herod's jurisdiction, he sent him to Herod, who himself also was at Jerusalem at that time. The case is transferred to Herod, conveniently in Jerusalem. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: When Herod saw Jesus, he was exceeding glad, for he was desirous to see him of a long season, because he had heard many things of him, and he hoped to have seen some miracle done by him. Henry notes Herod had heard many things and out of curiosity longed to see him; the poorest beggar asking a miracle for necessity was never denied, but this proud prince, asking only to gratify curiosity, is refused. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Then he questioned with him in many words, but he answered him nothing. Many questions meet no answer, for Herod might have seen Christ's works in Galilee and would not, so now he would see them and shall not, Henry notes. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: The chief priests and scribes stood and vehemently accused him. Before Herod, as before Pilate, accusation is vehement and standing. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Herod with his men of war set him at nought, mocked him, arrayed him in a gorgeous robe, and sent him again to Pilate. Mockery in a gorgeous robe replaces the miracle Herod wanted. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: The same day Pilate and Herod were made friends together, for before they were at enmity between themselves. Henry notes the friendships of wicked men are often formed by union in wickedness, agreeing in little except enmity to God and contempt of Christ. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Pilate, when he had called together chief priests, rulers, and people. The second hearing gathers all parties together. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Said unto them, Ye have brought this man as one that perverts the people, and behold, I, having examined him before you, have found no fault touching those things whereof ye accuse him. Examination before the accusers finds no fault in the accused charges. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: No, nor yet Herod, for I sent you to him, and lo, nothing worthy of death is done unto him. Herod's return without a death sentence becomes Pilate's second witness. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: I will therefore chastise him, and release him. Henry asks, if no fault be found, why chastise him? Pilate, declaring innocence and minded to release, yet to please the people would punish him as an evildoer. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: For of necessity he must release one unto them at the feast. The feast custom of release frames the coming choice. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: They cried out all at once, Away with this man, and release unto us Barabbas. One voice replaces the hearing: away with Jesus, release Barabbas. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Who for a certain sedition made in the city, and for murder, was cast into prison. Barabbas, seditionist and murderer, is the man preferred. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Pilate therefore, willing to release Jesus, spoke again to them. Pilate's willingness speaks a second time against the cry. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: But they cried, Crucify him, crucify him. The cry hardens from away with him to crucify him, twice. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: He said unto them the third time, Why, what evil has he done? I have found no cause of death in him: I will therefore chastise him, and let him go. The third declaration of innocence still offers chastisement instead of release. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: They were instant with loud voices, requiring that he might be crucified, and the voices of them and of the chief priests prevailed. Henry notes the fear of man brings many into the snare of doing an unjust thing against conscience rather than get into trouble, and Pilate yielded at length, lacking courage against so strong a stream. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Pilate gave sentence that it should be as they required. Sentence follows the voices, not the findings. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: He released unto them him that for sedition and murder was cast into prison, whom they had desired, but he delivered Jesus to their will. Barabbas goes free, Jesus is delivered to their will, to be crucified. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: As they led him away, they laid hold upon one Simon, a Cyrenian, coming out of the country, and on him they laid the cross, that he might bear it after Jesus. Simon, from the country, bears the cross after Jesus. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: There followed him a great company of people, and of women, which also bewailed and lamented him. Henry notes though many reproached and reviled him, yet some pitied him, and the procession includes bewailing women. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: But Jesus turning unto them said, Daughters of Jerusalem, weep not for me, but weep for yourselves, and for your children. Henry notes Christ's death was his victory and our deliverance, purchase of eternal life, so weep not for him, but for our sins and our children's, which caused his death, and for fear of miseries brought by slighting his love. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: For behold, the days are coming, in which they shall say, Blessed are the barren, and the wombs that never bore, and the paps which never gave suck. Coming days will reverse blessing: barrenness will seem blessed. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: Then shall they begin to say to the mountains, Fall on us, and to the hills, Cover us. Mountains and hills are invoked as cover from coming judgment. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: For if they do these things in a green tree, what shall be done in the dry? Henry notes if God delivered Christ, the green tree, to such sufferings as sacrifice for sin, what will he do with sinners themselves, dry trees, corrupt and good for nothing; the best saints compared with Christ are dry trees, and Christ's sufferings preach terror to obstinate transgressors. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: There were also two other, malefactors, led with him to be put to death. Two malefactors share the procession, framing the crucifixion. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: When they were come to the place called Calvary, there they crucified him, and the malefactors, one on the right hand, and the other on the left. Henry notes here is the Lamb of God led as a lamb to slaughter, to sacrifice, crucified between two thieves. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: Then said Jesus, Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do. And they parted his raiment, and cast lots. Henry notes as soon as Christ was fastened to the cross, he prayed for his crucifiers, for the great thing he died to purchase is forgiveness of sin. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: The people stood beholding. The rulers also with them derided him, saying, He saved others, let him save himself, if he be Christ, the chosen of God. Beholding becomes derision from rulers: saving others is turned into a taunt. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: The soldiers also mocked him, coming to him, and offering him vinegar. Soldiers join rulers in mockery, with vinegar offered. — Matthew Henry

37 COMMENTARY: And saying, If thou be the king of the Jews, save thyself. The kingship charge becomes a mocking condition: save thyself. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: A superscription also was written over him in letters of Greek, Latin, and Hebrew, THIS IS THE KING OF THE JEWS. Three languages proclaim the kingship his enemies mock. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: One of the malefactors hanged railed on him, saying, If thou be Christ, save thyself and us. Henry notes in the two thieves are shown the different effects the cross will have in gospel preaching: one hardened to the last, for no troubles of themselves will change a wicked heart. — Matthew Henry

40 COMMENTARY: But the other answering rebuked him, saying, Dost not thou fear God, seeing thou art in the same condemnation? The other thief rebukes railing with fear of God and shared condemnation. — Matthew Henry

41 COMMENTARY: And we indeed justly, for we receive the due reward of our deeds, but this man has done nothing amiss. Henry notes the penitent owned he deserved what was done, and believed Jesus suffered wrongfully. — Matthew Henry

42 COMMENTARY: And he said unto Jesus, Lord, remember me when thou comest into thy kingdom. Henry notes his faith: Christ in deepest disgrace, suffering as a deceiver, not delivered by his Father, yet believed in a life to come, desiring happiness there, not merely rescue from the cross; and his humility: all his request is, Lord, remember me. — Matthew Henry

43 COMMENTARY: And Jesus said unto him, Verily I say unto thee, Today shalt thou be with me in paradise. Henry notes Christ on the cross is gracious like Christ on the throne, pitying a poor penitent in greatest agony, opening heaven to penitent believers; the case is singular, teaching despair of none, yet giving no encouragement to put off repentance, for late repentance is seldom true and none can be sure of deathbed time. — Matthew Henry

44 COMMENTARY: It was about the sixth hour, and there was darkness over all the earth until the ninth hour. Three hours of darkness cover the earth at midday. — Matthew Henry

45 COMMENTARY: The sun was darkened, and the veil of the temple was rent in the midst. Darkened sun and rent veil together mark the death. — Matthew Henry

46 COMMENTARY: When Jesus had cried with a loud voice, he said, Father, into thy hands I commend my spirit, and having said thus, he gave up the ghost. Henry notes his death is magnified by attending wonders and explained by the words with which he breathed out his soul; he was willing to offer himself. — Matthew Henry

47 COMMENTARY: Now when the centurion saw what was done, he glorified God, saying, Certainly this was a righteous man. The centurion, seeing what was done, glorifies God with a verdict of righteousness. — Matthew Henry

48 COMMENTARY: All the people that came together to that sight, beholding the things done, smote their breasts, and returned. Beholders return smiting their breasts, a different response from derision. — Matthew Henry

49 COMMENTARY: All his acquaintance, and the women that followed him from Galilee, stood afar off, beholding these things. Acquaintance and Galilean women stand afar off, beholding to the end. — Matthew Henry

50 COMMENTARY: Behold, there was a man named Joseph, a counsellor, and he was a good man, and a just. Joseph of Arimathea, counsellor, good and just, steps forward where others stood afar. — Matthew Henry

51 COMMENTARY: The same had not consented to the counsel and deed of them; he was of Arimathea, a city of the Jews, who also himself waited for the kingdom of God. Non-consent and waiting for the kingdom mark Joseph apart from the council's deed. — Matthew Henry

52 COMMENTARY: This man went unto Pilate, and begged the body of Jesus. Begging the body from Pilate is Joseph's public service. — Matthew Henry

53 COMMENTARY: He took it down, wrapped it in linen, and laid it in a sepulchre hewn in stone, wherein never man before was laid. Henry notes many who make no outward show, like Joseph, will be far readier for real service when occasion comes than noisier professors. — Matthew Henry

54 COMMENTARY: That day was the preparation, and the sabbath drew on. Burial is hastened because the sabbath draws on. — Matthew Henry

55 COMMENTARY: The women also, which came with him from Galilee, followed after, and beheld the sepulchre, and how his body was laid. Galilean women follow and behold the sepulchre and the laying. — Matthew Henry

56 COMMENTARY: They returned, and prepared spices and ointments, and rested the sabbath day according to the commandment. Henry notes weeping must not hinder sowing, and though in tears for their Lord, they must prepare to keep holy the sabbath; worldly affairs must be ordered not to hinder sabbath work. — Matthew Henry
