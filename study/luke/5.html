Study Notes — Luke

Chapter 5

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: As people pressed upon him to hear the word of God, Jesus stood by the lake of Gennesaret. Gill notes the crowds were eager only to hear, and the lake, honoured by Christ's preaching, miracles, and resurrection appearances, became famous through him. — Matthew Henry; John Gill

2 COMMENTARY: He saw two ships by the lake, but the fishermen were out washing their nets. Gill notes the boats belonged to Peter and Andrew and to Zebedee's sons, and the men were cleansing nets after a fruitless night, which makes the coming miracle more illustrious. — John Gill

3 COMMENTARY: He entered Simon's ship, asked him to thrust out a little, and sat down and taught the people from it. Gill notes sitting to teach was the Jewish custom, and JFB notes the boat was a platform for teaching, as in Matthew 13. — Matthew Henry; John Gill; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: When he had left speaking, he told Simon to launch into the deep and let down the nets for a draught. Henry's lesson is that when preaching is done, Christ sends Peter back to his calling, and time spent in public religion need not hinder worldly business but may further it. — Matthew Henry; John Gill; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Master, we have toiled all night and taken nothing; nevertheless at thy word I will let down the net. Gill notes the objection is honest — night was the best fishing time, and they were wearied and empty — yet faith obeys at Christ's word; JFB adds that letting down again was a mere act of faith, since as a fisherman Peter knew how hopeless it was. — Matthew Henry; John Gill; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: They enclosed a great multitude of fishes, and their net brake. Gill explains the net was beginning to break under weight and number, yet not so as to lose the fish, gathered there by Christ's secret divine power; JFB likewise reads "was breaking." — John Gill; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: They beckoned to partners in the other ship, who came and filled both ships so that they began to sink. Gill identifies the partners as James and John, who lay at anchor near shore and had to be signalled by hand. — John Gill

8 COMMENTARY: Simon Peter fell at Jesus' knees, saying, Depart from me, for I am a sinful man, O Lord. Henry notes we must all, like Peter, own ourselves sinful men, yet beseech Christ not to depart, for woe if the Saviour departs from sinners; JFB adds Peter did not wish Christ to leave, but cried, in Isaiah's manner, that a sinner is not fit company for such glory. — Matthew Henry; John Gill; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: He was astonished, and all with him, at the draught taken. Gill notes the astonishment covered Andrew, the servants, and all in the boat, at a catch the like of which they had never seen. — John Gill

10 COMMENTARY: James and John, Zebedee's sons and Simon's partners, were likewise astonished; and Jesus said, Fear not, from henceforth thou shalt catch men. JFB notes "fear not" shows the Lord read Peter's speech aright, and "from henceforth" marks a new stage: the net of the gospel will draw men out of sin's depths into life. — John Gill; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: They brought their ships to land, forsook all, and followed him. Henry stresses the point: they forsook all when their calling prospered, and to quit riches for Christ when riches increase is thankworthy; JFB notes they had forsaken all before, do it again now, and after Pentecost finally abandon the trade. — Matthew Henry; John Gill; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: In a certain city a man full of leprosy fell on his face, beseeching, Lord, if thou wilt, thou canst make me clean. Henry notes he had the distemper in a high degree, representing our natural pollution by sin, with no soundness from crown to sole; Gill draws the same likeness at length. — Matthew Henry; John Gill

13 COMMENTARY: He put forth his hand, touched him, and said, I will: be thou clean; immediately the leprosy departed. Henry notes strong confidence and deep humility meet in the leper's words, and the answer to any sinner who doubts whether Christ will look on such a one is yes, he will. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: He charged him to tell no man, but go, show himself to the priest, and offer for cleansing as Moses commanded, for a testimony to them. Obedience to the law's testimony accompanies the healing, rather than replacing it. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: So much the more went fame abroad, and multitudes came to hear and be healed of infirmities. Henry's application follows: being saved from guilt and power of sin, let us spread abroad Christ's fame and bring others to hear and be healed. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: He withdrew into the wilderness and prayed. After fame and crowds, solitude and prayer are his retreat, not applause. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: As he taught, Pharisees and doctors of the law sat by, come from every town of Galilee, Judaea, and Jerusalem, and the power of the Lord was present to heal. Henry warns how many sit in assemblies not under the word but by it, as a tale told, not a message sent to them; JFB notes their presence is the highest testimony yet to his growing influence. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Men brought on a bed a man taken with palsy, seeking to bring him in and lay him before Jesus. The bearers' determination is the story's engine: they will get their friend to Christ somehow. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Unable to enter because of the multitude, they went on the housetop and let him down through the tiling with his couch into the midst before Jesus. Henry teaches that in applying to Christ we must be pressing and urgent, which is evidence of faith, pleasing to Christ and prevailing with him; JFB notes the housetop was the flat roof. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: When he saw their faith, he said, Man, thy sins are forgiven thee. Henry's counsel is to desire pardon of sin more than any earthly blessing or life itself, and to believe Christ's power to forgive; then souls cheerfully arise and go where he pleases. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: The scribes and Pharisees reasoned, who is this speaking blasphemies? Who can forgive sins but God alone? Their theology is right about God alone forgiving, and wrong about who stands before them. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: Jesus perceived their thoughts and answered, what reason ye in your hearts? The question exposes reasoning that never became speech, showing he reads hearts as well as bodies. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: Whether is easier, to say, thy sins be forgiven thee, or to say, rise up and walk? The easier saying to utter is the harder to prove, and Jesus forces the choice into the open. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: That ye may know the Son of man has power on earth to forgive sins, he said to the palsy patient, arise, take up thy couch, and go to thine house. JFB quotes Bengel's sweet saying: the bed had borne the man; now the man shall bear the bed. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Immediately he rose before them, took up what he lay on, and departed to his own house, glorifying God. The visible cure vindicates the invisible pardon, and the healed man becomes a worshipper on the way home. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: All were amazed, glorified God, and were filled with fear, saying, we have seen strange things today. Amazement, praise, and fear together are the crowd's honest verdict. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: After these things he saw Levi, a publican, sitting at the receipt of custom, and said, Follow me. Henry calls it a wonder of grace that Christ would call a publican, and a wonder that the call was made so effectual. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: He left all, rose up, and followed him. Like the fishermen, Levi forsakes a prospering calling at a word, and the feast that follows is the fruit of that forsaking. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Levi made him a great feast in his house, with a great company of publicans and others sitting down with them. The new disciple's first act is hospitality that gathers his old company around Jesus. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: Their scribes and Pharisees murmured, why do ye eat and drink with publicans and sinners? JFB notes Luke's phrase "their scribes" shows he was writing for Gentiles; Henry notes Christ patiently bore the contradiction of sinners against himself and his disciples. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: They that are whole need not a physician, but they that are sick. The physician goes where sickness is; separation from sinners would defeat his errand. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance. Henry sums the section: it was a wonder of grace that he came to call sinners to repentance and assure them of pardon. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: They asked why John's disciples and the Pharisees' disciples fast often and pray, but his eat and drink. The question assumes all godliness looks alike, and Jesus will answer with a wedding, not a rule. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: Can ye make the children of the bridechamber fast while the bridegroom is with them? Fasting during a wedding would contradict the occasion itself. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: The days will come when the bridegroom shall be taken away, and then shall they fast in those days. Henry notes the Lord trains his people gradually for allotted trials, fixing his disciples' services according to their strength and standing. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: No man puts a piece of a new garment upon an old, else both the new makes a rent and the new piece agrees not with the old. Patching the old order with new cloth tears both. — Matthew Henry

37 COMMENTARY: No man puts new wine into old bottles, else the new wine bursts the bottles, is spilled, and the bottles perish. New life needs a vessel that can hold it. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: New wine must be put into new bottles, and both are preserved. The point is preservation of both: the new is not wasted, nor the vessel destroyed. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: No man having drunk old wine straightway desires new, for he says, the old is better. Taste formed by the old resists the new, even when the new is offered by the bridegroom himself. — Matthew Henry
