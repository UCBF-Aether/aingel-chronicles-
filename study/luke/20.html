Study Notes — Luke

Chapter 20

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: On one of those days, as he taught the people in the temple and preached the gospel, the chief priests and scribes came upon him with the elders. Teaching in the temple draws the authorities together. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: And spoke, saying, Tell us, by what authority doest thou these things? Or who is he that gave thee this authority? The question demands credentials for temple action and teaching. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: He answered, I will also ask you one thing, and answer me. Question is answered with a question, as Henry notes Christ answered with a plain question about John's baptism, which the common people could answer. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: The baptism of John, was it from heaven, or of men? The counter-question tests whether they will own what they know. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: They reasoned with themselves: If we say, From heaven, he will say, Why then believed ye him not? Saying from heaven convicts their unbelief. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: But if we say, Of men, all the people will stone us, for they be persuaded that John was a prophet. Saying of men risks stoning by a people persuaded John was a prophet. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: And they answered, that they could not tell whence it was. Henry notes those that bury the knowledge they have are justly denied further knowledge, and it was just for Christ to refuse account of his authority to those who knew John's baptism was from heaven yet would not believe or own it. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: And Jesus said, Neither tell I you by what authority I do these things. Refusal mirrors refusal: hidden knowledge forfeits further knowledge. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Then began he to speak to the people this parable: A certain man planted a vineyard, let it forth to husbandmen, and went into a far country for a long time. Henry notes the parable is spoken against those resolved not to own his authority, though its evidence was so full. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: At the season he sent a servant to the husbandmen, that they should give him of the vineyard's fruit, but the husbandmen beat him and sent him away empty. The first servant's mission ends in beating and emptiness. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Again he sent another servant, and they beat him also, entreated him shamefully, and sent him away empty. The second servant fares worse in shame, same in emptiness. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Again he sent a third, and they wounded him also, and cast him out. The third is wounded and cast out, escalating the pattern. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Then said the lord of the vineyard, What shall I do? I will send my beloved son: it may be they will reverence him when they see him. The beloved son is sent last, in hope of reverence. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: But when the husbandmen saw him, they reasoned, This is the heir: come, let us kill him, that the inheritance may be ours. The heir's identity becomes the motive for murder, not reverence. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: So they cast him out of the vineyard, and killed him. What therefore shall the lord of the vineyard do unto them? The parable's question is put to its hearers. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: He shall come and destroy these husbandmen, and shall give the vineyard to others. And when they heard it, they said, God forbid. Henry notes though they could not but own such punishment just for such sin, yet they could not bear to hear of it; sinners persevere in ways whose end they dread. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: He beheld them, and said, What is this then that is written, The stone which the builders rejected, the same is become the head of the corner? Rejection and headship meet in one stone. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Whosoever shall fall upon that stone shall be broken, but on whomsoever it shall fall, it will grind him to powder. Falling on the stone breaks; the stone falling grinds to powder. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: The chief priests and scribes the same hour sought to lay hands on him, and they feared the people, for they perceived he had spoken this parable against them. Perception is accurate, and fear of the people delays the hands. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: They watched him, and sent forth spies, feigning themselves just men, that they might take hold of his words, to deliver him unto the power and authority of the governor. Henry notes the craftiest designs against Christ cannot hide, and wisdom from above directs teachers of God's way truly to avoid snares. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: They asked, Master, we know thou sayest and teachest rightly, neither acceptest thou any person, but teachest the way of God truly. Flattery prefaces the trap. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: Is it lawful for us to give tribute unto Caesar, or no? The trap forces a choice between Rome and the people. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: But he perceived their craftiness, and said, Why tempt ye me? Craftiness is named before it is answered. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Show me a penny. Whose image and superscription has it? They answered, Caesar's. The coin in hand answers the question's premise. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: Render therefore unto Caesar the things which be Caesar's, and unto God the things which be God's. Henry notes wisdom from above teaches duty to God, rulers, and all men so clearly that opposers will have no evil to say. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: They could not take hold of his words before the people, marvelled at his answer, and held their peace. The trap closes without a catch, and marvel replaces accusation. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: Then came certain Sadducees, which deny there is any resurrection, and they asked him. A new party, with a new doctrine to undermine, brings a new case. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: Saying, Master, Moses wrote unto us, If a man's brother die, having a wife, and die without children, his brother should take his wife, and raise up seed unto his brother. Moses' levirate law is cited as the case's ground. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: There were therefore seven brethren, and the first took a wife, and died without children. The case begins its sevenfold chain. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: And the second took her to wife, and he died childless. The second repeats the first, without children. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: And the third took her, and in like manner the seven also, and they left no children, and died. All seven in turn leave no children and die. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: Last of all the woman died also. The woman's death completes the case, leaving seven husbands and one wife. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: Therefore in the resurrection whose wife of them is she? For seven had her to wife. Henry notes it is common for those designing to undermine a truth to load it with difficulties, and we wrong ourselves and Christ's truth when we form notions of the spirit world by this world of sense. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: And Jesus answering said, The children of this world marry, and are given in marriage. Marriage belongs to this world, the case's world. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: But they which shall be accounted worthy to obtain that world, and the resurrection from the dead, neither marry, nor are given in marriage. The other world is not this world continued, but a different order. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: Neither can they die any more, for they are equal unto the angels, and are children of God, being children of the resurrection. Henry notes believers shall obtain the blessed resurrection, and in perfection of holiness there will be no occasion for preservatives from sin. — Matthew Henry

37 COMMENTARY: Now that the dead are raised, even Moses showed at the bush, when he calls the Lord the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob. Moses' own bush theophany proves resurrection. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: For he is not a God of the dead, but of the living, for all live unto him. Henry notes God called himself the patriarchs' God all-sufficient and exceeding great Reward, which he never fully gave them in this world, so there must be another life fulfilling the promise. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: Then certain scribes answering said, Master, thou hast well said. The scribes commend the answer that silenced the Sadducees. — Matthew Henry

40 COMMENTARY: And after that they durst not ask him any question at all. Questions cease, not because they are satisfied, but because they dare not ask. — Matthew Henry

41 COMMENTARY: And he said unto them, How say they that Christ is David's son? The questioner becomes questioner again, about the Messiah's sonship. — Matthew Henry

42 COMMENTARY: And David himself says in the book of Psalms, The LORD said unto my Lord, Sit thou on my right hand. David's own psalm calls the Messiah Lord. — Matthew Henry

43 COMMENTARY: Till I make thine enemies thy footstool. The Lord sits until enemies become a footstool. — Matthew Henry

44 COMMENTARY: David therefore calls him Lord, how is he then his son? Henry notes Christ, as God, was David's Lord, but Christ, as man, was David's son; the scribes are silenced by a question concerning the Messiah. — Matthew Henry

45 COMMENTARY: Then in the audience of all the people he said unto his disciples, Beware of the scribes, which desire to walk in long robes, and love greetings in the markets, and the highest seats in synagogues, and the chief rooms at feasts. Beware is spoken publicly, before all the people. — Matthew Henry

46 COMMENTARY: Beware of the scribes, which desire to walk in long robes, and love greetings in markets, and highest seats in synagogues, and chief rooms at feasts. Robes, greetings, seats, and rooms: the love of display is itemised. — Matthew Henry

47 COMMENTARY: Which devour widows' houses, and for a show make long prayers: the same shall receive greater damnation. Henry notes the scribes would receive severest judgment for defrauding poor widows and abusing religion, particularly prayer, as a pretence for worldly plans; dissembled piety is double sin. — Matthew Henry
