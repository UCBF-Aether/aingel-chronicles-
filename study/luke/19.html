Study Notes — Luke

Chapter 19

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: And Jesus entered and passed through Jericho. The entry into Jericho sets the stage for its chief publican. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Behold, there was a man named Zacchaeus, chief among the publicans, and he was rich. Chief publican and rich: the most despised office at its highest rank. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: He sought to see Jesus who he was, and could not for the press, because he was little of stature. Desire meets two obstacles, crowd and stature. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: He ran before, and climbed up into a sycamore tree to see him, for he was to pass that way. Henry notes those who sincerely desire a sight of Christ, like Zacchaeus, will break through opposition and take pains to see him. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: When Jesus came to the place, he looked up, saw him, and said, Zacchaeus, make haste, and come down, for today I must abide at thy house. Henry notes Christ invited himself to Zacchaeus' house; wherever Christ comes he opens the heart and inclines it to receive him, and he that has a mind to know Christ shall be known of him. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: He made haste, came down, and received him joyfully. Henry notes those Christ calls must humble themselves and come down, and we may well receive him joyfully, who brings all good with him. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: When they saw it, they all murmured, saying, he was gone to be guest with a man that is a sinner. The crowd murmurs at the guest choice, as Pharisees murmured at eating with sinners. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: Zacchaeus stood, and said unto the Lord: Behold, Lord, the half of my goods I give to the poor, and if I have taken anything from any man by false accusation, I restore him fourfold. Henry notes Zacchaeus gave proofs publicly that he was become a true convert, not looking to be justified by works as the Pharisee, but showing by good works, through grace, the sincerity of faith and repentance. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: And Jesus said, This day is salvation come to this house, forsomuch as he also is a son of Abraham. Henry notes Zacchaeus is declared happy, now turned from sin to God, saved from guilt and power of sin, with all salvation's benefits his, for Christ is come to his house and where Christ comes he brings salvation. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: For the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost. Henry notes he came into this lost world to seek and save it, when there was no salvation in any other, seeking those that sought him not and asked not for him. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: As they heard these things, he added a parable, because he was nigh to Jerusalem, and because they thought the kingdom of God should immediately appear. The parable corrects imminent-kingdom expectation. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: A certain nobleman went into a far country to receive for himself a kingdom, and to return. The kingdom is received elsewhere and returned to, not seized on arrival. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: He called his ten servants, delivered them ten pounds, and said, Occupy till I come. Henry notes those called to Christ he furnishes with gifts needful for their business, and from those to whom he gives power he expects service; the pound seems to point out the gospel gift, the same to all who hear it. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: But his citizens hated him, and sent a message after him, saying, We will not have this man to reign over us. Rejection travels after the nobleman, even into the far country. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: When he was returned, having received the kingdom, he commanded the servants to be called, to whom he had given the money, that he might know how much every man had gained by trading. Return brings reckoning, and gain by trading is the measure. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: Then came the first, saying, Lord, thy pound has gained ten pounds. Tenfold gain from one pound is reported as the pound's doing, not the servant's. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: And he said, Well, thou good servant: because thou hast been faithful in a very little, have thou authority over ten cities. Faithfulness in very little is rewarded with authority over ten cities. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: And the second came, saying, Lord, thy pound has gained five pounds. Fivefold gain is the second servant's report. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: And he said likewise to him, Be thou also over five cities. Reward matches gain: five cities for five pounds. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: And another came, saying, Lord, behold, here is thy pound, which I have kept laid up in a napkin. Preservation, not trading, is the third servant's account. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: For I feared thee, because thou art an austere man: thou takest up that thou laidst not down, and reapest that thou didst not sow. Fear of an austere master is pleaded as the reason for inaction. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: And he says, Out of thine own mouth will I judge thee, thou wicked servant. Thou knewest that I was an austere man, taking up that I laid not down, and reaping that I did not sow. The servant's own description becomes his judge. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: Wherefore then gavest not thou my money into the bank, that at my coming I might have required mine own with usury? Even the fearful servant could have banked the pound; inaction has no excuse on its own terms. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: And he said unto them that stood by, Take from him the pound, and give it to him that has ten pounds. The unused pound is transferred to the most fruitful servant. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: And they said unto him, Lord, he has ten pounds. The bystanders protest the apparent surplus, and the next verse answers them. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: For I say unto you, that unto every one which has shall be given, and from him that has not, even that he has shall be taken away from him. Henry notes the account resembles the talents parable, and the punishment of avowed enemies and false professors is shown; having, in this sense, means having used. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: But those mine enemies, which would not that I should reign over them, bring hither, and slay them before me. The citizens' rejection ends in judgment before the returned king. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: When he had thus spoken, he went before, ascending up to Jerusalem. Parable and prophecy give way to the ascent itself. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: When he was come nigh to Bethphage and Bethany, at the mount called Olivet, he sent two of his disciples. The entry is prepared from the Mount of Olives. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: Go into the village over against you, in which at your entering ye shall find a colt tied, whereon yet never man sat: loose him, and bring him hither. The colt's details — tied, never ridden — mark the preparation as specific, not improvised. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: If any man ask, Why do ye loose him? Thus shall ye say, Because the Lord has need of him. Henry notes Christ has dominion over all creatures and may use them as he pleases, and has all men's hearts under his eye and in his hand. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: And they that were sent went their way, and found even as he had said unto them. The word proves exact on arrival. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: As they were loosing the colt, the owners said, Why loose ye the colt? The predicted question is asked by the owners. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: And they said, The Lord has need of him. The predicted answer suffices, and the colt is released. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: They brought him to Jesus, cast their garments upon the colt, and set Jesus thereon. Garments become a saddle for the King. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: As he went, they spread their clothes in the way. The road itself is carpeted with garments. — Matthew Henry

37 COMMENTARY: When he was come nigh, even now at the descent of Olivet, the whole multitude of disciples began to rejoice and praise God with a loud voice for all the mighty works they had seen. The descent becomes a loud praise for mighty works seen. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: Saying, Blessed be the King that comes in the name of the Lord: peace in heaven, and glory in the highest. The King is blessed in the Lord's name, with peace in heaven and glory in the highest. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: Some Pharisees from among the multitude said, Master, rebuke thy disciples. Henry notes Christ's triumphs and disciples' joyful praises vex proud Pharisees, enemies to him and his kingdom. — Matthew Henry

40 COMMENTARY: And he answered, I tell you that, if these should hold their peace, the stones would immediately cry out. Henry notes Pharisees would silence Christ's praises, but they cannot, for as God can raise children to Abraham from stones, so he can bring praise out of children's mouths; and Christ, despising proud contempt, accepts humble praises. — Matthew Henry

41 COMMENTARY: When he was come near, he beheld the city, and wept over it. Triumph turns to tears at the sight of the city. — Matthew Henry

42 COMMENTARY: Saying, If thou hadst known, even thou, at least in this thy day, the things which belong unto thy peace! But now they are hid from thine eyes. Henry asks who can behold Jesus weeping over his murderers' coming miseries without seeing that God's likeness in believers consists much in goodwill and compassion. — Matthew Henry

43 COMMENTARY: For the days shall come upon thee, that thine enemies shall cast a trench about thee, and compass thee round, and keep thee in on every side. Siege is foretold in detail: trench, encirclement, confinement. — Matthew Henry

44 COMMENTARY: And shall lay thee even with the ground, and thy children within thee, and shall not leave in thee one stone upon another, because thou knewest not the time of thy visitation. Henry warns that though Jesus wept over Jerusalem, he executed awful vengeance upon it, delighting not in a sinner's death, yet surely bringing his threatenings on those who neglect salvation. — Matthew Henry

45 COMMENTARY: He went into the temple, and began to cast out them that sold therein, and them that bought. Tears over the city precede cleansing of its temple. — Matthew Henry

46 COMMENTARY: Saying, It is written, My house is the house of prayer, but ye have made it a den of thieves. The house of prayer has become a den of thieves, and Scripture names both. — Matthew Henry

47 COMMENTARY: He taught daily in the temple. But the chief priests, scribes, and chief of the people sought to destroy him. Daily teaching and daily plotting run side by side. — Matthew Henry

48 COMMENTARY: And could not find what they might do, for all the people were very attentive to hear him. The people's attentiveness frustrates the plot, for a time. — Matthew Henry
