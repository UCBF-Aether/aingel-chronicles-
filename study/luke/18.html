Study Notes — Luke

Chapter 18

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: He spoke a parable to this end, that men ought always to pray, and not to faint. Henry notes all God's people are praying people, and earnest steadiness in prayer for spiritual mercies is taught here. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Saying, There was in a city a judge, which feared not God, neither regarded man. The judge is defined by two negatives: no fear of God, no regard for man. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: And there was a widow in that city, and she came unto him, saying, Avenge me of mine adversary. The widow has only one plea, repeated: avenge me. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: He would not for a while, but afterward he said within himself, Though I fear not God, nor regard man. Refusal lasts a while, then self-talk begins. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Yet because this widow troubles me, I will avenge her, lest by her continual coming she weary me. The unjust judge acts to stop being troubled, not to do justice. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: And the Lord said, Hear what the unjust judge says. The judge's own words become the argument. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: And shall not God avenge his own elect, which cry day and night unto him, though he bear long with them? Henry notes the widow's earnestness prevailed even with the unjust judge, and our earnest prayer is pleasing to God, who will avenge his elect, though he bears long. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: I tell you that he will avenge them speedily. Nevertheless when the Son of man comes, shall he find faith on the earth? Speedy vengeance is promised, and the closing question doubts whether faith will endure to see it; Henry notes even to the end there will be ground for the same complaint of weakness of faith. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: He spoke this parable unto certain which trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others. The parable's target is named before it begins: self-trust plus contempt. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Two men went up into the temple to pray, the one a Pharisee, and the other a publican. Temple prayer brings opposites side by side. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: The Pharisee stood and prayed thus with himself, God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men are, extortioners, unjust, adulterers, or even as this publican. Henry notes what the Pharisee said shows he trusted himself that he was righteous, and though free from gross sins, commendable in itself, he was full of himself and his own goodness. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: I fast twice in the week, I give tithes of all that I possess. Fasting and tithing are listed as credentials, not as gifts. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: And the publican, standing afar off, would not lift up so much as his eyes unto heaven, but smote upon his breast, saying, God be merciful to me a sinner. Henry notes the publican's address was full of humility, repentance, and desire toward God; his prayer was short but to the purpose. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: I tell you, this man went down to his house justified rather than the other, for every one that exalts himself shall be abased, and he that humbles himself shall be exalted. Henry notes the favour and grace of God the Pharisee did not think worth asking, while the publican owned himself a sinner by nature and practice, guilty before God, depending only on mercy; God's glory is to resist the proud and give grace to the humble, and the self-condemned, not the self-righteous, are justified. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: And they brought unto him also infants, that he would touch them, but when his disciples saw it, they rebuked them. Infants, too little to do service, are brought, and disciples rebuke the bringing. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: But Jesus called them unto him, and said, Suffer little children to come unto me, and forbid them not, for of such is the kingdom of God. Henry notes none are too little or too young to be brought to Christ, who knows how to show kindness to those not capable of doing him service. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Verily, whosoever shall not receive the kingdom of God as a little child shall in no wise enter therein. Henry notes we must receive the kingdom as children, not by purchase, and call it our Father's gift. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: And a certain ruler asked him, Good Master, what shall I do to inherit eternal life? The ruler's question is earnest and well-phrased, yet it will be tested by one thing. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: And Jesus said, Why callest thou me good? None is good, save one, that is, God. The title 'good' is turned back on the questioner before the answer. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Thou knowest the commandments: Do not commit adultery, do not kill, do not steal, do not bear false witness, honour thy father and mother. The commandments are rehearsed as the ruler's own ground. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: And he said, All these have I kept from my youth up. Youth-long obedience is claimed without hesitation. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: When Jesus heard these things, he said, Yet lackest thou one thing: sell all thou hast, and distribute unto the poor, and thou shalt have treasure in heaven, and come, follow me. Henry notes many have much commendable in them, yet perish for lack of one thing; this ruler could not bear Christ's terms, which would part him from his estate. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: When he heard this, he was very sorrowful, for he was very rich. Sorrow, not anger, is the ruler's response, and riches are named as its cause. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: When Jesus saw that he was very sorrowful, he said, How hardly shall they that have riches enter into the kingdom of God! Hardness of entry is stated as a general rule from one case. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: For it is easier for a camel to go through a needle's eye, than for a rich man to enter into the kingdom of God. The camel and needle make hardness into impossibility. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: And they that heard it said, Who then can be saved? If riches bar the way, the hearers ask who can pass at all. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: And he said, The things which are impossible with men are possible with God. Impossibility with men is not impossibility with God. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: Then Peter said, Lo, we have left all, and followed thee. Peter's claim invites the accounting Henry warns against: speaking too much of what we have left and lost. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Verily, there is no man that has left house, parents, brethren, wife, or children, for the kingdom of God's sake, who shall not receive manifold more in this present time, and in the world to come life everlasting. The promise is manifold now and everlasting then, yet Henry urges shame at any regret or difficulty in leaving, rather than boasting of it. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: Who shall not receive manifold more in this present time, and in the world to come life everlasting. The twofold reward closes Peter's question: present manifold, future everlasting. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: Then he took unto him the twelve, and said, Behold, we go up to Jerusalem, and all things written by the prophets concerning the Son of man shall be accomplished. Henry notes the Spirit of Christ in Old Testament prophets testified beforehand his sufferings and the glory that should follow. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: For he shall be delivered unto the Gentiles, and shall be mocked, and spitefully entreated, and spitted on. The prophecy names mockery, spite, and spitting before death. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: And they shall scourge him, and put him to death, and the third day he shall rise again. Scourging, death, and third-day rising are stated together, yet only the suffering part will stick. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: And they understood none of these things, and this saying was hid from them, neither knew they the things spoken. Henry notes their prejudices were so strong they would not understand literally, intent on glory prophecies and overlooking suffering ones, reading Bibles by halves for smooth things, as self-love and worldly desire close understandings. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: As he was come nigh unto Jericho, a certain blind man sat by the wayside begging. Henry notes this poor blind man, not only blind but poor, is the fitter emblem of mankind which Christ came to heal and save. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: Hearing the multitude pass by, he asked what it meant. The blind man asks about a sound he cannot see. — Matthew Henry

37 COMMENTARY: And they told him, that Jesus of Nazareth passes by. The answer names Jesus, and the blind man acts on the name. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: And he cried, Jesus, thou son of David, have mercy on me. The cry names sonship and mercy in one breath. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: And they which went before rebuked him, that he should hold his peace, but he cried so much the more, Thou son of David, have mercy on me. Rebuke increases the cry, as Henry notes prayer of faith, guided by Christ's encouraging promises, shall not be in vain. — Matthew Henry

40 COMMENTARY: And Jesus stood, and commanded him to be brought unto him, and when he was come near, he asked him, What wilt thou that I shall do unto thee? The crowd's rebuked beggar is brought near and asked what he wants. — Matthew Henry

41 COMMENTARY: Saying, What wilt thou that I shall do unto thee? And he said, Lord, that I may receive my sight. The question is repeated in the text's own form, and the answer is single: sight. — Matthew Henry

42 COMMENTARY: And Jesus said, Receive thy sight: thy faith has saved thee. Sight is given as salvation, not as mere repair. — Matthew Henry

43 COMMENTARY: And immediately he received his sight, and followed him, glorifying God, and all the people, when they saw it, gave praise unto God. Henry notes grace ought to be thankfully acknowledged to God's glory, it is for God's glory if we follow Jesus as those whose eyes are opened, and we must praise God for mercies to others as well as ourselves. — Matthew Henry
