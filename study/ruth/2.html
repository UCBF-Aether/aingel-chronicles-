Study Notes — Ruth

Chapter 2

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Naomi had a kinsman of her husband's, Boaz, a mighty man of wealth from Elimelech's family. Henry's chapter outline makes him the providential figure whose field Ruth will glean. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

2 COMMENTARY: Ruth herself proposed to go and glean after him in whose sight she should find grace. Henry praises her humility and industry: she cheerfully stooped to her poor lot and would not eat the bread of idleness. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

3 COMMENTARY: Ruth's hap was to light on a part of the field belonging to Boaz. Henry notes that God wisely orders what seem to us small, uncertain events to serve his glory and the good of his people. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

4 COMMENTARY: Boaz came from Bethlehem greeting his reapers, "The LORD be with you," and they answered, "The LORD bless thee." Henry holds this pious, kind language up as proof that godly persons were still in Israel, and that true religion makes kind masters and faithful servants. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

5 COMMENTARY: Boaz asked his servant set over the reapers whose damsel this was. Henry notes that a good master inquires concerning strangers in his field and orders them to be well treated, taking care his servants do no wrong. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

6 COMMENTARY: The servant answered that she was the Moabitish damsel who came back with Naomi from Moab. Henry's theme here is the godly household: Boaz's men answered courteously and truthfully rather than cursing their master behind his back. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

7 COMMENTARY: Ruth had asked leave to glean and gather after the reapers, and had worked from morning until now, resting only a little in the house. Henry presents this as diligence that promises well for this world and the other: no honest labour is a reproach. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

8 COMMENTARY: Boaz told Ruth to abide in his field, gleaning only there and keeping close to his maidens. Henry reads this as the kindness of a master who protects the stranger under his care. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

9 COMMENTARY: Boaz assured Ruth she might drink from what the young men drew, having charged them not to touch her. Henry notes that masters must see not only that they do no hurt themselves, but that those under them do no wrong. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

10 COMMENTARY: Ruth fell on her face and asked why she, a stranger, had found grace in his sight. Henry observes that she humbly owned herself unworthy of favours, having been born and brought up a heathen, and that we should likewise think humbly of ourselves. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

11 COMMENTARY: Boaz answered that all Ruth had done for her mother-in-law since her husband's death had been fully shown to him. Henry sees in Boaz's kindness to Ruth a picture of the Lord Jesus Christ's kindness to poor sinners. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

12 COMMENTARY: Boaz prayed that the LORD would recompense Ruth's work and give her a full reward, since she had come to trust under his wings. Henry's passage stresses mutual kindness expressed in praying for one another. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

13 COMMENTARY: Ruth thanked Boaz for comforting her and speaking friendly to her handmaid, though she was not like one of his handmaidens. Henry notes her humble, grateful spirit in receiving kindness as a favour, not demanding it as a debt. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

14 COMMENTARY: At mealtime Boaz invited Ruth to eat of the bread and dip her morsel in the vinegar, and she ate and was sufficed, leaving some over. Henry treats this generous table as part of Boaz's ordered kindness to the stranger. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

15 COMMENTARY: When Ruth rose to glean, Boaz commanded his young men to let her glean even among the sheaves and not reproach her. Henry's lesson is that true religion teaches a man to behave aright in every state and condition. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

16 COMMENTARY: Boaz further ordered them to let fall handfuls of purpose for her and leave them for her to glean, rebuking none. Henry presents this deliberate generosity as the pattern of a kind master toward the poor. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

17 COMMENTARY: Ruth gleaned until evening, beat out what she had gathered, and it was about an ephah of barley. Henry remarks that it encourages industry that in all labour, even gleaning, there is profit, and Ruth was careful to secure what she had gained. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

18 COMMENTARY: Ruth carried her gleanings into the city, showed Naomi what she had gleaned, and gave her what she had reserved after being sufficed. Henry praises her regard for her mother and her care not to lose what she had wrought. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

19 COMMENTARY: Naomi asked where Ruth had gleaned and blessed him who had taken knowledge of her. Henry likens Naomi's questioning to a parent examining a child — to commend if well done, and with mildness to caution otherwise. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

20 COMMENTARY: Ruth told Naomi she had wrought with Boaz, and Naomi blessed the LORD, who had not left off his kindness to the living and the dead. Henry's nightly question applies here: "Where have I gleaned to-day?" and what improvement has been made in grace. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

21 COMMENTARY: Naomi told Ruth that Boaz was near of kin to them, one of their next kinsmen. Henry notes this kinship as the providential opening toward the rest and preferment that would follow. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

22 COMMENTARY: Boaz had also told Ruth to keep fast by his young men until the harvest was ended. Henry stresses Ruth's dutiful observance of direction: when the LORD deals bountifully, we should not be found in any other field. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

23 COMMENTARY: So Ruth kept fast by Boaz's maidens to glean until barley and wheat harvest ended, and dwelt with her mother-in-law. Henry contrasts Ruth, who kept at home helping her mother, with Dinah, whose going out to see the daughters of the land ended in disgrace; Ruth's humility and industry ended in preferment. — Matthew Henry (Concise)
