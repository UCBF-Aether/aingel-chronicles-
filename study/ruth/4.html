Study Notes — Ruth

Chapter 4

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Boaz went up to the gate and sat down, and called the kinsman of whom he had spoken to turn aside and sit down. Henry notes the whole matter was settled in the regular, legal manner under Moses' laws about inheritances. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

2 COMMENTARY: Boaz took ten men of the elders of the city and had them sit down as witnesses. Henry's emphasis is fair and open dealing in all matters of contract and trade, which all who would approve themselves true Israelites without guile must make conscience of. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

3 COMMENTARY: Boaz told the kinsman that Naomi, returned from Moab, was selling a parcel of land that had been their brother Elimelech's. Henry treats this as the opening of the legal bargain over the inheritance. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

4 COMMENTARY: Boaz invited the kinsman to redeem the land before the inhabitants and elders, or declare if he would not, since Boaz was next after him. Henry notes the settlement proceeded openly, in the regular and legal manner. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

5 COMMENTARY: The kinsman first said he would redeem it, but when Boaz added that buying the field from Naomi meant also buying it of Ruth the Moabitess, to raise up the name of the dead upon his inheritance, the bargain's true conditions emerged. Henry observes that many are likewise shy of the great redemption when they count its cost. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

6 COMMENTARY: The kinsman then refused, saying he could not redeem it lest he mar his own inheritance, and told Boaz to redeem his right to himself. Henry compares him to those who have nothing to say against religion but will part with it for fear of marring their worldly inheritance. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

7 COMMENTARY: In former time in Israel, a man plucked off his shoe and gave it to his neighbour to confirm a redemption or exchange, and this was a testimony in Israel. Henry cites this as the legal custom by which the right was resigned to Boaz. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

8 COMMENTARY: So the kinsman drew off his shoe and said to Boaz, "Buy it for thee." Henry notes the right was thus resigned, and adds that honesty will be found the best policy. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

9 COMMENTARY: Boaz then declared before the elders and all the people that they were witnesses he had bought all that was Elimelech's, and Chilion's and Mahlon's, from Naomi's hand. Henry observes that men seize opportunities for increasing their estates, but few know the value of godliness. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

10 COMMENTARY: Boaz further declared he had purchased Ruth the Moabitess, Mahlon's widow, to be his wife, to raise up the name of the dead upon his inheritance. Henry contrasts Boaz with the kinsman: God honoured Boaz by bringing him into the line of the Messiah, while the fearful kinsman's name and family were forgotten. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

11 COMMENTARY: All the people at the gate and the elders said they were witnesses, and prayed the LORD would make Ruth like Rachel and Leah, who built the house of Israel, and that Boaz would do worthily in Ephratah and be famous in Bethlehem. Henry notes prayer to God attended the marriage. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

12 COMMENTARY: They prayed Boaz's house would be like the house of Pharez, whom Tamar bare to Judah, of the seed the LORD would give him of Ruth. Henry's theme is that prayer attended the marriage and praise would attend the birth. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

13 COMMENTARY: Boaz took Ruth, she became his wife, and the LORD gave her conception, and she bare a son. Henry notes that through this son thousands and myriads were born to God, and Ruth, a Gentile witness, became a lineal ancestor of Christ. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

14 COMMENTARY: The women said to Naomi, "Blessed be the LORD, which hath not left thee this day without a kinsman, that his name may be famous in Israel." Henry observes praise to God attended the birth of the child, as prayer had attended the marriage. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

15 COMMENTARY: They said the child would be a restorer of Naomi's life and a nourisher of her old age, since her daughter-in-law, who loved her and was better to her than seven sons, had born him. Henry presents Ruth as a witness to the Gentile world that God had not utterly forsaken them. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

16 COMMENTARY: Naomi took the child, laid it in her bosom, and became nurse to it. Henry's passage frames this as the happy close of Naomi's bitterness: the empty returner now nurses her redeemer-grandson. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

17 COMMENTARY: The women her neighbours gave the child a name, saying a son was born to Naomi, and they called his name Obed, the father of Jesse, the father of David. Henry notes here is the descent of David from Ruth. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

18 COMMENTARY: These are the generations of Pharez: Pharez begat Hezron. Henry closes the book by pointing from this genealogy to Bethlehem-Judah's greater wonder yet to come, when another child of the same race would draw princes and wise men with treasures to his feet. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

19 COMMENTARY: Hezron begat Ram, and Ram begat Amminadab. Henry traces this line forward as the descent of David from Ruth, leading toward the Messiah in whom all nations shall be blessed. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

20 COMMENTARY: Amminadab begat Nahshon, and Nahshon begat Salmon. Henry's closing note keeps the genealogy's purpose in view: it is the line through which Ruth became instrumental in the happiness of all who shall be saved. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

21 COMMENTARY: Salmon begat Boaz, and Boaz begat Obed. Henry notes Boaz's honour: God brought him into the line of the Messiah, unlike the kinsman who feared lessening himself and was forgotten. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

22 COMMENTARY: Obed begat Jesse, and Jesse begat David. Henry ends with the book's great horizon: in that Seed shall all the nations of the earth be blessed, and his name shall endure for ever. — Matthew Henry (Concise)
