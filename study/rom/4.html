Study Notes — Romans

Chapter 4

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: What shall we say that Abraham our father, as pertaining to the flesh, has found? Henry notes that Abraham is the test case for justification. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: If Abraham were justified by works, he would have something to glory in, but not before God. JFB stresses that glorying before men is not glorying before God. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: What does Scripture say? Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him for righteousness. Henry notes that faith, not labour, was reckoned as righteousness. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: To him who works, the reward is reckoned not of grace but of debt. JFB stresses that wages and gifts belong to opposite economies. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: To him who works not, but believes on him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is counted for righteousness. Henry observes the scandal and comfort of the phrase: God justifies the ungodly. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: David also describes the blessedness of the man to whom God imputes righteousness without works. JFB notes that David's psalm confirms Abraham's story. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Blessed are they whose iniquities are forgiven and whose sins are covered. Henry stresses that forgiveness is the first half of imputed blessedness. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Blessed is the man to whom the Lord will not impute sin. JFB notes that non-imputation of sin and imputation of righteousness are two sides of one gift. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Does this blessedness come on the circumcised only, or on the uncircumcised also? For faith was reckoned to Abraham for righteousness. Henry observes that the timing of Abraham's justification settles the question. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: How was it reckoned? When Abraham was in circumcision or in uncircumcision? Not in circumcision, but in uncircumcision. JFB stresses the chronology: justification preceded the rite by years. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Abraham received circumcision as a sign and seal of the righteousness of the faith he had while uncircumcised, that he might be father of all who believe though uncircumcised. Henry notes that a seal confirms a gift; it does not create it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: He is also father of the circumcised who not only are circumcised but walk in the steps of Abraham's faith. JFB stresses that descent is traced by faith, not by blood or rite. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: The promise that Abraham would be heir of the world came not through the law but through the righteousness of faith. Henry observes that a promise so wide could never rest on so narrow a ground as law-keeping. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: If those of the law are heirs, faith is made void and the promise made of no effect. JFB notes that law and promise as grounds of inheritance exclude each other. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: The law works wrath, for where there is no law there is no transgression. Henry stresses that law, by defining transgression, exposes man to wrath. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Therefore the promise is of faith, that it might be by grace, to the end it might be sure to all the seed, not only to those of the law but to those of Abraham's faith. JFB notes that grace makes the promise certain, because it does not depend on man's performance. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: As it is written, Abraham is father of many nations, before God whom he believed, who gives life to the dead and calls things that are not as though they were. Henry observes that Abraham's faith rested on God's life-giving power. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Against hope Abraham believed in hope, that he might become father of many nations, according to what was spoken: so shall your seed be. JFB notes that faith hopes when nature gives no ground for hope. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Being not weak in faith, he did not consider his own body now dead, being about a hundred years old, nor the deadness of Sarah's womb. Henry stresses that faith looks at God's promise, not at the body's decay. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: He staggered not at God's promise through unbelief, but was strong in faith, giving glory to God. JFB notes that unwavering trust itself glorifies God. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: He was fully persuaded that what God had promised, he was able also to perform. Henry observes that faith measures God's ability, not the difficulty. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Therefore it was imputed to him for righteousness. JFB notes the therefore: this kind of faith is what God counts as righteousness. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: It was not written for his sake alone that it was imputed to him. Henry notes that Scripture records Abraham's justification as a pattern, not a private privilege. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: It was written also for us, to whom it shall be imputed if we believe on him who raised Jesus our Lord from the dead. JFB stresses that the same faith, directed to the same God, receives the same reckoning. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Jesus was delivered for our offences and raised again for our justification. Henry notes that his death pays for sin and his resurrection seals the acquittal. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
