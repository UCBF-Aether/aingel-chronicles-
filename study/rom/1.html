Study Notes — Romans

Chapter 1

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Paul opens as a servant, not a master, of Christ, and as an apostle set apart for the gospel. Henry stresses that his authority rests on his calling, not on himself. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: The gospel was no new invention; God had promised it beforehand through the prophets in the holy Scriptures. JFB notes this anchors the good news in the Old Testament. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: The gospel centres on God's Son, descended from David according to the flesh. Henry observes that Christ's true humanity and royal lineage fulfil the promise to David. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The same Son was declared with power to be the Son of God by his resurrection from the dead. JFB stresses that the resurrection is the public proof of his divine sonship. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Through Christ Paul received grace and apostleship, to bring all nations to the obedience of faith. Henry notes that faith itself is the obedience the gospel requires. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: The Roman believers are among those called to belong to Jesus Christ. JFB reads this as a reminder that their standing comes from God's call, not their own merit. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Paul greets all the beloved of God in Rome as called saints, wishing them grace and peace from God and Christ. Henry notes that grace is the fountain and peace the fruit. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Paul thanks God that the Romans' faith is spoken of throughout the whole world. JFB observes that a church in the capital city could not be hidden, for good or ill. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: God is Paul's witness that he serves him with his spirit in the gospel of his Son, and prays for the Romans without ceasing. Henry stresses that true service is inward, not merely formal. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Paul continually asks that he might at last, by God's will, come to Rome. JFB notes that even an apostle submits his travel plans to providence. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Paul longs to see them to impart some spiritual gift, that they may be established. Henry reads this as mutual strengthening, not one-sided superiority. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: The comfort Paul seeks is mutual: he will be encouraged by their faith as they are by his. JFB notes that even apostles need the fellowship of ordinary believers. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Paul had often planned to visit, that he might have fruit among them as among other Gentiles, but was hindered. Henry observes that hindrances in service are consistent with God's overruling purpose. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Paul calls himself a debtor to Greeks and barbarians, wise and unwise alike. JFB stresses that the gospel is owed to every kind of person, not only the learned. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: So far as it depends on him, Paul is ready to preach the gospel at Rome also. Henry notes that readiness, not success, is the servant's duty. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Paul is not ashamed of the gospel, for it is God's power for salvation to everyone who believes, Jew first and also Greek. JFB stresses that its apparent weakness hides divine power. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: In the gospel God's righteousness is revealed from faith to faith, as it is written that the just shall live by faith. Henry notes that this righteousness is received, not earned. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: God's wrath is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men who hold the truth in unrighteousness. JFB stresses that wrath is God's settled opposition to sin, not mere temper. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: What may be known of God is plain to men, because God has shown it to them. Henry observes that ignorance of God is therefore inexcusable, not innocent. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: God's invisible attributes, his eternal power and Godhead, are clearly seen in the things he made, so men are without excuse. JFB notes that creation preaches God's existence and power to every beholder. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Though men knew God, they did not glorify him as God nor give thanks, but became vain in their reasonings and their foolish hearts were darkened. Henry stresses that ingratitude darkens the mind. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Professing themselves wise, they became fools. JFB notes the irony: the pride of human wisdom is itself the mark of folly. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: They changed the glory of the incorruptible God into images of corruptible man, birds, beasts, and creeping things. Henry observes that idolatry degrades both God and the worshipper. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Therefore God gave them up to uncleanness through the lusts of their hearts, to dishonour their own bodies. JFB stresses that being given up is itself a judgment, not mere permission. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: They changed God's truth into a lie and worshipped the creature rather than the Creator, who is blessed for ever. Henry notes that idolatry reverses the whole order of worship. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: For this cause God gave them up to vile affections, even women changing the natural use into what is against nature. JFB observes that sin against nature follows sin against God. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Men likewise, leaving the natural use of women, burned in lust toward one another, receiving in themselves the due penalty of their error. Henry notes that such sin carries its own shame and recompense. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Because they did not like to retain God in their knowledge, God gave them over to a reprobate mind, to do what is not fitting. JFB stresses that a mind that rejects God is left to its own corruption. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Being filled with unrighteousness, fornication, wickedness, covetousness, and maliciousness, they are full of envy, murder, debate, deceit, and malignity. Henry reads the catalogue as proof that sin is not one fault but a flood. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: They become whisperers, backbiters, haters of God, despiteful, proud, boasters, inventors of evil, and disobedient to parents. JFB notes that sins of speech and pride sit beside grosser sins in God's sight. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: They are without understanding, covenant-breakers, without natural affection, implacable, and unmerciful. Henry observes that rejecting God unravels even natural human bonds. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: Though they know God's judgment, that those who commit such things are worthy of death, they both do them and approve those who do them. JFB stresses that approving another's sin shares its guilt. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
