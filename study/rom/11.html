Study Notes — Romans

Chapter 11

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Has God cast away his people? God forbid, for Paul himself is an Israelite, of Abraham's seed, of Benjamin's tribe. JFB notes that Paul's own conversion proves God has not abandoned Israel. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: God has not cast away his people whom he foreknew. Do you not know what Scripture says of Elijah, how he pleads with God against Israel? Henry observes that foreknowledge, not merit, is the ground of God's keeping. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Lord, they have killed your prophets and dug down your altars, and I am left alone, and they seek my life. JFB notes that Elijah's complaint mistakes a remnant's survival for total apostasy. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: God's answer to him was, I have reserved seven thousand men who have not bowed the knee to Baal. Henry stresses that God always keeps a remnant, even when his servants cannot see one. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Even so at this present time there is a remnant according to the election of grace. JFB notes that the present remnant follows the same pattern as Elijah's day. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: If by grace, then it is no more of works; otherwise grace is no more grace. But if of works, then it is no more grace. Henry observes that grace and works as grounds of salvation exclude each other. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: What then? Israel has not obtained what it seeks, but the election has obtained it, and the rest were blinded. JFB notes that the same gospel hardens some and saves others, according to election. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: According as it is written, God has given them a spirit of slumber, eyes that they should not see, and ears that they should not hear, to this day. Henry stresses that judicial blindness follows wilful resistance. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: David says, let their table become a snare, a trap, a stumbling block, and a recompense to them. JFB notes that even blessings become snares when abused. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Let their eyes be darkened that they may not see, and bow down their back always. Henry observes that the psalm's curse describes the spiritual condition of unbelieving Israel. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Have they stumbled that they should fall? God forbid; but through their fall salvation has come to the Gentiles, to provoke them to jealousy. JFB stresses that Israel's stumble is not a final fall; it opens the door to Gentiles. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: If their fall is the riches of the world, and their diminishing the riches of the Gentiles, how much more their fullness? Henry notes the argument: if a fall enriched the world, a restoration will enrich it more. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Paul speaks to the Gentiles: as apostle of the Gentiles, he magnifies his office. JFB notes that Paul honours his Gentile mission precisely as a means to stir Israel. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: If by any means he may provoke to emulation his flesh and save some of them. Henry observes that Paul's hope for Israel works through Gentile blessing. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: If the casting away of them is the reconciling of the world, what shall their receiving be but life from the dead? JFB notes that Israel's restoration is pictured as a resurrection. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: If the firstfruit is holy, the lump is also holy, and if the root is holy, so are the branches. Henry observes that the patriarchal root consecrates the people sprung from it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: If some branches were broken off, and you, a wild olive, were grafted in among them and partake of the root's fatness, do not boast. JFB notes that Gentiles are grafted contrary to nature, by grace alone. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Do not boast against the branches; you do not bear the root, but the root bears you. Henry warns Gentile believers against pride toward broken-off Israel. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: You will say, branches were broken off that I might be grafted in. JFB notes the true statement becomes a false boast when it forgets grace. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Well, because of unbelief they were broken off, and you stand by faith; be not highminded, but fear. Henry observes that standing by faith is a reason for fear, not pride. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: If God spared not the natural branches, take heed lest he also spare not you. JFB stresses that privilege does not protect presumption. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Behold God's goodness and severity: on those who fell, severity; but toward you, goodness, if you continue in his goodness, otherwise you also shall be cut off. Henry notes that goodness and severity are two faces of one God. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: They also, if they do not abide in unbelief, shall be grafted in, for God is able to graft them in again. JFB stresses that Israel's restoration is possible and promised. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: If you, cut from a wild olive, were grafted contrary to nature into a good olive, how much more shall the natural branches be grafted into their own olive? Henry observes that restoration is more natural than the Gentiles' grafting was. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Paul would not have the brethren ignorant of this mystery: blindness in part has happened to Israel until the fullness of the Gentiles comes in. JFB notes the mystery's limit: Israel's blindness is partial and temporary. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: So all Israel shall be saved, as it is written, there shall come out of Zion the Deliverer, who shall turn away ungodliness from Jacob. Henry reads this as a future turning of the Jewish people to their Messiah. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: This is God's covenant with them, when he shall take away their sins. JFB notes that the covenant's substance is forgiveness, not national privilege alone. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: As concerning the gospel they are enemies for your sakes, but as touching election they are beloved for the fathers' sakes. Henry observes that Israel is at once enemy and beloved, in different respects. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: God's gifts and calling are without repentance. JFB stresses that God does not revoke what he has given and called. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: As you in times past did not believe God, yet have now obtained mercy through their unbelief, so these also have not believed, that through your mercy they may obtain mercy. Henry notes the exchange: Gentile mercy came through Jewish unbelief, and Jewish mercy will come through Gentile mercy. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: Even so have these also now not believed, that through your mercy they also may obtain mercy. JFB stresses the symmetry of God's mercy to both peoples. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: God has concluded them all in unbelief, that he might have mercy upon all. Henry observes that universal unbelief is the ground for universal mercy, not for despair. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: O the depth of the riches both of God's wisdom and knowledge! How unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past finding out! JFB notes that the chapter ends in doxology, because God's ways exceed tracing. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: Who has known the Lord's mind, or who has been his counsellor? Henry observes that no creature can advise the Creator. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: Who has first given to him, that it shall be recompensed to him again? JFB notes that God is debtor to no one; every gift originates with him. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: Of him, and through him, and to him, are all things; to whom be glory for ever. Henry stresses that all things begin in God, continue through God, and return to God. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
