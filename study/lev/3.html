Study Notes — Leviticus

Chapter 3

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: A peace offering is a voluntary tribute of gratitude, eucharistic for benefits already received or votive for benefits prayed for, and because it is festive either a male or a female without blemish may serve. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: The offerer lays a hand on the victim's head, kills it at the tabernacle door, and the priests sprinkle its blood round about the altar, following the same significant act as in the burnt offering. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Unlike the burnt offering, which is wholly consumed, only the fat of the peace offering is burnt, with a small part for the priests and the rest for the offerer and his friends, forming a sacred feast shared by the LORD, His priests, and His people. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The kidneys with their fat and the caul above the liver are taken away with the inward fat, as the choicest parts reserved for burning on the altar. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: The priests burn these fat portions on the altar upon the burnt sacrifice, so that the peace offering's sweet savour rises together with the whole burnt offering. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: A flock peace offering follows the herd pattern, male or female without blemish being equally acceptable, since both are good for food and the offering is festive in character. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: A lamb brought as a peace offering is presented before the LORD under the same rule, the kind of animal varying with the offerer's circumstances rather than with the meaning of the rite. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: The lamb is killed before the tabernacle with the hand laid on its head, and Aaron's sons sprinkle its blood round about the altar, as with the herd offering. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: From the sheep the whole rump is taken off hard by the backbone together with the inward fat, a provision explained by the broad-tailed Eastern sheep, whose tails were large, fat-rich, and prized. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: The kidneys with their fat and the caul above the liver are removed with the rest of the inward fat, the same choice portions being reserved for the altar. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: The priest burns these portions on the altar as the food of the offering made by fire, while the remainder becomes the sacred feast of offerer and friends. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: A goat may equally be a peace offering, and whichever of the three animals is chosen, the same general directions govern the ceremony. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: The goat is killed before the tabernacle with the hand laid on its head, and the sons of Aaron sprinkle its blood round about the altar. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: From the goat, as from herd and flock, the fat covering and lying upon the inwards is offered by fire, the fat alone being the LORD's portion from the sacrifice. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: The kidneys with their fat and the caul above the liver are taken away with the inward fat, completing the altar portion of the goat offering. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: The priest burns these portions on the altar for a sweet savour, a reminder that all the fat is the LORD's, even though the rest of the animal is shared as food. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: The standing statute forbidding fat and blood rests on this definition of the reserved fat, while the blood prohibition guards reverence for the Messiah who would shed His blood as an atoning sacrifice. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
