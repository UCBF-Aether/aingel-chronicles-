Study Notes — Leviticus

Chapter 1

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: With the tabernacle completed, the rest of the law is announced to Moses by an audible voice from the divine glory over the mercy seat, rather than publicly from Sinai or privately on its summit. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Where the matter is spiritual, “children of Israel” embraces all the tribes, not the Levites alone. The offerings directed here are voluntary freewill offerings, over and above the standing obligations that could not be commuted. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: A burnt sacrifice is wholly consumed on the altar, with no part eaten by priest or offerer, signifying the offerer's whole self-dedication, body and soul. The animal must be a male without blemish, offered willingly at the door of the tabernacle. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Laying a hand on the victim's head both devotes the animal to God and confesses sin, praying that the offerer's guilt and its punishment might be transferred to the victim, so that it may be an acceptable atonement. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: For a voluntary sacrifice the offerer, not the priest, kills the bullock, on the north side of the altar. The priests sprinkle the blood, because the blood, as the life, is the essential part of the sacrifice, and its sprinkling applies the atonement and makes the offerer acceptable. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Flaying the victim and cutting it into pieces is part of the same ceremony: once the skin is stripped off and the carcass cut up, the pieces are disposed on the altar in the way best fitted for their being consumed by the fire. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Aaron's sons, the priests, put fire on the altar and lay the wood in order, preparing the altar for the whole burnt offering to be consumed upon it. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: The pieces, with the head and the fat, are laid in order on the wood over the fire. The fat named here is especially that about the kidneys, called suet. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Washing the inwards and legs in water symbolises the inward purity and the holy walk that become acceptable worshippers. The “sweet savour” expresses the offerer's piety, and is especially a sacrificial type of Christ. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: One who cannot afford a bullock may offer a ram or a he-goat from the flocks, with the same ceremonies observed in the act of offering. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: The flock offering follows the herd pattern: it is killed on the north side of the altar before the LORD, and Aaron's sons sprinkle its blood round about upon the altar. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: As with the herd offering, the victim is cut into pieces and, with its head and fat, laid in order on the wood that is on the altar fire. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: The inwards and legs are washed with water and the whole is burnt on the altar, a burnt sacrifice of a sweet savour, just as in the herd offering. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: The offering of fowls is appointed for the poor. The dove is chosen for its gentle nature and cleanly habits, while fiercer or grosser fowls are rejected, and the birds are offered in pairs, turtledoves or young pigeons according to season. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Because the bird is so small there is no blood for waste, so the priest prepares it at the altar itself, wringing off its head, burning it on the altar, and wringing out its blood at the side of the altar. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: The crop with its feathers is plucked away and cast beside the altar on the east side, by the place of the ashes, removing the refuse before the bird itself is burnt. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: The bird is cleft by its wings but not divided asunder, then burnt on the altar on the wood over the fire, a whole burnt sacrifice of a sweet savour unto the LORD. Throughout the varying scale of these offerings, the disposition of the offerer is what is looked to, not the costliness of the gift. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
