Study Notes — Leviticus

Chapter 11

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: These laws are addressed to both Moses and Aaron, civil and ecclesiastical rulers, indicating their twofold religious and sanitary purpose. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Israel is told which beasts may be eaten, the distinction of meats serving above all to keep them a distinct and peculiar people, preventing familiarity with neighbouring peoples' idolatries and vices. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Only beasts that both part the hoof, being cloven-footed, and chew the cud may be eaten, since ruminants digest food more fully and pass off poisonous properties through the salivary glands, while the parted hoof discharges foetid matter between the toes. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The camel is excluded because, though its foot divides to a certain extent into two large parts, the division is not complete, its toes resting on an elastic pad, and its flesh is tough as a beast of burden; its prohibition also keeps Israel apart from Ishmael's descendants. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: The coney, not the rabbit but the hyrax of the rock clefts, is unclean because it only appears to chew the cud and does not divide the hoof. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: The hare is unclean for the same reason, only appearing to ruminate by working its jaws, not being cloven-footed, and being subject to vermin and poisonous plants eaten to expel them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: The swine, though it divides the hoof, does not chew the cud, lacking that natural provision for purifying its system, and in hot climates its flesh is liable to produce leprosy, scurvy, and eruptions; its prohibition also guards against neighbouring idolatries. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Neither the flesh of these unclean beasts shall be eaten nor their carcasses touched, since both eating and contact defile under the distinction of meats. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: In the waters, whatever has fins and scales may be eaten, fins and scales being the means by which a fish's excrescences are carried off, as by perspiration in animals. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Whatever lacks fins and scales in seas or rivers, of all that move in the waters, is an abomination, since such creatures in hot climates cause malignant disorders and often prove mortal poison. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: These finless and scaleless creatures shall be an abomination, their flesh not eaten and their carcasses held in abhorrence, under the same sanitary and religious distinction. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Whatever has no fins nor scales in the waters is an abomination, a class including frogs, eels, and shellfish of all descriptions, many of which are suspected of a poisonous nature when plentifully eaten. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Among fowls, birds of prey and carrion-feeders are an abomination and shall not be eaten, no fewer than twenty species then known being listed, implying that unlisted birds, those subsisting on vegetable matter, are allowed. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: The vulture, here probably the kite or glede with its darting and hovering flight, and the kite after his kind are unclean, the prohibition extending to the whole species, since they devour carrion and putrefying flesh. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Every raven after his kind, including crow and pie, is unclean as a carrion bird within the birds-of-prey class. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: The owl, generally taken for the ostrich, the nighthawk with its nocturnal habits, the cuckoo, evidently another rapacious bird, and the hawk after his kind, including every falcon variety, are unclean. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: The little or horned owl, the cormorant supposed to be the gull, and the great owl, according to some the Egyptian ibis, are unclean within the same rapacious and aquatic grouping. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: The swan, frequenting marshy places and held sacred by Egyptians in heathen temples, the pelican with its pouch for catching and holding food, and the gier eagle, perhaps the kingfisher or the Egyptian vulture feeding on carrion, are unclean. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: The stork, declared unclean probably because it feeds on serpents and venomous reptiles, the heron, an aquatic bird of uncertain exact species, the lapwing or hoopoe, filthy from feeding on insects and worms, and the bat, noted for voracity and filthiness, are unclean. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: All winged creeping things going upon all four are an abomination, fowls here meaning all winged creatures, including insects, reptiles, and worms with more than four feet. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Flying creeping things with legs above their feet to leap may be eaten, a description that fits the locust exactly, which Arabs fry in olive oil and which, salted, dried, and fried, tastes not unlike red herrings. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: The locust, bald locust, beetle, and grasshopper after their kinds may be eaten, as lawful food within the leaping-insect exception. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: All other flying creeping things with four feet are an abomination, since they lack the leaping legs that mark the permitted locusts. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: For these creatures Israel shall be unclean, and whoever touches their carcass shall be unclean until evening, the touch rule enforcing cleanliness in a land where venomous reptiles creep into houses. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Whoever bears any part of such a carcass shall wash his clothes and be unclean until evening, the washing removing both ceremonial defilement and noxious matter. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Every beast that divides the hoof but is not cloven-footed nor chews the cud is unclean, a class including single-hoofed beasts like horses and asses and pawed beasts like lions and tigers. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Whatever goes upon its paws among beasts that go on all four is unclean, and whoever touches its carcass is unclean until evening, under the same pawed-beast rule. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: He who bears the carcass of a pawed beast shall wash his clothes and be unclean until evening, as with other unclean carcasses. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Among creeping things on the earth, the weasel, rather the mole, the mouse, placed among reptiles for its diminutive size, and the tortoise, a lizard with hard pointed tail scales, are unclean. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: The ferret, thought by some the newt or chameleon, the chameleon called warral by Arabs, the lizard, the snail, a sand lizard called chulca, and the mole, another lizard species, are unclean. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: These creeping things are unclean, and whoever touches them when dead shall be unclean until evening, a regulation that, though it often excluded people suddenly from society and worship, enforced attention to cleanliness against venom and noxious matter. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: Whatever a dead creeping thing falls upon, whether vessel of wood, raiment, skin, or sack, shall be unclean, since a carcass might fall inadvertently on clothes, skin bottles, or domestic articles. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: An earthen vessel into which one falls is wholly unclean and shall be broken, the porous vessel being unable to be cleansed of the defilement. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: All food on which such water comes shall be unclean, and all drink in such a vessel shall be unclean, extending the carcass defilement through water to food and drink. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: Everything on which part of their carcass falls, oven or ranges for pots, shall be unclean and broken down, the defilement reaching the very cooking vessels. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: A fountain or pit with plenty of water nevertheless remains clean, though what touches the carcass in it is unclean, since running water is not defiled as a vessel is. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

37 COMMENTARY: Sowing seed on which part of a carcass falls remains clean, dry seed being protected from the defilement that water would carry. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

38 COMMENTARY: If water has been put on the seed and a carcass falls on it, it shall be unclean, the water making the seed receptive to defilement as in the vessel rule. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

39 COMMENTARY: If a beast that may be eaten dies, whoever touches its carcass shall be unclean until evening, even though the animal itself was clean for food. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

40 COMMENTARY: He who eats of its carcass or bears it shall wash his clothes and be unclean until evening, the same washing and evening limit as for unclean carcasses. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

41 COMMENTARY: Every creeping thing on the earth is an abomination and shall not be eaten, restating the whole creeping class under the distinction of meats. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

42 COMMENTARY: Whatever goes on its belly, on all four, or on many feet among creeping things shall not be eaten, for they are an abomination. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

43 COMMENTARY: Israel shall not make themselves abominable or unclean with any creeping thing, lest they be defiled by them, the food distinction being a guard against defilement itself. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

44 COMMENTARY: For the LORD is their God, therefore they shall sanctify themselves and be holy, for He is holy, and shall not defile themselves with creeping things. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

45 COMMENTARY: The LORD who brought them up out of Egypt to be their God requires holiness, for He is holy, the exodus grounding the food laws in covenant identity. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

46 COMMENTARY: This is the law of beasts, fowl, water creatures, and creeping things, the whole chapter being one law of distinction. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

47 COMMENTARY: Its purpose is to make a difference between unclean and clean, and between the beast that may be eaten and that which may not, laws that probably gave divine sanction to ancient usages, some prohibited on physiological grounds and others for unwholesomeness in warm countries or connection with contemporary idolatry. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
