Study Notes — Esther

Chapter 1

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: In the days of Ahasuerus, who reigned from India to Ethiopia over a hundred and twenty-seven provinces, the story opens. Henry notes the vast empire is the stage: a decree from such a king touches the whole known world, and a deliverance there touches it too. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: When Ahasuerus sat on the throne of his kingdom in Shushan the palace, his power was settled. JFB notes Shushan was the winter capital, where the court and its records lay. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: In the third year of his reign he made a feast for all his princes and servants, the power of Persia and Media, nobles and princes of the provinces before him. Henry notes the feast gathers the empire's strength around one throne. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: For a hundred and eighty days he showed the riches of his glorious kingdom and the honour of his excellent majesty. JFB notes the long display was not one feast but successive entertainments of province after province, all to exhibit wealth. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: When these days expired, the king made a feast for all the people present in Shushan, great and small, seven days, in the court of the palace garden. Henry contrasts the two feasts: first the great, then all, so that none in the capital is left out of the king's bounty. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: White, green and blue hangings, fastened with fine linen and purple cords to silver rings and marble pillars, gold and silver beds on a pavement of red, blue, white and black marble, decked the court. JFB notes the colours and stones are Persian luxury at its height, the setting for what follows. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: They gave drink in diverse vessels of gold, and royal wine in abundance, according to the state of the king. Henry notes the abundance itself is the point: a king's feast is measured by excess. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: The drinking was according to law; none did compel, for the king appointed his officers to do according to every man's pleasure. JFB notes this Persian custom of free drinking is named because it explains the king's later merry command. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Also Vashti the queen made a feast for the women in the royal house belonging to Ahasuerus. Henry notes the separate women's feast follows the custom of the court, and keeps Vashti apart from the king's company until she is summoned. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: On the seventh day, when the king's heart was merry with wine, he commanded Mehuman, Biztha, Harbona, Bigtha, Abagtha, Zethar and Carcas, the seven chamberlains that served before him. JFB notes the seven chamberlains are the king's personal attendants, sent on an errand that wine had made reckless. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: To bring Vashti the queen before the king with the royal crown, to show the people and princes her beauty, for she was fair to look on. Henry notes the summons treats the queen as an ornament of the feast, to be displayed before a drinking company. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: But queen Vashti refused to come at the king's commandment by his chamberlains: therefore the king was very wroth, and his anger burned in him. JFB notes the refusal is stated without its reasons, and the king's wrath is the passion of a man crossed in public. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Then the king said to the wise men which knew the times, for so was the king's manner toward all that knew law and judgment. Henry notes even a drunken king turns to counsellors, and the question put to them is legal, not personal. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: And next unto him was Carshena, Shethar, Admatha, Tarshish, Meres, Marsena and Memucan, the seven princes of Persia and Media, which saw the king's face and sat first in the kingdom. JFB notes these are the king's inner council, whose advice in such a case becomes law. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: What shall we do unto queen Vashti according to law, because she has not performed the king's commandment by the chamberlains? The question is framed as law, though the offence is a wife's refusal, showing how a household matter is raised to affairs of state. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: And Memucan answered before the king and princes, Vashti has not done wrong to the king only, but also to all the princes and all the people in all the provinces. Henry notes the argument widens a private refusal into a public example, the usual plea for making an example. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: For this deed of the queen shall come abroad unto all women, so that they shall despise their husbands, when it shall be reported, Ahasuerus commanded Vashti to be brought in, but she came not. JFB notes the fear is imitation: one queen's refusal, once known, will be every wife's excuse. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Likewise shall the ladies of Persia and Media say this day unto all the king's princes, which have heard of the deed of the queen. Thus shall there arise too much contempt and wrath. Henry notes the council foresees a kingdom of household quarrels following one court quarrel. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: If it please the king, let a royal commandment go forth, written among the laws of the Persians and Medes, that it be not altered, that Vashti come no more before Ahasuerus; and let the king give her royal estate unto another that is better than she. JFB notes the irrevocable law is invoked for a divorce, and Henry adds that God's providence served its own purpose by it, making way for Esther to the crown. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: And when the king's decree shall be published throughout all his empire, for it is great, all wives shall give their husbands honour, both great and small. The decree's stated aim is domestic order in every house, great and small alike. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: And the saying pleased the king and the princes; and the king did according to the word of Memucan. Henry notes how easily a king in anger adopts a counsellor's severity as his own. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: For he sent letters into all the provinces, each in its own writing and to every people in its own language, that every man should bear rule in his own house, and that it should be published in the language of every people. JFB notes the multilingual letters show the empire's machinery at work for a household rule, and Clarke adds the parade of enactment served chiefly to deprive Vashti of her crown. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown; Adam Clarke
