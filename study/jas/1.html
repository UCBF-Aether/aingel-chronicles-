Study Notes — James

Chapter 1

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: James writes as a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ to the twelve tribes scattered abroad. Henry notes this epistle is directed chiefly to Jewish Christians, and its greeting opens a letter of practical duty rather than full doctrinal statement. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

2 COMMENTARY: Believers are told to count it all joy when they fall into divers temptations. Henry explains that Christianity teaches joy under troubles, since such exercises are sent from God's love and will brighten our graces now and our crown at last. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

3 COMMENTARY: The trying of faith works patience. Henry urges that in times of trial patience, not passion, be set to work, whatever is said or done. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

4 COMMENTARY: Patience is to have her perfect work, so that believers are perfect and entire, wanting nothing. When the work of patience is complete, Henry says, it furnishes all that is necessary for the Christian race and warfare. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

5 COMMENTARY: If any lack wisdom, let him ask of God, who gives liberally and upbraids not. Henry notes we should pray not so much for the removal of affliction as for wisdom to make a right use of it, in regulating our own spirit and managing our affairs. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

6 COMMENTARY: Asking must be in faith, nothing wavering, for the waverer is like a wave of the sea driven with the wind and tossed. A mind that rises and falls with second causes, Henry warns, will be unsteady in words and actions. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

7 COMMENTARY: Let not the wavering man think he shall receive anything of the Lord. The promise, Henry stresses, is to any that asks in single, prevailing regard to his spiritual and eternal interest. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

8 COMMENTARY: A double minded man is unstable in all his ways. Such unsteadiness cannot please God, even where it does not expose a man to contempt in the world. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

9 COMMENTARY: The brother of low degree is to rejoice in that he is exalted. No condition of life hinders rejoicing in God: those of low degree may rejoice that they are rich in faith and heirs of the kingdom. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

10 COMMENTARY: The rich are to rejoice in being made low, because as the flower of the grass they shall pass away. Henry adds that the rich may rejoice in humbling providences that lead to a humble and lowly disposition of mind. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

11 COMMENTARY: As the sun with burning heat withers the grass and its flower falls, so the rich man shall fade away in his ways. Worldly wealth is a withering thing, and trials teach us to seek happiness in and from God, not from perishing enjoyments. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

12 COMMENTARY: Blessed is the man that endures temptation, for when he is tried he shall receive the crown of life promised to them that love God. It is not every sufferer who is blessed, Henry notes, but he who with patience and constancy goes through difficulties in the way of duty. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

13 COMMENTARY: No man under temptation may say he is tempted of God, for God cannot be tempted with evil, neither does he tempt any man. Nothing sinful in heart or conduct can be ascribed to God, Henry insists; he is not the author of the dross, though his fiery trial exposes it. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

14 COMMENTARY: Every man is tempted when he is drawn away of his own lust and enticed. The origin of evil and temptation is in our own hearts, Henry says, and those who blame their constitution or condition wrong God as if he were the author of sin. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

15 COMMENTARY: When lust has conceived it brings forth sin, and sin when finished brings forth death. Henry's counsel is to stop the beginnings of sin, or all the evils that follow must be wholly charged upon ourselves. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

16 COMMENTARY: Do not err, beloved brethren: afflictions sent by God are designed to draw out our graces, not our corruptions. God has no pleasure in the death of men, and no hand in their sin; both sin and misery are owing to themselves. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

17 COMMENTARY: Every good and perfect gift is from above, from the Father of lights, with whom is no variableness or shadow of turning. As the sun is the same in nature though clouds make it seem to vary, so God is unchangeable, and our changes and shadows are not from any change in him. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

18 COMMENTARY: Of his own will God begat us with the word of truth, that we should be a kind of firstfruits of his creatures. A true Christian becomes as different a person from what he was before the renewing influences of divine grace as if he were formed over again. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

19 COMMENTARY: Believers are to be swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath. Instead of blaming God under trials, Henry says, we should open our ears and hearts to learn what he teaches by them. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

20 COMMENTARY: The wrath of man works not the righteousness of God. If men would govern their tongues, Henry notes, they must govern their passions, for the worst thing we can bring to any dispute is anger. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

21 COMMENTARY: Laying apart all filthiness and superfluity of naughtiness, believers are to receive with meekness the engrafted word, which is able to save their souls. This laying aside must reach sins of thought and affection as well as of speech and practice, like casting off a filthy garment. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

22 COMMENTARY: Be doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves. If we rested in hearing only, Henry warns, it would never bring us to heaven, even if we heard a sermon every day and an angel were the preacher. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

23 COMMENTARY: A hearer who is not a doer is like a man beholding his natural face in a glass. The word of truth, carefully attended to, sets before us the corruption of our nature and the disorders of our hearts and lives. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

24 COMMENTARY: Such a man beholds himself, goes his way, and straightway forgets what manner of man he was. In vain do we hear God's word and look into the gospel glass if we go away and forget our spots instead of washing them off, and forget our remedy instead of applying to it. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

25 COMMENTARY: Whoever looks into the perfect law of liberty and continues therein, being not a forgetful hearer but a doer of the work, shall be blessed in his deed. Henry stresses the distinctness: it is not for his deeds that any man is blessed, but in his deed; it is not talking but walking that will bring us to heaven. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

26 COMMENTARY: If any seem religious and bridle not his tongue, he deceives his own heart and his religion is vain. Taking more pains to seem religious than really to be so is a sign religion is in vain, and a slandering tongue cannot go with a truly humble, gracious heart. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

27 COMMENTARY: Pure religion before God is to visit the fatherless and widows in their affliction and to keep oneself unspotted from the world. True religion teaches us to do everything as in the presence of God, Henry says, and nothing avails in Christ Jesus but faith that works by love, purifies the heart, and obeys God's commands. — Matthew Henry (Concise)
