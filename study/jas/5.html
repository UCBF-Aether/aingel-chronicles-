Study Notes — James

Chapter 5

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Go to now, ye rich men, weep and howl for your miseries that shall come upon you. Public troubles are most grievous to those who live in pleasure and are secure and sensual, Henry notes, though all ranks suffer deeply at such times. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

2 COMMENTARY: Your riches are corrupted, and your garments are motheaten. All idolized treasures will soon perish, Henry says, except as they will rise up in judgment against their possessors. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

3 COMMENTARY: Your gold and silver is cankered, and the rust shall be a witness against you and eat your flesh as fire, for ye have heaped treasure for the last days. Take heed of defrauding and oppressing, Henry warns, and avoid the very appearance of it. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

4 COMMENTARY: The hire of the labourers kept back by fraud cries out, and their cries have entered into the ears of the Lord of sabaoth. God does not forbid lawful pleasures, Henry notes, but living in pleasure, especially sinful pleasure, is a provoking sin. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

5 COMMENTARY: Ye have lived in pleasure on the earth and been wanton, nourishing your hearts as in a day of slaughter. Is it no harm, Henry asks, for people to unfit themselves for minding the concerns of their souls by indulging bodily appetites? — Matthew Henry (Concise)

6 COMMENTARY: Ye have condemned and killed the just, and he does not resist you. When the just suffer by oppressors, Henry says, this is marked by God; and above all their other crimes, the Jews had condemned and crucified that Just One, Jesus Christ the righteous. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

7 COMMENTARY: Be patient therefore unto the coming of the Lord, as the husbandman waits for the precious fruit of the earth until he receives the early and latter rain. Consider him that waits for a crop of corn, Henry says, and will not you wait for a crown of glory? — Matthew Henry (Concise)

8 COMMENTARY: Be ye also patient and stablish your hearts, for the coming of the Lord draws nigh. In every sense the coming of the Lord drew nigh, Henry notes, and all his people's losses, hardships, and sufferings would be repaid. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

9 COMMENTARY: Grudge not one against another, lest ye be condemned, for the judge stands before the door. Men count time long because they measure it by their own lives, Henry observes, but all time is as nothing to God. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

10 COMMENTARY: Take the prophets who spoke in the name of the Lord as an example of suffering affliction and of patience. Let us serve our God and bear our trials as those who believe that the end will crown all. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

11 COMMENTARY: We count them happy which endure; ye have heard of the patience of Job and seen the end of the Lord, that the Lord is very pitiful and of tender mercy. God brought about things in Job's case so as plainly to prove this, Henry says, though it did not appear during his troubles but was seen in the event. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

12 COMMENTARY: Above all things, swear not, neither by heaven nor earth nor any other oath, but let your yea be yea and your nay, nay, lest ye fall into condemnation. The sin of swearing is condemned, Henry notes, and how many make light of common profane swearing, which expressly throws contempt upon God's name and authority. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

13 COMMENTARY: Is any among you afflicted? Let him pray. Is any merry? Let him sing psalms. In a day of affliction nothing is more seasonable than prayer, Henry says, for the spirit is then most humble and the heart broken and tender. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

14 COMMENTARY: Is any sick among you? Let him call for the elders of the church, and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord. It is necessary to exercise faith and hope under afflictions, Henry notes, and prayer is the appointed means for obtaining and increasing these graces. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

15 COMMENTARY: The prayer of faith shall save the sick, and the Lord shall raise him up, and if he has committed sins they shall be forgiven him. The saving of the sick is ascribed not to the anointing with oil but to prayer, Henry stresses, and the great thing to beg in sickness is the pardon of sin. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

16 COMMENTARY: Confess your faults one to another, and pray one for another, that ye may be healed, for the effectual fervent prayer of a righteous man avails much. To acknowledge our faults to each other tends greatly to peace and brotherly love, Henry says. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

17 COMMENTARY: Elias was a man subject to like passions as we are, and he prayed earnestly that it might not rain, and it rained not for three years and six months. The power of prayer is proved from the history of Elijah, Henry notes, and in prayer we must look not to the merit of man but to the grace of God. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

18 COMMENTARY: He prayed again, and the heaven gave rain, and the earth brought forth her fruit. It is not enough to say a prayer, Henry says, but we must pray in prayer: thoughts fixed, desires firm and ardent, graces exercised. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

19 COMMENTARY: If any of you err from the truth and one convert him, that is true conversion. It is no mark of a wise or holy man to boast of being free from error, Henry notes, and there is some doctrinal mistake at the bottom of every practical mistake. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

20 COMMENTARY: He which converts the sinner from the error of his way shall save a soul from death and hide a multitude of sins. Conversion turns a sinner from the error of his ways, not merely from one party to another, Henry says, and the salvation of one soul is of infinitely greater importance than preserving the lives of multitudes. — Matthew Henry (Concise)
