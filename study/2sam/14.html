Study Notes — 2 Samuel

Chapter 14

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Joab perceives the king's heart is toward Absalom. The general who killed Abner now works to bring home the king's exiled son, sensing a favour the king will not act on alone. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

2 COMMENTARY: Joab fetches a wise woman from Tekoah and tells her to feign mourning, wear mourning apparel, and anoint not herself with oil. A staged widow will say to the king what Joab cannot say himself. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

3 COMMENTARY: Joab puts the words in her mouth and sends her to speak to the king in that manner. The parable is planted, not spontaneous, and the king will judge a case that is really his own. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

4 COMMENTARY: The woman of Tekoah falls on her face, does obeisance, and cries, Help, O king. Her posture is a subject begging justice from the throne. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

5 COMMENTARY: The king asks what ails her, and she answers that she is indeed a widow, her husband dead. The fiction begins with a true-sounding grief. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

6 COMMENTARY: Her two sons strove in the field with none to part them, and one smote the other and slew him. The invented case mirrors Absalom's crime: a brother slain, a surviving brother under threat. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

7 COMMENTARY: The whole family rises against her, demanding the slayer for the life of his brother, and threatening to quench her coal left and destroy her heir. Blood vengeance would leave her childless and the family name extinguished. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

8 COMMENTARY: The king tells her to go to her house, and he will give charge concerning her. A royal promise of protection is given before the case is fully heard. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

9 COMMENTARY: She answers that the iniquity be on her and her father's house, and the king and his throne be guiltless. She shields the king from blame for whatever mercy he grants. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

10 COMMENTARY: The king promises that whoever speaks against her shall be brought to him and shall not touch her any more. Protection hardens into a royal guarantee. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

11 COMMENTARY: She presses further, asking the king to remember the Lord his God, that the revengers of blood not destroy her son, and he swears, As the Lord liveth, not one hair of her son shall fall to the earth. The oath binds the king to spare a fratricide. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

12 COMMENTARY: She asks leave to speak one word to the king, and he says, Say on. The parable's turn comes only after the king has committed himself. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

13 COMMENTARY: She asks why he has thought such a thing against the people of God, speaking as one faulty in that he does not fetch home his banished one. The widow pleads God's mercy and clemency toward poor guilty sinners, Henry notes, and the state of sinners is a state of banishment from God. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

14 COMMENTARY: We must needs die, and are as water spilt on the ground, which cannot be gathered up again; yet God devises means that his banished be not expelled from him. God pardons none to the dishonour of his law, Henry warns, nor any who are impenitent, nor to the encouragement of crimes. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

15 COMMENTARY: She explains she came because the people made her afraid, and she resolved to speak to the king, trusting he would hear and deliver her. The fiction's excuse preserves her boldness as a subject's appeal. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

16 COMMENTARY: The king will hear, she says, to deliver her out of the hand of the man who would destroy her and her son from the inheritance of God. Deliverance from destroyers is the king's office she invokes. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

17 COMMENTARY: The word of the king shall be comfortable, she says, for as an angel of God he discerns good and bad. Flattery and truth mix: the king's discernment is praised just as it is being used. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

18 COMMENTARY: The king asks her not to hide the thing he shall ask, and she invites him to speak. The parable's veil is about to lift. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

19 COMMENTARY: The king asks whether Joab's hand is with her in all this, and she confesses none can turn right or left from what the king says. David sees through the fiction at last. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

20 COMMENTARY: She confesses Joab did this to fetch about this form of speech, and praises the king's wisdom as an angel of God's. The device is owned, and its author named. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

21 COMMENTARY: The king tells Joab he has done this thing: go, bring the young man Absalom again. David was inclined to favour Absalom, Henry notes, yet for the honour of his justice he could do it only upon application made for him, showing the methods of divine grace through a Mediator who pleads. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

22 COMMENTARY: Joab falls on his face, bows, thanks the king, and says today he knows he has found grace in the king's sight. The mediator's success is counted as personal favour. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

23 COMMENTARY: Joab goes to Geshur and brings Absalom to Jerusalem. The banished son returns to the city, though not yet to the king's face. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

24 COMMENTARY: The king orders Absalom to turn to his own house and not see his face, so Absalom returns to his house and sees not the king's face. Recall without reconciliation is a half-pardon that breeds resentment. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

25 COMMENTARY: In all Israel none was so much praised as Absalom for beauty, from sole to crown without blemish. Nothing is said of his wisdom or piety, Henry observes: a poor commendation for a man with nothing else valuable, and many a polluted soul dwells in a fair body. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

26 COMMENTARY: When he polled his head at each year's end, because the hair was heavy, he weighed it at two hundred shekels. The hair was a burden, Henry notes, but he would not cut it while he could bear the weight, since what feeds pride is not complained of though uneasy. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

27 COMMENTARY: To Absalom are born three sons and one daughter named Tamar, a woman of fair countenance. The daughter's name recalls the sister whose wrong set all this in motion. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

28 COMMENTARY: Absalom dwells two full years in Jerusalem and sees not the king's face. Two years of proximity without presence turn a son's longing into a grievance. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

29 COMMENTARY: Absalom sends for Joab to send him to the king, but Joab will not come, and a second sending also fails. The mediator who fetched him home now avoids him. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

30 COMMENTARY: Absalom orders his servants to set Joab's barley field on fire, and they do. By insolent carriage toward Joab, Henry notes, Absalom brings Joab to plead for him. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

31 COMMENTARY: Joab comes to Absalom's house demanding why his field was set on fire. The arson achieves what two summonses could not: Joab's attention. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

32 COMMENTARY: Absalom answers that he sent for Joab to ask the king why he came from Geshur, since it had been good for him to be there still, and now demands to see the king's face, or be slain. By an insolent message to the king, Henry observes, he gains his wishes. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

33 COMMENTARY: Joab tells the king, Absalom is called, bows on his face to the ground before the king, and the king kisses Absalom. The father's compassion prevails to reconcile him to an impenitent son, and Henry asks: shall penitent sinners question the compassion of the Father of mercies? — Matthew Henry (Concise)
