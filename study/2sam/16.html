Study Notes — 2 Samuel

Chapter 16

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Just past the top of the hill, Ziba, Mephibosheth's servant, meets David with saddled asses loaded with bread, raisins, summer fruits, and wine. Provision arrives for a fugitive king at the moment he needs it. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

2 COMMENTARY: David asks what Ziba means by these, and Ziba explains the asses are for the household to ride, the bread and fruit for the young men, and the wine for the faint in the wilderness. The gift is practical and well-judged, which is what makes it persuasive. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

3 COMMENTARY: David asks where Mephibosheth is, and Ziba answers that he abides at Jerusalem, claiming the house of Israel will restore him his father's kingdom that day. Ziba belied Mephibosheth, Henry says plainly, and great men ought to be jealous of flatterers and careful to hear both sides. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

4 COMMENTARY: David at once gives Ziba all that pertained to Mephibosheth, and Ziba humbly begs grace in the king's sight. Flattery works instantly: David bears Shimei's curses better than Ziba's flatteries, Henry observes, for by flattery he was brought to pass a wrong judgment on another. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

5 COMMENTARY: At Bahurim a man of Saul's family, Shimei son of Gera, comes forth cursing still as he comes. The king's flight is met by the open hatred of the house he displaced. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

6 COMMENTARY: Shimei casts stones at David and all his servants, while all the people and mighty men are on David's right and left. The assault is public and brazen, done before the whole army. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

7 COMMENTARY: Shimei cries, Come out, come out, thou bloody man, thou man of Belial. The charge is false in its particulars, and Henry notes innocence is no defence against malice and falsehood. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

8 COMMENTARY: Shimei claims the Lord returned on David all the blood of Saul's house, in whose stead he reigned, and delivered the kingdom to Absalom. David, who once and again spared Saul's life while Saul sought his, is charged with the very blood he refused to shed. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

9 COMMENTARY: Abishai asks why this dead dog should curse the king, and begs leave to take off his head. The soldier's instinct is to silence the reviler with the sword. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

10 COMMENTARY: David answers, What have I to do with you, ye sons of Zeruiah? Let him curse, because the Lord has said to him, Curse David; who shall say, Wherefore hast thou done so? David sees the hand of God in the abuse, Henry says, and comforts himself that God will bring good out of his affliction. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

11 COMMENTARY: David tells Abishai and all his servants that his own son seeks his life, so how much more may this Benjamite, and bids them let him alone and curse, for the Lord has bidden him. A humble spirit, Henry observes, turns reproaches into reproofs and gets good from them instead of being provoked. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

12 COMMENTARY: It may be the Lord will look on David's affliction and requite him good for his cursing this day. Patience under abuse rests on hope that God repays not only services but sufferings. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

13 COMMENTARY: As David's men go by, Shimei goes along the hillside over against him, cursing, throwing stones, and casting dust. The abuse continues mile after mile, and David continues to bear it. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

14 COMMENTARY: The king and all the people with him come weary and refresh themselves there. The halt after the long abuse is the first rest of the flight. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

15 COMMENTARY: Absalom and all Israel come to Jerusalem, with Ahithophel. The usurper enters the capital David left, with the counsellor who betrayed David. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

16 COMMENTARY: When Hushai the Archite, David's friend, comes to Absalom, he cries, God save the king, God save the king. The loyal friend greets the usurper as king, beginning his double game. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

17 COMMENTARY: Absalom asks Hushai whether this is his kindness to his friend, and why he did not go with his friend. Suspicion is natural, and Hushai must answer it convincingly. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

18 COMMENTARY: Hushai answers that whom the Lord, this people, and all Israel choose, his will he be, and with him will he abide. The appeal to the nation's choice covers a loyalty that has not changed. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

19 COMMENTARY: Whom should he serve, Hushai asks, if not in the son's presence as he served in the father's? The argument is plausible enough to pass, and Absalom accepts it. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

20 COMMENTARY: Absalom asks Ahithophel to give counsel what they shall do. The wisest counsellors of the age, Henry notes, are now both in Absalom's camp, and he thinks himself sure of success with them. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

21 COMMENTARY: Ahithophel counsels Absalom to go in to his father's concubines left to keep the house, so all Israel shall hear he is abhorred of his father and the hands of his followers be strong. The counsel is wicked, Henry says, and they that advise men to sin certainly advise them to their hurt. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

22 COMMENTARY: A tent is spread on the housetop, and Absalom goes in to his father's concubines in the sight of all Israel. The private sin of the father is answered by a public sin of the son, fulfilling Nathan's word. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

23 COMMENTARY: Ahithophel's counsel in those days was as if a man enquired at the oracle of God, so it was both with David and Absalom. Yet miserable counsellors they both were, Henry observes: Hushai would never counsel Absalom wisely, and Ahithophel counselled wickedly, betraying him as effectually as a designed traitor, for honesty is the best policy in the long run. — Matthew Henry (Concise)
