Study Notes — 2 Samuel

Chapter 13

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: After this, Absalom's fair sister Tamar is loved by Amnon, David's son. From henceforward, Henry notes, David was followed with one trouble after another, and the like sins among his children were the beginnings of his punishment for adultery and murder. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

2 COMMENTARY: Amnon is so vexed he falls sick for his sister Tamar, a virgin, thinking it hard to do anything to her. Lust sickens into obsession, and the obstacle of her virginity only sharpens it. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

3 COMMENTARY: Amnon has a friend, Jonadab son of Shimeah, a very subtil man. False friends who counsel wickedly, Henry warns, prove as guilty of the outcome as of the sin itself. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

4 COMMENTARY: Jonadab asks why the king's son grows lean day by day, and Amnon confesses he loves Tamar, Absalom's sister. Confession to a subtil friend becomes the doorway to a plot. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

5 COMMENTARY: Jonadab counsels Amnon to lie down, make himself sick, and ask the king to let Tamar come and give him meat. The counsel dresses seduction as a sick man's request. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

6 COMMENTARY: Amnon lies down and makes himself sick, and when the king visits him he asks that Tamar come and make cakes in his sight. The father's visit is used to draw the sister into the trap. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

7 COMMENTARY: David sends home to Tamar, telling her to go to Amnon's house and dress him meat. David, too indulgent to his children, Henry observes, sends the daughter unsuspecting into her brother's house. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

8 COMMENTARY: Tamar goes to Amnon's house, kneads flour, makes cakes in his sight, and bakes them. Her innocent service is performed openly, before witnesses, in her brother's sight. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

9 COMMENTARY: She pours the cakes out before him, but he refuses to eat and orders all men out from him. The refusal clears the room, turning a household visit into a private assault. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

10 COMMENTARY: Amnon tells Tamar to bring the meat into the chamber, that he may eat from her hand, and she carries the cakes in. The last step of the plot moves her from the open room to the closed chamber. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

11 COMMENTARY: When she brings the meat to eat, he takes hold of her and says, Come lie with me, my sister. The request drops all pretence of sickness and service. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

12 COMMENTARY: She answers, Nay, my brother, do not force me, for no such thing ought to be done in Israel; do not this folly. Her protest names the act as a national shame, not merely a private wrong. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

13 COMMENTARY: She asks where she shall cause her shame to go, warns he will be as one of the fools in Israel, and urges him to speak to the king, who will not withhold her from him. Even in terror she offers a lawful path he refuses to take. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

14 COMMENTARY: He will not hearken, but being stronger than she, forces her and lies with her. Let no one, Henry says, expect good treatment from those capable of attempting their seduction; it is better to suffer the greatest wrong than to commit the least sin. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

15 COMMENTARY: Then Amnon hates her exceedingly, more than he had loved her, and says, Arise, be gone. Lust spent turns to hatred, and the woman is blamed for the man's own act. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

16 COMMENTARY: She protests that sending her away is a greater evil than the other done to her, but he will not hearken. Expulsion adds public disgrace to private violence. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

17 COMMENTARY: He calls his servant and orders, Put this woman out from me, and bolt the door after her. The door bolted behind her marks her as the offender in her brother's house. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

18 COMMENTARY: She wears a garment of divers colours, the robe of virgin daughters of the king, and his servant brings her out and bolts the door. The royal robe she wears makes the expulsion a public statement of her ruin. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

19 COMMENTARY: Tamar puts ashes on her head, rends her garment, lays her hand on her head, and goes on crying. Her mourning is open, unlike the secrecy of the crime. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

20 COMMENTARY: Absalom her brother asks if Amnon has been with her, tells her to hold her peace, for he is her brother, and not to regard the thing, so Tamar remains desolate in Absalom's house. Silence is imposed on the victim while vengeance is quietly planned. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

21 COMMENTARY: When David hears all these things, he is very wroth. Anger without action follows: the indulgent father who punished neither his own sin fully nor his son's will reap the consequence. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

22 COMMENTARY: Absalom speaks to Amnon neither good nor bad, for he hates Amnon because he forced Tamar. Hatred nursed in silence for two years ripens into murder. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

23 COMMENTARY: After two full years, Absalom has sheepshearers at Baalhazor and invites all the king's sons. The feast is the cover for revenge, timed long after suspicion has cooled. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

24 COMMENTARY: Absalom asks the king and his servants to come with him to the sheepshearing. The invitation to the king himself is the test of whether the gathering looks innocent. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

25 COMMENTARY: The king declines, lest they be chargeable to Absalom, but presses him not, and blesses him. David's blessing goes to a son already resolved on murder. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

26 COMMENTARY: Absalom then asks that his brother Amnon go with them, and the king asks why Amnon should go. The father's question shows he senses something, yet does not stop it. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

27 COMMENTARY: Absalom presses him until he lets Amnon and all the king's sons go. Indulgence again prevails over suspicion, and the victim is delivered to the feast. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

28 COMMENTARY: Absalom commands his servants to mark when Amnon's heart is merry with wine, and at his word to smite and kill him, fearing not, for Absalom commands it. Henry notes the aggravation: Absalom has Amnon slain when least fit to go out of the world, and engages his servants in the guilt. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

29 COMMENTARY: Absalom's servants do to Amnon as commanded, and all the king's sons rise, mount their mules, and flee. The murder scatters the royal house in terror. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

30 COMMENTARY: While they are on the way, tidings reach David that Absalom has slain all the king's sons, with none left. The first report exaggerates the crime, as bad news often does. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

31 COMMENTARY: The king rises, tears his garments, lies on the earth, and all his servants stand by with clothes rent. The court mourns a massacre that has not happened, on top of the murder that has. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

32 COMMENTARY: Jonadab answers that the king should not suppose all his sons are slain, for Amnon only is dead. The same subtil friend who counselled the first sin now corrects the report of the second. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

33 COMMENTARY: Jonadab repeats that the king should not take to heart that all his sons are dead, for Amnon only is dead. His certainty comes from knowing Absalom's purpose from the day of Tamar's wrong. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

34 COMMENTARY: But Absalom flees, and the watchman sees many people coming by the hillside. The murderer is already gone while the court still counts its losses. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

35 COMMENTARY: Jonadab tells the king the king's sons are coming, as he said, and as he finishes speaking they arrive weeping, with the king and all his servants weeping sore. Relief and grief arrive together. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

36 COMMENTARY: The king's sons come and lift up their voices and weep, and the king and all his servants weep very sore. The house mourns both the dead son and the exiled one. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

37 COMMENTARY: Absalom flees to Talmai son of Ammihud, king of Geshur, and David mourns for his son every day. The murderer finds refuge with his mother's kin, beyond his father's reach. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

38 COMMENTARY: Absalom remains in Geshur three years. Exile, not punishment, is the interval, and time does not heal the breach. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

39 COMMENTARY: King David's soul longs to go forth to Absalom, for he is comforted concerning Amnon, seeing he is dead. Instead of loathing Absalom as a murderer, Henry observes, David after a time longs for him: this was David's infirmity, and indulged children always prove crosses to godly parents whose foolish love neglects duty to God. — Matthew Henry (Concise)
