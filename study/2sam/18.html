Study Notes — 2 Samuel

Chapter 18

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: David numbers the people with him and sets captains of thousands and hundreds over them. The fugitive company is organised as an army before the battle it did not seek. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

2 COMMENTARY: David sends a third under Joab, a third under Abishai, and a third under Ittai the Gittite, and says he will surely go forth with them himself. Command is shared among the loyal captains, including the foreigner who swore to stay. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

3 COMMENTARY: The people answer that David shall not go forth, for if they flee or half die, the enemy will not care, but David is worth ten thousand of them, so he should succour them from the city. The king's life is the army's centre, and they will not risk it in the field. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

4 COMMENTARY: The king agrees to do what seems best to them, stands by the gate side, and all the people go out by hundreds and thousands. David yields to his people's counsel and watches his army march past him. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

5 COMMENTARY: The king charges Joab, Abishai, and Ittai, saying, Deal gently for my sake with the young man, even with Absalom, and all the people hear the charge. How David renders good for evil, Henry marvels: Absalom would have only David smitten, David would have only Absalom spared. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

6 COMMENTARY: The people go out into the field against Israel, and the battle is in the wood of Ephraim. Civil war is fought in a forest, where order breaks and the ground itself becomes a weapon. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

7 COMMENTARY: Israel is slain before David's servants, with a great slaughter of twenty thousand men that day. The rebels see, Henry says, what it is to take counsel against the Lord and his anointed. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

8 COMMENTARY: The battle scatters over all the country, and the wood devours more people than the sword. The forest kills more than the fighting, as fugitives are lost in its thickets. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

9 COMMENTARY: Absalom meets David's servants riding on a mule, which goes under the thick boughs of a great oak, and his head catches in the oak, leaving him hanging between heaven and earth while the mule goes on. The proud hair and the high ride become the snare. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

10 COMMENTARY: A man sees it and tells Joab, Behold, I saw Absalom hanged in an oak. The report is given to the captain who must decide what the king's charge allows. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

11 COMMENTARY: Joab asks why the man did not smite Absalom to the ground, offering ten shekels and a girdle. The captain treats the king's charge as an obstacle to be bought past. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

12 COMMENTARY: The man answers that for a thousand shekels he would not put forth his hand against the king's son, for in their hearing the king charged them to beware of the young man Absalom. The common soldier honours the public charge Joab would set aside. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

13 COMMENTARY: Otherwise, he says, he would have wrought falsehood against his own life, for no matter is hid from the king, and Joab himself would have set himself against him. Obedience protects the soldier twice over: from the king's wrath and from Joab's disavowal. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

14 COMMENTARY: Joab says he may not tarry thus, takes three darts, and thrusts them through Absalom's heart while he is yet alive in the oak. The captain disobeys the king's charge in the name of the nation's necessity. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

15 COMMENTARY: Ten young men bearing Joab's armour compass Absalom about, smite him, and slay him. The killing is finished by the captain's men, spreading responsibility across the company. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

16 COMMENTARY: Joab blows the trumpet, and the people return from pursuing Israel, for Joab holds back the people. With Absalom dead, the war's purpose ends, and pursuit stops. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

17 COMMENTARY: They take Absalom, cast him into a great pit in the wood, and lay a very great heap of stones on him, and all Israel flees to his tent. The heap of stones is a monument of shame, not the pillar Absalom built for himself. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

18 COMMENTARY: In his lifetime Absalom reared a pillar in the king's dale, saying he has no son to keep his name in remembrance, and called it Absalom's place. The monument he raised to his name stands empty beside the pit where his body lies. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

19 COMMENTARY: Ahimaaz son of Zadok asks to run and bear the king tidings that the Lord has avenged him of his enemies. The priest's son volunteers to carry the victory home. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

20 COMMENTARY: Joab refuses Ahimaaz tidings that day, because the king's son is dead, though he may bear tidings another day. The bearer of good news is held back because the news is mixed with death. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

21 COMMENTARY: Joab sends Cushi instead, bidding him tell the king what he has seen, and Cushi bows and runs. A foreign messenger is chosen to carry the hard part of the news. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

22 COMMENTARY: Ahimaaz again begs to run after Cushi, and Joab asks why he would run, having no tidings ready. Eagerness to run is not the same as having a message. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

23 COMMENTARY: Ahimaaz insists, Joab says Run, and Ahimaaz runs by the plain and overruns Cushi. The lighter runner on the easier road outpaces the appointed messenger. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

24 COMMENTARY: David sits between the two gates, and the watchman on the roof sees a man running alone. The king waits at the gate for news, as he waited for his army to march out. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

25 COMMENTARY: The watchman cries to the king, and David says if he is alone there is tidings in his mouth, and the runner draws near. A lone runner means a message, not a rout. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

26 COMMENTARY: The watchman sees another man running alone, and the king says he also brings tidings. Two runners, one message in two parts, approach the gate. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

27 COMMENTARY: The watchman thinks the foremost runs like Ahimaaz, and the king says he is a good man and comes with good tidings. Recognition of the runner raises hope before the message is heard. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

28 COMMENTARY: Ahimaaz calls, All is well, falls on his face before the king, and blesses the Lord who delivered up the men that lifted their hand against the king. By directing David to give thanks for the victory, Henry notes, Ahimaaz prepares him for the news of his son's death. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

29 COMMENTARY: The king asks, Is the young man Absalom safe? and Ahimaaz answers that he saw a great tumult when Joab sent him, but knew not what it was. The runner who outran the message cannot answer the only question the king cares about. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

30 COMMENTARY: The king tells Ahimaaz to turn aside and stand here, and he turns aside and stands still. The incomplete messenger is set aside for the one who saw. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

31 COMMENTARY: Cushi comes with tidings that the Lord has avenged the king of all who rose against him. The formal victory report is delivered, and the king's question still hangs unanswered. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

32 COMMENTARY: The king asks Cushi, Is the young man Absalom safe? and Cushi answers that the king's enemies and all who rise to do him hurt be as that young man is. The answer is a death sentence phrased as a wish, and David understands it at once. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

33 COMMENTARY: The king is much moved, goes up to the chamber over the gate, weeps, and cries, O my son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom, would God I had died for thee. Henry blames David for over-fondness for a graceless son, for quarrelling with divine justice, and for opposing the justice of the nation, which as king he ought to prefer before natural affection; yet in David's grief we may perceive a shadow of the Saviour's love, who wept over and suffered death for vile rebels. — Matthew Henry (Concise)
