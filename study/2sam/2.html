Study Notes — 2 Samuel

Chapter 2

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Even with God's promise secure, David still enquires of the Lord before moving, asking whether and where to go up. Henry draws the lesson that assurance of hope should quicken pious endeavour, not replace it, and God directs him to Hebron. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

2 COMMENTARY: David goes up with his two wives, Ahinoam and Abigail. The move is a household migration under God's direction, not a solitary military advance. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

3 COMMENTARY: His men go up with their households and settle in the cities of Hebron. In all our journeys, Henry notes, it is comfortable to see God going before us when by faith and prayer we set him before us. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

4 COMMENTARY: The men of Judah anoint David king over the house of Judah, and tell him the men of Jabeshgilead buried Saul. David rises gradually, Henry observes, as the kingdom of the Messiah, the Son of David, is set up by degrees. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

5 COMMENTARY: David blesses the men of Jabeshgilead for showing kindness to their lord Saul in burying him. His first royal act honours his predecessor rather than celebrating his own rise. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

6 COMMENTARY: David prays the Lord will show them kindness and truth, and promises to requite their kindness himself. Gratitude toward those who honoured Saul becomes part of how David secures loyalty. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

7 COMMENTARY: David urges them to be strong and valiant, telling them Judah has anointed him king. He appeals to them as a king already chosen, yet still courting the allegiance of Saul's people. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

8 COMMENTARY: Abner, Saul's captain, takes Ishbosheth across to Mahanaim and sets him up as a rival king. The nation in general refused David, Henry notes, and by this the Lord trained his servant for future honour. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

9 COMMENTARY: Ishbosheth is made king over Gilead, Jezreel, Ephraim, Benjamin, and all Israel. The division is a rival claim to the whole kingdom, not merely a tribal remnant. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

10 COMMENTARY: Ishbosheth, forty years old, reigns two years, while Judah follows David. The short, contested reign measures how much of Israel still clung to Saul's house. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

11 COMMENTARY: David reigns in Hebron over Judah seven years and six months. His long, partial reign is the slow road by which God brings him to the whole kingdom. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

12 COMMENTARY: Abner's men march from Mahanaim to Gibeon, and Joab's men meet them at the pool. Two armies face each other across the water, with civil war already arranged before a word is spoken. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

13 COMMENTARY: The two forces sit on opposite sides of the pool of Gibeon, poised for battle. The standoff shows a nation divided against itself, Judah against Israel, with no enemy but each other. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

14 COMMENTARY: Abner proposes that the young men arise and play before them, and Joab agrees. Henry warns that Abner meant fighting when he said play, and that fools make a mock at sin by trifling with human blood. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

15 COMMENTARY: Twelve men of Benjamin and twelve of David's servants rise to the contest. What was offered as sport becomes a matched, deadly duel between the two houses. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

16 COMMENTARY: Each man seizes his fellow and thrusts his sword into his side, so all fall together, and the place is named Helkathhazzurim. The mutual slaughter exposes the folly of treating war as a game. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

17 COMMENTARY: The duel ignites a sore battle, and Abner and Israel are beaten before David's servants. David is herein a type of Christ, Henry notes, whom Israel would not submit to though anointed by the Father. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

18 COMMENTARY: Joab, Abishai, and Asahel, Zeruiah's three sons, are on the field, and Asahel is as light of foot as a wild roe. His celebrated swiftness will prove his undoing, not his salvation. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

19 COMMENTARY: Asahel pursues Abner relentlessly, turning neither right nor left. Single-minded pursuit of one enemy blinds him to the danger he runs toward. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

20 COMMENTARY: Abner looks back and names his pursuer, and Asahel confirms it is he. The exchange marks the moment a fleeing general is forced to reckon with the man at his heels. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

21 COMMENTARY: Abner urges Asahel to turn aside and take a younger man's armour instead. Henry's lesson is that we are often betrayed by the accomplishments we are proud of, and Asahel's swiftness hastened his end. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

22 COMMENTARY: Abner warns again that he does not want to smite Asahel and then face his brother Joab. Even in flight, Abner foresees the blood-feud his self-defence would start. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

23 COMMENTARY: Asahel refuses to turn aside, and Abner strikes him under the fifth rib with the hinder end of his spear. Death often comes, Henry observes, by ways we least suspect. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

24 COMMENTARY: Joab and Abishai press on after Abner until sunset at the hill of Ammah. The pursuit continues past the point where one death might have ended it. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

25 COMMENTARY: Benjamin rallies behind Abner as one troop on a hilltop. A defeated army regains its footing only when it stops running and stands together. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

26 COMMENTARY: Abner calls to Joab asking whether the sword must devour for ever, warning of bitterness in the latter end. Henry notes how easy it is to use reason when it makes for us, and how civil war turns bitter for all concerned. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

27 COMMENTARY: Joab answers that had Abner not spoken, the pursuit would have lasted until morning. The same contest that looked pleasant in the morning looks dismal at night, and both sides are ready to repent of it. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

28 COMMENTARY: Joab blows the trumpet and the pursuit stops; they fight no more that day. Leaving off contention before it is meddled with further is, Henry notes, Solomon's advice and the wiser course. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

29 COMMENTARY: Abner and his men walk all night through the plain, cross Jordan, and reach Mahanaim. The defeated army retreats the long way home under cover of darkness. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

30 COMMENTARY: Joab musters his men and finds nineteen men and Asahel missing. The smallness of David's loss against Benjamin's heavy toll is counted precisely. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

31 COMMENTARY: David's servants slew three hundred and sixty of Benjamin and Abner's men. The lopsided count shows which side God was prospering, even in a battle neither side should have fought. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

32 COMMENTARY: Asahel is buried in his father's sepulchre at Bethlehem, and Joab's men reach Hebron at break of day. Henry notes distinctions are made between the dust of some and others, but in the resurrection the only lasting difference is between the godly and the ungodly. — Matthew Henry (Concise)
