Study Notes — 2 Samuel

Chapter 5

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: All the tribes come to David at Hebron, calling themselves his bone and flesh. His advances were gradual, Henry notes, that his faith might be tried and he might gain experience, typifying the Messiah's kingdom coming to its height by degrees. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

2 COMMENTARY: The tribes recall that David led Israel out and in even while Saul was king, and that the Lord said he would feed his people and be their captain. Past service and divine promise together make the case for his kingship. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

3 COMMENTARY: The elders make a league with David before the Lord at Hebron and anoint him king over Israel. He is now anointed a third time, and the whole nation, not only Judah, owns him. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

4 COMMENTARY: David was thirty when he began to reign, and reigned forty years. The round numbers frame a reign long enough to test him and to establish the kingdom he had waited for. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

5 COMMENTARY: Seven years and six months in Hebron over Judah, then thirty-three years in Jerusalem over all Israel and Judah. The divided reign gives way to a united one, by the same gradual steps Henry traces throughout. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

6 COMMENTARY: The Jebusites taunt David that even the blind and lame could hold Jerusalem against him. Henry observes that God's people's enemies are often most confident and secure just when their day to fall draws near. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

7 COMMENTARY: Nevertheless David takes the stronghold of Zion, the city of David. The pride and insolence of the Jebusites animated David, and the Lord God of hosts was with him. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

8 COMMENTARY: David promises chief command to whoever goes up by the gutter and smites the Jebusites, including the blind and lame he hates. The taunt is turned into the terms of the assault. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

9 COMMENTARY: David dwells in the fort, calls it the city of David, and builds round about from Millo inward. A captured stronghold becomes a capital, and a capital is built up as a permanent seat of government. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

10 COMMENTARY: David went on and grew great, and the Lord God of hosts was with him. His growth is attributed not to his own craft but to God's presence, the same presence Saul had forfeited. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

11 COMMENTARY: Hiram of Tyre sends cedar, carpenters, and masons, and they build David a house. Henry notes the house was not less fit to be dedicated to God for being built by the sons of the stranger, as Isaiah prophesied of the gospel church. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

12 COMMENTARY: David perceives the Lord established him king and exalted his kingdom for his people Israel's sake. Many have God's favour yet do not perceive it, Henry observes, but to be exalted and to perceive it is happiness; David owned it was for his people's sake. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

13 COMMENTARY: David takes more concubines and wives in Jerusalem, and more sons and daughters are born to him. The expanding household is the outward sign of a government rooted and built up. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

14 COMMENTARY: The sons born in Jerusalem begin with Shammuah, Shobab, Nathan, and Solomon. The list records the royal house taking root in the new capital, with Solomon named among the first four. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

15 COMMENTARY: Ibhar, Elishua, Nepheg, and Japhia continue the Jerusalem-born sons. Each name marks the kingdom's establishment in Zion rather than in Hebron. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

16 COMMENTARY: Elishama, Eliada, and Eliphalet complete the list of sons born in Jerusalem. Never had Israel appeared so great, Henry notes, as it began now to be under an established king. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

17 COMMENTARY: The Philistines, hearing David is anointed king over Israel, come up to seek him, and David goes down to the hold. The Philistines considered not, Henry says, that David had God's presence, which Saul had lost. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

18 COMMENTARY: The Philistines spread themselves in the valley of Rephaim. The attack on the newly set-up kingdom mirrors, Henry notes, how the Messiah's kingdom was attacked by the powers of darkness as soon as it appeared. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

19 COMMENTARY: David enquires of the Lord whether to go up and whether God will deliver the Philistines, and the Lord answers, Go up, for I will doubtless deliver them. David owns dependence on God for victory and refers himself to God's good pleasure. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

20 COMMENTARY: At Baalperazim David smites them, saying the Lord broke forth on his enemies as the breach of waters. God performed his promise, and David did not fail to improve his advantage. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

21 COMMENTARY: The Philistines leave their images behind, and David and his men burn them. The abandoned gods are destroyed rather than kept as trophies, showing whose victory it was. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

22 COMMENTARY: The Philistines come up yet again and spread in the valley of Rephaim. A second invasion tests whether David will again wait on God rather than trust his first victory. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

23 COMMENTARY: David enquires again, and the Lord tells him not to go up directly but to circle behind them against the mulberry trees. The changed orders show dependence is renewed each time, not presumed from the last battle. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

24 COMMENTARY: When David hears the sound of a going in the tops of the mulberry trees, he is to bestir himself, for then the Lord goes out before him. Henry's lesson is that David waited till God moved, and stirred then, but not till then. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

25 COMMENTARY: David does as the Lord commanded and smites the Philistines from Geba to Gazer. Trained up in dependence on God and his providence, he turns a second invasion into a rout across the whole front. — Matthew Henry (Concise)
