Study Notes — 2 Samuel

Chapter 12

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The Lord sends Nathan to David, and Nathan tells a parable of two men in one city, one rich and one poor. God will not suffer his people to lie still in sin, Henry notes, and by the parable Nathan draws from David a sentence against himself. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

2 COMMENTARY: The rich man has exceeding many flocks and herds. His abundance is the setting for a theft that needs no excuse. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

3 COMMENTARY: The poor man has nothing save one little ewe lamb, bought and nourished up, growing with him and his children, eating his meat, drinking his cup, lying in his bosom as a daughter. The lamb's tenderness makes the coming wrong unmistakable. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

4 COMMENTARY: A traveller comes to the rich man, who spares his own flock and herd but takes the poor man's lamb and dresses it for the wayfaring man. The injustice is plain precisely because the rich man loses nothing by sparing his own. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

5 COMMENTARY: David's anger is greatly kindled, and he swears the man who did this shall surely die. The king who cannot see his own sin judges another's with full severity. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

6 COMMENTARY: He shall restore the lamb fourfold, because he did this and had no pity. David's sentence measures the crime by its heartlessness, not only by its loss. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

7 COMMENTARY: Nathan says, Thou art the man, and speaks the Lord's word: I anointed thee king over Israel and delivered thee from Saul. Prudence in reproof and faithfulness meet here, Henry notes: Nathan is plain, Thou art the man. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

8 COMMENTARY: God gave David his master's house and wives, and the houses of Israel and Judah, and would have given more if that had been too little. The recital of gifts makes the sin not a want but a contempt of abundance. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

9 COMMENTARY: Wherefore hast thou despised the commandment of the Lord, to do evil in his sight, killing Uriah with the sword and taking his wife, slaying him with the sword of Ammon? God shows how much he hates sin even in his own people, and wherever he finds it he will not let it go unpunished. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

10 COMMENTARY: Therefore the sword shall never depart from David's house, because he despised God and took Uriah's wife. The punishment fits the crime's house: violence done in secret breeds violence at home. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

11 COMMENTARY: God will raise up evil against David out of his own house, taking his wives before his eyes and giving them to his neighbour. The sentence is pronounced openly, answering a sin done secretly. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

12 COMMENTARY: Thou didst it secretly, but I will do this before all Israel and before the sun. Concealment, which David bought with murder, is answered with publicity. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

13 COMMENTARY: David says to Nathan, I have sinned against the Lord, and Nathan answers, The Lord also hath put away thy sin; thou shalt not die. David says not a word to excuse himself, Henry notes, but freely owns it, and the true penitent is assured his sin is forgiven, not to die eternally nor be for ever put away from God. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

14 COMMENTARY: Howbeit, because by this deed David gave great occasion to God's enemies to blaspheme, the child born to him shall surely die. Pardon does not cancel chastening: Henry warns that even where pardon is obtained, the Lord visits transgression with the rod, and the sins of professors furnish enemies with matter for reproach. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

15 COMMENTARY: Nathan departs, and the Lord strikes the child Uriah's wife bore to David, and it is very sick. For one momentary gratification of a vile lust, Henry observes, David had to endure many days and years of extreme distress. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

16 COMMENTARY: David besought God for the child, fasted, went in, and lay all night upon the earth. God gives leave to be earnest in prayer for particular blessings from trust in his power and general mercy, even without a particular promise. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

17 COMMENTARY: The elders rise to lift him from the earth, but he will not, neither will he eat bread with them. His refusal of comfort while the child lives is the posture of a man pleading, not yet resigned. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

18 COMMENTARY: On the seventh day the child dies, and David's servants fear to tell him, reasoning that while the child lived he would not be comforted. The servants' fear shows how deep David's grief has run. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

19 COMMENTARY: David sees his servants whisper, perceives the child is dead, asks, and they answer, He is dead. The truth he feared is confirmed by a whisper before it is spoken aloud. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

20 COMMENTARY: Then David rises from the earth, washes, anoints himself, changes his apparel, comes into the house of the Lord and worships, then eats at his own house. He patiently submits to God's will in the death of one child, and the way to have creature comforts restored, Henry says, is cheerfully to resign them to God. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

21 COMMENTARY: His servants ask why he fasted and wept while the child lived, but rises and eats now the child is dead. The reversal puzzles those who watched only the outward grief. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

22 COMMENTARY: While the child was yet alive, David says, he fasted and wept, saying, Who can tell whether God will be gracious to me, that the child may live? Prayer while life remained was hope, not a bargain. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

23 COMMENTARY: But now he is dead, wherefore should I fast? Can I bring him back again? I shall go to him, but he shall not return to me. Resignation replaces pleading, and grief takes the shape of a hope beyond death. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

24 COMMENTARY: David comforts Bathsheba his wife, goes in to her, and she bears a son called Solomon, and the Lord loved him. God made up the loss to his advantage in the birth of another, Henry notes, and David, who penned Psalm 51, was willing to bear the shame of his sin ever before him. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

25 COMMENTARY: He sends by Nathan the prophet, and the child is called Jedidiah, because of the Lord. God by grace particularly owns and favours that son, ordering him to be called Beloved of the Lord. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

26 COMMENTARY: Joab fights against Rabbah of Ammon and takes the royal city. The war David neglected in Jerusalem is still being fought by his general, and now nears its end. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

27 COMMENTARY: Joab sends messengers saying he has fought against Rabbah and taken the city of waters. The report invites David to finish what his army began. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

28 COMMENTARY: Joab urges David to gather the rest of the people, encamp against the city, and take it, lest Joab take it and it be called after his name. The general yields the honour of the capture to the king. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

29 COMMENTARY: David gathers all the people, goes to Rabbah, fights against it, and takes it. The king at last goes in person to the war he once stayed home from. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

30 COMMENTARY: He takes the king's crown, a talent of gold with precious stones, and it is set on David's head, with great spoil from the city. The enemy's crown becomes the victor's, the visible end of the Ammonite war. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

31 COMMENTARY: He brings out the people and puts them under saws, harrows, and axes of iron, and through the brick-kiln, doing so to all Ammon's cities. Henry reads this severity as a sign David's heart was not yet made soft by repentance: we are most compassionate and forgiving to others when we most feel our need of the Lord's forgiving love. — Matthew Henry (Concise)
