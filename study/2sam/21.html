Study Notes — 2 Samuel

Chapter 21

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: A famine lasts three years, year after year, and David enquires of the Lord, who answers, It is for Saul and his bloody house, because he slew the Gibeonites. Every affliction arises from sin, Henry notes, and some troubles especially show they are sent to bring sin to remembrance; God's judgments often look a great way back. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

2 COMMENTARY: The king calls the Gibeonites, who are not Israelites but a remnant of the Amorites, whom Israel had sworn to, and whom Saul sought to slay in his zeal for Israel and Judah. The old oath, broken by a dead king, still binds the living nation. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

3 COMMENTARY: David asks the Gibeonites what he shall do and wherewith he shall make atonement, that they may bless the inheritance of the Lord. The king seeks not a payment but a blessing from the wronged party. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

4 COMMENTARY: The Gibeonites say they will have no silver or gold of Saul or his house, nor shall David kill any man in Israel for them, and David asks what they shall say. Money is no satisfaction for blood, Henry observes, and the Gibeonites refuse it. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

5 COMMENTARY: They answer that the man who consumed them and devised their destruction from all Israel's coasts must be answered for in his sons. Saul's posterity trod in his steps, Henry notes, for it is called a bloody house, and they are reckoned with for his sin as well as their own. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

6 COMMENTARY: Let seven men of his sons be delivered to them, they say, to be hanged to the Lord in Gibeah of Saul, whom the Lord chose, and the king says, I will give them. The number seven matches the public scale of the wrong, and the place is Saul's own city. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

7 COMMENTARY: But the king spares Mephibosheth son of Jonathan son of Saul, because of the Lord's oath between David and Jonathan. Covenant spares one son of Saul's house even while justice takes seven others. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

8 COMMENTARY: The king takes the two sons of Rizpah daughter of Aiah, whom she bore to Saul, Armoni and Mephibosheth, and the five sons of Michal daughter of Saul, whom she brought up for Adriel. The victims are named, not counted anonymously, and Rizpah's sons head the list. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

9 COMMENTARY: He delivers them to the Gibeonites, who hang them in the hill before the Lord, and all seven fall together, put to death in the days of harvest, at the beginning of barley harvest. They are sacrificed at harvest's beginning, Henry says, to turn away God's wrath, which had withheld harvest mercies for years past; in vain do we expect mercy unless we do justice upon our sins. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

10 COMMENTARY: Rizpah takes sackcloth, spreads it on the rock, and stays from harvest's beginning until water drops on the bodies from heaven, suffering neither birds by day nor beasts by night to touch them. A mother's vigil guards the dishonoured dead through the whole dry season. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

11 COMMENTARY: It is told to David what Rizpah, Saul's concubine, has done. The report of her devotion reaches the king who ordered the deaths. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

12 COMMENTARY: David goes and takes the bones of Saul and Jonathan from the men of Jabeshgilead, who stole them from the street of Bethshan where the Philistines hanged them. The bones of the first king and his son are recovered from the city that once honoured them. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

13 COMMENTARY: He brings up the bones of Saul and Jonathan, and gathers the bones of those who were hanged. The honoured dead and the executed dead are gathered together, and both receive burial. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

14 COMMENTARY: The bones of Saul and Jonathan are buried in Benjamin in Zelah, in the sepulchre of Kish his father, and all the king commanded is performed, and after that God is entreated for the land. When God sends rain, Henry notes, the bodies are buried, for then it appears God is entreated; when justice is done on earth, vengeance from heaven ceases. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

15 COMMENTARY: The Philistines have war again with Israel, and David goes down with his servants to fight, and David waxes faint. These events seem to be toward the end of David's reign, Henry observes, and even strong saints sometimes wax faint in spiritual conflict. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

16 COMMENTARY: Ishbibenob, of the sons of the giant, whose spear weighs three hundred shekels of brass, girded with a new sword, thinks to have slain David. The old king, faint in battle, faces a giant's son with a new sword. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

17 COMMENTARY: But Abishai succours David, smites the Philistine, and kills him, and David's men swear he shall go no more out with them to battle, lest he quench the light of Israel. David faints but does not flee, Henry says, and God sends help in time of need; those who stand their ground shall be relieved and made more than conquerors. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

18 COMMENTARY: Again there is battle at Gob, and Sibbechai the Hushathite slays Saph, of the sons of the giant. The giant brood is cut down one by one in successive battles. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

19 COMMENTARY: Again there is battle in Gob, where Elhanan son of Jaareoregim, a Bethlehemite, slays the brother of Goliath, whose spear staff is like a weaver's beam. The brother of the champion David slew as a youth falls to a Bethlehemite. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

20 COMMENTARY: Yet again there is battle in Gath, where a man of great stature has six fingers on each hand and six toes on each foot, four and twenty in number, also born to the giant. The catalogue of giants grows stranger, as if the old enemy keeps producing new forms. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

21 COMMENTARY: When he defies Israel, Jonathan son of Shimeah, David's brother, slays him. Defiance is answered by a kinsman of the king, not by the king himself. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

22 COMMENTARY: These four were born to the giant in Gath, and fell by the hand of David and his servants. Death, Henry reflects, is a Christian's last enemy and a son of Anak, but through him who triumphed for us, believers shall be more than conquerors at last, even over that enemy. — Matthew Henry (Concise)
