Study Notes — 2 Samuel

Chapter 24

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Again the anger of the Lord is kindled against Israel, and he moves David against them to say, Go, number Israel and Judah. God is not the author of sin, Henry insists, but as righteous Judge he permits Satan's suggestion to become the occasion for punishing Israel's own sins, as he once brought famine for Saul's sin and now pestilence for David's. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: The king tells Joab, captain of the host, to go through all Israel's tribes from Dan to Beersheba and number the people, that he may know the number. The worst thing in the numbering, Henry says, is that David does it in the pride of his heart, a proud conceit of his own greatness and a proud confidence in his own strength. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Joab answers that the Lord his God add to the people a hundredfold, however many they are, and the king's eyes may see it, but why does the king delight in this thing? Even Joab discerns David's folly and vain-glory, and respectfully tries to divert his pride. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Notwithstanding, the king's word prevails against Joab and the captains, and they go out from the king's presence to number Israel. It is an unhappiness to great men, Henry notes, to have about them those who will aid them in what is evil, and Joab applies himself with reluctancy to the unpleasing task. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: They pass over Jordan and pitch in Aroer, on the right side of the city in the midst of the river of Gad, toward Jazer. The count begins in the most distant places in the east, beyond Jordan. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Then they come to Gilead and Tahtimhodshi, to Danjaan and about to Zidon. The circuit moves northward through Gilead to Dan and toward the coast. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: They come to the stronghold of Tyre and all the cities of the Hivites and Canaanites, and go out to the south of Judah, even to Beersheba. From Tyre in the east to Beersheba in the south, the whole land is traversed. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: When they have gone through all the land, they come to Jerusalem at the end of nine months and twenty days. Above nine months are spent in the account, with great trouble and amazement occasioned in the country. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Joab gives the sum to the king: in Israel eight hundred thousand valiant men drawing the sword, and in Judah five hundred thousand men. Whether the numbers fed David's pride or mortified it we are not told, Henry observes, but the people are very many, evidence of a fruitful land maintaining thousands in a narrow compass. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: David's heart smites him after numbering the people, and he says to the Lord, I have sinned greatly; take away the iniquity of thy servant, for I have done very foolishly. During the nine months he was not sensible of his sin, Henry notes, but when the account is laid before him, his conscience awakens just when he promised himself pleasure, and it is a good sign of grace to have a heart that smites for sin. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: When David is up in the morning, the word of the Lord comes to Gad, David's seer. Gad, always at hand as confessor and counsellor, is directed by God before David can speak to him. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Go and say to David, the Lord says, I offer thee three things; choose one, that I may do it to thee. David must be corrected, Henry says, for pride is first among the seven things God hates, and the punishment must answer the sin by making fewer the people he was proud of. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Gad tells David: shall seven years of famine come, or wilt thou flee three months before thine enemies, or three days' pestilence in thy land? Now advise, and see what answer to return to him that sent me. God gives leave to choose the rod, Henry observes, to humble David more, and that he may bear the chosen rod more patiently. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: David says, I am in a great strait; let us fall now into the hand of the Lord, for his mercies are great, and let me not fall into the hand of man. Sin brings men into straits, Henry notes, and David casts himself on God, whose tender mercies are not cruel as men's are, and God chooses the shortest scourge. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: The Lord sends a pestilence on Israel from morning to the appointed time, and seventy thousand men die from Dan to Beersheba. What David made the matter of his pride, God makes the matter of his punishment. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: When the angel stretches his hand on Jerusalem to destroy it, the Lord repents of the evil and says to the destroying angel, It is enough, stay now thine hand, and the angel is by Araunah the Jebusite's threshingfloor. God repents of the judgment, Henry says, and David is thereby made more penitent. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: David speaks to the Lord when he sees the angel smiting the people: Lo, I have sinned and done wickedly, but these sheep, what have they done? Let thine hand be against me and my father's house. The shepherd-king asks that the blow fall on him, not on the flock. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Gad comes that day and tells David to go up and rear an altar to the Lord in Araunah's threshingfloor. The place where the angel stopped becomes the place of reconciliation. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: David, according to Gad's word, goes up as the Lord commanded. Obedience follows confession without delay. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Araunah looks, sees the king and his servants coming, goes out, and bows before the king with his face to the ground. The Jebusite landowner receives his conqueror as a suppliant king. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Araunah asks why the king has come to his servant, and David says, To buy the threshingfloor, to build an altar to the Lord, that the plague may be stayed from the people. The purchase has a stated purpose: an altar where judgment stopped. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: Araunah tells David to take and offer what seems good, with oxen for burnt sacrifice and threshing instruments and yokes for wood. He generously offers not only the ground but the sacrifices and implements, all gratis. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: All these things Araunah, as a king, gives to the king, and says, The Lord thy God accept thee. He gives as a king, Henry notes, with a prince's spirit, esteeming David highly and desiring the plague stayed, counting the honour of its staying at his floor a valuable consideration. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: The king answers, Nay, but I will surely buy it of thee at a price, neither will I offer burnt offerings to the Lord my God of that which costs me nothing. Two generous souls are well met, Henry says: Araunah is willing to give, but David is determined to buy, for those know not what religion is whose chief care is to make it cheap and easy to themselves. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: David builds an altar to the Lord there and offers burnt and peace offerings, so the Lord is entreated for the land and the plague is stayed from Israel. The book that began with a king's house ends with an altar on a purchased floor, and judgment gives way to entreated mercy. — Matthew Henry
