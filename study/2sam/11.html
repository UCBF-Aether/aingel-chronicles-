Study Notes — 2 Samuel

Chapter 11

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: At the time when kings go forth to battle, David sends Joab and all Israel against Ammon and besieges Rabbah, but David tarries still at Jerusalem. Henry traces the sin to neglect of business: when we are out of the way of our duty, we are in the way of temptation. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: In the evening David rises from his bed, walks on the roof, and sees a woman washing herself, very beautiful to look upon. Love of ease gave the tempter advantage, Henry notes, and a wandering eye let the sin in, as Eve's sin came in at the eye. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: David sends and enquires after the woman, and is told she is Bathsheba, daughter of Eliam, wife of Uriah the Hittite. The enquiry is the first step downhill; when men begin to do evil, Henry warns, they cannot soon stop. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: David sends messengers and takes her, lies with her, and she returns to her house. Henry counts the aggravations: David, a king entrusted with justice, wrongs Uriah, one of his own worthies now hazarding his life in the field, and Bathsheba, a lady of good reputation. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: The woman conceives and sends to tell David, I am with child. The hidden work of darkness is coming to light, and Bathsheba's message puts David under pressure to protect her and conceal what was done. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: David sends to Joab, saying, Send me Uriah the Hittite, and Joab sends him. The plan is to father the child upon Uriah himself, so that no prosecutor remains to expose the wrong. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: When Uriah comes, David demands how Joab, the people, and the war prosper. The pretended concern for the army covers a design that has nothing to do with the war. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: David tells Uriah to go down to his house and wash his feet, and sends a mess of meat after him from the king. The gift is meant to make the night at home pleasant and complete the cover. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: But Uriah sleeps at the door of the king's house with his lord's servants and does not go down to his house. His firm resolution defeats the plot, and Providence, Henry says, keeps him to it so the design's failure may awaken David's conscience. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Told Uriah did not go home, David asks why, since he came from his journey. The king's question exposes how much he needs Uriah to do the ordinary thing. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Uriah answers that the ark, Israel, and Judah abide in tents, and Joab and the servants are encamped in the open fields, so shall he go home to eat and drink and lie with his wife? Henry calls this a noble, generous resolution of a public-spirited man, mortified to the delights of sense while the army endures hardness. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: David tells Uriah to tarry today also, and tomorrow he will let him depart, so Uriah abides in Jerusalem that day and the morrow. The delay is a second chance for the cover to work. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: David calls Uriah, makes him eat and drink, and makes him drunk, yet at evening Uriah goes out to lie with his lord's servants and does not go home. Henry condemns making a man drunk as the devil's work, robbing a man of reason and drawing him into sin. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: In the morning David writes a letter to Joab and sends it by Uriah's own hand. The bearer of his own death warrant is the man whose honour David has already destroyed. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: The letter orders Joab to set Uriah in the forefront of the hottest battle and retire from him, that he may be smitten and die. Adulteries have often occasioned murders, Henry observes, and one wickedness must be covered and secured with another. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: Joab, observing the city, assigns Uriah to a place where he knows valiant men are. The general obeys an unjust order without protest, becoming party to the murder. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: The men of the city fight with Joab, some of David's servants fall, and Uriah the Hittite dies also. The innocent, valiant man ready to die for his prince's honour dies by his prince's hand. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Joab sends and tells David all the things concerning the war. The report must now be managed, for the death it carries is no ordinary casualty. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Joab charges the messenger that when he has finished telling the matters of the war, he must be ready for the king's wrath. The rehearsed message shows the killing was planned to look like a tactical error. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: If the king asks why they approached so near the city when they would shoot from the wall, the messenger is to answer with the example of Abimelech, slain by a millstone at Thebez. A precedent of folly is prepared to excuse the folly that killed Uriah. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: The rehearsal ends with the line, Thy servant Uriah the Hittite is dead also. The name Joab feared to report plainly is buried at the end of the message, as Uriah was buried in the battle. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: The messenger goes and shows David all Joab sent him for. The report is delivered exactly as ordered, and the king hears his own crime reported back as news. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: The messenger reports that the men prevailed, came out into the field, and Israel pursued them to the gate. The narrative of a hard fight sets up the deaths that follow. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: The shooters shot from the wall, some of the king's servants are dead, and Uriah the Hittite is dead also. The casualty list now includes the man David ordered killed. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: David answers that the sword devours one as well as another, and bids Joab make the battle stronger against the city. The cold maxim treats a murdered man as a routine loss of war, and Henry notes how fleshly lusts harden the heart and sear the conscience. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: When Uriah's wife hears her husband is dead, she mourns for him. The grief is real, and it falls on a woman who does not yet know the king arranged the death. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: When mourning passes, David sends, fetches her to his house, and she becomes his wife and bears him a son, but the thing David did displeased the Lord. The marriage that seems to settle everything is the very thing God condemns, and Scripture's faithfulness in recording it, Henry says, is written for our learning, that he who thinks he stands may take heed lest he fall. — Matthew Henry
