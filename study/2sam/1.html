Study Notes — 2 Samuel

Chapter 1

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The blow that opened David's way to the throne came while he was himself sorely distressed at Ziklag. Henry notes that those who commit their concerns to the Lord will quietly abide his will rather than grasp at advancement. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: The messenger arrives in torn clothes with earth on his head, the outward marks of defeat and mourning. His obeisance shows he comes to David as the man now looked to for leadership. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: David asks where the man comes from, and learns he escaped from Israel's camp. JFB observes that David's eager question sprang from generous patriotism, not from ambition for the throne. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The report is unrelieved disaster: the army fled, many fell, and Saul and Jonathan are dead. David hears in one sentence both national defeat and personal loss. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: David presses the messenger for how he knows this, testing the story before he acts on it. His caution shows he desired not Saul's death and was not impatient to take the throne. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: The young man claims he chanced upon Gilboa and found Saul leaning on his spear with chariots closing in. JFB warns this tale is a fiction invented to ingratiate himself with David, since the inspired account of Saul's death stands elsewhere. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Saul, looking behind him, calls the stranger, who answers that he is at hand. The invented detail is meant to make the messenger an eyewitness and so win David's trust. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: The man names himself an Amalekite, a people Israel was at war with and David had just fought. His identity makes his claim to have touched the king all the more perilous. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: In the man's story Saul begs to be slain because anguish has come upon him while life remains. The plea is framed to make the killing look like mercy rather than murder. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: The man says he slew Saul and brought the crown and bracelet as proof. JFB explains the crown as a small metallic cap or wreath and the bracelet as an armlet, both marks of royal dignity. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: David tears his clothes, and all his men do likewise, in sincere mourning. Henry stresses that David was sincere in grieving for Saul, not merely performing grief for advantage. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: They mourn, weep, and fast until evening for Saul, Jonathan, the people of the Lord, and Israel. Their grief humbles them under God's heavy hand on the nation, not only over two men. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: David formally asks the young man who he is, and he answers that he is the son of a stranger, an Amalekite. JFB notes the question is now put judicially, as the opening of a trial. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: David asks how the man was not afraid to destroy the Lord's anointed. His reverence for Saul as God's anointed had governed him through great temptation, and he will not excuse another for violating it. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: David orders one of his young men to fall upon the Amalekite, and he is slain. Public justice is done without regard to David's own private interest in the dead king's crown. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: David declares the man's blood is on his own head, because his own mouth testified that he slew the Lord's anointed. If he did as he said, he deserved death for treason; if he lied, his lie proved against himself. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: David then composes a lamentation over Saul and Jonathan. JFB notes it was customary in the East to celebrate great kings and warriors in funeral songs, and this elegy became a national song. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: David orders the children of Judah to be taught the use of the bow, as recorded in the book of Jasher. JFB reads this as David remedying Israel's inferiority in archery, which had helped cause the disaster, and Henry takes the song's title to be "the bow." — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: The song opens calling Israel's beauty slain on the high places, with the chorus "how are the mighty fallen." JFB explains "the beauty of Israel" as the gazelle or antelope, the Eastern type of beauty and graceful form. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: David forbids publishing the news in Gath or Askelon, lest Philistine daughters rejoice. The grief of God's people, Henry notes, is sharpened by the triumph it gives their enemies. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: David curses Gilboa with no dew or rain, because there the shield of the mighty was vilely cast away. JFB observes that casting away the shield was counted a national disgrace, and the mountains of Gilboa remain naked and sterile. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Jonathan's bow never turned back and Saul's sword never returned empty, praising their unflinching warfare. David commends Saul only for what he truly was, saying nothing of piety or goodness he did not have. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Saul and Jonathan, lovely and pleasant in life, were undivided in death, swifter than eagles and stronger than lions. Henry notes Jonathan was a dutiful son and Saul an affectionate father, dear to each other. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: The daughters of Israel are called to weep over Saul, who clothed them in scarlet and gold. JFB notes the Eastern fondness for bright dress and ornaments, and that David celebrates Saul's real good qualities alongside Jonathan's. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: The chorus returns over Jonathan himself, slain in his high places. The burden of the poem, JFB observes, is the praise of Jonathan, with which it begins and ends. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: David calls Jonathan his brother and his love wonderful, passing the love of women. Henry observes that next to the love between Christ and his people, the affection springing from it produces the strongest friendship. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: The lament closes as it opened: the mighty are fallen and the weapons of war perished. The repeated chorus fixes the nation's loss in memory rather than softening it. — Matthew Henry (Concise); Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
