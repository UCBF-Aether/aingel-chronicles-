Study Notes — 2 Samuel

Chapter 3

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The long war between the houses of Saul and David ends with David growing stronger and Saul's house weaker. Henry compares it to the believer's war, where grace grows stronger as corruption grows weaker. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

2 COMMENTARY: David's firstborn in Hebron is Amnon, by Ahinoam the Jezreelitess. The length of the war tried David's faith and patience, Henry notes, and made his settlement at last the more welcome. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

3 COMMENTARY: His second son is Chileab by Abigail, and his third Absalom by Maacah, daughter of the king of Geshur. The growing family marks the growing house, though its later divisions will bring David grief. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

4 COMMENTARY: The fourth son is Adonijah by Haggith, and the fifth Shephatiah by Abital. Each son's name records a household expanding in Hebron while the war still drags on. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

5 COMMENTARY: The sixth son is Ithream by Eglah, completing the list of sons born to David in Hebron. The family record closes the first section as evidence of a house waxing stronger. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

6 COMMENTARY: While the war continues, Abner makes himself strong for the house of Saul. The strongest man in Saul's camp is building his own power inside a cause that is already failing. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

7 COMMENTARY: Ishbosheth accuses Abner of going in to Saul's concubine Rizpah. The charge touches Abner's honour, and proud men, Henry notes, are often too proud to bear even the suspicion of guilt. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

8 COMMENTARY: Abner, enraged, protests that he has shown kindness to Saul's house against Judah and has not delivered Ishbosheth into David's hand. His anger reveals a servant who served himself while claiming loyalty. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

9 COMMENTARY: Abner swears to do for David as the Lord has sworn, transferring the kingdom to him. Men who fight against God while pursuing their own purposes, Henry observes, are still used by God for his designs, then thrown aside. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

10 COMMENTARY: Abner names the goal plainly: David's throne over Israel and Judah, from Dan to Beersheba. The oath turns a personal quarrel into the public transfer of the whole kingdom. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

11 COMMENTARY: Ishbosheth cannot answer Abner a word, because he fears him. A king who depends on his general's strength is powerless the moment that general turns. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

12 COMMENTARY: Abner sends messengers to David asking whose the land is, and offering his hand to bring all Israel to him. The man who made Ishbosheth king now bargains away the kingdom he built. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

13 COMMENTARY: David agrees to a league, but requires first the return of Michal, Saul's daughter. Henry sees real generosity in this, remembering that David once owed his life to her affection. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

14 COMMENTARY: David demands Michal from Ishbosheth, recalling the hundred Philistine foreskins he paid as her price. The demand asserts his lawful marriage and his standing as Saul's son-in-law. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

15 COMMENTARY: Ishbosheth takes Michal from her husband Phaltiel and sends her to David. Political settlement rides roughshod over a household, and the cost is borne by others. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

16 COMMENTARY: Phaltiel follows weeping to Bahurim until Abner sends him back. The small, human grief beside the great treaty reminds the reader what power arrangements cost ordinary people. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

17 COMMENTARY: Abner tells Israel's elders that they sought David as king in times past. He now uses the Lord's own promise for David as his argument, having resisted it while it served him not to. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

18 COMMENTARY: Abner urges them to act now, because the Lord has spoken that by David he will save Israel from the Philistines and all enemies. The promise Abner once fought becomes the ground of his defection. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

19 COMMENTARY: Abner speaks also to Benjamin and then reports to David in Hebron what seemed good to Israel and to Benjamin. The tribe of Saul's own house is brought into the settlement, not bypassed. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

20 COMMENTARY: Abner comes to David with twenty men, and David makes them a feast. The former enemies eat together in Hebron, sealing the league with hospitality. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

21 COMMENTARY: Abner promises to gather all Israel to make a league with David, that he may reign over all his heart desires, and David sends him away in peace. The kingdom seems within reach without another battle. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

22 COMMENTARY: David's servants and Joab return from a raid with spoil, but Abner is already gone in peace. Joab arrives too late to stop a peace he did not make, and his resentment builds. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

23 COMMENTARY: Joab is told Abner came to the king and was sent away in peace. The report strikes at Joab's pride and his private grievance over Asahel. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

24 COMMENTARY: Joab confronts David, asking why he sent Abner away quite gone. His protest dresses revenge as statecraft, accusing Abner of coming to deceive and spy. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

25 COMMENTARY: Joab insists Abner came to know David's goings out and comings in and all he does. Suspicion is Joab's weapon against a rival he cannot defeat in open argument. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

26 COMMENTARY: Without David's knowledge, Joab sends messengers to bring Abner back from the well of Sirah. The secret recall undoes the king's public peace before David knows it is threatened. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

27 COMMENTARY: Joab takes Abner aside in the gate to speak quietly and smites him under the fifth rib for the blood of Asahel. Henry is blunt: Joab acted wickedly, whatever justice he claimed for his brother. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

28 COMMENTARY: David declares himself and his kingdom guiltless for ever from Abner's blood. Publicly disowning the murder is the only course left to a king whose general has acted behind his back. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

29 COMMENTARY: David lays the guilt on Joab's head and his father's house, with a curse of lasting affliction. Henry notes the guilt of blood brings a curse upon families, and if men do not avenge it, God will. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

30 COMMENTARY: Joab and Abishai slew Abner because he had slain Asahel at Gibeon. Revenge for a battlefield death is carried out as a private murder in peacetime. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

31 COMMENTARY: David orders Joab and all the people to rend their clothes, gird sackcloth, and mourn before Abner, following the bier himself. The king's public mourning clears him before all Israel. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

32 COMMENTARY: Abner is buried in Hebron, and David weeps aloud at the grave with all the people. Genuine grief for a fallen enemy becomes the proof of David's innocence. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

33 COMMENTARY: David laments, asking whether Abner died as a fool dies. Henry notes it is sad to die like a fool, as do those who shorten their own days and make no provision for another world. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

34 COMMENTARY: Abner's hands were not bound nor his feet fettered; he fell as a man falls before wicked men. The lament insists Abner was murdered, not defeated in fair combat. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

35 COMMENTARY: David swears he will taste no bread until sunset, and the people are pleased. His fasting shows a grief that is personal, not merely a state ceremony. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

36 COMMENTARY: All the people took notice, and whatever the king did pleased them. A king's visible integrity, Henry observes, is noticed by all his willing people and pleases them. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

37 COMMENTARY: All Israel understood that day that it was not of the king to slay Abner. The public mourning achieved what a denial alone could not: the nation believed David. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

38 COMMENTARY: David tells his servants that a prince and a great man has fallen that day in Israel. Even a defector and enemy is honoured for the stature he held. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

39 COMMENTARY: David confesses he is weak though anointed king, and the sons of Zeruiah are too hard for him, leaving vengeance to the Lord. Henry notes David ought to have done his duty and trusted God with the issue, while carnal policy spared Joab; the Son of David may long delay, but never fails to punish impenitent sinners. — Matthew Henry (Concise)
