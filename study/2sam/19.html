Study Notes — 2 Samuel

Chapter 19

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: It is told to Joab that the king weeps and mourns for Absalom. The army's commander learns the victory is being mourned by the king it was won for. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

2 COMMENTARY: The victory that day turns into mourning for all the people, for they hear how the king is grieved for his son. A nation's deliverance is spoiled by its king's private grief. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

3 COMMENTARY: The people steal into the city that day as people ashamed steal away when they flee in battle. Victors enter like the defeated, because their king treats the day as a defeat. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

4 COMMENTARY: The king covers his face and cries aloud, O my son Absalom, O Absalom, my son, my son! To continue lamenting so bad a son, Henry says, was very unwise and unworthy. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

5 COMMENTARY: Joab comes into the house and tells the king he has shamed the faces of his servants, who saved his life and his family's lives that day. The reproof is plain and deserved, though Henry notes Joab censures without proper respect to his sovereign. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

6 COMMENTARY: Joab charges that David loves his enemies and hates his friends, declaring he regards neither princes nor servants. The king's grief, in Joab's eyes, tells his army their sacrifice counted for nothing. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

7 COMMENTARY: Joab swears that unless David arises, goes forth, and speaks comfortably to his servants, not one will tarry with him that night. The threat is blunt: a king who will not thank his army will lose it. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

8 COMMENTARY: The king arises and sits in the gate, and all the people come before the king, while Israel flees to his tent. David takes the reproof and counsel prudently and mildly, Henry notes, and timely giving way prevents the ill effects of mistaken measures. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

9 COMMENTARY: All the people are at strife through the tribes, saying the king saved them from their enemies and delivered them from the Philistines, yet now fled from Absalom. The tribes rehearse David's record as the ground for his recall. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

10 COMMENTARY: Absalom, whom they anointed over them, is dead in battle, and they ask why no word is spoken of bringing the king back. With the usurper dead, the obstacle to return is gone. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

11 COMMENTARY: David sends to Zadok and Abiathar, bidding them speak to Judah's elders, asking why they are last to bring the king back to his house. The priests' persuasion, Henry says, is part of God's providence bringing the people to resolve the recall. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

12 COMMENTARY: Ye are my brethren, my bones and flesh, David says; why then are ye last to bring back the king? Judah, his own tribe, is shamed for lagging behind the rest. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

13 COMMENTARY: Say to Amasa, Art thou not of my bone and flesh? God do so to me if thou be not captain of the host before me continually in Joab's room. Amasa's interest is engaged, and the rebel commander is bought into the restoration. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

14 COMMENTARY: Amasa bows the heart of all Judah as the heart of one man, and they send word to the king: Return, and all thy servants. David stirs not till he receives this invitation, Henry observes, as the Lord Jesus will rule in those who invite him to the throne of their hearts. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

15 COMMENTARY: The king returns to Jordan, and Judah comes to Gilgal to meet him and conduct him over. The return begins with his own tribe escorting him across the river. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

16 COMMENTARY: Shimei son of Gera hastens down with Judah to meet David. The man who cursed the fleeing king is first among the penitents to greet the returning one. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

17 COMMENTARY: A thousand Benjamites are with Shimei, and Ziba with fifteen sons and twenty servants, and they pass over Jordan before the king. The delegation is large, and it includes the flatterer whose lie cost Mephibosheth his estate. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

18 COMMENTARY: A ferry boat goes over to carry the king's household and do what he thinks good, and Shimei falls down before the king as he crosses Jordan. The penitent meets the king at the river, the boundary of exile and return. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

19 COMMENTARY: Shimei begs the king not to impute iniquity nor remember what his servant did perversely the day the king went out of Jerusalem. Those who now slight the Son of David, Henry warns, would gladly make peace when he comes in glory, but it will be too late; Shimei, by contrast, loses no time. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

20 COMMENTARY: Shimei confesses he has sinned, and says he comes first this day of all the house of Joseph to meet the king. First repentance is offered as evidence of sincerity. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

21 COMMENTARY: Abishai answers, Shall not Shimei be put to death for cursing the Lord's anointed? The soldier who once asked to take Shimei's head asks again, now with a legal charge. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

22 COMMENTARY: David asks what he has to do with the sons of Zeruiah, that they should be adversaries this day, and whether any man shall be put to death this day in Israel, for does he not know he is king again? The day of return is a day of amnesty, and David will not stain it with an execution. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

23 COMMENTARY: The king tells Shimei, Thou shalt not die, and swears to him. Shimei's abuse was personal, Henry notes, and with the usual right feeling of good men, David could more easily forgive it. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

24 COMMENTARY: Mephibosheth son of Saul comes down to meet the king, having neither dressed his feet, nor trimmed his beard, nor washed his clothes since the king departed. His unkempt state is his testimony: he has mourned the king's absence the whole time. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

25 COMMENTARY: When he comes to Jerusalem, the king asks why he did not go with him. The question puts Ziba's lie to the test, and Mephibosheth's appearance already answers it. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

26 COMMENTARY: Mephibosheth answers that his servant deceived him, for he said he would saddle an ass to ride to the king, because his servant is lame. The lame man was betrayed by the servant meant to carry him. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

27 COMMENTARY: Ziba slandered his servant to the king, Mephibosheth says, but the king is as an angel of God, so let him do what is good in his eyes. He submits his cause entirely to the king's discernment. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

28 COMMENTARY: All his father's house were but dead men before the king, yet David set him among those eating at his table, so what right has he to cry any more to the king? Gratitude for past kindness outweighs the claim for present justice. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

29 COMMENTARY: The king says, Why speakest thou any more of thy matters? Thou and Ziba divide the land. David recalls the forfeiture in part, splitting the estate between the slanderer and the slandered rather than fully restoring it. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

30 COMMENTARY: Mephibosheth answers, Yea, let him take all, forasmuch as the king is come again in peace to his own house. A good man, Henry notes, contentedly bears his own losses while he sees Israel in peace and the Son of David exalted. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

31 COMMENTARY: Barzillai the Gileadite comes down from Rogelim and goes over Jordan with the king to conduct him over. The aged benefactor of Mahanaim escorts the king he fed in exile. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

32 COMMENTARY: Barzillai is fourscore years old, and provided the king sustenance at Mahanaim, for he is a very great man. The service was great, and the man who gave it counts it an honour, Henry observes. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

33 COMMENTARY: The king invites Barzillai to come over with him, and he will feed him in Jerusalem. Royal gratitude offers the court as a home for old age. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

34 COMMENTARY: Barzillai asks how long he has to live, that he should go up with the king to Jerusalem. The question is not ingratitude but a reckoning of remaining days. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

35 COMMENTARY: At fourscore he cannot discern good and evil, taste what he eats or drinks, or hear singers any more, and asks why he should be a burden to the king. A good man, Henry says, would not go anywhere to be burdensome, and would rather be so to his own house than to another's. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

36 COMMENTARY: He will go a little way over Jordan with the king, and asks why the king should recompense him with such a reward. A short escort is all he offers, and he counts even that beyond desert. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

37 COMMENTARY: Let him turn back, he prays, that he may die in his own city and be buried by his father and mother's grave, but behold his servant Chimham, let him go over with the king. It is good, Henry notes, especially for old people, to think and speak much of dying: the grave is ready, let me go and get ready for it. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

38 COMMENTARY: The king answers that Chimham shall go over with him, and he will do to him what seems good to Barzillai, and whatever Barzillai requires he will do. The son of the benefactor inherits the king's favour. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

39 COMMENTARY: All the people go over Jordan, the king kisses Barzillai and blesses him, and Barzillai returns to his own place. Parting is sealed with a kiss and a blessing, and the old man goes home to die. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

40 COMMENTARY: The king goes on to Gilgal with Chimham, conducted by all Judah and half of Israel. The escort itself, Judah whole and Israel half, foreshadows the quarrel to come. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

41 COMMENTARY: All Israel comes to the king asking why Judah stole him away and brought him and his household over Jordan. The tribes feel despised, having been left out of the escort. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

42 COMMENTARY: Judah answers that the king is near of kin to them, asking why they are angry, and whether they have eaten at the king's cost or taken a gift. Kinship is pleaded as a right, and profit denied. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

43 COMMENTARY: Israel answers that they have ten parts in the king and more right in David than Judah, asking why Judah despised them and did not speak first of bringing back the king. The fiercer words of Judah, Henry warns, produce very bad effects, and much evil might be avoided if men watched against pride and remembered that a soft answer turns away wrath. — Matthew Henry (Concise)
