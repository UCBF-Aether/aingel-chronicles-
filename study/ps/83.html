Study Notes — Psalms

Psalm 83

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Silence, peace, and stillness are three ways of saying God seems to connive: Henry reads the prayer as asking God to speak by prophets for encouragement, by providence against enemies, and by deliverance for his people, since with God saying and doing are one. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

2 COMMENTARY: Tumult and lifted head name heat, pride, and noise: enemies talk big, hoping to run God's people down with clamour, which is itself the reason to ask God to speak in wrath. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

3 COMMENTARY: Crafty counsel against hidden ones invokes the serpent's subtlety: God's people are hidden in secrecy, life hid with Christ and unknown to the world, and in safety under special protection, yet enemies consult to ruin whom God resolves to preserve. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

4 COMMENTARY: Cut off from being a nation, name no more remembered, is extirpation aimed at memory itself: Henry sees the wish that church and religion have no being in the world, Bibles and records destroyed with the people. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

5 COMMENTARY: One consent among clashing interests is Satan's kingdom undivided: Henry turns the confederacy into a rebuke, that if Herod and Pilate can be made friends to crucify, church friends should be unanimous to serve. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

6 COMMENTARY: Names listed are a roll of broken bonds: Edomites and Ishmaelites, descendants of Abraham, lead, and apostates from the church, Henry notes, have been its bitterest enemies. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

7 COMMENTARY: Gebal, and Ammon, and Amalek, the Philistines with the inhabitants of Tyre, names the nations drawn into the confederacy: the Edomites and Ishmaelites, both descendants from Abraham, lead the van — for apostates from the church have been its most bitter and spiteful enemies, witness Julian. These were allied to Israel in blood and yet in alliance against Israel. There are no bonds of nature so strong but the spirit of persecution has broken through them: the brother shall betray the brother to death. Moab and Ammon were the children of righteous Lot but, as an incestuous, so a degenerate race. The Philistines were long a thorn in Israel's side, and very vexatious. How the inhabitants of Tyre, who in David's time were Israel's firm allies, come in among their enemies, I know not — but that Assur, that is, the Assyrian, also is joined with them is not strange, or that, as the word is, they were an arm to the children of Lot. See how numerous the enemies of God's church have always been: Lord, how are those increased that trouble it! God's heritage was as a speckled bird, all the birds round about were against her — which highly magnifies the power of God in preserving to himself a church in the world, in spite of the combined force of earth and hell. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

8 COMMENTARY: Assur joined as an arm to Lot's children shows how numerous the church's enemies have always been: a speckled bird with all birds round about against her magnifies the power preserving a church in the world. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

9 COMMENTARY: Midian, Sisera, and Jabin at Kishon are precedents to plead: former defeats may be prayed back, because God is the same still to his people and against their enemies, with no variableness. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

10 COMMENTARY: Which perished at Endor, they became as dung for the earth, is the precedent prayed from: he prays that their armies might be destroyed as the armies of former enemies had been. Do to them as to the Midianites — let them be routed by their own fears, for so the Midianites were, more than by Gideon's 300 men. Do to them as to the army under Sisera, general under Jabin king of Canaan, which God discomfited at the brook Kishon, near to which was Endor. They became as dung on the earth: their dead bodies were thrown like dung laid in heaps, or spread, to fatten the ground; they were trodden to dirt by Barak's small but victorious army. And this was fitly made a precedent here, because Deborah made it so to aftertimes when it was fresh: So let all thy enemies perish, O Lord! — that is, So they shall perish. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

11 COMMENTARY: Nobles like Oreb and Zeeb, princes like Zebah and Zalmunna, target leaders because princes set the people on: their malice was covetous, taking God's pleasant places for themselves, and their lot is to be made easy prey as those princes were. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

12 COMMENTARY: Who said, Let us take to ourselves the houses of God in possession, is the princes' malice: they said, Let us take to ourselves the houses of God in possession — the pleasant places of God, so the word is — by which we may understand the land of Canaan, which was a pleasant land and was Immanuel's land, or the temple, which was indeed God's pleasant place, or, as Dr. Hammond suggests, the pleasant pastures, which these Arabians, who traded in cattle, did in a particular manner seek after. The princes and nobles aimed to enrich themselves by this war, and their armies must be made as dung for the earth, to serve their covetousness and their ambition. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

13 COMMENTARY: O my God, make them like a wheel, as the stubble before the wind, is the similitude prayed: that God would make them like a wheel, that they might be in continual motion, unquiet, unsettled, and giddy in all their counsels and resolves, that they might roll down easily and speedily to their own ruin. Or, as some think, that they might be broken by the judgments of God, as the corn is broken, or beaten out, by the wheel which was then used in threshing. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

14 COMMENTARY: Fire in wood and flame on mountains are similitudes of a tempest pursued: Henry's prayer is that enemies be driven as stubble before wind, unquiet and unsettled in counsel until ruin. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

15 COMMENTARY: The tempest is not mere scattering, like stubble before wind, but pursuit to utter ruin: God's storm raises terrors in sinners' own hearts, and so makes them completely miserable. Henry's point is that God can deal with the proudest, most daring defier of his justice and make him afraid as a grasshopper — it is the torment of devils that they tremble. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

16 COMMENTARY: Fill their faces with shame, that they may seek thy name, O Lord — judgment aimed at repentance. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

17 COMMENTARY: Confounded, troubled forever, shamed, and perishing close the imprecation as doom, not mere anger: Henry reads the prayer as prophecy reaching all enemies of the gospel church, whoever opposes Christ's kingdom. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

18 COMMENTARY: If shame does not convert them, its perfecting will still redound to God's glory, so that other men may know and own what the enemies themselves will not. Henry spells out the three truths proved by God's triumphs: JEHOVAH is self-existent and self-sufficient, he is Most High above all gods and kings, and his kingdom rules over all the earth, even nations that do not know him. — Matthew Henry (Complete)
