Psalm 88

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The psalm's only words of comfort stand at its head: before any complaint, Heman calls God the God of his salvation, looking for salvation from God and depending on him as its author. Henry's portrait is of a man of prayer at all times, especially in affliction, crying day and night and stretching out his hands as one who would take hold of mercy, fearing to come short of it. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

2 COMMENTARY: Prayer is asked to come before God to be accepted, not before men to be seen, as the Pharisees' prayers were. Henry's point is the referral itself: incline thy ear to my cry, and give what answer thou pleasest — the petitioner does not dictate the answer, he leaves himself to God's ear. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

3 COMMENTARY: For my soul is full of troubles, and my life draweth nigh unto the grave, is a man of sorrows, and therefore some make him, in this psalm, a type of Christ, whose complaints on the cross, and sometimes before, were much to the same purport with this psalm. He cries out: My soul is full of troubles; so Christ said, Now is my soul troubled; and, in his agony, My soul is exceedingly sorrowful even unto death, like the psalmist's here, for he says, My life draws nigh unto the grave. Heman was a very wise man, and a very good man, a man of God, and a singer too, and one may therefore suppose him to have been a man of a cheerful spirit, and yet now a man of sorrowful spirit, troubled in mind, and upon the brink of despair. Inward trouble is the sorest trouble, and that which, sometimes, the best of God's saints and servants have been severely exercised with. The spirit of man, of the greatest of men, will not always sustain his infirmity, but will droop and sink under it; who then can bear a wounded spirit? — Matthew Henry (Complete)

4 COMMENTARY: Counted already with those going down to the pit, a man without strength, Heman is a man of sorrows on the brink of despair, and Henry makes him a type of Christ, whose soul was troubled and exceedingly sorrowful even unto death. Inward trouble is the sorest trouble: the spirit of even the greatest man will droop and sink under it, and who then can bear a wounded spirit? — Matthew Henry (Complete)

5 COMMENTARY: Counted among the dead, like slain in the grave remembered no more and cut off from the supporting hand, is the sufferer's dark apprehension: Henry stresses it as melancholy's conclusion, not doctrine, since the man still cries day and night to the God of his salvation. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

6 COMMENTARY: Laid in the lowest pit, in darkness and in the deeps, he is as low as possible — condition low, spirits low, sinking with no way of escape open, brought to the last extremity and ready to give up all for gone. Henry warns that good men may thus form dismal apprehensions and dark conclusions about their afflictions through the power of melancholy and the weakness of faith. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

7 COMMENTARY: Thy wrath lieth hard upon me, and thou hast afflicted me with all thy waves, is the psalmist complaining most of God's displeasure against him, which infused the wormwood and the gall into the affliction and the misery: Thy wrath lies hard upon me. Could he have discerned the favour and love of God in his affliction, it would have lain light upon him; but it lay hard, very hard, upon him, so that he was ready to sink and faint under it. The impressions of this wrath upon his spirits were God's waves with which he afflicted him, which rolled upon him, one on the neck of another, so that he scarcely recovered from one dark thought before he was oppressed with another; these waves beat against him with noise and fury; not some, but all, of God's waves were made use of in afflicting him and bearing him down. Even the children of God's love may sometimes apprehend themselves children of wrath, and no outward trouble can lie so hard upon them as that apprehension. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

8 COMMENTARY: Friends deserting him gives occasion not to accuse them of treachery, but to complain to God with an eye to his hand: providence had removed them, made them incapable of service, or alienated their affections, for every creature is to us only what God makes it to be. Worse, he is made an abomination to them — not merely shy of him but sick of him, looked on with abhorrence because poor and in distress, like a vessel in which there is no pleasure. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

9 COMMENTARY: The eye mourns, yet weeping must not hinder praying: we must sow in tears, and prayers and tears together shall be accepted together, as God says, 'I have heard thy prayers, I have seen thy tears.' Henry pairs this with the daily, day-and-night calling and the hands stretched out to catch at mercy, so that grief vents itself without displacing prayer. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

10 COMMENTARY: Wilt thou shew wonders to the dead? shall the dead arise and praise thee? is the psalmist expostulating with God concerning the present deplorable condition he was in: Wilt thou do a miraculous work to the dead, and raise them to life again? Shall those that are dead and buried rise up to praise thee? No; they leave it to their children to rise up in their room to praise God; none expects that they should do it; and wherefore should they rise, wherefore should they live, but to praise God? The life we are born to at first, and the life we hope to rise to at last, must thus be spent. But shall thy lovingkindness to thy people be declared in the grave, either by those or to those that lie buried there? And thy faithfulness to thy promise, shall that be told in destruction? Shall thy wonders be wrought in the dark, or known there, and thy righteousness in the grave, which is the land of forgetfulness, where men remember nothing, nor are themselves remembered? Departed souls may indeed know God's wonders and declare his faithfulness, justice, and lovingkindness; but deceased bodies cannot; they can neither receive God's favours in comfort nor return them in praise. Now we will not suppose these expostulations to be the language of despair, as if he thought God could not help him or would not, much less do they imply any disbelief of the resurrection of the dead at the last day; but he thus pleads with God for speedy relief: Lord, thou art good, thou art faithful, thou art righteous; these attributes of thine will be made known in my deliverance, but, if it be not hastened, it will come too late; for I shall be dead and past relief, dead and not capable of receiving any comfort, very shortly. Job often pleaded thus. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

11 COMMENTARY: Shall lovingkindness be declared in the grave, or faithfulness in destruction? Henry insists this is not despair nor disbelief in the resurrection, but a plea for speedy relief: God's goodness, faithfulness and righteousness will be made known in deliverance, but if it is not hastened it will come too late, when he is dead and past relief, incapable of receiving comfort — the plea Job often made. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

12 COMMENTARY: Wonders in the dark and righteousness in the land of forgetfulness press the same plea: departed souls may indeed know and declare God's wonders, faithfulness and lovingkindness, but deceased bodies can neither receive his favours in comfort nor return them in praise. The life we are born to and the life we hope to rise to must be spent praising God, and that is why relief must come while praise is still possible. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

13 COMMENTARY: Because deliverance is deferred, he resolves to pray the more, not less: though prayers are not answered immediately, we must not give over, for the vision is for an appointed time and at the end shall speak. Morning prayer 'prevents' God not by waking sooner than one who neither slumbers nor sleeps, but by rising earlier than usual, before the day's business crowds in — God often prevents our prayers with mercies, so let us prevent his mercies with prayers. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

14 COMMENTARY: Why cast off his soul, why hide his face? Henry hears wonder in the question: that God should cast off an old servant who is resolved not to cast him off, when his gifts and callings are without repentance. Nothing grieves a child of God so much as God's hiding his face, nor is anything so dreaded as his casting off the soul — if a clouded sun darkens the earth, a sun that abandons it leaves a dungeon. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

15 COMMENTARY: Afflicted and ready to die from his youth up, he suffers terrors that distract him: continual frightful apprehensions of God's wrath, terror at falling into his hands and receiving his doom, perspiring and trembling at divine displeasure. Henry notes even those designed for God's favours may for a time suffer his terrors, and that the terrors almost took his reason, putting him out of possession of his own soul — a piteous case to be met with compassion, and a yoke borne from youth that breeds habitual seriousness. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

16 COMMENTARY: Fierce wrath going over him and terrors cutting him off are, in Henry's explanation, the same thing: God's terrors are his fierce wrath fastening on a guilty conscience, and they had almost taken his life, leaving him ready to give up the ghost. What is hell, he asks, but that eternal excision by which damned sinners are for ever cut off from God and all happiness? — Matthew Henry (Complete)

17 COMMENTARY: The terrors came round daily like water and compassed him together, so that from every side he was assaulted with a variety of troubles and had no comfortable gale from any point of the compass. They broke in together like an inundation, daily and all the day, with no rest, no respite, no breathing-time, no lucid intervals and no gleam of hope — a wise and good man surrounded until he could find no shelter or lee anywhere. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

18 COMMENTARY: Lover and friend hast thou put far from me, and mine acquaintance into darkness — the psalm ends unrelieved, yet still addressed to God. — Matthew Henry (Complete)
