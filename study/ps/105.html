Study Notes — Psalms

Psalm 105

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Giving thanks, calling upon his name, and making known his deeds among the people are the first duties of praise: give thanks to him as one who has always been our bountiful benefactor and requires only thanks for his favours — poor returns for rich receivings. Call upon his name as one you depend upon for further favours, for praying for further mercies is accepted as acknowledgment of former mercies: because he has inclined his ear unto me, therefore will I call upon him. And make known his deeds, that others may join with you in praising him. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

2 COMMENTARY: Singing unto him, singing psalms, and talking of all his wondrous works continue the call: sing psalms to God's honour as those who rejoice in him and desire to testify that joy for others' encouragement and to transmit it to posterity, as memorable things anciently were handed down by songs when writing was scarce. And talk of all his wondrous works as we talk of things we are full of and much affected with, desiring to fill others with them — God's wondrous works ought to be the subject of our familiar discourses with families and friends, as we sit in the house and as we go by the way, not merely for entertainment but for exciting devotion and encouraging our own and others' faith and hope in God, and even sacred things may be the matter of common talk, provided it be with due reverence. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

3 COMMENTARY: Glorying in his holy name, and letting the hearts of them rejoice that seek the Lord, means: let those disposed to glory not boast of their own accomplishments and achievements but of their acquaintance with God and their relation to him — praise his holy name comes to the same thing, for in glorying in him we give glory to him. And let the hearts of seekers rejoice, for they have chosen well, are well fixed and well employed, and may be sure their labour will not be in vain, for he will not only be found but will be found the rewarder of those that diligently seek him — and if those have reason to rejoice that seek the Lord, much more those that have found him. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

4 COMMENTARY: Seeking the Lord and his strength, and seeking his face evermore, means placing your happiness in him and then pursuing that happiness in all the ways he has appointed: seek the Lord and his strength, that is, the ark of his strength, seeking him in the sanctuary, in the way he has appointed us to seek him; seek his strength, that is, his grace, the strength of his Spirit to work in you what is good, which we cannot do but by strength derived from him, for which he will be enquired of — those who would be strengthened in the inward man must fetch strength from God by faith and prayer. Seek his strength, and then seek his face, for by his strength we hope to prevail with him for his favour, as Jacob did; and seek his face evermore — seek to have his favour to eternity, and therefore continue seeking it to the end of your probation, seeking it while you live in this world and you shall have it while you live in the other world, and even there shall be for ever seeking it in infinite progression and yet for ever satisfied in it. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

5 COMMENTARY: Remembering his marvellous works, his wonders, and the judgments of his mouth is the argument to quicken us to praise: consider both what he has said and what he has done to engage us for ever to him, and you will see yourselves under all possible obligations to give thanks and call upon his name, if you remember the wonders which should make deep and durable impressions upon you — the wonders of his providence which he wrought for you and those gone before you, the marvellous works that will be had in everlasting remembrance with the thoughtful and the grateful, and the wonders of his law, which he has written to you and entrusted you with, the judgments of his mouth as well as the judgments of his hand. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

6 COMMENTARY: O ye seed of Abraham his servant, ye children of Jacob his chosen, is the relation we stand in to him: you are the seed of Abraham his servant, born in his house, and thereby entitled to the privilege of his servants, protection and provision, and also bound to the duty of servants — to attend your Master, consult his honour, obey his commands, and do what you can to advance his interests. You are the children of Jacob his chosen, chosen and beloved for the fathers' sake, and therefore ought to tread in the steps of those whose honours you inherit. You are the children of godly parents — do not degenerate. You are God's church upon earth, and if you do not praise him, who should? — Matthew Henry (Complete)

7 COMMENTARY: He is the Lord our God, his judgments are in all the earth, is our interest in him: we depend upon him, are devoted to him, and from him our expectation is — should not a people seek unto their God and praise their God? He is Jehovah our God, self-existent and self-sufficient, of irresistible power and incontestable sovereignty: his judgments are in all the earth, for he governs the whole world in wisdom and gives law to all nations, even to those that know him not, and the earth is full of the proofs of his power. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

8 COMMENTARY: Remembering his covenant for ever, the word which he commanded to a thousand generations, is the first subject of praise: in all the marvellous works God did for Israel he remembered his covenant, and he will remember it for ever, for it is the word which he commanded to a thousand generations. See the power of the promise — it is the word he commanded and which will take effect; and the perpetuity of the promise — commanded to a thousand generations, its entail shall not be cut off. In the parallel place it is expressed as our duty, be ye mindful always of his covenant: God will not forget it, and therefore we must not. The promise is called a covenant because there was something required on man's part as the condition of the promise. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

9 COMMENTARY: The covenant which he made with Abraham, and his oath unto Isaac, names the persons with whom it was made — grandfather, father and son, all eminent believers — and its ratifications, made sure by all that is sacred: is that sure which is sworn to? It is his oath to Isaac and to Abraham, for God swore by himself. Is that sure which has passed into a law? He confirmed the same for a law, a law never to be repealed. Is that sure which is reduced to a mutual contract and stipulation? This is confirmed for an everlasting covenant, inviolable. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

10 COMMENTARY: Confirming the same unto Jacob for a law, and to Israel for an everlasting covenant, carries the same ratifications forward: a law never to be repealed, and an everlasting covenant, inviolable — the promise made sure by oath, by law, and by mutual contract alike, so that its entail shall not be cut off. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

11 COMMENTARY: Saying, unto thee will I give the land of Canaan, the lot of your inheritance, is the covenant itself: the patriarchs had a right to it, not by providence but by promise, and their seed should be put in possession of it, not by the common ways of settling nations but by miracles — God will give it to them himself, as it were with his own hand, as their lot which he assigns and measures out to them, a sure title by virtue of their birth, coming to them by descent and not by purchase, by God's favour and not any merit of their own. Heaven is the inheritance we have obtained, and this is the promise God has promised us, even eternal life, as Canaan was the promise he promised them. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

12 COMMENTARY: When they were but a few men in number, yea, very few, and strangers in it, shows how exposed the patriarchs were to injuries: to the three renowned patriarchs God's promises were very rich, yet his performances in this world were so little proportionable that, if he had not prepared for them a city in the other world, he would have been ashamed to be called their God — yet even in this world he was not wanting to them, but that he might appear to do uncommon things for them he exercised them with uncommon trials. They were few, very few: Abraham was called alone, had but two sons and cast one out; Isaac had but two, and one was forced for many years to flee his country; Jacob had more, but some of them, instead of being a defence, exposed him when, as he himself pleads, he was but few in number and therefore might easily be destroyed by the natives. God's chosen are but a little flock, few, very few, and yet upheld. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

13 COMMENTARY: When they went from one nation to another, from one kingdom to another people, shows their unsettled state: they were strangers, and therefore most likely to be abused and meet with strange usage, and the less able to help themselves, their religion making them looked upon as strangers and hooted at as speckled birds — and though the whole land was theirs by promise, yet they were so far from producing and pleading their grant that they confessed themselves strangers in it. They went from one nation to another, from one part of that land to another, for it was then held by divers nations, nay, from one kingdom to another people, from Canaan to Egypt, from Egypt to the Philistines, which could not but weaken and expose them, yet they were forced to it by famine. Though frequent removals are neither desirable nor commendable, yet sometimes there is a just and necessary occasion for them, and they may be the lot of some of the best men. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

14 COMMENTARY: He suffered no man to do them wrong, yea, he reproved kings for their sakes, shows how they were guarded by special providence, whose wisdom and power were the more magnified by their being so many ways exposed: they were not able to help themselves, and yet no men were suffered to wrong them — even those that hated them and would gladly have done them mischief had their hands tied and could not do what they would, as the terror of God was upon the cities round about them, an unaccountable restraint, so that though provoked they did not pursue after the sons of Jacob. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

15 COMMENTARY: Saying, touch not mine anointed, and do my prophets no harm, shows even crowned heads checked and chidden for the patriarchs' sakes: he reproved kings in dreams and visions, saying it is at your peril if you touch them, nay, it shall not be in your power to do it. Pharaoh king of Egypt was plagued and Abimelech king of Gerar sharply rebuked for wronging Abraham. Even kings themselves are liable to God's rebukes if they do wrong; God's prophets are his anointed, for they have the unction of the Spirit, that oil of gladness; those who offer to touch God's prophets with design to harm them may expect to hear of it one way or other, for God is jealous for his prophets — whoso touches them touches the apple of his eye; even those that touch the prophets, nay that kill the prophets, cannot do them any real harm; and God's anointed prophets are dearer to him than anointed kings themselves, as Jeroboam's hand was withered when stretched out against a prophet. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

16 COMMENTARY: Moreover he called for a famine upon the land, he brake the whole staff of bread, shows them reduced to great extremity even in Canaan, the land of promise: all judgments are at God's call, and no place is exempt from their visitation and jurisdiction when God sends them forth with commission. To try the patriarchs' faith God broke the whole staff of bread, even in that good land, that they might plainly see God designed them a better country than that was. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

17 COMMENTARY: He sent a man before them, even Joseph, who was sold for a servant, shows God's gracious care for their relief: it was in obedience to his precept and dependence upon his promise that they were sojourners in Canaan, and therefore he could not in honour suffer any evil to befall them or any good thing to be wanting to them. As he restrained one Pharaoh from doing them wrong, so he raised up another to do them kindness by preferring and entrusting Joseph, whose story is here abstracted: he was to be the shepherd and stone of Israel and save that holy seed alive. Many years before the famine began he was sent before them to nourish them in it — so vast are the foresights and forecasts of providence, and so long its reaches. But in what character did he go to Egypt who was to provide for the church's reception there? Not as ambassador, nor so much as factor or commissary, but sold thither for a servant, a slave for term of life, without any prospect of ever being set at liberty — low enough, and far enough, one would think, from any probability of being great. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

18 COMMENTARY: Whose feet they hurt with fetters, he was laid in iron, shows Joseph brought lower still: unjustly charged with a crime no less heinous than a rape upon his mistress, the iron entered into his soul, that is, was very painful to him, and the false accusation which caused his imprisonment did in a special manner grieve him and went to his heart — yet all this was the way to his preferment. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

19 COMMENTARY: Until the time that his word came, the word of the Lord tried him, shows Joseph continuing a prisoner, neither tried nor bailed, until the time appointed of God for his release, when his word came — his interpretations of the dreams came to pass, and the report thereof came to Pharaoh's ears by the chief butler. And then the word of the Lord cleared him: the power God gave him to foretell things to come rolled away the reproach his mistress had loaded him with, for it could not be thought God would give such power to so bad a man as he was represented to be. God's word tried him, tried his faith and patience, and then it came in power to give command for his release. There is a time set when God's word will come for the comfort of all that trust in it: at the end it shall speak, and not lie. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

20 COMMENTARY: The king sent and loosed him, even the ruler of the people, and let him go free, follows God's word: God gave the word, and then the king sent and loosed him, for the king's heart is in the hand of the Lord. Pharaoh, finding him to be a favourite of heaven, discharged him from his imprisonment and let him go free — God has often, by wonderful turns of providence, pleaded the cause of oppressed innocency. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

21 COMMENTARY: He made him lord of his house, and ruler of all his substance, shows Joseph advanced to the highest posts of honour: lord high chamberlain of his household, nay, lord-treasurer, the ruler of all his substance, prime-minister of state, lord-president of his council, and general of his forces — according to thy word shall all my people be ruled. He made him lord chief justice, to judge even his senators and punish those disobedient. In all this Joseph was designed to be a father to the church that then was, to save the house of Israel from perishing by the famine, made great that he might do good, especially in the household of faith — and a figure of Christ to come, who because he humbled himself and took the form of a servant was highly exalted and has all judgment committed to him. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

22 COMMENTARY: To bind his princes at his pleasure, and teach his senators wisdom, is Joseph's prime-ministry in action: to command his princes at his pleasure and teach them wisdom, and as lord chief justice to judge even his senators — the servant sold for a slave now ruling the rulers, a figure of Christ exalted after humbling himself. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

23 COMMENTARY: Israel also came into Egypt, and Jacob sojourned in the land of Ham, follows Joseph being sent before and put in capacity of maintaining all his father's house: Israel came into Egypt, where he and all his were very honourably and comfortably provided for many years. Thus the New-Testament church has a place provided for her even in the wilderness, where she is nourished for a time, times, and half a time — verily she shall be fed. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

24 COMMENTARY: And he increased his people greatly, and made them stronger than their enemies, shows them wonderfully multiplied according to the promise that Abraham's seed should be as the sand of the sea: in Egypt he increased his people greatly, they multiplied like fishes, so that in a little time they became stronger than their enemies and formidable to them, and Pharaoh took notice of it — the children of Israel are more and mightier than we. When God pleases, a little one shall become a thousand, and God's promises, though they work slowly, work surely. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

25 COMMENTARY: He turned their heart to hate his people, to deal subtilly with his servants, is their affliction in Egypt: God's goodness to his people exasperated the Egyptians against them, and though their old antipathy to the Hebrews was laid asleep for a while, yet now it revived with more violence than ever — formerly they hated them because they despised them, now because they feared them. They dealt subtilly with them, setting all their politics on work to find ways to weaken, waste and prevent their growth, making their burdens heavy and their lives bitter, and slaying their male children as soon as they were born. Malice is crafty to destroy: Satan has the serpent's subtlety with his venom. It was God that turned the Egyptians' hearts against them, for every creature is that to us that he makes it to be, a friend or an enemy — though God is not the author of men's sins, yet he serves his own purposes by them. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

26 COMMENTARY: He sent Moses his servant, and Aaron whom he had chosen, names the instruments of deliverance: he sent Moses his servant on this errand and joined Aaron in commission with him. Moses was designed to be their lawgiver and chief magistrate, Aaron their chief priest, and therefore, that they might respect them the more and submit to them the more cheerfully, God made use of them as their deliverers. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

27 COMMENTARY: They shewed his signs among them, and wonders in the land of Ham, is the means of accomplishing the deliverance, the plagues of Egypt: Moses and Aaron observed their orders, in summoning them just as God appointed them, and they rebelled not against his word as Jonah did, who, when he was sent to denounce God's judgments against Nineveh, went to Tarshish. Moses and Aaron were not moved, either with a foolish fear of Pharaoh's wrath or a foolish pity of Egypt's misery, to relax or retard any of the plagues which God ordered them to inflict on the Egyptians, but stretched forth their hand to inflict them as God appointed. Those that are instructed to execute judgment will find their remissness construed as a rebellion against God's word. The plagues of Egypt are here called God's signs, and his wonders; they were not only proofs of his power, but tokens of his wrath, and to be looked upon with admiration and holy awe. They showed the words of his signs, so it is in the original, for every plague had an exposition going along with it; they were not, as the common works of creation and providence, silent signs, but speaking ones, and they spoke aloud. They are all or most of them here specified, though not in the order in which they were inflicted: the plague of darkness; the turning of the river Nilus, which they idolized, into blood, and all their other waters, which slew their fish; the frogs, shoals of which their land brought forth, which poured in upon them, not only in such numbers, but with such fury, that they could not keep them out of the chambers of their kings; flies of divers sorts swarmed in their air, and lice in their clothes; hail-stones shattered their trees, even the strongest timber-trees in their coasts, and killed their vines, and their other fruit-trees; locusts and caterpillars destroyed all the herbs which were made for the service of man and ate the bread out of their mouths; and, having mentioned all the plagues but those of the murrain and boils, he concludes with that which gave the conquering stroke, and that was the death of the first-born, in the dead of the night the joys and hopes of their families, the chief of their strength and flower of their land, were all struck dead by the destroying angel. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

28 COMMENTARY: He sent darkness, and made it dark, and they rebelled not against his word, is the plague of darkness, one of the last though here mentioned first: God sent darkness, and coming with commission it came with efficacy — his command made it dark. And then they, the people of Israel, rebelled not against God's word, namely a command which some think was given them to circumcise all among them uncircumcised, in doing which the three days' darkness would be a protection to them. The old translation follows the Septuagint and reads, they were not obedient to his word, which may be applied to Pharaoh and the Egyptians, who notwithstanding the terror of this plague would not let the people go — but there is no ground for it in the Hebrew. Moses and Aaron themselves observed their orders in summoning the plagues just as God appointed, and rebelled not against his word as Jonah did when sent to denounce judgment against Nineveh: they were not moved by foolish fear of Pharaoh's wrath or foolish pity of Egypt's misery to relax or retard any plague God ordered, but stretched forth their hand to inflict them as appointed, for those instructed to execute judgment will find remissness construed as rebellion against God's word. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

29 COMMENTARY: He turned their waters into blood, and slew their fish, is the turning of the river Nile, which they idolized, into blood, and all their other waters, which slew their fish — so they were deprived not only of their drink but of the daintiest of their meat. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

30 COMMENTARY: Their land brought forth frogs in abundance, in the chambers of their kings, is the plague of frogs: shoals of them poured in upon Egypt, not only in such numbers but with such fury that they could not keep them out of the chambers of their kings and great men, whose hearts had been full of vermin, more nauseous and more noxious — contempt of, and enmity to, both God and his Israel. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

31 COMMENTARY: He spake, and there came divers sorts of flies, and lice in all their coasts, shows God making use of the meanest, weakest and most despicable animals for punishing and humbling proud oppressors, to whom the impotency of the instrument cannot but be a great mortification as well as an undeniable conviction of divine omnipotence. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

32 COMMENTARY: He gave them hail for rain, and flaming fire in their land, is hail shattering their trees: instead of rain to cherish their trees, he gave them hail to crush them, and with it thunder and lightning, to such a degree that the fire ran along upon the ground as if it had been a stream of kindled brimstone. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

33 COMMENTARY: He smote their vines also and their fig trees, and brake the trees of their coasts, continues the hail: hail-stones shattered even the strongest timber-trees in their coasts and killed their vines and other fruit-trees, instead of rain to cherish them. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

34 COMMENTARY: He spake, and the locusts came, and caterpillars, and that without number, shows what variety of judgments God has wherewith to plague proud oppressors who will not let his people go: God did not bring the same plague twice, but when there was occasion for another it was still a new one, for he has many arrows in his quiver. Locusts and caterpillars are God's armies, and how weak soever they are singly, he can raise such numbers of them as to make them formidable. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

35 COMMENTARY: And did eat up all the herbs in their land, and devoured the fruit of their ground, is the locusts' work: they destroyed all the herbs made for man's service and ate the bread out of their mouths, the armies of God raised in numbers formidable enough to strip a land. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

36 COMMENTARY: He smote also all the firstborn in their land, the chief of all their strength, is the conquering stroke: having mentioned all the plagues but those of the murrain and boils, Henry concludes with the death of the firstborn — in the dead of night the joys and hopes of their families, the chief of their strength and flower of their land, were all struck dead by the destroying angel. They would not release God's first-born, and therefore God seized theirs by way of reprisal, and thereby forced them to dismiss his too, when it was too late to retrieve their own — for when God judges he will overcome, and those will certainly sit down losers at last that contend with him. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

37 COMMENTARY: He brought them forth also with silver and gold, and there was not one feeble person among their tribes, shows the mercies accompanying deliverance: in bondage they had been impoverished, yet they came out rich and wealthy — God not only brought them forth but brought them forth with silver and gold, empowering them to ask and collect their neighbours' contributions, which were indeed but part of payment for the service they had done them, and inclining the Egyptians to furnish what they asked, for their wealth was his and their hearts in his hand. And their lives had been made bitter and their bodies and spirits broken by bondage, yet when God brought them forth there was not one feeble person, none sick, none so much as sickly, among their tribes: they went out that very night the plague swept away Egypt's firstborn, yet went out all in good health and brought not with them any of the diseases of Egypt — surely never was the like, that among so many thousands there was not one sick. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

38 COMMENTARY: Egypt was glad when they departed, for the fear of them fell upon them, shows them brought out with honour: they had been trampled upon and insulted over, yet God had so wonderfully owned them and pleaded their cause that the fear of Israel fell upon Egypt, and they owned themselves baffled and overcome. God can and will make his church a burdensome stone to all that heave at it and seek to displace it, so that those shall think themselves happy that get out of its way — and when God judges, he will overcome. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

39 COMMENTARY: He spread a cloud for a covering, and fire to give light in the night, is God's special care of them in the wilderness: for shelter, besides the canopy of heaven he provided another heavenly canopy, spreading a cloud for a covering, which was to them not only a screen and umbrella but a cloth of state — a cloud was often God's pavilion, and now it was Israel's, for they also were his hidden ones. And for guidance and refreshment in the dark he appointed a pillar of fire to give light in the night, that they might never be at a loss. God graciously provides against all his people's grievances and furnishes them with convenient succours for every condition, for day and night, till they come to heaven, where it will be all day to eternity. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

40 COMMENTARY: The people asked, and he brought quails, and satisfied them with the bread of heaven, shows him feeding them with necessaries and dainties: sometimes he furnished their tables with wild fowl, the people asking and he bringing quails, and when they were not thus feasted yet they were abundantly satisfied with the bread of heaven — those are curious and covetous indeed who will not be so satisfied, for man did eat angels' food, and that constantly and on free-cost. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

41 COMMENTARY: He opened the rock, and the waters gushed out, they ran in the dry places like a river, shows every bit and drop miraculous: common providence fetches waters from heaven and bread out of the earth, but for Israel divine power brings bread from the clouds and water from the rocks, so far is the God of nature from being tied to the laws and courses of nature. The water did not only gush out once, but it ran like a river, plentifully and constantly, and attended their camp in all their removes — hence they are said to have the rock follow them — and, which increased the miracle, this river of God ran in dry places, yet was not drunk in and lost, as one would expect, by the sands of the desert of Arabia. To this that promise alludes, I will give rivers in the desert, to give drink to my chosen. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

42 COMMENTARY: For he remembered his holy promise, and Abraham his servant, is the first reason God did all this for them: because he would himself perform the promises of the word — they were unworthy and unthankful, yet he did those great things in their favour because he remembered the word of his holiness, that is, his covenant with Abraham his servant, and he would not suffer one iota or tittle of that to fall to the ground. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

43 COMMENTARY: And he brought forth his people with joy, and his chosen with gladness, contrasts their sorrow with their deliverance: they had spent their days in sorrow and sighing by reason of bondage, but now he brought them forth with joy and gladness — when Egypt's cry for grief was loud, their firstborn all slain, Israel's shouts for joy were as loud, both when they looked back upon the land of slavery out of which they were rescued and when they looked forward to the pleasant land to which they were hastening. God now put a new song into their mouth. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

44 COMMENTARY: And gave them the lands of the heathen, and they inherited the labour of the people, is their entrance at length into Canaan: he gave them the lands of the heathen, putting them in possession of what they had long been put in hopes of, and what the Canaanites had taken pains for God's Israel had the enjoyment of — they inherited the labour of the people, and the wealth of the sinner is laid up for the just. The Egyptians had long inherited their labours, and now they inherited the labours of the Canaanites — thus sometimes one enemy of the church is made to pay another's scores. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

45 COMMENTARY: That they might observe his statutes, and keep his laws, praise ye the Lord, is the second reason God did all this: because he would have them perform the precepts of the word, to bind them to which was the greatest kindness he could put upon them. He put them in possession of Canaan, not that they might live in plenty and pleasure, in ease and honour, and make a figure among the nations, but that they might observe his statutes and keep his laws — that, being formed into a people, they might be under God's immediate government and revealed religion the basis of their national constitution, that having a good land given them they might out of its profits bring sacrifices to God's altar, and that, God having thus done them good, they might the more cheerfully receive his law, concluding it also designed for their good, and be sensible of their obligations in gratitude to live in obedience to him. We are therefore made, maintained and redeemed, that we may live in obedience to the will of God — and the hallelujah concluding the psalm may be taken both as thankful acknowledgment of God's favours and as cheerful concurrence with this great intention of them. Has God done so much for us, and yet does he expect so little from us? Praise ye the Lord. — Matthew Henry (Complete)
