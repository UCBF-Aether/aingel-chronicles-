Study Notes — Psalms

Psalm 90

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Henry opens with comfort, not history: God himself has been his people's home in every age, so that patriarchs in tents and Israelites in bondage were still at rest in him. Believers are at home in God, and that is their shelter through every toil and tribulation of this world. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

2 COMMENTARY: Before mountains, earth, or time itself, God already was, and his being has neither beginning nor end nor change. Henry draws the comfort straight from that eternity: our comforts die, but God is an ever-living God to those who have him for theirs. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

3 COMMENTARY: God's sovereign word turns man back to dust, yet in the same word Henry hears a call to return in repentance, and a promise of return at the resurrection. Death is the general sentence on all, body to earth and soul to God, until God bids the dead arise again. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

4 COMMENTARY: A thousand years, long beyond any human life, are to God as yesterday past or a single night-watch, because between time and eternity there is no proportion at all. Henry adds that all those years lie present to the Eternal Mind, so delay is no argument against the resurrection. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

5 COMMENTARY: Human life is a dying life, gliding down time's stream into eternity as if swept by a flood, and a dreaming life, since men asleep to their frailty imagine great things until death wakes them. We begin to die as soon as we are born, and every day carries us nearer the end. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

6 COMMENTARY: Like grass green in the morning and cut down by evening, man flourishes briefly and then withers, whether age winters him or disease mows him down in summer. Henry's point is the suddenness of the change: death remakes us shortly, perhaps suddenly, and all flesh is as grass. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

7 COMMENTARY: Israel is taught to own God's anger, not mere nature, as the cause of its miseries: death is a debt to divine justice, since sin entered and death by sin, not a debt nature alone could claim. Afflictions may come from God's love, as Job's did, but rebukes for sin must be traced to his displeasure. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

8 COMMENTARY: God sets both open sins and secret sins before him in the light of his face, and Henry insists the secret departures of the heart lie at the bottom of the overt acts. Covering sin is folly, because what is hidden from men is already discovered and reckoned by God. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

9 COMMENTARY: Under the wilderness sentence, Israel's years passed away in wrath like a tale told: long in the living, told in a moment, and never to be recalled like a spoken word. Henry applies it generally, warning that we spend our years carelessly, to little purpose, instead of dispatching our business with industry. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

10 COMMENTARY: Seventy years, or eighty by strength, is the common stint of life since Moses' day, and even that span is labour and sorrow, soon cut off. Henry notes the sting: added strength in old age often only prolongs misery, since the years have come in which there is no pleasure. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

11 COMMENTARY: No one fully comprehends the power of God's anger, and few seriously consider it as they ought; mockers at sin plainly do not know it. Henry warns that God's wrath equals the gravest apprehensions of the most thoughtful, and what is felt hereafter is infinitely worse than what is feared here. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

12 COMMENTARY: Numbering our days is an art to be prayed for, not merely calculated: we must live under a constant sense of life's shortness and uncertainty, and compare our work with our remaining days so as to mind it with double diligence. Done rightly, it bends the heart to true wisdom, which is serious godliness in practice. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

13 COMMENTARY: Though the wilderness decree could not be revoked, Moses still prays for God to turn back toward his servants and show some token of restored favour. Henry reads the prayer as asking how long they must lie under wrath, and notes that God's renewed laws of sacrifice afterward signalled that he had relented toward them. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

14 COMMENTARY: Early mercy is prayed for without any plea of merit: God's tender mercy must come speedily, even in the morning of life, and a soul satisfied with his lovingkindness is abundantly satisfied with that alone. Such mercy is a full fountain of joy, constant even in this vale of tears. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

15 COMMENTARY: Gladness is asked in measure against affliction, day for day, because God sets the one over against the other. Henry's comfort is that returns of lovingkindness are reckoned a sufficient recompense for trouble: the cup of trembling gives way to the cup of salvation. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

16 COMMENTARY: God's work must appear upon his servants before his glory appears to their children, because servants cannot work for God unless he first works upon and in them. Henry hears a distinction here: reform for the generation under sentence, and the promised performance for the children who would enter Canaan. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

17 COMMENTARY: The beauty of the Lord is holiness shining in conduct and consolation gladdening the heart, the visible token that God favours his people. And because God's working in us does not discharge our own endeavour, the prayer is repeated earnestly: prosper the work of our hands, and establish us in it. — Matthew Henry (Complete)
