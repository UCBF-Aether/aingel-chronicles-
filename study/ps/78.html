Study Notes — Psalms

Psalm 78

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The psalmist calls his people to hear God's law with inclined ears, as pupils before a teacher. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

2 COMMENTARY: He will open his mouth in a parable and utter dark sayings — weighty, excellent things that deserve serious attention, not riddles meant to hide their meaning. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

3 COMMENTARY: These are things heard and known, told by the fathers, not new inventions. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

4 COMMENTARY: Henry makes transmission a debt repaid to parents and owed to posterity: the deposit to hand down is praise, power in deed, and wonders done, before language, liberty, or property. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

5 COMMENTARY: The law arrives with a charge attached: testimony and statute in Jacob exist so parents train children, and grown children in turn declare them, keeping the church from becoming a thing of one age. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

6 COMMENTARY: The teaching passes down so that children yet unborn may arise and declare it to their own children. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

7 COMMENTARY: Henry names the twofold fruit of remembering: encouragement to conform, with hope set on God and commands kept, since works duly considered strengthen both trust and obedience. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

8 COMMENTARY: The warning is against an inherited pattern: a generation that professed covenant yet never set its heart aright, whose spirit flew off at every occasion, shows hypocrisy as the high road to apostasy. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

9 COMMENTARY: Ephraim, armed with bows, turned back in the day of battle — equipped yet faithless when tested. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

10 COMMENTARY: Ephraim's armed retreat becomes the psalm's exhibit: covenant-breaking here is not ignorance but refusal to walk in a law already given. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

11 COMMENTARY: Forgetfulness here is not a lapse of memory but a moral act: wonders shown are dropped, and with them the obligation they imposed. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

12 COMMENTARY: Zoan stands for wonders done in full sight of the fathers, with Egypt as the stage where mercy and judgment were both displayed, aggravating later unbelief. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

13 COMMENTARY: The sea passage is remembered as a road, not a marvel alone: waters heaped while a people walked through, so that deliverance itself became instruction. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

14 COMMENTARY: Guidance by day and by night marks a people never left to find their own road, in light or in darkness, cloud and fire together. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

15 COMMENTARY: Rock water in a dry place answers the charge of a God who cannot provide: depths are opened where no depth was expected. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

16 COMMENTARY: Streams running like rivers turn a single miracle into abundance: provision is not measured out drop by drop. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

17 COMMENTARY: Sin after mercy is the psalm's aggravation: provocation continues in the very wilderness where provision had just arrived. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

18 COMMENTARY: Appetite becomes a trial of God: the question is not whether food will come, but whether the Provider can be trusted to furnish a table where none is seen. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

19 COMMENTARY: Speech against God follows appetite: doubt is voiced as a challenge to divine power in a place with no visible supply. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

20 COMMENTARY: They reasoned from the rock's water to doubt bread and flesh, as if one mercy proved nothing about the next. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

21 COMMENTARY: Wrath kindled is presented as judicial, not temperamental: fire against Jacob answers a settled refusal to believe, and anger rises for that refusal. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

22 COMMENTARY: The root of the anger was unbelief: they believed not in God and trusted not in his salvation. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

23 COMMENTARY: Even after refusal, heaven's doors open: command over clouds shows a Provider who gives before the people deserve, making the gift itself a rebuke to distrust. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

24 COMMENTARY: He rained down manna, the corn of heaven, for them to eat. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

25 COMMENTARY: Manna as angels' food lifts provision above mere subsistence: heaven's corn is sent to the full, so that want cannot excuse the murmur. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

26 COMMENTARY: Winds are instruments, not accidents: east and south winds at God's power bring the promised flesh, showing providence commanding nature's movements. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

27 COMMENTARY: Flesh rained as dust and birds as sand turn desire into excess: the answer to lust is abundance, and abundance itself becomes the setting for judgment. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

28 COMMENTARY: And he let it fall in the midst of their camp, round about their habitations, is God making the winds his caterers: though the winds seem to blow where they list, yet when he pleased he could make them fetch in provisions. He caused an east wind to blow and a south wind — either a south-east wind, or an east wind first to bring in the quails from that quarter and then a south wind to bring in more — so that he rained flesh upon them, and that of the most delicate sort, not butchers' meat, but wild-fowl, and abundance of it, as dust, as the sand of the sea, so that the meanest Israelite might have sufficient, and it cost them nothing, no, not the pains of fetching it from the mountains, for he let it fall in the midst of their camp, round about their habitation. See how good God is even to the evil and unthankful, and wonder that his goodness does not overcome their badness. See what little reason we have to judge of God's love by such gifts of his bounty: these dainty bits are no tokens of his peculiar favour. Christ gave dry bread to the disciples that he loved, but a sop dipped in the sauce to Judas that betrayed him. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

29 COMMENTARY: They eat to fullness on their own desire, and Henry's point is that God grants the craving, so the granting exposes what the craving was worth. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

30 COMMENTARY: They were not estranged from their lust, but while their meat was yet in their mouths, is lust's insatiable nature: though he gave them their own desire, they were not estranged from their lust — their appetite was insatiable, they were well filled and yet they were not satisfied, for they knew not what they would have. Such is the nature of lust: it is content with nothing, and the more it is humoured the more humoursome it grows. Those that indulge their lust will never be estranged from it. Or it intimates that God's liberality did not make them ashamed of their ungrateful lustings, as it would have done if they had had any sense of honour. But what came of it? While the meat was yet in their mouth, rolled under the tongue as a sweet morsel, the wrath of God came upon them and slew the fattest of them, those that were most luxurious and most daring. They were fed as sheep for the slaughter: the butcher takes the fattest first. We may suppose there were some pious and contented Israelites, that did eat moderately of the quails and were never the worse — for it was not the meat that poisoned them, but their own lust. Let epicures and sensualists here read their doom: the end of those who make a god of their belly is destruction; the prosperity of fools shall destroy them, and their ruin will be the greater. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

31 COMMENTARY: Judgment falls where strength was proudest: the fattest slain and chosen men struck down, so that the very vigour Israel trusted in is cut off. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

32 COMMENTARY: For all this they sinned still, and believed not for his wondrous works, is judgments failing where mercies failed: the judgments of God upon them did not reform them, nor attain the end, any more than his mercies. For all this, they sinned still — they murmured and quarrelled with God and Moses as much as ever. Though God was wroth and smote them, yet they went on frowardly in the way of their heart. They believed not for his wondrous works: though his works of justice were as wondrous and as great proofs of his power as his works of mercy, yet they were not wrought upon by them to fear God, nor convinced how much it was their interest to make him their friend. Those hearts are hard indeed that will neither be melted by the mercies of God nor broken by his judgments. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

33 COMMENTARY: Days consumed in vanity and years in trouble make sin its own punishment: a life spent chasing its own desire ends empty, not merely punished from outside. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

34 COMMENTARY: When he slew them, then they sought him and enquired early after God — trouble drove them back to prayer. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

35 COMMENTARY: And they remembered that God was their rock, and the high God their redeemer, is a plausible but counterfeit repentance: when he slew them, or condemned them to be slain, then they sought him — they confessed their fault, and begged his pardon. When some were slain others in a fright cried to God for mercy, and promised they would reform and be very good; then they returned to God, and enquired early after him. So one would have taken them to be such as desired to find him. And they pretended to do this because, however they had forgotten it formerly, now they remembered that God was their rock — and therefore now that they needed him they would fly to him and take shelter in him — and that the high God was their Redeemer, who brought them out of Egypt and to whom therefore they might come with boldness. Afflictions are sent to put us in mind of God as our rock and our redeemer — for, in prosperity, we are apt to forget him. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

36 COMMENTARY: Flattery with the mouth and lies with the tongue expose counterfeit repentance: words of submission without a heart set right are the psalm's recurring fraud. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

37 COMMENTARY: A heart not right and a covenant not kept go together: outward engagement without inward steadfastness collapses at the first pressure. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

38 COMMENTARY: Compassion interrupts deserved destruction: anger turned away many times, not stirred to its full, is Henry's evidence that pardon, not mere delay, governs the history. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

39 COMMENTARY: God remembers flesh: a wind passing and not returning is the ground of forbearance toward a people too frail to bear full wrath. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

40 COMMENTARY: Repeated provocation in wilderness and desert grieves as well as angers: Henry stresses a God sinned against in the place of his own provision. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

41 COMMENTARY: They turned back, tempted God, and limited the Holy One of Israel by their unbelief. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

42 COMMENTARY: They remembered not his hand, nor the day when he delivered them from the enemy, is the occasion of their limiting God: that which occasioned their limiting God for the future was their forgetting his former favours. They remembered not his hand, how strong it is and how it had been stretched out for them, nor the day when he delivered them from the enemy, Pharaoh, that great enemy who sought their ruin. There are some days made remarkable by signal deliverances, which ought never to be forgotten — for the remembrance of them would encourage us in our greatest straits. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

43 COMMENTARY: Signs in Egypt and wonders at Zoan are recalled as forgotten evidence: the plagues were instruction, meant to cure Egyptian idolatry as well as to force an exit. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

44 COMMENTARY: Rivers turned to blood strike at Egypt's lifeblood: floods undrinkable show a judgment aimed at the source of a nation's confidence. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

45 COMMENTARY: Flies devouring and frogs destroying are not random pests but a mixed plague: the land's own creatures turn against its people. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

46 COMMENTARY: Increase given to caterpillar and labour to locust reverses harvest: what hands worked for is eaten by what hands cannot control. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

47 COMMENTARY: He destroyed their vines with hail, and their sycamore trees with frost, is one of Egypt's specified plagues: the hail, which destroyed their trees, especially their vines, the weakest of trees, and their cattle, especially their flocks of sheep, the weakest of their cattle, which were killed with hot thunderbolts — and the frost, or congealed rain, as the word signifies, was so violent that it destroyed even the sycamore-trees. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

48 COMMENTARY: He gave up their cattle also to the hail, and their flocks to hot thunderbolts, continues that plague: the hail destroyed their trees, especially their vines, the weakest of trees, and their cattle, especially their flocks of sheep, the weakest of their cattle, which were killed with hot thunderbolts. God can make the weakest and most despicable animals instruments of his wrath when he pleases; what they want in strength may be made up in number — and here the very hail is made his instrument against Egypt's weakest wealth. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

49 COMMENTARY: Fierceness, wrath, indignation, and trouble arrive together, by evil angels as agents: Henry reads the final plague as anger given a way, not anger losing control. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

50 COMMENTARY: A way made to anger spares no soul from death and gives life to pestilence: restraint is lifted, and the plague runs its appointed course. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

51 COMMENTARY: And smote all the first-born in Egypt, the chief of their strength in the tabernacles of Ham, is the last and sorest of Egypt's plagues, and that which perfected Israel's deliverance: it was first in intention, but last in execution — for, if gentler methods would have done the work, this would have been prevented. The anger of God was the cause of it: wrath had now come upon the Egyptians to the uttermost, Pharaoh's heart having been often hardened after less judgments had softened it, so God now stirred up all his wrath — he cast upon them the fierceness of his anger, anger in the highest degree, wrath and indignation the cause, and trouble, tribulation and anguish, the effect. The angels of God were the instruments: he sent evil angels among them — not evil in their own nature, but in respect to the errand upon which they were sent; they were destroying angels, or angels of punishment, which passed through all the land of Egypt, with orders, according to the weighed paths of God's anger, not to kill all, but the first-born only. Good angels become evil angels to sinners. The execution itself was very severe: he spared not their soul from death, but suffered death to ride in triumph among them and gave their life over to the pestilence, which cut the thread of life off immediately — for he smote all the first-born in Egypt, the chief of their strength, the hopes of their respective families. Children are the parents' strength, and the first-born the chief of their strength. Thus, because Israel was precious in God's sight, he gave men for them and people for their life. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

52 COMMENTARY: Sheep led like a flock contrasts with Egypt's ruin: the same God who plagued oppressors guides his own through wilderness as a shepherd guides, not drives. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

53 COMMENTARY: Safety without fear while the sea covers enemies inverts the Exodus terror: those who feared drowning watch drowning fall on their pursuers. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

54 COMMENTARY: The border of the sanctuary and the mountain purchased by God's right hand mark arrival as possession, not accident: a people brought to a place already claimed for them. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

55 COMMENTARY: Heathen cast out, inheritance divided by line, tribes settled in tents: conquest becomes order, with each tribe given a measured place. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

56 COMMENTARY: After settlement in Canaan the children proved like their fathers, bringing old corruptions into new homes: they tempted and provoked God, failed to keep his testimonies, and turned back like a deceitful bow that promises the mark but breaks at the draw. Henry stresses there was no hold in their professions, and their relapses into idolatry were spiritual adultery that moved a jealous God. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

57 COMMENTARY: Henry sets this relapse against the whole history: after settlement they turned back like their fathers, unfaithful in the very land where God had settled them, so the bow image marks a people failing at the moment of use. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

58 COMMENTARY: High places and graven images provoke jealousy after settlement: in Canaan itself, the old wilderness sin takes a settled, idolatrous form. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

59 COMMENTARY: When God heard this, he was wroth, and greatly abhorred Israel, is God's displeasure at Canaan idolatry: when God heard this, when he heard the cry of their iniquity, which came up before him, he was wroth, he took it very heinously, as well he might, and he greatly abhorred Israel, whom he had greatly loved and delighted in. Those that had been the people of his choice became the generation of his wrath. Presumptuous sins, idolatries especially, render even Israelites odious to God's holiness and obnoxious to his justice. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

60 COMMENTARY: Shiloh forsaken is God withdrawing his tent from among men: the tabernacle placed there is left, because the people for whom it stood have left him. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

61 COMMENTARY: And delivered his strength into captivity, and his glory into the enemy's hand, is God giving up all into the enemy's hands: God permits the Philistines to take the ark prisoner, and carry it off as a trophy of their victory, to show that he had not only forsaken the tabernacle, but even the ark itself, which shall now be no longer a token of his presence. He delivered his strength into captivity, as if it had been weakened and overcome, and his glory fell under the disgrace of being abandoned into the enemy's hand. When the ark has become as a stranger among Israelites, no marvel if it soon be made a prisoner among Philistines. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

62 COMMENTARY: A people given to the sword and an inheritance under wrath show judgment turning inward: the nation becomes the object of the anger once aimed at Egypt. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

63 COMMENTARY: The fire consumed their young men, and their maidens were not given to marriage, is Israel's armies routed: he gave his people over unto the sword, to the sword of his own justice and of the enemy's rage, for he was wroth with his inheritance — and that wrath of his was the fire which consumed their young men, in the prime of their time, by the sword or sickness, and made such a devastation of them that their maidens were not praised, that is, were not given in marriage, which is honourable in all, because there were no young men for them to be given to, and because the distresses and calamities of Israel were so many and great that the joys of marriage-solemnities were judged unseasonable, and it was said, Blessed is the womb that beareth not. General destructions produce a scarcity of men. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

64 COMMENTARY: Henry reads the priests' fall as just, not accidental: Hophni and Phinehas had made themselves vile, and priesthood aggravated rather than protected them, since they went into battle without call or warrant. Their widows made no lamentation because ceremony was swallowed by substantial grief — Phinehas's widow died herself after naming her son Ichabod. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

65 COMMENTARY: God awaking as from sleep is Henry's image of seeming delay ending: after the ark's capture, divine action resumes with the force of a mighty man roused. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

66 COMMENTARY: Enemies smitten in the hinder parts and put to perpetual reproach reverse the shame: Philistine triumph becomes lasting disgrace. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

67 COMMENTARY: God never sent the ark back to Shiloh in Ephraim, and Henry says its ruins stand as monuments of divine justice. Yet moving the ark was not removing it: Shiloh lost it, Israel did not, and the rejection of Shiloh was the election of Zion, with Judah chosen because Jesus, greater than Joshua, would come from that tribe. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

68 COMMENTARY: Judah chosen and Zion loved close the long reversal: after Ephraim's failure and Shiloh's loss, the establishment moves to the tribe and mountain God delights in. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

69 COMMENTARY: He built his sanctuary like high palaces — stately above princes' palaces and firm as the earth while it was to stand, a house God himself is said to build. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

70 COMMENTARY: He chose David also his servant, and took him from the sheepfolds, is God setting a good government over them: he set a monarchy, and a monarch after his own heart — he chose David his servant out of all the thousands of Israel, and put the sceptre into his hand, out of whose loins Christ was to come, and who was to be a type of him. The meanness of his beginning: his extraction indeed was great, for he descended from the prince of the tribe of Judah, but his education was poor. He was bred not a scholar, not a soldier, but a shepherd. He was taken from the sheep-folds, as Moses was — for God delights to put honour upon the humble and diligent, to raise the poor out of the dust and to set them among princes, and sometimes he finds those most fit for public action that have spent the beginning of their time in solitude and contemplation. The Son of David was upbraided with the obscurity of his original: Is not this the carpenter? David was taken, he does not say from leading the rams, but from following the ewes, especially those great with young, which intimated that of all the good properties of a shepherd he was most remarkable for his tenderness and compassion to those of his flock that most needed his care. This temper of mind fitted him for government, and made him a type of Christ, who, when he feeds his flock like a shepherd, does with a particular care gently lead those that are with young. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

71 COMMENTARY: From following the ewes great with young he brought him to feed Jacob his people, and Israel his inheritance, is the greatness of David's advancement: God preferred him to feed Jacob his people. It was a great honour that God put upon him, in advancing him to be a king, especially to be king over Jacob and Israel, God's peculiar people, near and dear to him — but withal it was a great trust reposed in him when he was charged with the government of those that were God's own inheritance. God advanced him to the throne that he might feed them, not that he might feed himself, that he might do good, not that he might make his family great. It is the charge given to all the under-shepherds, both magistrates and ministers, that they feed the flock of God. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

72 COMMENTARY: David fed them with integrity of heart and guided them by skilfulness of hands — the shepherd-king as the psalm's closing picture of God's care. — Matthew Henry (Complete)
