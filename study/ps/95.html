Study Notes — Psalms

Psalm 95

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Singing to the Lord must be done with holy joy, for spiritual joy is the heart and soul of thankful praise: it is God's gracious will that when we give him glory as infinitely perfect and blessed we should at the same time rejoice in him as our Father and King, a God in covenant with us. We praise with our voice, speaking and singing forth out of a heart filled with love, joy and thankfulness — ourselves much affected with his greatness and goodness, forward to own it, desirous to be more affected, and willing to kindle the same affection in others — and in concert, not others without me nor I alone, but others with me. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

2 COMMENTARY: Coming before his presence with thanksgiving means coming together in the courts of his house, where his people are wont to attend him and expect his manifestations of himself. Whenever we come into God's presence we must come with thanksgiving that we are admitted to such a favour, and whenever we have thanks to give we must come before his presence, set ourselves before him, and present ourselves to him in the ordinances he has appointed. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

3 COMMENTARY: He is great and therefore greatly to be praised, infinite and immense, with all perfection in himself. His great power makes him a great King above all gods — above deputed deities, magistrates to whom he said 'ye are gods,' whom he manages and serves his purposes by and to whom they are accountable, and above all counterfeit deities, pretenders and usurpers, whom he can and will famish and vanquish. His great possessions follow: men reckoned great for large territories that are yet an inconsiderable part of the universe — how great then is the God whose is the whole earth and its fulness, not only under his feet in incontestable dominion but in his hand in actual directing and disposing of all. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

4 COMMENTARY: The deep places of the earth, out of our sight — subterraneous springs and mines — are in his hand, and the height of the hills, out of our reach, whatever grows or feeds upon them, is his also. Taken figuratively, the meanest of the children of men, as the low places of the earth, are not beneath his cognizance, and the greatest, as the strength of the hills, are not above his control, for whatever strength is in any creature is derived from God and employed for him. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

5 COMMENTARY: The sea is his, and all that is in it, the waves fulfilling his word, for he made it, gathered its waters and fixed its shores; and the dry land, though given to the children of men, is his too, for he still reserved the property to himself, his hands having formed it when his word made it appear. His being Creator makes him, without dispute, owner of all — and as a gospel psalm Henry applies it to Christ, the mighty God and God over all, Mediator and great King above all gods, by whom as eternal Word all things were made and who sets one foot on sea and the other on earth as sovereign Lord of both. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

6 COMMENTARY: Worshipping, bowing down and kneeling is humble reverence and holy awe, becoming those who know the infinite distance between us and God, how much we are in danger of his wrath and in need of his mercy. Though bodily exercise alone profits little, it is our duty to glorify God with our bodies by outward expressions of reverence, seriousness and humility — kneeling before the Lord our Maker, unlike idolaters who kneel before gods they themselves made, for we kneel before a God who made us and all the world and is therefore our rightful proprietor: his we are, and not our own. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

7 COMMENTARY: For he is our God; and we are the people of his pasture, and the sheep of his hand. To day if ye will hear his voice, is God as our God in special relation, and the duty that relation requires: he is our God, and therefore it is expected we should praise him; who will, if we do not? What else did he make us for but that we should be to him for a name and a praise? First, he is our Creator, and the author of our being; we must kneel before the Lord our Maker. Idolaters kneel before gods which they themselves made; we kneel before a God who made us and all the world and who is therefore our rightful proprietor; for his we are, and not our own. Second, he is our Saviour, and the author of our blessedness. He is here called the rock of our salvation, not only the founder, but the very foundation, of that work of wonder, on whom it is built. That rock is Christ; to him therefore we must sing our songs of praises, to him that sits upon the throne and to the Lamb. Third, we are therefore his, under all possible obligations: we are the people of his pasture and the sheep of his hand. All the children of men are so; they are fed and led by his Providence, which cares for them, and conducts them, as the shepherd the sheep. We must praise him, not only because he made us, but because he preserves and maintains us, and our breath and ways are in his hand. All the church's children are in a special manner so; Israel are the people of his pasture and the sheep of his hand; and therefore he demands their homage in a special manner. The gospel church is his flock. Christ is the great and good Shepherd of it. We, as Christians, are led by his hand into the green pastures, by him we are protected and well provided for, to his honour and service we are entirely devoted as a peculiar people, and therefore to him must be glory in the churches throughout all ages. And the duty required of all those that are the people of Christ's pasture and the sheep of his hand is that they hear his voice, for he has said, My sheep hear my voice. We are his people, say they. Are you so? Then hear his voice. If you call him Master, or Lord, then do the things which he says, and be his willing obedient people. Hear the voice of his doctrine, of his law, and, in both, of his Spirit; hear and heed; hear and yield. Hear his voice, and not the voice of a stranger. If you will hear his voice; some take it as a wish, O that you would hear his voice! that you would be so wise, and do so well for yourselves. Christ's voice must be heard to-day; this the apostle lays much stress upon, applying it to the gospel day. While he is speaking to you see that you attend to him, for this day of your opportunities will not last always; improve it, therefore, while it is called to-day. Hearing the voice of Christ is the same with believing. To-day, if by faith you accept the gospel offer, well and good, but to-morrow it may be too late. In a matter of such vast importance nothing is more dangerous than delay. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

8 COMMENTARY: Hardening the heart is the sin warned against, inconsistent with the believing, obedient ear required: if we will hear his voice and profit by it, we must not harden our hearts, for seed sown on rock never brought fruit to perfection. The Jews believed not the gospel because their hearts were hardened — unconvinced of the evil of sin and their danger by it, they regarded not the offer of salvation and would not bend to Christ's yoke nor yield to his demands — and a hardened heart is the sinner's own act and deed, for which he alone shall bear the blame. The example is Israel at Meribah, where they quarrelled with God and Moses, and Massah, the day of temptation in the wilderness, asking 'Is the Lord among us or is he not?' — days of temptation being days of provocation, and hardness of heart lying at the bottom of all our distrusts and quarrels with God, a heart that receives not divine impressions, conforms not to the divine will, will not melt, will not bend. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

9 COMMENTARY: Tempting and proving God, though they saw his work, was unbelief aggravated: they questioned whether they might take his word and insisted on further security, sending spies, and when those discouraged them protested against the sufficiency of divine power and promise, proposing a captain to return to Egypt. The aggravation is that they saw what he had done in bringing them out of Egypt and what he was doing every day — bread rained from heaven, water out of the rock that followed them — unquestionable evidences of his presence, yet with them even seeing was not believing, because they hardened their hearts, though they had seen what Pharaoh got by hardening his. God imputes it to ignorance and mistake: they had not known his ways, neither providence's ways toward them nor commandments' ways toward him, and they erred in heart, wandering out of duty and obedience as corrupt affections pervert judgment. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

10 COMMENTARY: Forty years long was I grieved with this generation, and said, It is a people that do err in their heart, and they have not known my ways, is the cause of Israel's sin and God's resentment of it: see what God imputed it to: it is a people that do err in their hearts, and they have not known my ways. Men's unbelief and distrust of God, their murmurings and quarrels with him, are the effect of their ignorance and mistake. First, of their ignorance: they have not known my ways. They saw his work and he made known his acts to them; and yet they did not know his ways, the ways of his providence, in which he walked towards them, or the ways of his commandments, in which he would have them to walk towards him: they did not know, they did not rightly understand and therefore did not approve of these. Note, the reason why people slight and forsake the ways of God is because they do not know them. Secondly, of their mistake: they do err in their heart; they wander out of the way; in heart they turn back. Note, sins are errors, practical errors, errors in heart; such there are, and as fatal as errors in the head. When the corrupt affections pervert the judgment, and so lead the soul out of the ways of duty and obedience, there is an error of the heart. And God's resentment of their sin: forty years long was I grieved with this generation. The sins of God's professing people do not only anger him, but grieve him, especially their distrust of him; and God keeps an account how often and how long they grieve him. See the patience of God towards provoking sinners; he was grieved with them forty years, and yet those years ended in a triumphant entrance into Canaan made by the next generation. If our sins have grieved God, surely they should grieve us, and nothing in sin should grieve us so much as that. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

11 COMMENTARY: Unto whom I sware in my wrath that they should not enter into my rest, is the sentence passed upon them for their sin: Unto whom I swore in my wrath, If they shall enter into my rest, then say I am changeable and untrue. Observe, first, whence this sentence came — from the wrath of God. He swore solemnly in his wrath, his just and holy wrath; but let not men therefore swear profanely in their wrath, their sinful brutish wrath. God is not subject to such passions as we are; but he is said to be angry, very angry, at sin and sinners, to show the malignity of sin and the justice of God's government. That is certainly an evil thing which deserves such a recompence of revenge as may be expected from a provoked Deity. Second, what it was: that they should not enter into his rest, the rest which he had prepared and designed for them, a settlement for them and theirs, that none of those who were enrolled when they came out of Egypt should be found written in the roll of the living at their entering into Canaan, but Caleb and Joshua. Third, how it was ratified: I swore it. It was not only a purpose, but a decree; the oath showed the immutability of his counsel; the Lord swore, and will not repent. It cut off the thought of any reserve of mercy. God's threatenings are as sure as his promises. — Matthew Henry (Complete)
