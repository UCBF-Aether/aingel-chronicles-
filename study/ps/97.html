Psalm 97

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Reign as joy to earth and gladness to isles rests on rescue from vanity: Christ's elevation honours and enriches the earth in part, and there is room in him for the multitude of isles, Gentile countries included. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

2 COMMENTARY: Clouds and darkness round about him speak the equity of Christ's government alongside its mystery: there is incontestable justice in all its acts, legislative and judicial, yet sometimes his dispensations are altogether unaccountable, his way in the sea and his path in the great waters, and we are not aware what he designs or drives at, nor is it fit we should be let into the secrets of his government — there is a depth in his counsels we must not pretend to fathom. But still righteousness and judgment are the habitation of his throne, a golden thread of justice running through the whole web of his administration: in this he resides, in this he rules, and therefore his throne is for ever, because his sceptre is a right sceptre and his throne is established in righteousness. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

3 COMMENTARY: A fire goeth before him, and burneth up his enemies round about, is the wrath against Christ's kingdom compared to consuming fire: the opposition which the Jews gave to the setting up of Christ's kingdom turned to their own ruin. Their persecuting the apostles, and forbidding them to speak to the Gentiles, filled up their sin, and brought wrath upon them to the uttermost. That wrath is here compared, first, to consuming fire, which goes before him, and burns up his enemies, that have made themselves like chaff and stubble, and have set the briers and thorns before him in battle. This fire of divine wrath will not only burn the rubbish upon the hills, but will even melt the hills themselves like wax. When our God appears as a consuming fire even rocks will be wax before him. The most resolute and daring opposition will be baffled at the presence of the Lord. His very presence is enough to shame and sink it, for he is the Lord of the whole earth, by whom all the children of men are manageable and to whom they are accountable. Men hate and persecute God's people, because they think him absent, that the Lord has forsaken the earth; but, when he manifests his presence, they melt. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

4 COMMENTARY: His lightnings are amazing lightnings striking terror upon many: the judgments God brought upon the enemies of Christ's kingdom were such as all the world took notice of with terror, the earth seeing and trembling and the ears of all that heard made to tingle. Henry sees it fulfilled in the destruction of Jerusalem and the Jewish nation by the Romans about forty years after Christ's resurrection, which like fire wholly destroyed that people and like lightning astonished all their neighbours — and the heavens declare God's righteousness in it, with all the people seeing his glory in those lasting monuments of his justice. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

5 COMMENTARY: The hills melting like wax are the enemies of Christ's kingdom — called hills for their height, strength and immovable obstinacy, the princes of this world who crucified the Lord of glory — baffled at his presence. The fire of divine wrath will not only burn the rubbish upon the hills but melt the hills themselves like wax, for when God appears as a consuming fire even rocks will be wax before him, and his very presence is enough to shame and sink the most resolute and daring opposition, because he is Lord of the whole earth, by whom all men are manageable and to whom they are accountable. Men hate and persecute God's people because they think him absent, that the Lord has forsaken the earth — but when he manifests his presence, they melt. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

6 COMMENTARY: The heavens declaring his righteousness means it is as conspicuous and illustrious as the heavens themselves: the angels of heaven declare it, employed as messengers in his government and therefore knowing more of it than any creature, and who can contradict or dispute what the heavens declare? In extent it covers upper and lower world alike — all men on earth are under his government, either served by him or serving himself, and all the people may see his glory, the glory of God in the face of Christ shining in distant countries, the gospel preached in all languages and miracles wrought in all nations. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

7 COMMENTARY: Confounded be all they that serve graven images, that boast themselves of idols: worship him, all ye gods, is idolaters put to confusion by the setting up of Christ's kingdom: confounded be all those who serve graven images, the Gentile world, who did service to those that by nature are no gods, who boasted themselves of idols as their protectors and benefactors. Did those that served idols boast of them, and shall the servants of the living God distrust him, or be ashamed of him? Let those be ashamed that serve graven images. First, this is a prayer for the conversion of the Gentiles, that those who have been so long serving dumb idols may be convinced of their error, ashamed of their folly, and may, by the power of Christ's gospel, be brought to serve the only living and true God, and may be as much ashamed of their idols as ever they were proud of them. Second, this is a prophecy of the ruin of those that would not be reformed and reclaimed from their idolatry: they shall be confounded by the destruction of Paganism in the Roman empire, which was fulfilled about 300 years after Christ, so much to the terror of idolaters that some think it was the revolution under Constantine that made even the mighty men say to the rocks, Fall on us and hide us. This prayer and prophecy are still in force against antichristian idolaters, who may here read their doom: confounded be all those that worship graven images. And the last clause carries the angels' worship: perhaps we should not have found this truth in those words, Worship him, all you gods, if we had not been directed to it by the inspired apostle, who, from the Septuagint version of those words, makes the Messiah to be introduced into the upper world at the ascension with this charge, Let all the angels of God worship him, which helps us to a key to this whole psalm, and shows us that it must be applied to the exalted Redeemer, who has gone into heaven, and is on the right hand of God, which intimates that all power is given him both in heaven and earth, angels, authorities, and powers, being made subject unto him. This speaks the honour of Christ, that he has such worshippers, and the honour of all good Christians, that they have such fellow-worshippers. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

8 COMMENTARY: Zion hearing and being glad, with the daughters of Judah rejoicing, is the bright side of the Messiah's kingdom, like the pillar of cloud and fire dark toward the Egyptians and bright toward Israel: the kingdom is set up in spite of opposition, and while the earth saw and trembled, Zion heard and was glad at the conversion of some and the confusion of others, the conquest of all that stood out against Christ. Not Zion only, where the temple was, but the common people, the inhabitants of the villages, triumph in Christ's victories — though the command is 'let the earth rejoice,' only the sons of Zion and daughters of Judah actually do, for all should bid the Messiah welcome but few do. Their joy is because of his judgments, both of his mouth and of his hand, the word of his gospel and his works in propagating it, miracles and marvellous providences. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

9 COMMENTARY: The Lord high above all the earth and exalted far above all gods is Zion's confession in those judgments: he has manifested his sovereignty in the kingdom of nature and his command of all its powers, his dominion over all nations and over all hearts, exalted far above all gods — all deputed gods, that is, princes, and all counterfeit gods, that is, idols. The exaltation of Christ and the advancement of God's glory among men thereby are the rejoicing of all the saints. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

10 COMMENTARY: Love proved by hating evil, preservation of saints' souls, deliverance from the wicked hand: Henry presses preservation beyond life spared, to soul kept from sin, apostasy, and despair, and preserved safely to the heavenly kingdom. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

11 COMMENTARY: Light sown for the righteous is provision made for their comfort: the subjects of Christ's kingdom are told to expect tribulation in the world, to suffer by its malice and not share in its mirth, yet light is sown for them — designed and prepared for them. What is sown will come up again in due time, though like winter seed it may lie long under the clods and seem lost and buried, yet it will return in a rich and plentiful increase, for God's goodness shall be sure of a harvest in the appointed weeks and those that sow in tears shall without fail reap in joy. Gladness is sure to the upright in heart, to those only sincere in religion, for the joy of the hypocrite is but for a moment and there is no serenity without a lasting sincerity. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

12 COMMENTARY: Rejoicing in the Lord and giving thanks at the remembrance of his holiness gives Zion's joy its rules: let it be pure and holy joy — those who love the Lord must hate evil, every thing offensive to him that would throw them out of his favour, for true love to God shows itself in real hatred of sin, and divine comforts should put the mouth out of taste for sensual pleasures. Let it terminate in God, all streams of comfort flowing in Christ's kingdom leading back to the fountain, all lines of joy meeting in him as in the centre. And let it express itself in praise and thanksgiving, particularly for his holiness — the infinite purity, rectitude and perfection of the divine nature — giving thanks not only for its glory as an honour to him but as a favour to us, an unspeakable favour if through grace we are partakers of his holiness, which above all his attributes the angels celebrate, while sinners tremble and saints rejoice at its remembrance. — Matthew Henry (Complete)
