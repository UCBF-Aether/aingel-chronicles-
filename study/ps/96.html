Study Notes — Psalms

Psalm 96

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Singing a new song three times over is singing to Father, Son and Holy Ghost, as in the beginning when the morning stars sang together, now in the church militant, and ever in the church triumphant — and we have reason to do it often and need to be often reminded and stirred up to it. A new song is an excellent song, the product of new affections clothed with new expressions, a song for new favours and compassions new every morning, a New-Testament song for the new covenant and its precious privileges, a song that shall be ever new and never wax old or vanish away. And all the earth shall sing it, not the Jews only, to whom God's service had been appropriated, but all redeemed from the earth, a prophecy of the calling of the Gentiles, who shall have both cause and call to sing it, from day to day, for its subject — the great salvation wrought by the Lord Jesus — can never be exhausted. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

2 COMMENTARY: Sing unto the LORD, bless his name; shew forth his salvation from day to day, is honouring God with songs: three times we are here called to sing unto the Lord; sing to the Father, to the Son, to the Holy Ghost, as it was in the beginning, when the morning stars sang together, is now, in the church militant, and ever shall be, in the church triumphant. We have reason to do it often, and we have need to be often reminded of it, and stirred up to it. Sing unto the Lord, that is, bless his name, speak well of him, that you may bring others to think well of him. First, sing a new song, an excellent song, the product of new affections, clothed with new expressions. We speak of nothing more despicable than an old song, but the newness of a song recommends it; for there we expect something surprising. A new song is a song for new favours, for those compassions which are new every morning. A new song is New-Testament song, a song of praise for the new covenant and the precious privileges of that covenant. A new song is a song that shall be ever new, and shall never wax old nor vanish away; it is an everlasting song, that shall never be antiquated or out of date. Second, let all the earth sing this song, not the Jews only, to whom hitherto the service of God had been appropriated, who could not sing the Lord's song in a strange land; but let all the earth, all that are redeemed from the earth, learn and sing this new song. This is a prophecy of the calling of the Gentiles; all the earth shall have this new song put into their mouths, shall have both cause and call to sing it. Third, let the subject-matter of this song be his salvation, the great salvation which was to be wrought out by the Lord Jesus; that must be shown forth as the cause of this joy and praise. Fourth, let this song be sung constantly, not only in the times appointed for the solemn feasts, but from day to day; it is a subject that can never be exhausted. Let day unto day utter this speech, that, under the influence of gospel devotions, we may daily exemplify a gospel conversation. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

3 COMMENTARY: Declaring his glory among the heathen is praise with sermons: salvation by Christ is spoken of as a work of wonder, in which God's glory shines very brightly, in the face of Christ. In Old-Testament times it was a glory to be revealed, as heaven's happiness is now, but in the fulness of time it was declared, a full discovery made even to babes of what prophets and kings desired and wished to see and might not. What was then discovered only among the Jews is now declared among the heathen, among all people, nations long sitting in darkness seeing this great light — and the apostles' commission to preach the gospel to every creature is copied from this. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

4 COMMENTARY: The Lord is great and therefore greatly to be praised and to be feared — great and honourable to his attendants, great and terrible to his adversaries — and even the new song proclaims him great as well as good, for his goodness is his glory, and the everlasting gospel is 'Fear God, and give glory to him.' He is great in sovereignty over all that pretend to be deities, none daring to vie with him, to be feared above all gods, whether deified princes or rather idols: all the earth being called to sing must be convinced that Jehovah is the one only living and true God, infinitely above all rivals and pretenders — he is great and they are little, he is all and they are nothing, for an idol is nothing in the world. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

5 COMMENTARY: The gods of the nations are idols because they were all made-gods, creatures of men's fancies, while our God is great in his right even to the noblest part of creation, for it is his own work and derives its being from him: the Lord made the heavens and all their hosts, the work of his fingers, so nicely and curiously made. He is Creator of the sun, moon and stars themselves, those lights of heaven which the nations imagined to be gods and worshipped as such. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

6 COMMENTARY: Honour and majesty before him, strength and beauty in his sanctuary, show his glory manifested in both the upper and lower world, among angels in heaven and saints on earth: splendour and majesty are in his immediate presence above, where angels cover their faces as unable to bear the dazzling lustre, and strength and beauty are in his sanctuary both above and below. In God there is everything awful and yet everything amiable — attending him in his sanctuary we behold his beauty, for God is love, and experience his strength, for he is our rock — so let us go forth in his strength, enamoured with his beauty. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

7 COMMENTARY: Giving to the Lord, O kindreds of the people, marks gospel times, when the kindreds shall be invited and admitted into God's service and be as welcome as ever the Jews were, the court of the Gentiles no longer an outward court but laid in common with the court of Israel. We give not as if God needed anything or could receive anything from us which was not his own before, much less be benefited by it, but in our best affections, adorations and services we return to him what we have received, freely, as what we give — debt, rent, tribute, what must be paid, yet if it come from holy love God accepts it as a gift — acknowledging him sovereign Lord and paying homage, glory and strength, glory and empire, subscribing that thine is the kingdom and therefore thine the power and the glory, giving glory to God and not taking it to ourselves nor giving it to any creature. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

8 COMMENTARY: Giving the glory due to his name means giving it to the discovery he has been pleased to make of himself to the children of men: in all acts of religious worship this is what we must aim at, to honour God and pay him some of that reverence we owe him as the best of beings and the fountain of our being. Bringing an offering into his courts means bringing ourselves first, the offering up of the Gentiles, offering sacrifices of praise continually, often appearing before God in public worship and never appearing before him empty. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

9 COMMENTARY: Worshipping in the beauty of holiness means worshipping in the solemn assembly where divine institutions are religiously observed, the beauty of which is their holiness, their conformity to the rule, and worshipping with holy hearts, sanctified by God's grace, devoted to his glory, purified from sin's pollutions. And all the earth must fear before him, all acts of worship performed from a principle of the fear of God and with holy awe and reverence. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

10 COMMENTARY: Saying among heathen that the Lord reigns is gospel preaching in Henry's reading: Christ's government settles the world, preserves it till the elect are called in, and establishes the church on rock against hell's gates. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

11 COMMENTARY: Heaven rejoicing and earth glad, the sea roaring with its fulness, is the call to rejoice in the honour put upon the Messiah and the great trust lodged in his hand. The dialect is poetical: the days of the Messiah are joyful days, and as far as his grace and government are submitted to they bring joy along with them — we have reason to give joy into that place, that soul, into which Christ is admitted, as Samaria had great joy when it received the gospel and the eunuch went on his way rejoicing. It is also the duty of every one to bid Christ and his kingdom welcome, though he comes conquering and to conquer, yet peaceably — and first of all there is joy in heaven, in the presence of the angels, who sang their anthems when the First-begotten was brought into the world. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

12 COMMENTARY: The field joyful and the trees of the wood rejoicing mean that the whole creation has reason to rejoice in the setting up of Christ's kingdom: as by the sin of the first Adam the whole creation was made subject to vanity, so by the grace of the second Adam it shall, first or last, be delivered from the bondage of corruption into the glorious liberty of the children of God. God graciously accepts the holy joy and praises of all hearty well-wishers to Christ's kingdom, be their capacity ever so mean — the sea can but roar, and how trees can show they rejoice Henry says he knows not, but he that searches the heart knows what is the mind of the Spirit and understands the broken language of the weakest. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

13 COMMENTARY: Coming to judge with righteousness and truth means ruling first, not only final assize: laws consonant to eternal equity, administrations faithful to promise, and hearts ruled by truth's commanding power. — Matthew Henry (Complete)
