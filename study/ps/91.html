Study Notes — Psalms

Psalm 91

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: He that dwelleth in the secret place of the most High shall abide under the shadow of the Almighty, is a great truth laid down in general, that all those who live a life of communion with God are constantly safe under his protection, and may therefore preserve a holy serenity and security of mind at all times: he that dwells, that sits down, in the secret place of the Most High, shall abide under the shadow of the Almighty — he that by faith chooses God for his guardian shall find all that in him which he needs or can desire. Note, first, it is the character of a true believer that he dwells in the secret place of the Most High: he is at home in God, returns to God, and reposes in him as his rest; he acquaints himself with inward religion, and makes heart-work of the service of God, worships within the veil, and loves to be alone with God, to converse with him in solitude. Second, it is the privilege and comfort of those that do so that they abide under the shadow of the Almighty: he shelters them, and comes between them and everything that would annoy them, whether storm or sunshine. They shall not only have an admittance, but a residence, under God's protection; he will be their rest and refuge for ever. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

2 COMMENTARY: Whatever others say, the psalmist says of Jehovah, 'He is my refuge' — chosen and confided in, unlike idols, which are refuges of lies, and unlike the idolaters' Mahuzzim, strongholds that deceived them. He is a fortress that will not fail, because he is neither fickle nor false, neither weak nor mortal: God and not man, so there is no danger of being disappointed in him, and we know whom we have trusted. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

3 COMMENTARY: The fowler's snare is laid unseen and catches the unwary suddenly, and the pestilence seizes unawares with no guard against it — and Henry applies the promise to both lives: the natural life, preserved from threatening dangers we do not even apprehend, as the bird is unaware of the snare, and the spiritual life, protected by grace from Satan's temptations as snares and from the contagion of sin as pestilence, with a defence created upon all its glory. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

4 COMMENTARY: God covers his people with the tenderness of a hen gathering chickens under her wings — calling them under protection when danger is seen, cherishing and keeping them warm, helpless as chickens yet invited to trust under the wings of the God of Israel. But wings and feathers are weak and easily broken through, so truth is added as shield and buckler: God is as willing to guard as the hen, and as able as a man of war in armour. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

5 COMMENTARY: Fear is removed, not danger denied: by night there are thieves, storms, and creatures of fancy often most frightful of all, and by day an arrow flies unseen and destruction wastes at noon even among friends who cannot secure us. Henry's security is that grace keeps believers from disquieting, distrustful fear — wisdom from causeless fear, faith from inordinate fear — because the arrow, though it may hit, cannot hurt the spiritual life but perfects it: its point is off, its poison out, and every bullet has its commission under the Father's will. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

6 COMMENTARY: Nor for the pestilence that walketh in darkness; nor for the destruction that wasteth at noonday, is God keeping believers not only from evil, but from the fear of evil: here is, first, great danger supposed — the mention of it is enough to frighten us: night and day we lie exposed, and those that are apt to be timorous will in neither period think themselves safe. When we are retired into our chambers, our beds, and have made all as safe as we can about us, yet there is terror by night, from thieves and robbers, winds and storms, besides those things that are the creatures of fancy and imagination, which are often most frightful of all. There is also a pestilence that walketh in darkness, as that was which slew the first-born of the Egyptians, and the army of the Assyrians. No locks nor bars can shut out diseases, while we carry about with us in our bodies the seeds of them. But surely in the day-time, when we can look about us, we are not so much in danger — yes, there is an arrow that flieth by day too, and yet flies unseen; there is a destruction that wasteth at high-noon, when we are awake and have all our friends about us; even then we cannot secure ourselves, nor can they secure us. It was in the day-time that that pestilence wasted which was sent to chastise David for numbering the people, on occasion of which some think this psalm was penned. But, second, here is great security promised to believers in the midst of this danger: Thou shalt not be afraid. God by his grace will keep thee from disquieting distrustful fear — that fear which hath torment — in the midst of the greatest dangers. Wisdom shall keep thee from being causelessly afraid, and faith shall keep thee from being inordinately afraid. Thou shalt not be afraid of the arrow, as knowing that though it may hit thee it cannot hurt thee: if it take away the natural life, yet it shall be so far from doing any prejudice to the spiritual life that it shall be its perfection. A believer needs not fear, and therefore should not fear, any arrow, because the point is off, the poison is out. O death! where is thy sting? It is also under divine direction, and will hit where God appoints and not otherwise. Every bullet has its commission. Whatever is done our heavenly Father's will is done and we have no reason to be afraid of that. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

7 COMMENTARY: When death rides in triumph and thousands and ten thousands fall by sickness or sword at either hand, the sight alone is enough to frighten, and pestilence falling so near might seem likely to infect — yet it shall not come nigh. Henry's condition is purity kept in general corruption, trusting God with safety in general desolation, awakened to prepare for one's own death but not amazed into lifelong bondage through fear of death, as the sprinkling of blood secured Israel's firstborn while thousands fell. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

8 COMMENTARY: Seeing only with the eyes is itself part of the promise: God's people have the satisfaction of seeing not only his promises fulfilled to them but his threatenings fulfilled on those who hate them, as Israel saw the firstborn of Egypt destroyed — punishment of oppressors and enlargement of the oppressed — while they themselves stood unhurt and untouched. As it aggravates sinners' damnation to behold the reward of the righteous, so it magnifies saints' salvation to behold the destruction of the wicked. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

9 COMMENTARY: Making the Most High a habitation means being continually with God and resting in him, making his name both temple and strong tower, dwelling in love and so dwelling in God. It is duty to be at home in God by choice, conversing, delighting and depending on him, and privilege to be welcomed as a man to his own habitation, without let from the arrests of the law or clamours of conscience, kept in perfect peace — and the psalmist adds his own experience, that the refuge he commends has room and shelter enough for both, as in his Father's house there are many mansions. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

10 COMMENTARY: No evil befalling means, in Henry's careful reading, that whatever trouble comes shall have no real evil in it: it comes from God's love and shall be sanctified, coming not for hurt but for good, grievous for the present yet yielding so well that the sufferer himself will own no evil befell him. The dwelling is covered too, so no plague does damage to him or his — Seneca's line quoted approvingly: no evil can befall a good man. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

11 COMMENTARY: The charge to the angels is given by the Lord of the angels himself, over every particular believer, not only over the church in general — a mark of the great care God takes of the saints, that angels themselves are charged with them and employed for them. The promise is limited and extensive at once: keep thee in thy ways, as long as thou keepest in the way of duty — the word the devil left out when quoting it to tempt Christ — yet in all thy ways, where no danger appears and where danger is most imminent, wherever the saints go, as servants are charged with children. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

12 COMMENTARY: Bearing up in angels' hands denotes both great ability and great affection: able to bear saints out of danger's reach, and doing it with the tenderness of a nurse carrying a little child — which speaks us helpless and them helpful. Their ministrations are condescending: they keep the saints' feet, lest they dash against a stone, stumble and fall into sin and into trouble. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

13 COMMENTARY: Treading on lion and adder is triumph over the powers of darkness: the devil is the roaring lion, the old serpent, the red dragon, so that Paul's promise that the God of peace shall tread Satan under our feet seems to refer here. Christ has broken the serpent's head and spoiled our spiritual enemies, and through him we are more than conquerors, called as Joshua called Israel's captains to set feet on the necks of vanquished enemies — a promise fulfilled in Christ's miraculous power over creation and in providence preserving us from ravenous creatures. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

14 COMMENTARY: God himself now speaks, and Henry describes those to whom the promises belong by three characters: they know God's name, acquainting themselves with him by the name through which he has made himself known; they have set their love on him as its only adequate object, fixed with resolution never to remove it to a rival; and they call on him, keeping constant correspondence by prayer. To them he promises deliverance in due time, double — in trouble and out of trouble — and to set them on high, out of trouble's reach, above the stormy region, looking down on this world with holy contempt and up to the other with holy ambition, honoured by covenant and communion with himself. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

15 COMMENTARY: He shall call upon me, and I will answer him: I will be with him in trouble; I will deliver him, and honour him, is God himself speaking words of comfort to the saints, declaring the mercy he had in store for them: first, that he will, in due time, deliver them out of trouble — I will deliver him, denoting a double deliverance, living and dying, a deliverance in trouble and a deliverance out of trouble. If God proportions the degree and continuance of our troubles to our strength, if he keeps us from offending him in our troubles, and makes our death our discharge, at length, from all our troubles, then this promise is fulfilled. Second, that he will, in the meantime, be with them in trouble. If he does not immediately put a period to their afflictions, yet they shall have his gracious presence with them in their troubles: he will take notice of their sorrows, and know their souls in adversity, will visit them graciously by his word and Spirit, and converse with them, will take their part, will support and comfort them, and sanctify their afflictions to them, which will be the surest token of his presence with them in their troubles. Third, that herein he will answer their prayers: He shall call upon me — I will pour upon him the spirit of prayer — and then I will answer, answer by promises, answer by providences, bringing in seasonable relief, and answer by graces, strengthening them with strength in their souls; thus he answered Paul with grace sufficient. Fourth, that he will exalt and dignify them: I will set him on high, out of the reach of trouble, above the stormy region, on a rock above the waves. They shall be enabled, by the grace of God, to look down upon the things of this world with a holy contempt and indifference, to look up to the things of the other world with a holy ambition and concern — and then they are set on high. I will honour him: those are truly honourable whom God puts honour upon by taking them into covenant and communion with himself and designing them for his kingdom and glory. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

16 COMMENTARY: With long life will I satisfy him, and shew him my salvation, is a sufficiency of life in this world, crowned with eternal life in the other: With length of days will I satisfy him — that is, first, they shall live long enough: they shall be continued in this world till they have done the work they were sent into this world for and are ready for heaven, and that is long enough. Who would wish to live a day longer than God has some work to do, either by him or upon him? Second, they shall think it long enough: for God by his grace shall wean them from the world and make them willing to leave it. A man may die young, and yet die full of days, satisfied with living. A wicked worldly man is not satisfied, no, not with long life; he still cries, Give, give. But he that has his treasure and heart in another world has soon enough of this; he would not live always. And, sixth, that they shall have an eternal life in the other world. This crowns the blessedness: I will show him my salvation — show him the Messiah, so some; good old Simeon was then satisfied with long life when he could say, My eyes have seen thy salvation, nor was there any greater joy to the Old-Testament saints than to see Christ's day, though at a distance. It is more probably that the word refers to the better country, that is, the heavenly, which the patriarchs desired and sought: he will show him that, bring him to that blessed state, the felicity of which consists so much in seeing that face to face which we here see through a glass darkly and, in the meantime, he will give him a prospect of it. All these promises, some think, point primarily at Christ, and had their accomplishment in his resurrection and exaltation. — Matthew Henry (Complete)
