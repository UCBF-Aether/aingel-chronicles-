Study Notes — Genesis

Chapter 33

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill and Adam Clarke, all public-domain works, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Jacob looked up and saw Esau coming with four hundred men, and arranged his children with Leah, Rachel, and the maidservants. JFB notes he placed the dearest last, so they would be least exposed, and his fears were now gone after prevailing with God. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: The maidservants and their children went foremost, Leah and hers next, and Rachel and Joseph last. The order was a father's protective care in the face of an uncertain meeting. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Jacob himself went ahead of them all and bowed to the ground seven times as he approached his brother. JFB describes the Eastern obeisance: bowing with the body parallel to the ground, advancing, and bowing again until standing before the superior. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Esau ran to meet Jacob, embraced him, fell on his neck, kissed him, and they wept. Henry's point is that God holds all hearts in his hands and can turn an enemy to peace when a man's ways please the Lord. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Esau asked who the women and children were, and Jacob answered that they were the children God had graciously given his servant. JFB notes Jacob could not give even a common answer without the language of piety. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: The maidservants came near with their children and bowed. The whole household now shared in the humble, respectful greeting. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Leah with her children came near and bowed, and then Joseph and Rachel bowed as well. Each group in turn acknowledged Esau as the elder brother. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Esau asked what Jacob meant by all the droves he had met, and Jacob said they were to find grace in his lord's sight. The present was presented as a plea for favour, not as payment. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Esau replied that he had enough and told Jacob to keep what he had for himself. The refusal at first showed a brother content, not a creditor demanding settlement. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Jacob urged Esau to receive the present, saying that seeing Esau's face in favour was like seeing the face of God. The comparison expresses relief: a dreaded meeting had turned gracious. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Jacob pressed his blessing on Esau, because God had dealt graciously with him and he had enough, and Esau took it. JFB notes that in the East a superior's acceptance of a gift is proof of friendship and, from an enemy, of reconciliation. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Esau proposed that they travel together, with himself going before Jacob, as escort and companion. JFB reads this as both friendship and protection, which Jacob prudently declined. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Jacob pleaded that the children were tender and the flocks and herds with young could not be overdriven for a day without dying. Henry uses this tender care as a picture of the good Shepherd who gently leads those with young. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Jacob asked Esau to go on ahead, while he followed softly at the pace of cattle and children until he came to Esau in Seir. JFB notes Jacob may have meant to visit Seir by a roundabout route, but whether he did so is not recorded. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Esau offered to leave some of his men with Jacob, but Jacob asked what need there was, seeking only grace in his lord's sight. Henry warns against too close an intimacy with ungodly relations that could become a snare or an offence. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: So Esau returned that day on his way to Seir, and the brothers parted in peace. The parting closed twenty years of estrangement without a quarrel. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Jacob journeyed to Succoth, built a house, and made booths for his cattle, which gave the place its name. JFB notes Succoth means booths, the first station where Jacob halted on arriving in Canaan. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Jacob came safely to Shalem, a city of Shechem in Canaan, and pitched his tent before the city. JFB notes Shalem may mean peace, a tribute to the God who had promised a safe return, or it may be a place-name near Shechem. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: He bought the parcel of ground where he had spread his tent from Hamor's sons for a hundred pieces of money. JFB notes he was thus the first patriarch to become a landowner in Canaan, and the price was literally in lambs, probably coin bearing a lamb's figure. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: There Jacob built an altar and called it El-elohe-Israel, God, the God of Israel. Henry and JFB agree this was real thanks, not words only: where Jacob pitched a tent, God had an altar. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
