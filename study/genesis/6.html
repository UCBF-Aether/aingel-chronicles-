Study Notes — Genesis

Chapter 6

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill and Adam Clarke, all public-domain works, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: When men began to multiply on the earth, daughters were born to them. The chapter opens with a growing, spreading humanity, setting the scene for the corruption that follows. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: The sons of God saw the daughters of men, that they were fair, and took wives of all they chose. JFB reads the sons of God as the professedly religious line of Seth, marrying into apostate Cain's line, so that mixed marriages spread corruption through households. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: God said his Spirit would not always strive with man, for he was flesh, yet his days would be 120 years. JFB reads this as a long respite: God had preached repentance through prophets like Enoch and Noah, but the people were incorrigible, and judgment was given a fixed delay. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Giants, men of renown, were on the earth in those days, born of these unions and present also afterward. JFB notes the Hebrew points less to great stature than to reckless, violent, impious characters who spread devastation. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: God saw the wickedness of man was great, and every imagination of his heart only evil continually. Henry stresses the corruption reached the heart itself, the citadel of human life, with no exception and no pause. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: The LORD repented that he had made man, and it grieved him at his heart. JFB explains this in human language: God does not change, but his visible dealings change from long-suffering mercy to judgment. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: God said he would destroy man, beast, creeping thing and fowl from the earth, for he repented he had made them. The judgment reaches the whole order man was set over, because man's corruption had filled the earth. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: But Noah found grace in the eyes of the LORD. JFB stresses the awful contrast: in a world of professed religion, only one family of piety remained. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Noah was a just man and perfect in his generations, and walked with God. JFB clarifies perfect does not mean sinless, which no fallen man is, but sincere: living by faith and desiring to do God's will. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Noah fathered Shem, Ham and Japheth. The three sons are named now because the ark and the covenant will preserve this one household. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: The earth was corrupt before God and filled with violence. Without well-ordered government or fear of God, JFB notes, men did what was right in their own eyes and misery followed. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: God looked on the earth and saw it corrupt, for all flesh had corrupted its way. The verdict is universal: corruption was not a few bad actors but the way of the whole world. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: God told Noah the end of all flesh had come before him, for the earth was filled with violence, and he would destroy them with the earth. Henry notes how startling this was: nature and public opinion gave no sign of flood, yet Noah believed God's word over appearances. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: God commanded an ark of gopher wood, with rooms, pitched inside and outside. JFB explains gopher as durable cypress, rooms as small cabins, and pitch as bitumen that would make the vessel watertight. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: The ark's measure was 300 cubits long, 50 wide and 30 high. JFB notes it was not a ship built to sail, but an immense floating house, designed only to float and hold its cargo. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Noah was to make a window, finish it to a cubit above, set a door in the side, and build lower, second and third decks. JFB reads the window as a skylight and the raised roof as a slope to shed water. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: God announced he would bring a flood of waters to destroy all flesh with the breath of life, and everything on earth would die. JFB notes the repeated I, even I, stresses certainty, and the flood is presented as God's moral judgment, not mere natural accident. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: But with Noah God would establish his covenant, and Noah, his sons, his wife and his sons' wives would enter the ark. JFB calls this a special promise of deliverance, given to make Noah confident in it before any flood appeared. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Of every living thing, two of every sort, male and female, were to be brought into the ark to keep them alive. Preservation, not just Noah's family, is part of the covenant plan. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Of birds, cattle and creeping things after their kind, two of every kind would come to Noah. The animals come by God's direction, sorted by kind, so the ark's company is ordered rather than random. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Noah was to take all food that is eaten and gather it, for food for himself and for them. God employs ordinary means: a long voyage needs stored provision, planned in advance. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Noah did according to all God commanded him. JFB stresses he began without delay and followed the directions faithfully at every step, which is why his obedience is held up as faith in action. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
