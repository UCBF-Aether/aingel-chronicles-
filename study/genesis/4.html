Study Notes — Genesis

Chapter 4

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill and Adam Clarke, all public-domain works, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Eve bore Cain and said she had gotten a man from the LORD. Henry suggests she hoped this first son might be the promised seed, a hope that would be disappointed. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: She bore his brother Abel, who became a keeper of sheep while Cain tilled the ground. Each son had a calling, which Henry takes as God's will that everyone have useful work. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: In time Cain brought an offering of the fruit of the ground to the LORD. Worship itself was no new invention: from the beginning, people brought offerings to God. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Abel brought the firstlings of his flock and their fat, and the LORD had respect to Abel and his offering. Henry stresses Abel came in faith, as a sinner trusting God's appointed way, while Cain did not. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: But to Cain and his offering God had no respect, so Cain was very angry and his face fell. Rejection exposed a proud, unbelieving heart, not merely a faulty gift. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: God asked Cain why he was angry and why his face had fallen. The question gives Cain a chance to examine his own heart before anger hardens into action. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: God promised that if Cain did well he would be accepted, but warned that sin lay crouching at the door and must be ruled. Henry notes God notices every angry and envious look, and calls Cain to master the passion before it masters him. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Cain talked with Abel in the field and rose up and killed him. Henry calls this the progress of cherished envy: malice in the heart ends in murder by the hands. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: God asked where Abel was, and Cain answered he did not know, asking if he was his brother's keeper. He covers deliberate murder with a deliberate lie, denying responsibility for the brother he should have protected. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: God said Abel's blood cried to him from the ground. Henry notes the Hebrew word is plural, bloods, as if every life Abel might have fathered also cries out, and murder cannot be buried deep enough to silence it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Cain was now cursed from the earth, which had opened its mouth to receive his brother's blood. Unlike Adam's curse, which fell on the ground, this curse falls directly on Cain himself. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: The ground would no longer yield its strength to him, and he would be a fugitive and wanderer. His punishment fits his chosen portion: the earth he loved becomes a curse to him. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Cain complained his punishment was greater than he could bear. Henry notes he complains of his suffering, not of his sin, showing hardness of heart. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Cain feared that being driven from God's presence, anyone who found him would kill him. Banishment from God's face is the deepest part of the sentence, worse than wandering itself. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: God set a mark on Cain so that no one finding him would kill him, and promised sevenfold vengeance on his killer. Even in judgment, God restrains vengeance and preserves Cain's life for his own wise ends. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Cain went out from God's presence and dwelt in the land of Nod, east of Eden. To go out from God's presence is to leave the place of worship and fellowship, not merely to travel east. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Cain knew his wife, she bore Enoch, and Cain built a city named after his son. The city-building shows Cain settling into the world he chose, seeking security apart from God. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Enoch fathered Irad, and the line runs through Mehujael and Methusael to Lamech. This brief genealogy traces Cain's line forward to the next figure of note, Lamech. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Lamech took two wives, Adah and Zillah. This is the first polygamy recorded, a departure from the one-man-one-woman pattern of Eden, noted without approval. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Adah bore Jabal, father of those who dwell in tents and keep cattle. Cain's line is credited with the arts of nomadic herding life, showing skill can flourish apart from godliness. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: His brother Jubal became father of all who handle harp and organ. Music also arises in Cain's line, a good gift used in a family that has left God's presence. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Zillah bore Tubal-cain, instructor of workers in brass and iron, and his sister was Naamah. Metalwork completes the picture: Cain's descendants pioneer crafts and technology. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Lamech boasted to his wives that he had killed a man for wounding him, and a young man for hurting him. His song celebrates revenge without remorse, the opposite of repentance. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: If Cain was avenged sevenfold, Lamech claimed seventy-sevenfold for himself. He twists God's protection of Cain into a licence for his own vengeance. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Adam knew his wife again, and she bore Seth, saying God had appointed another seed instead of Abel, whom Cain slew. Seth's birth renews the godly line after Abel's death. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Seth fathered Enos, and then men began to call upon the name of the LORD. Henry and JFB read this as the renewal of public worship: after Abel, the godly line again openly invokes God. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
