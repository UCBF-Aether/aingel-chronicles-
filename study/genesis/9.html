Study Notes — Genesis

Chapter 9

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill and Adam Clarke, all public-domain works, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: God blessed Noah and his sons, saying be fruitful, multiply and replenish the earth. The blessing renews the creation mandate of chapter 1 for a post-flood world. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: The fear and dread of people would be on every beast, bird, creeping thing and fish, delivered into human hands. Henry notes this fear is God's protection for humanity, keeping dangerous creatures at a distance. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Every moving thing that lives would be food for them, as the green herb had been given before. The grant of animal food is new after the flood, but Henry warns it warrants use, not abuse by gluttony or cruelty. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: But flesh with its life, that is, its blood, they must not eat. Blood is set apart as the sign of life itself, and therefore not to be treated as ordinary food. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: God would require the blood of human life at the hand of every beast and of every man, even a brother. Human life is uniquely protected: its taking demands an accounting. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Whoever sheds man's blood, by man shall his blood be shed, for in God's image man was made. The image of God, not mere usefulness, is the ground of the law against murder. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Noah's family is again told to be fruitful, multiply abundantly and increase on the earth. Repetition stresses the point: the world is to be repopulated, not left empty after judgment. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: God spoke to Noah and his sons with him, establishing the covenant that follows. The covenant is addressed to the whole household, not to Noah alone. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: The covenant is with Noah's sons and their seed after them. It reaches beyond the ark's generation to all their descendants. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: It is also with every living creature with them: birds, cattle and beasts that came out of the ark. The covenant's scope is wider than humanity alone, covering the animal order preserved in the ark. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: God promises never again to cut off all flesh by flood waters, nor to destroy the earth by flood. This is a universal negative promise: whatever judgments come later, a flood will not again unmake the world. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: God said the bow in the cloud would be the token of the covenant between him and the earth. A visible sign accompanies the promise, so future generations can see and remember it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: God set his bow in the cloud, and it would be the token of the covenant between him and the earth. The bow, a weapon turned upward, is read by Henry as a sign of peace: war is over, and the sign hangs in the sky where storms gather. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: When God brings a cloud over the earth and the bow is seen, the sign appears precisely when fear would naturally arise. The token is given for stormy times, not only for fair weather. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: God will remember the everlasting covenant between himself and every living creature, and waters will no more become a flood to destroy all flesh. Remembering here means acting on the promise, as in chapter 8. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: The bow in the cloud will be looked on to remember the everlasting covenant between God and every living creature. The sign is for God to remember in the sense of keeping, and for people to trust. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: God tells Noah this bow is the token of the covenant he has established between himself and all flesh on earth. The verse seals the covenant with a plain summary: sign, parties and promise all named together. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: The sons of Noah who went out of the ark were Shem, Ham and Japheth, and Ham is the father of Canaan. The genealogy now narrows: Ham's line will matter through Canaan in the story ahead. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: These three sons of Noah are the ancestors from whom the whole earth was overspread. All post-flood humanity traces to this one household, which is why the flood story is a story of all peoples. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Noah began to be a husbandman and planted a vineyard. After worship and covenant, ordinary work resumes: farming the renewed earth. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Noah drank of the wine and became drunk, and lay uncovered in his tent. Henry notes even a righteous man can fall through excess: Noah's drunkenness is recorded without excuse, as a warning, not as approval. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Ham, father of Canaan, saw his father's nakedness and told his two brothers outside. The fault is not merely seeing but exposing: Ham spreads his father's shame instead of covering it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Shem and Japheth took a garment, laid it on their shoulders, walked backward and covered their father, without looking. Their care is the opposite of Ham's: they protect Noah's dignity and refuse to gaze on his shame. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: When Noah awoke from his wine and learned what his younger son had done, he pronounced judgment. The story turns from the act to its consequence, which falls on Canaan, Ham's son. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Noah said cursed be Canaan, a servant of servants shall he be to his brethren. The curse names Canaan, not Ham directly, and looks ahead to the later subjection of Canaan's descendants. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Noah blessed the LORD, the God of Shem, and said Canaan shall be his servant. Shem's blessing is stated as God's blessing: the LORD is called Shem's God, marking the line through which God's people will come. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: God shall enlarge Japheth, and he shall dwell in the tents of Shem, and Canaan shall be his servant. Japheth's enlargement and sharing in Shem's tents is read as the later inclusion of Japheth's peoples in the blessings of Shem's line. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Noah lived after the flood 350 years. The post-flood lifespan shows Noah living long into the new world he helped repopulate. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: All Noah's days were 950 years, and he died. The patriarch who survived the flood still dies, closing his life under the same sentence as Adam's line. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
