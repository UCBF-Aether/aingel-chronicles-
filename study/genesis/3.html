Study Notes — Genesis

Chapter 3

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill and Adam Clarke, all public-domain works, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The serpent, more subtle than other beasts, questioned God's word to the woman. JFB stresses the real serpent was the tool of Satan, who attacked when the woman was alone and cast doubt on God's goodness. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: The woman answered that they could eat freely of the garden's trees. She rightly began with God's generosity, even as the tempter tried to narrow her view to the one exception. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: She added that the forbidden tree must not be eaten or even touched, lest they die. JFB notes the softened wording, lest ye die for surely ye shall die, already shows the tempter's doubt taking hold. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The serpent flatly denied the penalty: ye shall not surely die. This is the tempter's open contradiction of God's word, replacing warning with a promise of impunity. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: He promised their eyes would be opened and they would be as gods, knowing good and evil. JFB notes the promise was half true in a terrible way: they would learn evil by experience, not gain godlike wisdom. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Seeing the tree as good for food, pleasant to the eyes and desirable for wisdom, the woman ate and gave to her husband, who also ate. JFB traces the pattern of all temptation: outward attraction, inward desire, then the act that enslaves. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Their eyes were opened, and they knew they were naked, so they sewed fig leaves together. Knowledge came, but as shame and a useless self-made covering, not as the wisdom promised. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Hearing God's voice walking in the garden in the cool of the day, they hid among the trees. JFB notes God's approach was the familiar, gentle one they had welcomed before; guilt now made them flee it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: God called, Where art thou? The question is not for God's information but for Adam's conscience, inviting confession rather than hiding. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Adam said he heard God's voice and was afraid because he was naked, so he hid. JFB calls this an evasive confession: it admits fear and shame but not yet the sin itself. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: God asked who told him he was naked, and whether he had eaten of the forbidden tree. The questions press Adam from the symptom, shame, to the cause, disobedience. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Adam blamed the woman God had given him, saying she gave him the fruit and he ate. JFB notes he blames God as well as Eve, turning a gift into an excuse. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Eve blamed the serpent, saying it beguiled her and she ate. Both answers shift blame outward, and JFB stresses the sin was far more than eating: it was self-love, ingratitude and preference of the creature over the Creator. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: God pronounced doom first on the serpent, cursed above all cattle and beasts, to go on its belly and eat dust. JFB reads a double curse: on the creature itself, and on the spiritual serpent whose power would be destroyed. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: God put enmity between the serpent and the woman, and between their seeds, promising the woman's seed would bruise the serpent's head while it bruised his heel. JFB reads this as the first promise of the Messiah, whose fatal blow to Satan would cost him suffering. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: To the woman God greatly multiplied sorrow in childbearing, and set her desire toward her husband who would rule over her. JFB notes her place changes from equal partner to humble subjection, with pain added to motherhood. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: To Adam God cursed the ground for his sake: in sorrow he would eat of it all his life. Work that was once easy becomes toil, because the ground itself now resists the worker. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Thorns and thistles would spring up, yet he would eat the herb of the field. The ground's fruitfulness is now mixed with weeds, so food requires clearing as well as planting. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: In the sweat of his face he would eat bread until he returned to dust, for dust he was and to dust he would return. JFB notes man did not die at once, but his body was changed toward dissolution and his fellowship with God was already broken. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Adam called his wife Eve, because she was the mother of all living. JFB reads the name as hope: even after the sentence, she is named for life and for the promised Saviour to come through her. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: God made coats of skins and clothed them. JFB sees divine institution of sacrifice here: covering now requires a life given, pointing to worship through a Redeemer rather than fig leaves. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: God said man had become as one of us, knowing good and evil, and must not now take also of the tree of life. JFB reads this not as irony but as compassion over what sin had made of a man once formed in God's image. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: So God sent man out of Eden to till the ground from which he was taken. Exile is both judgment and mercy: he may not seize back by eating what obedience had forfeited. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: God placed cherubim and a flaming sword at the east of Eden to keep the way to the tree of life. JFB renders it as God dwelling between the cherubim, so that access to life now runs through mediation, as later at the mercy seat. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
