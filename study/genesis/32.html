Study Notes — Genesis

Chapter 32

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill and Adam Clarke, all public-domain works, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: As Jacob went on his way, angels of God met him, a reassuring sign as he returned to Canaan. JFB links this to the ladder vision, as a pledge of God's continued presence and protection. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Jacob called the place Mahanaim, meaning two camps, because God's host seemed encamped beside his own. JFB places it between Gilead and the Jabbok, near that brook. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Jacob sent messengers ahead to Esau in Seir, a prudent step to learn his brother's temper before meeting him. JFB notes the road lay near the wild district where Esau had settled in power. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The message was humble and respectful: Jacob called Esau lord and himself servant, and said he had stayed with Laban until now. Henry notes that yielding and respectful speech goes far toward pacifying offence. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Jacob listed his oxen, asses, flocks, and servants to show he returned with abundance and needed nothing from Esau. JFB reads the message as paying respectful homage, not begging for help. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: The messengers reported that Esau was coming to meet Jacob with four hundred men, leaving his intentions unclear. JFB notes Esau's studied reserve gave Jacob reason to dread the worst, especially with a helpless family to protect. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Jacob was greatly afraid and distressed, and divided his people, flocks, herds, and camels into two companies. Henry observes that lively fear can live alongside humble confidence in God's power and promise. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: His plan was that if Esau struck one company, the other might escape. The division was prudence under fear, not a loss of trust. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Jacob prayed to the God of Abraham and Isaac, the Lord who had told him to return to his country and kindred. JFB calls this the first recorded example of prayer in the Bible: short, earnest, and aimed directly at the occasion. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Jacob confessed he was unworthy of the least of God's mercies, recalling that he had crossed Jordan with only his staff and now had become two companies. Henry counts this thankful acknowledgement and humble confession as the pattern of true prayer. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: He pleaded for deliverance from Esau, fearing his brother would strike mothers and children alike. The prayer states the fear plainly and leaves the whole affair to God. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Jacob rested his plea on God's own word: that God would do him good and make his seed as countless as sand. Henry notes that the best we can say to God in prayer is what he has said to us. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: That night Jacob selected a present for Esau from what he had at hand. JFB stresses that prayer did not replace prudence: Jacob combined earnest prayer with active, foresighted effort. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: The present was large and varied — two hundred she-goats, twenty he-goats, two hundred ewes, and twenty rams. Its size showed the respect due to a superior and the seriousness of Jacob's wish for peace. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Thirty milch camels with their colts, forty cows, ten bulls, twenty she-asses, and ten foals completed the gift. JFB notes the milch camels alone were of immense value, since she-camels were a chief part of Arab wealth. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Jacob put each drove in his servants' hands separately, with space between drove and drove. JFB explains the prudence: the gift would look more imposing, Esau's anger would have time to cool between droves, and a refusal of the first could warn the rest. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: The foremost servant was told exactly what to say when Esau asked whose he was, where he was going, and whose the droves were. JFB notes every servant was given the same words, so the message would clearly come from Jacob himself. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Each servant was to say the droves were Jacob's present to his lord Esau, and that Jacob himself was behind them. The repeated, identical message was meant to impress Esau step by step. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: The second, third, and all following droves received the same command, so Esau would hear one consistent account. Uniformity, not variety, was the point. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: They were also to add that Jacob was behind them, keeping his own approach after the gift. Jacob hoped the present going before him would appease Esau before they met face to face. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: The present went on ahead, while Jacob himself lodged that night with his company. JFB notes he lodged only part of the night, restless before the crossing. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: That night Jacob took his two wives, two maidservants, and eleven sons across the ford of the Jabbok. JFB identifies the Jabbok with the modern Zerka, a stream running from Gilead into the Jordan. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Having sent his family and all he had across the brook, Jacob was left alone. JFB pictures him returning to the north bank to seek God's blessing in silent prayer on the means he had set in motion. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Alone, Jacob wrestled with a man until daybreak. JFB notes the wrestler is called both angel and God, and is widely taken to be the angel of the covenant appearing to encourage his distressed servant. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: When the man saw he did not prevail, he touched Jacob's hip, and it was put out of joint as they wrestled. The injury marked the struggle as real and costly, not a mere contest of strength. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: At daybreak the man said, let me go, but Jacob answered that he would not let go unless he was blessed. JFB notes Jacob knew whom he wrestled with and clung to God's promise to do him good. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Asked his name, Jacob answered plainly, Jacob. The question brought his old history — the name tied to grasping — into the open before the new name was given. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: His name was changed to Israel, because as a prince he had power with God and with men and had prevailed. Henry notes the new name celebrates not craft but true valour in prayer. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Jacob asked the man's name, but was answered with a question and a blessing instead. JFB explains the refusal kept Jacob from being puffed up by his victory or supposing he could command the angel. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: Jacob called the place Peniel, because he had seen God face to face and his life was preserved. Henry notes the name honours God's grace: Jacob had seen God's appearance and obtained his favour. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: As Jacob passed over Peniel the sun rose on him, and he limped on his thigh. JFB compares the lameness to a thorn in the flesh: a lasting reminder that the victory was granted in grace, not won by strength alone. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: Because of this, Israelites do not eat the sinew on the hip joint to this day. JFB explains the sinew as the nerve fastening the thigh bone in its socket, and the abstinence as a traditional usage rather than a Mosaic law. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
