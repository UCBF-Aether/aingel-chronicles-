Study Notes — Genesis

Chapter 49

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill and Adam Clarke, all public-domain works, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Jacob calls his sons together to tell them what shall befall them in the last days, speaking not as a dying saint only but as an inspired prophet of the tribes to come. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: He bids them gather and listen to Israel their father, a summons to unity, since they are to remain one people and not be separated as Abraham's and Isaac's sons were. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Reuben is addressed as firstborn, might, and beginning of strength, with the excellency of dignity and power that primogeniture should have carried. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Reuben is unstable as water, boiling up with passion in his incest, and shall not excel, so his tribe never produces a judge, prophet, or ruler and is first carried captive. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Simeon and Levi are brethren in disposition as in blood, united by similar tempers, and their instruments of cruelty recall the slaughter at Shechem. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Jacob refuses to join their secret council or assembly, dissociating his honour from their anger, in which they slew a man and hamstrung oxen in self-will. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Their anger is cursed for its fierceness and cruelty, not their persons, and they shall be divided in Jacob and scattered in Israel, a sentence later turned to blessing for Levi as priests. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Judah is he whom his brothers shall praise, a play on his name, and his hand shall be on his enemies' neck, with his father's children bowing before him, marking his pre-eminence among the tribes. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Judah is a lion's whelp, going up from the prey and crouching as a lion, so none dare rouse him, picturing a tribe growing from dependence to victorious strength. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: The sceptre shall not depart from Judah nor a lawgiver from between his feet until Shiloh comes, and to him shall the gathering of the peoples be, a promise of enduring rule ending in the promised one. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Binding his foal to the vine and washing his garments in wine pictures Shiloh's kingdom as rural peace and plenty, with vines so abundant that even a donkey is tied to the choicest vine. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: His eyes dark with wine and teeth white with milk picture the richness of Judah's hill country, famed for vineyards and pastures, where wine and milk are plentiful. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Zebulun shall dwell at the haven of the sea and be a haven of ships, with his border toward Zidon, a seafaring tribe mentioned before his older brother Issachar for his coming superiority. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Issachar is a strong ass couching between two burdens, stout and strong-bodied, a tribe built for labour and endurance. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Seeing rest is good and the land pleasant, Issachar bows his shoulder to bear and becomes a servant to tribute, preferring to buy peace on the fertile plain of Esdraelon rather than live in constant jeopardy. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Dan shall judge his people as one of the tribes of Israel, put on exact equality with Leah's and Rachel's sons despite being a secondary wife's son, with a ruler of his own. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Dan shall be a serpent by the way, an adder biting the horse's heels so its rider falls backward, picturing a small border tribe striking with stealth and suddenness. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Jacob breaks off to say he has waited for the Lord's salvation, a prayer for relief when he foresees the troubles and backslidings that will come on his posterity, especially Dan. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Gad, a troop shall overcome him, but he shall overcome at the last, or, by the heel, a border tribe often raided yet fighting back, whose name itself sounds like a troop. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Out of Asher his bread shall be fat, and he shall yield royal dainties, since the rich soil of his settlement will supply plenty of food fit for kings. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Naphtali is a hind let loose, giving goodly words, picturing swiftness and freedom, with speech that is graceful and persuasive. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Joseph is a fruitful bough by a well, whose branches run over the wall, an image of progressive growth and luxuriant fruitfulness for the two tribes sprung from him. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: The archers have sorely grieved him, shot at him, and hated him, and JFB reads the archers as his brothers, Potiphar, and his wife, whose envy, revenge, and temptation were the arrows. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: His bow abides in strength and his arms are made strong by the mighty God of Jacob, since his steady virtue, innocence, patience, and faith resisted every trial, and from God comes Israel's shepherd and stone. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: The God of his father shall help him and the Almighty bless him with blessings of heaven above, rain and dew, of the deep beneath, springs and rivers, and of breast and womb, numerous progeny and cattle. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: His father's blessings prevail above the blessings of his progenitors, even to the everlasting hills, and rest on Joseph, the one separate from his brothers, as greater in extent, definiteness, and nearness of fulfilment. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Benjamin shall ravin as a wolf, devouring prey in the morning and dividing spoil at night, a border tribe marked by impetuosity, fierceness, and warlike appetite. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: All these are the twelve tribes of Israel, and Jacob blesses each according to his blessing, so each tribe's portion and character become its heraldic motto under God's foreordaining love. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Jacob charges them to bury him with his fathers in the cave in Ephron's field, declaring with his latest breath, before all his family, that he dies in the same faith as Abraham. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: He names the cave of Machpelah, bought by Abraham for a burial possession, grounding his burial wish in a purchase already made, not a new claim. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: There Abraham and Sarah, Isaac and Rebekah, and Leah were buried, so Jacob's burial joins a family grave already full of the patriarchs and their wives. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: The field and cave were bought from the Hittites, a legal possession in Canaan, so Jacob's claim to a grave there rests on purchase, not conquest. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: Having finished commanding his sons, Jacob gathers up his feet into the bed, yields up the ghost, and is gathered to his people, a phrase distinct from mere burial. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
