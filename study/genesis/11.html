Study Notes — Genesis

Chapter 11

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill and Adam Clarke, all public-domain works, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The descendants of Noah were still held together by one common language, and Henry and JFB note that many were unwilling to separate even though God had directed mankind to spread out and fill the earth. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: They settled in the fertile valley of Shinar, watered by the great rivers, and chose it as the centre of their union and power rather than moving onward. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: With no stone in that region, they used brick, some sun-dried and some kiln-burnt, with bitumen as a strong cement, which is the ordinary building material of that country. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: A tower reaching to heaven is a figure of speech for great height. The real fault, the commentators stress, was not building a city as such, but building it to defeat God's purpose by preventing their scattering. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: God's coming down is spoken in human terms: it pictures God taking notice of and inspecting the work, not a literal descent from place to place. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: One people with one language could carry their design through, which shows both the strength of united purpose and why God intervened before their rebellion hardened further. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Confounding their language meant a failure in speech, so that people understood only those of their own group. In this way God easily undid their plan and compelled the very dispersion they had tried to prevent. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: The scattering was God's doing, and once it happened the building stopped, since the united workforce that the project depended on was gone. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: The place was called Babel because there God confused human speech, and the unfinished city stands as a reminder that plans made against God's direction come to nothing. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: The record now turns to Shem's line, tracing the family through which God's purposes will run after the flood, beginning with Arphaxad born two years after it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Arphaxad's long life after his son's birth, with other sons and daughters, shows the line continuing and widening even as the years of each generation begin to shorten. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: The line moves one step forward from Arphaxad to Shelah, keeping the chosen family in view through the generations between the flood and Abram. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Shelah's years after Shelah's son, with further children, continue the steady record of one generation succeeding another on the way to Abram. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Eber is the next named link, and his name is remembered in the line because the family history is being traced person by person, not merely by peoples. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Eber's long remaining years, with other sons and daughters, underline that the chosen line was preserved through ordinary family life over a great span of time. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Peleg follows Eber, and the commentators connect his lifetime with the division of peoples that followed Babel, as mankind spread into separate nations. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Peleg's years after his son, with further children, keep the genealogy moving forward in the same measured pattern from father to son. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Reu is the next link in the chain from Shem toward Abram, and the record notes his age at his son's birth to fix each generation in its place. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Reu's remaining years, with other sons and daughters, show the family branching out while the main line that the history follows stays clearly marked. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Serug follows Reu, bringing the line nearer to Abram's own time as the ages at fatherhood continue to fall. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Serug's years after his son, with further children, continue the pattern of a line preserved generation by generation. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Nahor is the next father in the line, and his naming here matters because his family will soon meet Abram's own story directly. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Nahor's remaining years, with other sons and daughters, show the wider family growing around the single line the history is tracing. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Terah, Abram's father, is the next link, so the long genealogy from Shem now arrives at the household from which Abram himself will come. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Terah's years after his son, with further children, close the long backward look and set the stage for Abram's own family. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Terah's three sons, Abram, Nahor, and Haran, are named together because the story that follows turns on all three households, not on Abram alone. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: The record narrows to Terah's family: Haran fathered Lot, who will travel with Abram and whose story runs alongside his uncle's. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Haran died in his homeland, Ur, a city given to fire-worship, and JFB notes that Terah's family shared in that idolatry, which makes Abram's later call out of it all the sharper. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Abram married Sarai and Nahor married Milcah, Haran's daughter, so the two brothers' households were closely joined before the journey to Canaan began. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: Sarai's childlessness is noted here because it will make God's later promise of a son to Abram a matter of faith against all ordinary expectation. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: Terah took Abram, Lot, and Sarai out of Ur toward Canaan, but they stopped and settled in Haran, on the road toward the Euphrates crossing into the promised land. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: Terah died in Haran at 205 years, and the commentators note the puzzle this age raises about the order of his sons' births, which is eased if Abram was not the eldest son. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
