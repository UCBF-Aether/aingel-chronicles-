Study Notes — Genesis

Chapter 16

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill and Adam Clarke, all public-domain works, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Sarai was still childless after many years, and she had an Egyptian maid named Hagar, likely one of the servants gained in Egypt, who now becomes central to a plan born of impatience. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown; John Gill

2 COMMENTARY: Sarai told Abram that God had kept her from bearing, and urged him to take Hagar, hoping to gain children through her, since a maid's children counted as her mistress's. Gill notes both acted from eager desire for the promised seed, without consulting God. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown; John Gill

3 COMMENTARY: After ten years in Canaan, when Abram was eighty-five, Sarai herself gave Hagar to him as a secondary wife, a step Gill says nothing could excuse except their longing for the promised child. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown; John Gill

4 COMMENTARY: Hagar conceived at once, and then despised Sarai in her eyes, reproaching her mistress for barrenness, an ungrateful turn after Sarai herself had proposed the arrangement. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown; John Gill

5 COMMENTARY: Sarai, stung by Hagar's contempt, appealed to Abram and blamed him for the wrong, asking God to judge between them, as people often blame others for troubles their own plans have made. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Abram, seeking peace, left Hagar in Sarai's hand, and Sarai dealt harshly with her until she fled. Gill notes Abram gave too large a commission and should have judged between them himself. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown; John Gill

7 COMMENTARY: The angel of the Lord found Hagar by a spring on the road to Shur, toward Egypt, meeting a runaway servant in the wilderness with a question rather than a rebuke. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown; John Gill

8 COMMENTARY: The angel called her by name and station, Hagar, Sarai's maid, and asked where she had come from and where she was going, reminding her of her duty before giving her any promise. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown; John Gill

9 COMMENTARY: She was told to return to her mistress and submit, a hard command that required humility and trust that God could deal with her situation better than flight could. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown; John Gill

10 COMMENTARY: Along with the command to return came a promise that her offspring would be multiplied beyond numbering, so obedience was not left without hope. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: She was told she would bear a son and call him Ishmael, because God had heard her affliction, so the child's very name would remember that God listens to the afflicted. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Ishmael would be a wild man, at odds with others and others with him, yet living in the presence of his brethren, a picture of a free, restless people that would not be easily ruled. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Hagar named God as the God who sees her, amazed that the all-seeing God had looked on so lowly a runaway with love and care, and that she had lived after seeing him. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown; John Gill

14 COMMENTARY: The well was therefore called Beer-lahai-roi, the well of the Living One who sees, between Kadesh and Bered, keeping the memory of this meeting in the very name of the place. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Hagar bore Abram a son, so the household's first child came not through Sarai but through her maid, setting up the tensions that would follow. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Abram named the boy Ishmael, as the angel had directed, accepting the name God had given and with it the promise and the trouble bound up in this son. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
