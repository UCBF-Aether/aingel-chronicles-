Study Notes — Genesis

Chapter 25

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill and Adam Clarke, all public-domain works, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: After Sarah's death Abraham took another wife, Keturah, a second marriage in old age that Henry reads as provision for his household, not a return to his former life. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Keturah bore him six sons, Zimran, Jokshan, Medan, Midian, Ishbak and Shuah, in whom the promise of a greatly increased posterity was in part fulfilled. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Jokshan fathered Sheba and Dedan, and Dedan's sons were Asshurim, Letushim and Leummim, peoples who would settle to the east. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Midian's sons were Ephah, Epher, Hanoch, Abida and Eldaah, completing the list of Keturah's grandchildren. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Abraham gave all that he had to Isaac, keeping the main inheritance clearly with the son of promise. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: To the sons of his concubines Abraham gave gifts and sent them eastward away from Isaac while he still lived, providing for them without dividing the inheritance. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Abraham lived 175 years, a hundred years after he first came to Canaan, a long sojourn in a strange country. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Abraham died in a good old age, an old man full of years, and was gathered to his people, the quiet end of a life of faith. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Isaac and Ishmael together buried him in the cave of Machpelah, in Ephron's field before Mamre, a burial that brought the long-divided brothers together. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: The field Abraham had bought from the sons of Heth became his grave and Sarah's, the one piece of Canaan he owned outright. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: After Abraham's death God blessed Isaac, who lived near Beer-lahai-roi, the blessing passing to the next generation at the well where God had once met Hagar. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: This is the account of Ishmael, whom Hagar the Egyptian bore to Abraham, a separate record kept before the story turns to Isaac's line. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Ishmael's sons by name, in order of birth, were Nebaioth, Kedar, Adbeel, Mibsam, Mishma, Dumah and Massa, the first of his twelve princes. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: His other sons were Hadar, Tema, Jetur, Naphish and Kedemah, completing the twelve names. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: These twelve are named again as a group, showing that the twelve princes correspond to twelve tribes. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: These are Ishmael's sons, and these are their names by their villages and encampments, twelve princes according to their nations. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Ishmael lived 137 years, then died and was gathered to his people, the same span as his father's later years would echo in Isaac's life. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: His descendants settled from Havilah to Shur, east of Egypt toward Assyria, living apart from their brothers, as had been foretold of Ishmael. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: This is the account of Isaac, Abraham's son, whom Abraham fathered, returning the story to the promised line after the notices of Keturah and Ishmael. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Isaac was forty when he married Rebekah, daughter of Bethuel the Aramean of Paddan-aram and sister of Laban, a marriage within Abraham's kindred as charged. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Isaac prayed to the Lord for Rebekah because she was childless, and the Lord granted his prayer, so Rebekah conceived, showing that promised children come by prayer as well as by promise. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: The children struggled within her, and Rebekah, troubled by the strife, went to ask the Lord what it meant. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: The Lord told her two nations were in her womb, two peoples would be separated, one stronger than the other, and the elder would serve the younger, an oracle that overturned the custom of primogeniture. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: When her days were fulfilled, twins were in her womb, the oracle beginning to take shape in the birth itself. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: The first came out red and hairy all over like a garment, and they called him Esau, a name matching his appearance. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: His brother came out holding Esau's heel, and they called him Jacob, the heel-grasper, a name that would follow him through his life of supplanting. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: The boys grew up, Esau a skilful hunter and a man of the field, Jacob a quiet man dwelling in tents, two very different characters under one roof. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Isaac loved Esau because he ate of his game, while Rebekah loved Jacob, a divided affection in the household that would later cause great trouble. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Jacob was cooking stew when Esau came in from the field faint with hunger, a small, ordinary moment that became a turning point. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: Esau asked Jacob for some of the red stew, saying he was faint, and for that reason he was called Edom, the red one. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: Jacob answered, first sell me your birthright, seizing his brother's hunger to press for the firstborn's privilege. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: Esau said he was about to die and asked what use the birthright was to him, valuing present hunger over a future inheritance. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: Jacob made him swear first, and Esau swore and sold his birthright, making the bargain binding with an oath. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: Jacob gave Esau bread and lentil stew, and Esau ate, drank, rose and went his way, thus despising his birthright, a careless contempt Henry calls the heart of the sin. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
