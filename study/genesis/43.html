Study Notes — Genesis

Chapter 43

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill and Adam Clarke, all public-domain works, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The famine is sore in the land, so the pressure that forced the first journey has not lifted and the family cannot live on what was brought before. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Only when the Egyptian corn is eaten up does Jacob say to go again, and JFB notes about a year seems to have passed, so necessity at last overcomes his reluctance. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Judah reminds his father of the governor's solemn protest that they will not see his face without their brother, stating the condition plainly before any new journey. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: If Benjamin is sent, Judah says, they will go and live and not die, since the journey is now the only way to feed the household. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: If Benjamin is not sent, Judah warns, they will not go down, since facing the governor without him would be useless and dangerous. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Jacob asks why they dealt so ill with him as to tell the man they had another brother, a father's grief speaking, since that honest answer created the condition he now fears. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: The brothers explain that the man asked closely about their father and brother, so their answers were drawn out by questioning, not volunteered lightly. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Judah urges that the lad be sent with him, so they may go and live, putting the choice starkly between risking Benjamin and letting the household starve. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Judah offers himself as surety for Benjamin, promising to bear the blame for ever if he does not bring him back, a pledge that later shapes his great speech. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Judah argues that delay has already cost them a second journey, urging that action now would have brought them home twice over. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Jacob yields and orders a present of the land's best fruits, balm, honey, spices, myrrh, nuts, and almonds, following the Eastern custom of never approaching a man of power empty-handed. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Double money is to be carried, one sum to repay what was returned and one for new corn, since honesty in this case is also the safest policy. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Benjamin is now to be taken, and the command is short and stark, marking the moment Jacob surrenders the son he had guarded. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Jacob commits them all to God Almighty for mercy before the man, resigned to a heavy trial and praying it may be overruled for good. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: The men take the present, the double money, and Benjamin, and stand before Joseph, so the company that returns is complete at last. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Seeing Benjamin with them, Joseph orders his steward to bring them to his house and prepare a meal, a hospitality that will puzzle men who expect punishment. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: The steward, the upper servant who manages a great Egyptian house, carries out Joseph's order, and the brothers are taken in as guests, not prisoners. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: The men are afraid at being brought into Joseph's house, fearing the returned money will be used as an occasion to seize them and their asses. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: At the door they come near to the steward and speak with him, explaining themselves honestly before they are accused. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: They begin their account, saying they came down the first time to buy food, establishing that their first journey was innocent before they mention the money. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: They recount finding each man's money in his sack at the lodging place, a plain account that matches their earlier fear and their present honesty. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: They add that they brought the money back in full weight, together with other money for new corn, and cannot tell who put it there, so no charge of keeping it can stand. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: The steward answers with peace, saying their God has given them treasure, and brings Simeon out to them, turning fear into relief. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: The steward brings them into Joseph's house, gives water to wash their feet and fodder for their asses, the ordinary hospitality that calms men who expected a trap. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: They make the present ready against Joseph's coming at noon, having heard they will dine there, so the gift is prepared with care before the host appears. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: When Joseph comes home they bring the present into the house and bow to the earth, so the dream of bowing is fulfilled again, this time with Benjamin among them. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Joseph asks after their welfare and their aged father, the very questions that show his heart is on his family, not on their supposed crime. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: They answer that their father is alive and well, and bow again, confirming to Joseph the news he most wanted and could not yet openly seek. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Joseph lifts his eyes to Benjamin, his own mother's son, and greets him with a blessing, singling out the brother he has not seen since childhood. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: Joseph's affection overcomes him and he hurries to his chamber to weep in private, keeping his feelings hidden until his plan is complete. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: Washing his face and restraining himself, Joseph orders the meal served, so private emotion gives way again to the governor's composure. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: Tables are set apart, Joseph by himself, the brothers by themselves, and the Egyptians by themselves, because Egyptians counted eating with Hebrews an abomination, chiefly over the sacred cattle the Hebrews ate. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: The brothers are seated in birth order, firstborn to youngest, a minute Egyptian custom that astonishes them, since no stranger could know their ages. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: Portions are sent from Joseph's table, and Benjamin's is five times any other's, a distinguished mark of favour in Eastern custom, where honour is shown by quantity, and they drink and are merry with him. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
