Study Notes — Genesis

Chapter 29

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill and Adam Clarke, all public-domain works, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Jacob went on his journey with a light heart after the vision at Bethel, lifting up his feet and resuming the road, since tokens of divine favour quicken duty. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: He looked and saw a well in the field, with three flocks lying by it, for the flocks were watered from that well, and a great stone covered its mouth. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: All the flocks were gathered there, the stone was rolled from the well's mouth, the sheep were watered, and the stone was put back, a shared custom for a scarce water source. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Jacob asked the shepherds where they were from, and they answered, from Haran, the very place he sought. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: He asked if they knew Laban son of Nahor, and they said they knew him, bringing him within reach of his mother's family. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: He asked if Laban was well, and they said he was well, and pointed out Rachel his daughter coming with the sheep, providence bringing the family to him at the well. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Jacob said it was still high day, not time to gather the cattle, and urged them to water the sheep and go feed them, a practical suggestion from a fellow shepherd. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: They answered they could not until all the flocks were gathered and the stone rolled away, explaining the custom that governed the well. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: While he was still speaking, Rachel came with her father's sheep, for she was a shepherdess, as unmarried daughters of great shepherds often tended flocks. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: When Jacob saw Rachel and Laban's sheep, he went near, rolled the stone from the well's mouth and watered Laban's flock, rendering no small service to the young shepherdess. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Jacob kissed Rachel and lifted up his voice and wept, overcome by finding kindred after his long exile. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Jacob told Rachel he was her father's brother's son, Rebekah's son, and she ran and told her father, bringing the news home swiftly. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: When Laban heard of Jacob his sister's son, he ran to meet him, embraced and kissed him, and brought him to his house, and Jacob told Laban all these things. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Laban said, surely you are my bone and my flesh, and Jacob stayed with him a month, a stranger freely entertained until he was expected to tell his errand and work. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Laban asked Jacob, because he was his brother, whether he should serve for nothing, and told him to name his wages, turning kinship into an honest bargain. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Laban had two daughters, the elder Leah and the younger Rachel, the two women whose rivalry would shape Jacob's household. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Leah had tender eyes, while Rachel was beautiful in form and appearance, a contrast the narrative notes without praising one at the other's expense beyond the fact. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Jacob loved Rachel and offered to serve Laban seven years for Rachel his younger daughter, a proposal by which a poor suitor paid in service instead of gifts or cattle. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Laban answered that it was better to give her to Jacob than to another man, and told him to stay, accepting the bargain. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Jacob served seven years for Rachel, and they seemed to him but a few days because of the love he had for her, severe work made light by love. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: When the seven years were complete, Jacob asked Laban to give him his wife, since his days were fulfilled and he wanted to go in to her. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Laban gathered all the men of the place and made a feast, the public wedding celebration at which the deception would be hidden. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: In the evening Laban brought Leah his daughter to Jacob, and Jacob went in to her, the bride's veil concealing her identity through the wedding day. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Laban gave his maid Zilpah to Leah as her handmaid, a servant who would later become part of the household's tangled story. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: In the morning Jacob saw it was Leah, and he confronted Laban, asking what he had done, since he had served for Rachel, and why Laban had deceived him. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Laban excused himself by the custom of the country, that the younger must not be given before the firstborn, a custom he should have told Jacob honourably at the start. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Laban proposed that Jacob fulfil Leah's week of festivities, and then Rachel would also be given to him for another seven years of service. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Jacob did so, fulfilling Leah's week, and Laban gave him Rachel his daughter as wife, so Jacob now had two wives and a second debt of service. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Laban gave his maid Bilhah to Rachel as her handmaid, matching the gift he had made to Leah. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: Jacob went in also to Rachel, and he loved Rachel more than Leah, and served Laban another seven years, love and labour now bound together for fourteen years. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: When the Lord saw that Leah was unloved, he opened her womb, while Rachel remained barren, grace meeting the neglected wife where human love had failed. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: Leah conceived and bore a son and called him Reuben, saying the Lord had looked on her affliction, and now her husband would love her. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: She conceived again and bore a son and called him Simeon, saying the Lord had heard that she was unloved and had given her this son also. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: She conceived again and bore a son and called him Levi, saying now her husband would be joined to her, because she had borne him three sons. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: She conceived again and bore a son and called him Judah, saying now she would praise the Lord, and then she stopped bearing, praise replacing rivalry as the motive for the name. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
