Study Notes — Genesis

Chapter 40

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill and Adam Clarke, all public-domain works, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: After these things, Pharaoh's butler and baker offended their lord the king of Egypt. JFB notes both were officers of great rank: the butler oversaw the royal vineyards and cellars, and the baker supervised all provision for the royal table. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh was angry with his two officers, the chief butler and the chief baker. Their high standing made the offence a court matter, not a household quarrel. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: He put them in ward in the house of the captain of the guard, in the prison where Joseph was bound. JFB notes they were held until their case could be investigated, in an outer part of Potiphar's house where the royal prison stood. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The captain of the guard charged Joseph with them, and he served them, and they continued a season in ward. JFB notes it was Potiphar himself, not merely the keeper, who gave Joseph this charge, as if by now satisfied of Joseph's innocence. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Each of them dreamed a dream in one night, each according to its interpretation, the butler and the baker of Egypt's king, bound in prison. Henry notes it was not so much the prison as their dreams that made them sad, and JFB stresses the providence in both dreaming on the same night. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Joseph came to them in the morning, looked on them, and saw they were sad. Henry praises this compassion: it is often a relief to those in trouble simply to be noticed. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Joseph asked Pharaoh's officers why they looked so sad that day. The question opened the conversation that led to the interpretations. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: They said they had dreamed a dream with no interpreter, and Joseph answered that interpretations belong to God, and asked them to tell him their dreams. JFB notes Joseph first directed them to God, then volunteered, through divine help, to aid them. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: The chief butler told his dream: in his dream a vine was before him. The dream's setting, JFB notes, pictures the king at exercise attended by his butler with a cooling draught. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: In the vine were three branches, which budded, blossomed, and brought forth ripe grapes. The growth from branch to ripe cluster is the dream's hopeful movement. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh's cup was in his hand, and he took the grapes, pressed them into the cup, and gave the cup to Pharaoh. JFB notes everything was done in the king's presence: the cup washed, the juice pressed into it, and the cup handed over, lightly resting on the fingertips. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Joseph said the three branches are three days. The interpretation turns the dream's threefold pattern into a short, fixed time. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Within three days Pharaoh would lift up the butler's head and restore him to his place, and he would deliver Pharaoh's cup as before. Henry notes the butler's dream foretold advancement, and Joseph gave the good news plainly. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Joseph asked the butler to think on him when it was well with him, show him kindness, mention him to Pharaoh, and bring him out of that house. JFB notes the earnest appeal reveals a sadness and longing for release that even Joseph's piety could not dispel. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Joseph explained that he had been stolen from the land of the Hebrews, and here too had done nothing to deserve the dungeon. Henry notes Joseph states his innocence mildly, without reflecting on his brothers who sold him or on his mistress and master. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: When the chief baker saw the interpretation was good, he told his dream too: he had three white baskets on his head. Hope raised by another's good news moved him to speak. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: In the uppermost basket were all manner of bakemeats for Pharaoh, and the birds ate them out of the basket on his head. JFB explains the baskets were wicker, carried piled on the head from kitchen to dining rooms, and birds snatching food was a frequent occurrence in Egyptian palaces. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Joseph answered that the three baskets are three days. The same number that promised restoration to the butler now frames a darker outcome. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Within three days Pharaoh would lift the baker's head from off him, hang him on a tree, and birds would eat his flesh. JFB notes Joseph described an Egyptian form of execution: decapitation, then the headless body gibbeted by the highway until devoured by birds. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: On the third day, Pharaoh's birthday, he made a feast for all his servants and lifted up the heads of the chief butler and chief baker among them. JFB notes the birthday was a holiday season honoured by free pardon to prisoners, and Henry adds that Joseph's interpretations came to pass on the very day fixed. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: He restored the chief butler to his butlership, and the butler gave the cup into Pharaoh's hand. The dream's picture — cup pressed and handed to the king — became fact. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: But he hanged the chief baker, as Joseph had interpreted to them. Henry notes Joseph was not at fault for bringing the baker no better tidings: an interpreter cannot make the message otherwise than it is. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Yet the chief butler did not remember Joseph, but forgot him. JFB calls this human nature — men forget in prosperity those who were companions in adversity — while Henry adds that Joseph learned by disappointment to trust in God only. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
