Study Notes — Genesis

Chapter 36

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill and Adam Clarke, all public-domain works, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: This chapter opens the generations, that is, the history of the leading men and events, of Esau, who is Edom. JFB traces the name Edom to his red colour at birth, his craving for the red pottage, and the fierce character of his descendants. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Esau took wives from Canaan's daughters, Adah and Aholibamah, named here with their fathers. JFB notes Esau's wives appear under different names elsewhere, since women in that age often bore two names, especially on moving to a new settlement. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Bashemath, Ishmael's daughter and Nebajoth's sister, was Esau's third wife. JFB identifies her with Mahalath, since both stand in the same relation to Ishmael and Nebajoth. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Esau took his whole household, cattle, and substance and went to a country away from his brother Jacob. JFB reads the move as providence at work: Esau's vast temporal prosperity, fulfilling the promise to him, was itself the occasion of his leaving Canaan and making way for Jacob's return. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Their riches were too great for them to dwell together, and the land where they sojourned could not bear both households' cattle. Outward prosperity, not quarrel alone, forced the separation. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Thus Esau dwelt in Mount Seir, and the verse repeats that Esau is Edom. JFB notes Seir was divinely assigned as his possession, while Henry contrasts Seir in hand with Canaan held only in promise. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: The dukes of Esau's sons are now listed, beginning with Eliphaz's line. JFB explains that duke here does not mean a British peer, but a tribal head like a sheik, emir, or Highland chieftain, with fourteen such heads flourishing at once. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: This verse names the dukes that came from Eliphaz, Esau's firstborn, in the land of Edom. The list shows Edom organised by tribes named for Esau's sons, each under its own chief. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: This verse names the dukes that came from Reuel, son of Bashemath, in Edom. The same tribal pattern repeats in a second branch of Esau's family. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: This verse names the dukes that came from Aholibamah, daughter of Anah. All three wives' lines thus produced recognised tribal chiefs in Edom. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: The verse sums up the preceding lists: these are the sons of Esau, who is Edom, and these are their dukes. The summary closes the account of Esau's own descendants before turning to the older inhabitants. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: The chapter now lists the sons of Seir the Horite, the native inhabitants of the land. JFB notes these native dukes were incorporated with the Edomite race, so Edom's chiefs were not all of Esau's blood. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: This verse lists Lotan's children, with his sister Timna, among the Horite families. The detail preserves the native lineages that were absorbed into Edom. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: This verse lists Shobal's children among the Horites. Each family is recorded because its head later appears as a duke in the land of Seir. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: This verse lists Zibeon's children, Ajah and Anah. The genealogy keeps the Horite branches distinct before their chiefs are named together. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: This Anah is singled out as the one who found the mules, or in several ancient versions the water springs, in the wilderness while feeding his father's asses. JFB notes that such a discovery was distinction enough among a pastoral people to earn a permanent mention. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: This verse lists Anah's children, Dishon and Aholibamah. The names link the Horite stock to Esau's household through Aholibamah, Esau's wife. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: This verse lists Dishon's children among the Horites. The record moves branch by branch through Seir's seven sons. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: This verse lists Ezer's children among the Horites. The lists are brief because their purpose is to account for the dukes that follow. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: This verse lists Dishan's children among the Horites, completing the seven branches of Seir. The full Horite stock is now before the reader. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: These are the dukes that came from the Horites, named by their fathers, in the land of Seir. JFB's point stands over the whole list: native chiefs held rank alongside Esau's descendants in Edom. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: This verse continues the Horite dukes, naming Dishon, Ezer, and Dishan among their dukes. The second half of the list completes the native chiefs. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: The chapter now lists the kings who reigned in Edom before any king reigned over Israel. JFB stresses that royal power was not built on the ruins of the dukedoms but existed at the same time as them. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: Bela son of Beor reigned in Edom, from the city Dinhabah, opening the king list. Henry notes the pomp of Edom's kings would later try Israel's faith while they were slaves in Egypt, since God's people had to wait for God's time. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: When Bela died, Jobab son of Zerah of Bozrah reigned in his stead. The succession shows Edom's kingship passing from ruler to ruler, not by a single dynasty. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: When Jobab died, Husham of the land of Temani reigned in his stead. Each king is tied to a place, grounding the list in Edom's geography. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: When Husham died, Hadad son of Bedad, who struck Midian in Moab, reigned from Avith. The military note is the one deed remembered for this king. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: When Hadad died, Samlah of Masrekah reigned in his stead. The bare succession continues the record of kings before Israel had a king. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

37 COMMENTARY: When Samlah died, Saul of Rehoboth by the river reigned in his stead. The river town marks another centre of Edomite rule. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

38 COMMENTARY: When Saul died, Baal-hanan son of Achbor reigned in his stead. The list's steady rhythm underlines how long Edom had kings while Israel waited. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

39 COMMENTARY: When Baal-hanan died, Hadar reigned from Pau, with his wife Mehetabel named. The final king's household detail closes the king list on a personal note. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

40 COMMENTARY: The chapter closes by recapitulating the dukes of Esau by families, places, and names. JFB notes this is a second listing, now arranged by residence rather than by descent. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

41 COMMENTARY: This verse names dukes Timnah, Alvah, and Jetheth by their places. The recapitulation maps Edom's chiefs to the districts they held. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

42 COMMENTARY: This verse names dukes Aholibamah, Elah, and Pinon by their places. The place-names show Edom settled and divided, not merely a wandering clan. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

43 COMMENTARY: This verse names dukes Kenaz, Teman, Mibzar, Magdiel, and Iram, completing the list. The chapter ends where it began: he is Esau, the father of the Edomites, now shown as a settled people with chiefs and kings. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

No note for verse(s) 4, 5, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
