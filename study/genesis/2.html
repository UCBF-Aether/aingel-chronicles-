Study Notes — Genesis

Chapter 2

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill and Adam Clarke, all public-domain works, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Heaven, earth and all their host were finished: creation was complete, not ongoing. Henry stresses God ceased from creating, though he still upholds what he made. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: On the seventh day God ended his work and rested. Henry is clear God did not rest from weariness, but as one well pleased, marking the work done. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: God blessed the seventh day and set it apart as holy, because he rested in it. This is the beginning of Sabbath keeping: one day in seven kept for holy rest and holy work. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: This verse turns from the broad creation account to the detailed story of earth and humanity. The name LORD God now appears, pointing to God's personal, covenant dealings with people. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Before any plant of the field had grown, there was no rain and no man to till the ground. The scene is set as a world waiting for the gardener God will place in it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: A mist went up and watered the whole face of the ground. JFB and Henry take this as God's early provision of moisture before rain and farming began. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: God formed man from dust and breathed into him the breath of life, and man became a living soul. The two parts matter: a body from the earth, and life directly from God. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: God planted a garden in Eden, in the east, and put the man there. Eden is presented as a prepared home, not a wilderness man had to tame first. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: God made every pleasant and good tree grow there, including the tree of life and the tree of knowledge of good and evil. Henry notes the garden supplied both delight and a test of obedience. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: A river flowed out of Eden to water the garden and divided into four heads. The garden was well watered at its source, a picture of abundance rather than scarcity. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: The first river, Pison, is described as circling Havilah, a land of gold. JFB identifies these rivers as real ancient waterways, grounding Eden in a known geography. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Havilah's gold is called good, and bdellium and onyx are named there. The detail shows Eden's region as richly provided, not barren. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: The second river, Gihon, circles the land of Cush. Naming each river in turn traces the water outward from Eden into the wider world. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: The third river is Hiddekel, flowing east of Assyria, and the fourth is the Euphrates. These familiar names anchor the garden's rivers to lands readers later knew. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: God put the man in the garden to dress it and keep it. Work came before the fall: tending the garden was a dignified calling, not a punishment. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: God freely allowed the man to eat of every tree in the garden. The command begins with generous permission, not with prohibition. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Only the tree of knowledge of good and evil was forbidden, on pain of death. Henry stresses the test was small in itself, which makes obedience a matter of trust in God's word. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: God said it was not good for man to be alone, and promised a helper fit for him. Companionship is presented as part of God's good design, not an afterthought. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: God brought the beasts and birds to Adam to see what he would name them, and the name he gave stuck. Naming shows man's authority over the creatures and his wisdom in discerning their kinds. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Adam named all cattle, birds and beasts, but found no helper fit for himself among them. No animal could supply the companionship and likeness a human partner would. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: God caused a deep sleep to fall on Adam and took a rib from his side, closing up the flesh. The making of woman is God's own surgery, done while Adam rested in trust. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: From the rib God made a woman and brought her to the man. Henry famously notes she was taken from his side, to stand beside him as an equal companion, not from head or foot. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Adam recognised her as bone of his bones and flesh of his flesh, and called her Woman because she was taken out of man. The name marks both sameness and distinction: she shares his nature, yet is a distinct person. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Therefore a man leaves father and mother and cleaves to his wife, and they become one flesh. This states the marriage pattern: a new household, a lasting bond, and a union deeper than kinship alone. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Both were naked and not ashamed. Before sin, there was no shame between them, because there was no guilt to hide. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
