Study Notes — Genesis

Chapter 38

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill and Adam Clarke, all public-domain works, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: At that time Judah went down from his brothers and turned in to Hirah the Adullamite. JFB notes the phrase marks an interval near about that time, not a precise date, and Henry frames the whole chapter as proof that God's choice is of grace, not merit. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Judah saw a Canaanite's daughter, Shuah's daughter, took her, and went in to her. JFB compares Judah to Esau here: casting off religious restraint, he married into a Canaanite family, and the family that sprang from it was infamous for wickedness. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: She conceived and bore a son, and he called his name Er. The firstborn's birth begins the line that will carry Judah's name forward, for better and worse. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: She conceived again and bore a son, and she called his name Onan. The second son enters a household already marked by an unsuitable marriage. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: She conceived yet again and bore Shelah, at Chezib when she bore him. The third son, the youngest, will later become the centre of the dispute with Tamar. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Judah took a wife for Er his firstborn, whose name was Tamar. The marriage joins Tamar to Judah's house before Er's character is exposed. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Er, Judah's firstborn, was wicked in the sight of the Lord, and the Lord slew him. Henry reads this as an awful example of God's utter displeasure at sin, proclaimed in punishment. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Judah told Onan to go in to his brother's wife, marry her, and raise up seed to his brother. JFB notes this was the first instance of the custom later written in Moses' law: a brother marrying a childless widow so the child would count as the dead brother's heir. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Onan knew the seed would not be his, so when he went in to his brother's wife he spilled it on the ground, lest he give seed to his brother. His act was a deliberate refusal of the duty Judah had laid on him. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: What Onan did displeased the Lord, and he slew him also. The second death, like the first, is presented as divine judgment, not accident. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Judah told Tamar to remain a widow at her father's house until Shelah grew up, fearing Shelah might die as his brothers had. The instruction kept Tamar waiting on a promise Judah did not intend to keep. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: In process of time Shuah's daughter, Judah's wife, died, and Judah was comforted and went up to Timnath to his sheep-shearers with his friend Hirah. JFB notes sheep-shearing, toward the end of March, was a festive season when masters entertained friends and servants. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Tamar was told that her father-in-law was going up to Timnath to shear his sheep. The news reached her because Judah's movements were public, and it gave her the occasion for her plan. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Tamar put off her widow's garments, covered herself with a veil, wrapped herself, and sat in an open place by the way to Timnath, because Shelah was grown and she had not been given to him. Her disguise was a protest against Judah's broken promise, carried out by deception. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: When Judah saw her, he thought her a harlot, because she had covered her face. The veil that hid her identity also created the misunderstanding on which the rest of the chapter turns. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Judah turned to her by the way and asked to come in to her, not knowing she was his daughter-in-law, and she asked what he would give her. His ignorance does not excuse him in Henry's reading: the chapter is a record of profligate conduct, not of innocence. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Judah promised a kid from the flock, and Tamar asked for a pledge until he sent it. The bargain turned a passing encounter into a transaction with proof attached. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Asked what pledge, Tamar named his signet, bracelets, and staff, and he gave them to her, and she conceived by him. JFB notes the signet alone was worth more than a kid, and the bracelets were likely a ribbon or lace attaching the seal to the staff, making the pledge unmistakably his. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Tamar arose, went away, laid aside her veil, and put on her widow's garments again. The return to widow's dress hid the encounter and restored her outward standing. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Judah sent the kid by his friend the Adullamite to recover the pledge, but he did not find the woman. The honest attempt to pay the bargain failed because the woman had vanished with the disguise. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Hirah asked the men of the place where the harlot by the wayside was, and they said there was no harlot in that place. The local denial shows Tamar was not known there in that character. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Hirah returned to Judah saying he could not find her, and the men of the place said there was no harlot there. The failed search left Judah holding an unpaid bargain and an unredeemed pledge. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Judah said, let her take the pledge, lest we be shamed, since he had sent the kid and Hirah had not found her. Shame, not justice, ended his effort to recover his signet and staff. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: About three months later Judah was told Tamar had played the harlot and was with child, and he ordered her brought forth to be burnt. JFB notes fathers in patriarchal times held power of life and death over their households, and burning was an ancient punishment for adultery. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: As she was brought forth, Tamar sent to her father-in-law, saying she was with child by the man whose these were, and asked him to discern whose signet, bracelets, and staff they were. The pledge, kept for this moment, turned accuser into accused. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Judah acknowledged the pledges and said Tamar had been more righteous than he, because he had not given her to Shelah, and he knew her again no more. The confession admits his broken promise as the root of the affair. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: In the time of her travail, behold, twins were in her womb. The twins continue Judah's line, which Henry notes with wonder, since Christ himself would spring from this Judah. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: As she travailed, one put out his hand, and the midwife bound a scarlet thread on it, saying this came out first. The thread marked the firstborn claim before the birth was finished. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: As he drew back his hand, his brother came out, and she cried, how hast thou broken forth, so his name was called Pharez, meaning breach. The unexpected order gave the younger twin the firstborn place in the story. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: Afterward came his brother who had the scarlet thread on his hand, and his name was called Zarah. The thread remained as the sign of the hand that had first appeared. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
