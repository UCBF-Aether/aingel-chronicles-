Study Notes — Genesis

Chapter 41

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill and Adam Clarke, all public-domain works, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: After two full years Joseph's release comes only when God's timing is ripe, so the long delay is presented as patient waiting under providence rather than forgotten hope. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: The fat cows rising from the Nile fit Egypt, whose plenty depended on the river, and they picture years of abundance feeding on rich pasture. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: The lean cows standing beside the fat ones set up a stark contrast, so that plenty and want are seen side by side before the want consumes the plenty. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The lean cows eating the fat ones, without growing fat themselves, pictures a famine so severe that it swallows up the memory of former plenty. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: A second dream in the same night repeats the message in a new image, the ears of grain, so that the warning is doubled and cannot be dismissed as a passing fancy. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: The thin ears, blasted by the east wind, represent a harvest ruined by a hot, withering wind, a familiar destroyer of grain in Egypt. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: As with the cows, the worthless ears devour the good ones, showing that famine does not merely follow plenty but absorbs its whole surplus. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh's troubled spirit drives him to Egypt's magicians and wise men, yet their united skill fails, clearing the way for a wisdom plainly not their own. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: The chief butler's belated confession is self-serving, aimed at pleasing his master, yet God uses even this tardy reminder to bring Joseph to mind. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: The butler recalls Pharaoh's anger and the prison, reminding the king of the setting in which a young Hebrew had proved able to interpret dreams. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: The butler and the baker each dreamed in one night, and the pairing of their dreams underlines that both carried a meaning that needed interpreting. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: The butler points to a young Hebrew servant in prison whose interpretations came true, presenting Joseph as tested, not merely recommended. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Events matched the interpretations exactly, one man restored and the other executed, so Joseph's gift had already been proved by outcome, not by claim alone. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Once God's set time has come, no prison can hold Joseph; he is hurried out, shaved and changed to Egyptian custom, and brought before the king. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh states his need plainly, a dream no one can interpret, and Joseph's answer turns the attention at once from himself to God. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Joseph disclaims any power of his own and promises only that God will give a peaceful, fitting answer, a model of humility before power. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh retells the dream from the riverbank, the scene fixed where Egypt's life depended on the Nile, so the symbols are drawn from Egypt itself. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: The seven well-favoured cows grazing in the meadow picture years when the Nile's rich grasses feed the land generously. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: The second herd is strikingly ugly and gaunt, worse than any seen in Egypt, so the coming want is shown as extreme, not ordinary scarcity. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: The lean cows eating the fat ones repeats the first dream's warning in its sharpest form: famine will consume the years of plenty. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: That the lean cows stay lean after eating shows the famine's depth; even swallowing plenty leaves the land still starving. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: The second dream turns from cattle to grain, with seven full ears on one stalk picturing an unusually rich harvest. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Seven thin ears, blasted by the east wind, picture harvests withered before they can feed anyone, a ruin familiar in Egypt's climate. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: The thin ears devouring the good ones, and the magicians' failure to explain it, together show a message from God that human skill cannot reach. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Joseph declares the two dreams are one message: God is showing Pharaoh what he is about to do, so the dreams are revelation, not puzzle. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: The seven good cows and seven good ears both mean seven years, and the repetition across two images confirms that plenty is the first part of the message. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: The seven lean cows and thin ears both mean seven years of famine, matching the plenty year for year, so the warning is measured and complete. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Joseph stresses that this is what God has shown, not his own guess, grounding the interpretation in divine disclosure rather than cleverness. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Seven years of great plenty are promised first, and Henry notes the goodness of God in sending plenty before famine so provision can be made. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: Seven years of famine will follow and swallow the plenty, so that the abundance will be forgotten in the land under the weight of want. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: The coming famine will be so severe that the former plenty will seem as nothing, a warning against trusting present comfort to last. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: The dream was doubled because the thing is established by God and will happen soon, so repetition here means certainty and urgency, not doubt. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: Interpretation leads at once to practical counsel: Pharaoh should find a discerning and wise man, since foreknowledge is useless without a capable steward. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: Officers are to be set over the land to take a fifth part of the produce in the good years, turning surplus into a government reserve rather than leaving it to private sale. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: Food is to be gathered in the cities and kept under Pharaoh's hand, so that storage is local, guarded, and ready where people will need it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: The stored food is a reserve for the famine years, so the land will not perish; wise foresight, Joseph argues, is how a nation survives a foreseen crisis. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

37 COMMENTARY: The counsel pleases Pharaoh and his servants, showing that the plan commended itself as practical wisdom, not only as a striking interpretation. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

38 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh asks whether anyone can be found with the Spirit of God in him, and even this faint acknowledgment of the true God marks Joseph's gift as divine. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

39 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh reasons that since God has shown Joseph all this, no one is as discerning and wise, so the interpreter is the natural choice to carry out his own plan. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

40 COMMENTARY: Joseph is set over Pharaoh's house, with all the people ruled by his word, and only the throne kept above him, an extraordinary rise from prison in a single day. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

41 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh's formal declaration, setting Joseph over all Egypt, is the preliminary to the public investiture that follows. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

42 COMMENTARY: The signet ring, fine linen dress, and gold chain are the insignia of high office, each marking authority, honour, and rank in Egyptian custom. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

43 COMMENTARY: Riding in the second chariot, with the cry to bow the knee before him, proclaims Joseph publicly as second only to Pharaoh throughout the land. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

44 COMMENTARY: Pharaoh's oath that no one shall move hand or foot without Joseph is a proverbial expression of almost total delegated power, ratified in council. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

45 COMMENTARY: The new Egyptian name and marriage into a distinguished priestly family complete Joseph's naturalisation, removing popular dislike of him as a foreigner. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

46 COMMENTARY: At thirty Joseph goes out over all Egypt, first surveying the land to fix the sites and sizes of the storehouses the plan will need. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

47 COMMENTARY: The earth bringing forth by handfuls pictures a luxuriant crop, with reapers grasping whole handfuls of ears at a time. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

48 COMMENTARY: Joseph gathers the food of the seven good years and lays it up city by city, so the surplus of each district is stored where it was grown. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

49 COMMENTARY: The grain grows beyond measure, until counting stops, showing a surplus large enough not only for Egypt but for neighbouring lands as well. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

50 COMMENTARY: Two sons are born to Joseph before the famine, and the naming that follows shows his private piety keeping pace with his public success. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

51 COMMENTARY: Manasseh's name records that God has made Joseph forget his toil and his father's house, not by erasing memory but by easing its pain. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

52 COMMENTARY: Ephraim's name records fruitfulness in the land of affliction, so Joseph owns God's providence both in forgetting sorrow and in new blessing. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

53 COMMENTARY: The seven years of plenty end exactly as predicted, a reminder that prosperity has its term and must be used while it lasts. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

54 COMMENTARY: Famine begins in all lands as Joseph had said, yet in Egypt there is bread, because foresight had turned prediction into provision. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

55 COMMENTARY: When Egypt itself hungers, Pharaoh sends the people to Joseph with the order to do whatever he says, trusting the steward he had appointed. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

56 COMMENTARY: Joseph opens the storehouses and sells to the Egyptians, but even careful distribution cannot soften a famine that grows severe over all the land. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

57 COMMENTARY: People from all surrounding countries come to Egypt to buy grain, so Joseph's stored plenty becomes the means of life far beyond Egypt's borders. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
