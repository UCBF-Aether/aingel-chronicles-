Study Notes — Genesis

Chapter 42

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill and Adam Clarke, all public-domain works, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Jacob learns from common rumour that there is corn in Egypt, and his household, already worn down by scarcity, must use the natural means within reach rather than wait for a miracle. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Jacob sends his sons down to buy corn so the family may live and not die, turning the famine into a journey that will unexpectedly bring them face to face with Joseph. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Ten brothers go down to buy corn, a full company of the family acting together, which makes their later bowing before Joseph a fulfilment involving them all. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Benjamin is kept at home because Jacob fears harm may befall him, showing that the loss of Joseph still shapes the father's care for Rachel's remaining son. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: The same failed rains that dried Egypt also struck Canaan, so caravans pour across the desert for grain, and Israel's sons must join a journey full of painful associations. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Joseph, at the height of his power, directs the sales himself, likely overseeing the border storehouses where he may have expected messengers from his father's house. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Joseph knows his brothers at once while they do not know him, since he was a lad at parting and now wears official robes, and he speaks roughly to draw out news of his family. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: That they do not recognise him is no wonder: they see a governor, not the boy they sold, while he has ten faces to judge by and has long expected them. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Joseph remembers his dreams and charges them as spies come to see the land's naked, unfortified places, a charge that tests them and begins to humble them. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: The brothers protest they came only to buy food, insisting they are honest men, which sets up Joseph's demand for proof of their story. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: They claim to be true men, not spies, using honesty as their defence, though Joseph's test will probe whether their conduct now matches their words. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Joseph repeats the spy charge, holding his stern ruler's part, not from vindictiveness but to press them toward honesty about themselves. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: The brothers explain that one brother is not, a phrase for one cut off from the living, and that the youngest remains with their father, unknowingly telling Joseph his own story. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Joseph answers that they are still spies, keeping the pressure on so that the test he is about to impose cannot be evaded. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Joseph swears by Pharaoh's life, in Egyptian court style, and makes their release depend on bringing Benjamin, the proof that will test their truth. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: One brother is to be sent for Benjamin while the rest wait, so their words can be proved and their honesty put to a real test. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: All are put in ward for three days, a confinement designed to bring them to reflection, and it works, as conscience begins to speak. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: On the third day Joseph softens the terms, saying they shall live if they act honestly, since he fears God, and the governor's mercy begins to show through his severity. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: One brother is to stay bound while the rest carry corn home, so the family's hunger is met at the same time as the test is held in place. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: They must bring Benjamin down, and only then will their words be verified and they escape death, making Benjamin the key to the whole trial. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: The brothers confess to one another that they are guilty concerning their brother, reading their present distress as the just measure of what they once did to Joseph. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Reuben reminds them he warned them not to sin against the lad, and now his blood is required, so even the brother who tried to save Joseph shares the guilt of the company. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: They speak freely before Joseph because an interpreter stands between them, not knowing he understands every word of their remorse. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Joseph turns away to weep, then takes Simeon and binds him before their eyes, choosing the brother who may have been the most violent against him as the hostage for their return. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Joseph fills their sacks with corn and secretly returns each man's money, a private generosity he could afford from his own purse, meant as kindness, not fraud. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: They load their asses and set out by the desert route toward Hebron, carrying the corn home while the hostage and the test remain behind them. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: At a lodging place one brother finds his money in his sack, and the discovery throws them into greater perplexity, since it gives the governor a new handle against them. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Their hearts fail and they tremble, asking what God has done to them, so that even an apparent kindness is read as a judgment from heaven. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Back in Canaan they tell Jacob all that befell them, and Henry notes how the report, money and all, troubled a father who already distrusted sons he had not seen with Joseph since. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: They report the lord of the land speaking roughly and taking them for spies, repeating the charge exactly as Joseph had pressed it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: They repeat their protest that they are true men, not spies, carrying their defence home to their father as they had made it in Egypt. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: They repeat the account of twelve brothers, one not, and the youngest with their father, so Jacob hears again the painful tally of his family. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: They report the governor's terms, one brother left behind and Benjamin to be brought, which is the condition Jacob must now weigh. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: They report the promise that if Benjamin comes they may trade and the bound brother will be restored, so the way home for Simeon runs through Benjamin. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: Emptying their sacks, every man's money is found, and father and sons alike are afraid, since innocence does not remove the danger of how it will look. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: Jacob cries that he is bereaved, Joseph not, Simeon not, and Benjamin now threatened, concluding all things are against him, though Henry notes they were in fact working for his good. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

37 COMMENTARY: Reuben offers his two sons as pledge for Benjamin's return, a thoughtless and unwarrantable bargain meant only to assure his father of the utmost care. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

38 COMMENTARY: Jacob refuses to let Benjamin go down, fearing that calamity on the way would bring his grey hairs down with sorrow to the grave. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
