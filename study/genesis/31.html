Study Notes — Genesis

Chapter 31

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill and Adam Clarke, all public-domain works, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Jacob learned, apparently by report, that Laban's sons resented his prosperity and claimed it came from their father's wealth. Henry's point is that overvaluing wealth breeds envy, even between kin. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Laban's changed face told Jacob the relationship had soured, though Jacob had kept a good conscience. JFB notes that prosperity often stirs envy, and a good name cannot always be kept in a censorious world. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: God, not resentment, set Jacob's move in motion: he was told to return to his own land with the promise of God's presence. JFB draws the lesson to seek God's direction in journeys and settlements, not to act from fretfulness alone. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Jacob called Rachel and Leah to him at the flock, since he could not leave it, to tell them his settled plan. Henry and JFB both stress that a husband should consult his wives rather than act in secret. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Jacob pointed to two things: Laban's changed attitude, and the God of his father who had stayed with him through it. The contrast frames the whole speech — human favour failed, divine faithfulness did not. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Jacob reminded his wives that he had served Laban with all his strength, so his case rested on faithful service, not on scheming. JFB notes he laid out his grounds of dissatisfaction before mentioning God's blessing and command. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Laban had repeatedly changed Jacob's wages, yet God had not allowed him to harm Jacob. The verse shows a pattern: human unfairness met by divine protection. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Whatever wage Laban named, the flocks bore in Jacob's favour, so the outcome pointed away from Laban's control. Henry reads this as God overruling a dishonest bargain. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Jacob's conclusion was plain: God had taken the cattle from Laban and given them to him. The wealth was therefore a gift of providence, not a theft. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: In a dream at mating time Jacob saw the marked rams, a picture confirming that the increase came by God's ordering. The dream prepared him to trust the coming move. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: The angel of God called Jacob by name in the dream, and Jacob answered that he was listening. The personal call marks this as direction, not mere fancy. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Jacob was told to notice that all the rams were marked, because God had seen all that Laban was doing to him. Nothing in Laban's dealing had escaped God's notice. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: God identified himself as the God of Bethel, where Jacob had anointed a pillar and made a vow, and now called him to leave and return home. Henry's lesson is to remember past vows and communion with God when a hard decision comes. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Rachel and Leah answered together, fully approving Jacob's plan, since they had grievances of their own. JFB, quoting Henry, notes that a true helpmeet does not hinder a husband in what God calls him to do. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: The sisters felt treated as strangers: their father had sold them and used up the price. That sense of being cut off from any share at home made leaving easier to accept. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: They agreed that the riches God had taken from their father belonged to them and their children, and urged Jacob simply to do what God had said. Their consent rested on God's word, not on gain alone. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Jacob set his family on camels for the journey, in the quick, simple way of pastoral people moving camp. JFB pictures tents struck, baggage stowed, and a plain emptied within hours. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Jacob carried away only his own cattle and goods, the increase God had given him in Padan-aram. JFB stresses he took nothing of Laban's to make up for his losses. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: While Laban was away shearing sheep, Rachel stole her father's household images. Henry sees here how deep the family's drift toward idolatry ran, even after leaving idolatrous Chaldea. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Jacob left without telling Laban, so the departure was secret. JFB judges the secrecy prudent, since open notice might have meant detention by force or trickery. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Jacob crossed the river and headed for the hill country of Gilead, putting distance between his household and Laban's. The direction marks a clean break toward Canaan. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Laban heard of the flight on the third day and at once gave chase. Unencumbered, he travelled fast, while Jacob's large household and flocks moved slowly. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Laban caught up with Jacob after seven days at Mount Gilead, bringing his kinsmen with him. The meeting was therefore a confrontation, not a quiet farewell. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: God came to Laban in a dream by night and warned him not to speak to Jacob either good or bad. Henry's point is that God can bridle a wicked man's malice without changing his heart. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Laban overtook Jacob where Jacob had pitched his tent in the hills, and the two camps faced each other. The dream had already limited what Laban could do there. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Laban charged Jacob with stealing away and carrying off his daughters as if they were captives. JFB notes he said nothing of the wages charge, and his complaint about a proper send-off rang hollow after his own conduct. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Laban claimed he would have sent Jacob off with music and celebration if only he had been told. JFB calls this hypocritical cant, given how Laban had treated Jacob. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Laban added that he had not even been allowed to kiss his children goodbye, and called Jacob's course foolish. The charge tried to turn a flight from unfairness into a family offence. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Laban admitted he had power to hurt Jacob, but said the God of Jacob's father had warned him the night before. Restraint here came from God's warning, not from Laban's goodwill. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: Laban then raised his grave charge: Jacob had stolen his gods. JFB explains these teraphim as small human figures kept as superstitious talismans, not objects of true worship. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: Jacob answered that fear had driven him: he thought Laban might seize his daughters by force. His reply is honest about his motive, even though it does not cover Rachel's theft. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: Confident of his innocence and unaware of Rachel's act, Jacob boldly invited a search and pronounced a severe penalty on whoever held the images. JFB notes the boldness came from a clear conscience about his own conduct. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: Laban searched Jacob's tent, Leah's tent, and the servants' tents, and found nothing. The fruitless search began to clear Jacob, tent by tent. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: Rachel had hidden the images in the camel's saddle-bag and sat on them, so Laban's search of her tent found nothing. JFB notes the pack saddle was commonly used as a seat or cushion. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: Rachel excused herself from rising by pleading the custom of women, and Laban searched without finding the images. Henry treats the whole episode as part of the household's tangled idolatry and deceit. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: With the search over, Jacob's anger broke out and he demanded what offence justified so hot a pursuit. JFB notes that, passion running high, his charges ranged widely over twenty years of grievance. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

37 COMMENTARY: Jacob challenged Laban to produce anything of his found among Jacob's goods and let both companies judge between them. The appeal to witnesses shows Jacob wanted an open verdict, not a shouting match. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

38 COMMENTARY: For twenty years Jacob's care had kept Laban's ewes from miscarrying, and he had not eaten the rams of the flock. JFB notes that in the East females were seldom killed for food except when barren, so this was careful, honest shepherding. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

39 COMMENTARY: When animals were torn by beasts, Jacob bore the loss himself instead of bringing the carcass to Laban. JFB explains that shepherds were held responsible for losses unless they could prove wild beasts caused them. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

40 COMMENTARY: Jacob described days of drought and nights of frost, with sleep driven from his eyes, as the price of his service. JFB adds that great and long provocation ruffles even disciplined tempers, and allows much for Jacob here. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

41 COMMENTARY: In twenty years Jacob had served fourteen for his daughters and six for his cattle, while Laban changed his wages ten times. The summary turns a long quarrel into a single, checkable account. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

42 COMMENTARY: Jacob credited his survival to the God of Abraham and the fear of Isaac: without God, Laban would have sent him away empty. Henry notes Jacob speaks of God as his father's God, counting himself unworthy yet beloved for his father's sake. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

43 COMMENTARY: Laban answered by claiming everything in sight — daughters, children, flocks — as his own, yet admitted he could do nothing about it that day. Henry notes he could neither justify himself nor condemn Jacob, so he changed tack. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

44 COMMENTARY: Laban proposed a covenant between them, with something to stand as witness. JFB describes the ancient ratification: a circle of stones for seats, a pillar set upright, a sacrifice, and a shared feast of reconciliation. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

45 COMMENTARY: Jacob set up a stone as a pillar, the first visible mark of the agreement. In a time when writing was little used, a stone monument kept the memory of a bargain. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

46 COMMENTARY: Jacob told his kinsmen to gather stones into a heap, and they ate together on the heap. Henry notes that eating and drinking together was the ancient token of hearty reconciliation. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

47 COMMENTARY: Laban and Jacob each named the heap in his own tongue, both names meaning a heap of witness. The double naming shows two peoples making one agreement. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

48 COMMENTARY: Laban declared the heap a witness between them that day, which fixed the name Galeed. The place itself was to remind both sides of what they had promised. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

49 COMMENTARY: The place was also called Mizpah, from Laban's prayer that the Lord would watch between them when they were apart. The name turns absence itself into a reason to trust God's oversight. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

50 COMMENTARY: The covenant bound Jacob not to ill-treat Laban's daughters or take other wives, with God as witness even where no man was present. Henry's lesson is that God judges between contending parties, and wrongdoing is at the wrongdoer's peril. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

51 COMMENTARY: Laban pointed to the heap and pillar he had set between them as the boundary mark of the agreement. Both markers were to stand where both men could see them. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

52 COMMENTARY: Heap and pillar were to witness that neither man would cross them to harm the other. JFB notes the difference in their oaths: Laban appealed to the God of Abraham and Nahor, while Jacob swore by the fear of Isaac, wary of the idolatry in Nahor's line. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

53 COMMENTARY: Laban invoked the God of Abraham, Nahor, and their father as judge, and Jacob swore by the fear of his father Isaac. The two oaths reveal two different religious standpoints under one treaty. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

54 COMMENTARY: Jacob offered sacrifice on the mountain and invited his kinsmen to eat bread, and they stayed the night there. Henry notes that peace with God puts true comfort into peace with friends. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

55 COMMENTARY: Early in the morning Laban kissed and blessed his children and grandchildren, then went home. Henry closes the chapter with the remark that God is often better to us than our fears, overruling men's spirits in our favour. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
