Study Notes — Genesis

Chapter 34

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill and Adam Clarke, all public-domain works, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Dinah, Leah's daughter, went out to see the daughters of the land. Henry warns that young people are never safer than under pious parents' care, and that going abroad among strangers to true religion exposes them to danger. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Shechem, son of Hamor the prince of the country, saw Dinah, took her, and defiled her. JFB notes the wrong followed from free mixing in the society of the place, where a young and inexperienced girl was flattered by a ruler's son. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Shechem's soul clung to Dinah, he loved her and spoke kindly to her. His attachment, JFB notes, must have grown through time and opportunity, not a single glance. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Shechem asked his father Hamor to get Dinah for him as his wife. The request treated marriage as the remedy for the wrong already done. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Jacob heard that Dinah had been defiled, but his sons were in the field, so he held his peace until they came. JFB explains that in a family by different wives, full brothers were the guardians and avengers of a sister's welfare. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Hamor came out to Jacob to talk with him about the matter. The fathers' meeting might have led to an amicable arrangement, before the brothers' arrival changed the temper of the talks. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Jacob's sons came in from the field, grieved and very angry, because Shechem had wrought folly in Israel by lying with Jacob's daughter. JFB notes good men could not but grieve, but their anger should have been governed, not left to harden into revenge. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Hamor proposed that Shechem's soul longed for Dinah, and asked Jacob's family to give her to him in marriage. JFB observes the prince and his son showed no jealousy of the powerful shepherds and seemed eager for friendly intercourse. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Hamor broadened the offer into intermarriage and shared dwelling, trade, and possession of the land. The proposal would have joined the two peoples, not merely two persons. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: He promised they could dwell, trade, and get possessions in a land open before them. The offer was generous in worldly terms, which made the brothers' answer more consequential. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Shechem himself then spoke to Dinah's father and brothers, asking to find grace in their eyes. JFB notes Jacob showed weakness in yielding so much of the negotiation to his fiery sons. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Shechem offered any dowry and gift they named, if only they would give him Dinah as wife. JFB explains the gift as betrothal presents to bride and kin, and the dowry as a settlement upon her. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Jacob's sons answered Shechem and Hamor deceitfully, because Dinah had been defiled. JFB condemns the deceit sharply: they used a show of religion as a cloak for a cruel and treacherous design. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: They said they could not give their sister to an uncircumcised man, for that would be a reproach to them. The objection was true in form — circumcision was the sign of the covenant — but false in intent. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: They would consent only if every male among the Shechemites was circumcised, as Jacob's household was. JFB notes they required nothing more: no teaching of the true God, only the outward rite. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: On that condition they would give their daughters and take Shechemite daughters, dwell together, and become one people. The bargain promised union on the basis of a rite they did not mean honestly. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: If the Shechemites would not be circumcised, Jacob's sons said they would take Dinah and leave. The threat made the condition look like a genuine boundary rather than a trap. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: The words pleased Hamor and Shechem. Their ready acceptance shows they took the proposal at face value. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: The young man did not delay, because he delighted in Jacob's daughter and was more honourable than all his father's house. His eagerness, not suspicion, drove him to act at once. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Hamor and Shechem came to the gate of their city, the place where public business was done, and spoke to its men. JFB notes the gate was where every public communication was made. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: They told the citizens the men were peaceable and the land was large enough for shared dwelling and trade. The pitch presented Jacob's household as profitable neighbours, not as a threat. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: The single condition repeated to the city was that every male be circumcised, as Jacob's men were. The leaders asked their people to accept a sacred rite for the sake of alliance. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Hamor and Shechem added a worldly motive: the newcomers' cattle, substance, and beasts would become theirs if they consented. JFB sees in the people's ready submission either great affection for the ruling family or Eastern despotism. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: All who went out of the city gate listened to Hamor and Shechem, and every male was circumcised. Henry notes the Shechemites submitted to the rite only to please their prince and enrich themselves. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: On the third day, when the men were sore, Simeon and Levi, Dinah's brothers, each took a sword and came boldly on the city. Henry traces the chain: foolish pleasure to seduction, wrath to revenge, revenge to treachery, treachery to murder. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: They slew all the males, then slew Hamor and Shechem with the sword and took Dinah out of Shechem's house. JFB stresses that Simeon and Levi alone, with their retainers, were the guilty actors in the massacre. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: The other sons of Jacob then came upon the slain and spoiled the city, because their sister had been defiled. Revenge, once begun, widened into plunder. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: They took sheep, oxen, asses, and all that was in city and field. The spoil shows the attack had become a raid, not a rescue. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: They took all the wealth, the little ones, and the wives captive, and spoiled everything in the houses. Henry notes how one sin leads on to another, spreading desolation like fire. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: Jacob told Simeon and Levi they had troubled him and made him stink among Canaanites and Perizzites, leaving his few household exposed to united vengeance. JFB notes Jacob dwelt on consequences, probably because that was the only argument likely to reach his hardened sons, and that only God's restraint saved the family. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: The brothers answered with a question: should Shechem have dealt with their sister as with a harlot? Their retort shows no repentance, only the original grievance restated as justification. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
