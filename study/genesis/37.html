Study Notes — Genesis

Chapter 37

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill and Adam Clarke, all public-domain works, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Jacob dwelt in Canaan, the land where his father had been a stranger. JFB notes Jacob sojourned at Mamre near Hebron, in the same manner and for the same motives as Abraham and Isaac before him. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Joseph, seventeen years old, was feeding the flock with his brothers, alongside the sons of Bilhah and Zilpah, and he brought their evil report to his father. JFB explains Joseph held a chief shepherd's oversight, so he reported not as a tale-bearer but as a faithful steward. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Israel loved Joseph more than all his children, because he was the son of his old age, and made him a coat of many colours. JFB notes the Hebrew phrase can also mean a wise son, and the patched coloured coat was a dress of distinction. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: When his brothers saw their father's greater love, they hated Joseph and could not speak peaceably to him. JFB notes withholding the usual peace greeting was an unmistakable sign of hostility, and Henry calls Jacob's open partiality great indiscretion in a family by different mothers. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Joseph dreamed a dream and told it to his brothers, and they hated him yet more. JFB notes dreams were much attended to in ancient times, and this dream was evidently symbolical, its meaning easily discerned. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Joseph asked his brothers to hear the dream he had dreamed. The telling itself, not just the dream, sharpened the division. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: In the dream they were binding sheaves, and Joseph's sheaf rose and stood upright while theirs bowed to it. The farm image made the meaning plain to everyone who heard it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: His brothers answered with scorn: would he indeed reign and have dominion over them, and they hated him more for his dreams and words. Henry notes they rightly understood the dream, yet committed crimes to defeat it, and thereby became instruments of accomplishing it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Joseph dreamed yet another dream: sun, moon, and eleven stars bowed to him. The second dream, under different emblems, made fulfilment seem certain, as JFB notes from the pattern of repeated dreams. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Joseph told this dream to his father and brothers, and his father rebuked him, asking if father, mother, and brothers would indeed bow to him. The rebuke shows even Jacob felt the claim's boldness, though he did not dismiss it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: His brothers envied him, but his father observed the saying. Henry notes Joseph was given the prospect of advancement early, to support him under long troubles to come, though he dreamed of preferment and not of imprisonment. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: His brothers went to feed their father's flock in Shechem. JFB notes Shechem's vale was richly watered, worth a journey of some fifty miles from Hebron for the sake of its herbage. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Israel asked Joseph if his brothers were not feeding the flock in Shechem, and said he would send him to them, and Joseph answered, here am I. Henry praises Joseph's ready obedience: the best-beloved child should be the most ready to obey. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Jacob sent Joseph from the vale of Hebron to see whether it was well with his brothers and the flocks, and to bring word back, and Joseph came to Shechem. The errand was a father's care, not a spy's mission. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: A man found Joseph wandering in the field and asked what he sought. The wandering shows Joseph arrived at Shechem to find his brothers already gone. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Joseph said he sought his brothers and asked where they fed their flocks. His plain answer kept the errand open and honest. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: The man said they had left for Dothan, having been heard to say, let us go to Dothan, and Joseph followed and found them there. JFB notes Dothan means two wells, and the move may have been forced by the hatred their name had earned after the Shechem massacre. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: When they saw Joseph afar off, before he came near, they conspired to kill him. Henry stresses the deliberation: this was malice aforethought, in cold blood, not a sudden quarrel. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: They said to one another, behold, this dreamer comes. JFB notes the phrase is literally master of dreams, a bitter sneer, since false claims to dreams were detested as a kind of blasphemy. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Their plan was to kill him, cast him into a pit, and say an evil beast had devoured him, and then see what would become of his dreams. The lie was planned together with the murder. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Reuben heard it and delivered Joseph from their hands, saying they should not kill him. Henry notes Reuben, the firstborn with most reason to be jealous, proved Joseph's best friend, and God overruled all for his own purpose. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Reuben proposed instead to cast Joseph into a pit in the wilderness and lay no hand on him, secretly intending to rescue him and return him to his father. JFB notes the pits were dry wells, and Reuben's plan was thwarted so that deliverance came by other means. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: When Joseph came to his brothers, they stripped him of his coat of many colours. JFB pictures his astonishment at the cold reception and rough usage from brothers he had approached in unsuspecting affection. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: They took him and cast him into a pit, which was empty with no water in it. Henry calls their tender mercies cruel: they left him to perish with hunger and cold. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: They sat down to eat bread, then looked up and saw a company of Ishmaelites from Gilead, with camels bearing spicery, balm, and myrrh, going down to Egypt. JFB notes the brothers' cool indifference at table, and that the caravan was a mixed Arab trading company bound for Egypt's embalming trade. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Judah asked what profit there was in killing their brother and concealing his blood. The question shifted the plan from murder to sale, without softening the betrayal. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Judah proposed selling Joseph to the Ishmaelites and not laying hands on him, since he was their brother and flesh, and his brothers agreed. JFB notes they had no wish to commit more crime than necessary for their end, and knew the merchants trafficked in slaves. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Midianite merchants passed by, drew Joseph out of the pit, and sold him to the Ishmaelites for twenty pieces of silver, who brought him into Egypt. JFB notes the sum, if in shekels, was small, and providence used both brothers and merchants, each following his own course, to work out God's purposes. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Reuben returned to the pit, found Joseph gone, and rent his clothes. JFB notes Reuben had taken a roundabout route to rescue Joseph secretly, and found his excellent intentions painfully defeated. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: Reuben returned to his brothers and cried, the child is not, and I, where shall I go? His distress was for his father as much as for himself, since he would have to face Jacob without Joseph. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: They took Joseph's coat, killed a kid of the goats, and dipped the coat in blood. Henry notes one sin taught them another: to hide the crime with lying, since he who covers his sin shall not prosper long. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: They sent the coat to their father, asking him to know whether it was his son's coat or not. JFB marvels that their sneer, calling Joseph thy son's coat, did not awaken Jacob's suspicion, but extreme grief is blind. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: Jacob knew the coat and concluded an evil beast had devoured Joseph, who was without doubt torn in pieces. The brothers' deception worked because Jacob read the bloody coat as proof, not as a claim to be tested. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: Jacob rent his clothes, put sackcloth on his loins, and mourned for his son many days. JFB explains the rent garment and coarse black sackcloth as the common signs of Eastern mourning. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: All his sons and daughters rose to comfort him, but he refused comfort, saying he would go down to the grave mourning for his son. JFB notes he meant not the earth, since Joseph was supposed torn in pieces, but the place of departed souls where he expected to meet his son. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: The Midianites sold Joseph in Egypt to Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh and captain of the guard. Henry closes the chapter in wonder at providence: brothers, merchants, and Potiphar each got their ends, yet God's design — Israel's descent into Egypt and eventual deliverance — was already in train. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
