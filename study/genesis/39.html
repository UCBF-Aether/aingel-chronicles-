Study Notes — Genesis

Chapter 39

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill and Adam Clarke, all public-domain works, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Joseph was brought down to Egypt, and Potiphar, Pharaoh's officer and captain of the guard, bought him from the Ishmaelites. JFB notes Potiphar's name means devoted to the sun, fixing his home in the Delta, and that providence drew him to Joseph so Joseph could be trained in a court-connected house for higher service. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: The Lord was with Joseph, and he was a prosperous man in his Egyptian master's house. JFB notes Joseph, though changed in condition, was not changed in spirit: stripped of his coat, he kept his character, and in an idolater's house he still worshipped the true God. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: His master saw that the Lord was with Joseph and made all he did prosper. Henry's point is that God's presence makes all we do prosperous, and a good servant can be a blessing to the place where he lives. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Joseph found grace in Potiphar's sight, served him, and was made overseer of his house, with all Potiphar had put into his hand. JFB notes the observant master soon saw Joseph's superior qualities and made him his confidential servant. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: From the time Joseph was made overseer, the Lord blessed the Egyptian's house for Joseph's sake, in house and field alike. JFB adds that such blessing usually follows faithful service in the ordinary course of things, and even worldly masters admire religion supported by a consistent life. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Potiphar left all he had in Joseph's hand and knew nothing of it except the bread he ate, and Joseph was a goodly person and well favoured. The trust was total, and the closing note about Joseph's appearance prepares the temptation that follows. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: After these things his master's wife cast her eyes on Joseph and said, lie with me. JFB notes Egyptian women enjoyed more freedom than women in most Eastern countries then, giving her constant opportunity to meet Joseph. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Joseph refused, telling his master's wife that his master knew nothing of what was in the house with him and had committed all to his hand. The refusal appeals first to trust betrayed, before it appeals to God. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: None was greater in the house than Joseph, and nothing was kept from him except Potiphar's wife, so he asked how he could do this great wickedness and sin against God. JFB calls this the true principle of moral purity: sin is against God, and that argument alone is sufficient. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: As she spoke to Joseph day by day, he did not listen to her, neither to lie with her nor to be with her. Henry stresses the constancy of the urging, and Joseph's refusal even to keep company that would feed the temptation. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: About this time Joseph went into the house to do his business, and none of the men of the house were within. The empty house set the scene for the crisis: opportunity, privacy, and a tempter in authority. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: She caught him by his garment, saying, lie with me, and he left his garment in her hand, fled, and got out. Henry notes Joseph would not stay to parley with temptation but fled from it, as escaping for his life. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: When she saw he had left his garment in her hand and fled forth, her disappointment turned to revenge. JFB notes she vowed revenge and accused Joseph first to the household servants, then to her lord. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: She called the men of her house and said Potiphar had brought in a Hebrew to mock them, claiming Joseph came to lie with her and she cried aloud. JFB calls this an affected aspersion on her husband for keeping a Hebrew, the very abomination of Egyptians, in the house. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: She added that when Joseph heard her lift her voice and cry, he left his garment with her and fled out. The true fact — the abandoned garment — was now retold as proof of guilt. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: She laid up his garment by her until his lord came home. Keeping the garment preserved the false evidence for Potiphar's return. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: She spoke to her lord in the same words, saying the Hebrew servant Potiphar had brought came to mock her. Henry notes it is no new thing for the best of men to be falsely accused of the worst crimes by those who are themselves the worst of criminals. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: She repeated that as she lifted her voice and cried, Joseph left his garment with her and fled. The story told to the servants was now told, unchanged, to the master. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: When Potiphar heard his wife's words, his wrath was kindled. The verse records anger, not inquiry, and Henry notes Potiphar believed the accusation. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Joseph's master took him and put him in the prison where the king's prisoners were bound, and he was there in the prison. JFB describes the roundhouse attached to such an officer's dwelling, partly a dungeon, where state prisoners were kept under strict watch. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: But the Lord was with Joseph, showed him mercy, and gave him favour in the sight of the keeper of the prison. Henry notes that those with a good conscience in prison have a good God there, and JFB suggests the keeper may already have known Joseph's innocence. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: The keeper committed all the prisoners to Joseph's hand, and whatever they did there, Joseph was the doer of it. Trust returned to Joseph even in prison, as it had in Potiphar's house. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: The keeper looked not to anything under Joseph's hand, because the Lord was with him and made what he did prosper. Henry closes the chapter with the lesson that a good man will do good wherever he is, and will be a blessing even in bonds. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
