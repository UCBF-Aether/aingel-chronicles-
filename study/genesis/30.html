Study Notes — Genesis

Chapter 30

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill and Adam Clarke, all public-domain works, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: When Rachel saw she bore Jacob no children, she envied her sister and demanded children from Jacob, or else she would die, envy grieving at another's good rather than trusting God who made the difference. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Jacob's anger was kindled against Rachel, and he asked, am I in God's stead, who has withheld the fruit of the womb, a faithful reproof that put the matter back with God. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Rachel said, here is my maid Bilhah, go in to her, so she may bear on my knees and I may have children through her, following the custom by which a maid's children counted as her mistress's. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Rachel gave Jacob Bilhah her handmaid as wife, and Jacob went in to her, a remedy born of envy rather than prayer. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Bilhah conceived and bore Jacob a son, the first child credited to Rachel's household. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Rachel called him Dan, saying God had judged her and heard her voice and given her a son, the name carrying a mark of rivalry with her sister. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Bilhah conceived again and bore Jacob a second son, extending Rachel's line through her maid. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Rachel called him Naphtali, saying she had wrestled greatly with her sister and prevailed, a name that openly recorded the contest. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: When Leah saw she had stopped bearing, she took Zilpah her maid and gave her to Jacob as wife, answering rivalry with rivalry. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Zilpah bore Jacob a son, Leah's household gaining through her maid as Rachel's had. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Leah called him Gad, saying a troop comes, a name of good fortune for the new son. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Zilpah bore Jacob a second son, continuing Leah's answer to Rachel. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Leah called him Asher, saying happy am I, for the daughters will call me blessed, a name of joy rather than open strife. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Reuben went out in wheat harvest and found mandrakes in the field and brought them to his mother Leah, a plant then prized, rightly or wrongly, as an aid to childbearing. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Rachel asked Leah for some of her son's mandrakes, and Leah answered sharply, asking if it was a small matter to have taken her husband, and whether Rachel would take her son's mandrakes too. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Rachel bargained that Jacob would lie with Leah that night in exchange for the mandrakes, trading a night with her husband for the coveted plants. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: God listened to Leah, and she conceived and bore Jacob a fifth son, a child Henry reads as given in answer to prayer, not to the mandrakes themselves. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Leah called him Issachar, saying God had given her a hire, because she had given her maid to her husband, the name recalling the mandrake bargain. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Leah conceived again and bore Jacob a sixth son, her own childbearing resuming after the maid's children. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Leah called him Zebulun, saying God had endowed her with a good dowry, and now her husband would dwell with her, because she had borne him six sons. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Afterwards Leah bore a daughter and called her Dinah, the only daughter named in this record of Jacob's children. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: God remembered Rachel, listened to her and opened her womb, the long-barren wife at last receiving the gift she had demanded in envy. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Rachel conceived and bore a son and said, God has taken away my reproach, the shame of childlessness lifted. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: She called him Joseph, saying, the Lord shall add to me another son, a name that looked forward in hope to a further child. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: When Rachel had borne Joseph, Jacob said to Laban, send me away, so I may go to my own place and country, the birth of his beloved wife's son prompting him to seek independence. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Jacob asked Laban for his wives and children, for whom he had served, saying Laban knew his service, a claim for what fourteen years of labour had earned. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Laban asked Jacob to stay, saying he had learned by experience that the Lord had blessed him for Jacob's sake, prosperity that Laban wanted to keep. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Laban told Jacob to name his wages, and he would give them, opening a new bargain after the marriage service was done. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Jacob reminded Laban how he had served him and how Laban's cattle had fared under his care, appealing to a record both men knew. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: Jacob said Laban had little before he came, and it had increased greatly, and the Lord had blessed Laban wherever Jacob went, and now he asked when he would provide for his own house. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: Laban asked what he should give Jacob, and Jacob answered that Laban need give him nothing, if he would do one thing for him, a proposal that would let Jacob earn his flock by selection rather than gift. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: Jacob proposed to pass through Laban's flock that day, removing every speckled, spotted and dark sheep and spotted goat as his wages, a flock marked by colour that could be counted honestly. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: Jacob said his righteousness would answer for him later, when Laban came to check his wages, since any animal not speckled or spotted in his possession would count as stolen. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: Laban agreed, saying, behold, let it be according to your word, accepting the terms as fair. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: That same day Laban removed the male goats that were ringstraked and spotted, the female goats that were speckled and spotted, and every dark sheep, and gave them to his sons, and put three days' journey between them and Jacob, a move that stripped Jacob's starting flock of the very markings he was to earn. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: Laban set three days' journey between himself and Jacob, and Jacob fed the rest of Laban's flocks, left with the plain-coloured animals from which his wages must somehow come. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

37 COMMENTARY: Jacob took rods of green poplar, hazel and chestnut, peeled white streaks in them and exposed the white, preparing the peeled rods for his plan at the watering troughs. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

38 COMMENTARY: He set the rods he had peeled before the flocks in the gutters of the watering troughs, where the flocks came to drink, so they would conceive as they came to drink. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

39 COMMENTARY: The flocks conceived before the rods and brought forth ringstraked, speckled and spotted cattle, the marked offspring increasing in Jacob's favour. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

40 COMMENTARY: Jacob separated the lambs and set the faces of the flocks toward the ringstraked and dark animals of Laban's flock, keeping his own flocks apart and managing the breeding with care. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

41 COMMENTARY: Whenever the stronger cattle conceived, Jacob laid the rods before their eyes in the troughs, so they would conceive among the rods. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

42 COMMENTARY: When the cattle were feeble he did not put the rods in, so the feebler were Laban's and the stronger Jacob's, a selective practice that steadily enriched Jacob. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

43 COMMENTARY: The man increased exceedingly, gaining many cattle, maidservants, menservants, camels and donkeys, so that, as Henry notes, the blessing promised at his departure was fulfilled in his estate as well as his family. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
