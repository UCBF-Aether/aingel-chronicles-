Study Notes — Acts

Chapter 21

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: After we were gotten from them, and had launched, we came with a straight course unto Coos, and the day following unto Rhodes, and from thence unto Patara. Henry's voyage rule is providence: providence must be acknowledged when our affairs go on well, and the straight course to Coos, Rhodes, and Patara is affairs going on well. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Finding disciples, we tarried there seven days: who said to Paul through the Spirit, that he should not go up to Jerusalem. Henry says wherever Paul came, he inquired what disciples were there, and found them out, and foreseeing his troubles, from love to him and concern for the church, they wrongly thought it would be most for the glory of God that he should continue at liberty, but their earnestness to dissuade him from it renders his pious resolution the more illustrious. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: When we had accomplished those days, we departed and went our way, and they all brought us on our way, with wives and children, till we were out of the city, and we kneeled down on the shore, and prayed. Henry says he has taught us by example, as well as by rule, to pray always, to pray without ceasing, and their last farewell was sweetened with prayer, on the shore with wives and children. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: When we had taken our leave one of another, we took ship, and they returned home again. Henry's farewell sweetened with prayer is the leave-taking itself: they took leave one of another and took ship, after the shore prayer. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: When we had finished our course from Tyre, we came to Ptolemais, and saluted the brethren, and abode with them one day. Henry's finding disciples continues: wherever Paul came, he inquired what disciples were there, and found them out, and at Ptolemais he salutes the brethren and abides with them one day. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: The next day we that were of Paul's company departed, and came unto Caesarea, and we entered into the house of Philip the evangelist, which was one of the seven, and abode with him. Henry's finding disciples is the house of Philip: wherever Paul came, he inquired what disciples were there, and found them out, and at Caesarea they enter and abide with Philip the evangelist. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: As we tarried there many days, there came down from Judaea a certain prophet, named Agabus. Henry's express warning begins with Agabus's coming: Paul had express warning of his troubles, that when they came, they might be no surprise or terror to him. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: When he was come unto us, he took Paul's girdle, and bound his own hands and feet, and said, Thus saith the Holy Ghost, So shall the Jews at Jerusalem bind the man that owneth this girdle, and shall deliver him into the hands of the Gentiles. Henry says Paul had express warning of his troubles, that when they came, they might be no surprise or terror to him, and the general notice given us, that through much tribulation we must enter into the kingdom of God, should be of the same use to us. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: When we heard these things, both we, and they of that place, besought him not to go up to Jerusalem. Henry says their weeping began to weaken and slacken his resolution, and it was a trouble to him that they should so earnestly press him to do that in which he could not gratify them without wronging his conscience. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Then Paul answered, What mean ye to weep and to break mine heart? for I am ready not to be bound only, but also to die at Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus. Henry's cross-bearing answer is the readiness: has not our Master told us to take up our cross, and Paul is ready not to be bound only, but also to die at Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus, though their weeping breaks his heart. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: When he would not be persuaded, we ceased, saying, The will of the Lord be done. Henry says when we see trouble coming, it becomes us to say, not only, The will of the Lord must be done, and there is no remedy, but, Let the will of the Lord be done, for his will is his wisdom, and he doeth all according to the counsel of it, and when a trouble is come, this must allay our griefs, that the will of the Lord is done, and when we see it coming, this must silence our fears, that the will of the Lord shall be done, and we ought to say, Amen, let it be done. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: After those days we took up our carriages, and went up to Jerusalem. Henry's resolution held is the going up: the will of the Lord be done having been said, they take up their carriages and go up to Jerusalem. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: There went with us also certain of the disciples of Caesarea, and brought with them one Mnason of Cyprus, an old disciple, with whom we should lodge. Henry says it is honourable to be an old disciple of Jesus Christ, to have been enabled by the grace of God to continue long in a course of duty, stedfast in the faith, growing more and more experienced, to a good old age, and with these old disciples one would choose to lodge, for the multitude of their years shall teach wisdom, and Mnason is that old disciple. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: When we were come to Jerusalem, the brethren received us gladly. Henry says many brethren at Jerusalem received Paul gladly, and adds the searching thought: we think, perhaps, that if we had him among us, we should gladly receive him, but we should not, if, having his doctrine, we do not gladly receive that. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: When he had saluted them, he declared particularly what things God had wrought among the Gentiles by his ministry. Henry says Paul ascribed all his success to God, and to God they gave the praise, for God had honoured him more than any of the apostles, yet they did not envy him, but on the contrary, glorified the Lord, and they could not do more to encourage Paul to go on cheerfully in his work. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: When they heard it, they glorified the Lord, and said unto him, Thou seest, brother, how many thousands of Jews there are which believe, and they are all zealous of the law. Henry's glorifying and the thousands together show the tension: they glorified the Lord for God's work among the Gentiles, yet report many thousands of Jews who believe and are all zealous of the law, the very zeal the compliance plan must meet. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: They are informed of thee, that thou teachest all the Jews which are among the Gentiles to forsake Moses, saying that they ought not to circumcise their children, neither to walk after the customs. Henry says the weakness and evil of the human heart strongly appear, when we consider how many, even of the disciples of Christ, had not due regard to the most eminent minister that even lived, for not the excellence of his character, nor the success with which God blessed his labours, could gain their esteem and affection, seeing that he did not render the same respect as themselves to mere ceremonial observances, and how watchful should we be against prejudices. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: What is it therefore? the multitude must needs come together: for they will hear that thou art come. Henry's prudence plan begins with the multitude's coming: James and the elders of the church at Jerusalem asked Paul to gratify the believing Jews by some compliance with the ceremonial law, thinking it prudent in him to conform thus far. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: Do therefore this that we say to thee: We have four men which have a vow on them. Henry's compliance plan is the four men with a vow: James and the elders ask Paul to gratify the believing Jews by compliance with the ceremonial law, thinking it prudent to conform thus far. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Them take, and purify thyself with them, and be at charges with them, that they may shave their heads: and all may know that those things, whereof they were informed concerning thee, are nothing; but that thou thyself also walkest orderly, and keepest the law. Henry says it was great weakness to be so fond of the shadows, when the substance was come, for the religion Paul preached tended not to destroy the law, but to fulfil it, since he preached Christ, the end of the law for righteousness, and repentance and faith, in which we are to make great use of the law, yet the elders thought it prudent for Paul to conform thus far and walk orderly, keeping the law, to answer the report. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: As touching the Gentiles which believe, we have written and concluded that they observe no such thing, save only that they keep themselves from things offered to idols, and from blood, and from strangled, and from fornication. Henry's distinction is preserved in the restatement: the Gentiles observe no such ceremonial thing, save the necessary things of the council's letter, while Paul's compliance is asked for the sake of the believing Jews. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: Then Paul took the men, and the next day purifying himself with them entered into the temple, to signify the accomplishment of the days of purification, until that an offering should be offered for every one of them. Henry is frank about the compliance: the apostles were not free from blame in all they did, and it would be hard to defend Paul from the charge of giving way too much in this matter, for it is vain to attempt to court the favour of zealots, or bigots to a party, and this compliance did not answer, for the very thing by which he hoped to pacify the Jews provoked them, and brought him into trouble, but the all-wise God overruled both their advice and Paul's compliance with it, to serve a better purpose than was intended, and integrity and uprightness will be more likely to preserve us than insincere compliances. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: When the seven days were almost ended, the Jews which were of Asia, when they saw him in the temple, stirred up all the people, and laid hands on him. Henry says in the temple, where Paul should have been protected as in a place of safety, he was violently set upon, and they falsely charged him with ill doctrine and ill practice against the Mosaic ceremonies. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: Crying out, Men of Israel, help: This is the man, that teacheth all men every where against the people, and the law, and this place: and further brought Greeks also into the temple, and hath polluted this holy place. Henry says it is no new thing for those who mean honestly and act regularly, to have things laid to their charge which they know not and never thought of, and it is common for the wise and good to have that charged against them by malicious people, with which they thought to have obliged them. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: For they had seen before with him in the city Trophimus an Ephesian, whom they supposed that Paul had brought into the temple. Henry says see what false, mistaken notions of good people and good ministers, many run away with, and the supposition about Trophimus, seen before in the city, is that mistaken notion made into a charge. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: All the city was moved, and the people ran together: and they took Paul, and drew him out of the temple: and forthwith the doors were shut. Henry's temple safety fails: in the temple, where Paul should have been protected as in a place of safety, he was violently set upon, and the city moved, the people ran together, took Paul, and drew him out. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: As they went about to kill him, tidings came unto the chief captain of the band, that all Jerusalem was in an uproar. Henry says God often makes those a protection to his people, who have no affection to them, but only have compassion for sufferers, and regard to the public peace, and the tidings coming to the chief captain is that protection beginning. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: Who immediately took soldiers and centurions, and ran down unto them: and when they saw the chief captain and the soldiers, they left beating of Paul. Henry says God seasonably interposes for the safety of his servants, from wicked and unreasonable men, and the captain immediately taking soldiers and running down, so that they left beating Paul, is that interposition. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: Then the chief captain came near, and took him, and commanded him to be bound with two chains; and demanded who he was, and what he had done. Henry's protection that binds is the captain's taking: God makes a protector of one with no affection but regard to the public peace, and the captain takes Paul, binds him with two chains, and demands who he is, which both protects him and leads to his opportunity to speak. — Matthew Henry

37 COMMENTARY: As Paul was to be led into the castle, he said unto the chief captain, May I speak unto thee? Henry's opportunity to speak begins with the request: God seasonably interposes for the safety of his servants, and gives them opportunities to speak for themselves, to plead for the Redeemer, and to spread abroad his glorious gospel, and Paul's May I speak unto thee is that opportunity sought. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: Art not thou that Egyptian, which before these days madest an uproar, and leddest out into the wilderness four thousand men that were murderers? Henry says see what false, mistaken notions of good people and good ministers, many run away with, and the captain's Egyptian is that mistaken notion, taking Paul for a murderous uproar-maker. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: But Paul said, I am a man which am a Jew of Tarsus, a city in Cilicia, a citizen of no mean city: and, I beseech thee, suffer me to speak unto the people. Henry's correction is Paul's answer: a Jew of Tarsus, a citizen of no mean city, correcting the false, mistaken notion, and beseeching leave to speak unto the people, for God gives his servants opportunities to speak for themselves and plead for the Redeemer. — Matthew Henry

40 COMMENTARY: When he had given him licence, Paul stood on the stairs, and beckoned with the hand unto the people. And when there was made a great silence, he spake unto them in the Hebrew tongue, saying. Henry closes on the opportunity given: God seasonably interposes for the safety of his servants, from wicked and unreasonable men, and gives them opportunities to speak for themselves, to plead for the Redeemer, and to spread abroad his glorious gospel, and the licence, the beckoning, the great silence, and the Hebrew tongue are that opportunity. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 2, 3, 9, 18, 34, 35, 36 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
