Study Notes — Acts

Chapter 23

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Paul, earnestly beholding the council, said, Men and brethren, I have lived in all good conscience before God until this day. Henry says see here the character of an honest man: he sets God before him, and lives as in his sight, he makes conscience of what he says and does, and, according to the best of his knowledge, he keeps from whatever is evil, and cleaves to what is good, and he is conscientious in all his words and conduct, so those who thus live before God may, like Paul, have confidence both toward God and man. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: The high priest Ananias commanded them that stood by him to smite him on the mouth. Henry's angry rebuke follows this treatment: though the answer of Paul contained a just rebuke and prediction, he seems to have been too angry at the treatment he received in uttering them, and the smiting on the mouth is that treatment. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Then said Paul unto him, God shall smite thee, thou whited wall: for sittest thou to judge me after the law, and commandest me to be smitten contrary to the law? Henry says though the answer contained a just rebuke and prediction, Paul seems to have been too angry at the treatment he received in uttering them, for great men may be told of their faults, and public complaints may be made in a proper manner, but the law of God requires respect for those in authority. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: They that stood by said, Revilest thou God's high priest? Henry's respect-for-authority point is the bystanders' challenge: great men may be told of their faults in a proper manner, but the law of God requires respect for those in authority, and the question forces Paul to that law. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Then said Paul, I wist not, brethren, that he was the high priest: for it is written, Thou shalt not speak evil of the ruler of thy people. Henry's correction is Paul's appeal to the written law: he wist not that Ananias was the high priest, and it is written, Thou shalt not speak evil of the ruler of thy people, which is the respect the law of God requires for those in authority. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: But when Paul perceived that the one part were Sadducees, and the other Pharisees, he cried out in the council, Men and brethren, I am a Pharisee, the son of a Pharisee: of the hope and resurrection of the dead I am called in question. Henry says the Pharisees were correct in the faith of the Jewish church, while the Sadducees were no friends to Scripture or divine revelation, denying a future state, with neither hope of eternal happiness nor dread of eternal misery, and when called in question for his being a Christian, Paul might truly say he was called in question for the hope of the resurrection of the dead, and it was justifiable in him, by this profession of his opinion on that disputed point, to draw off the Pharisees from persecuting him, and to lead them to protect him from this unlawful violence. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: When he had so said, there arose a dissension between the Pharisees and the Sadducees: and the multitude was divided. Henry says how easily can God defend his own cause: though the Jews seemed perfectly agreed in their conspiracy against religion, yet they were influenced by very different motives, and there is no true friendship among the wicked, and in a moment, and with the utmost ease, God can turn their union into open enmity, and the dissension dividing the multitude is that turning. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: For the Sadducees say that there is no resurrection, neither angel, nor spirit: but the Pharisees confess both. Henry's doctrinal divide is the dissension's ground: the Sadducees denied a future state, neither angel nor spirit, while the Pharisees confess both, and were correct in the faith of the Jewish church on this point. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: There arose a great cry: and the scribes that were of the Pharisees' part arose, and strove, saying, We find no evil in this man: but if a spirit or an angel hath spoken to him, let us not fight against God. Henry's protection by Pharisees is the scribes' striving: Paul's profession drew off the Pharisees from persecuting him, and led them to protect him from unlawful violence, and the Pharisee scribes, finding no evil in him, strive against fighting God. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: When there arose a great dissension, the chief captain, fearing lest Paul should have been pulled in pieces of them, commanded the soldiers to go down, and to take him by force from among them, and to bring him into the castle. Henry says the chief captain rescued him out of the hands of cruel men, but the event he could not tell, and whoever is against us, we need not fear, if the Lord stand by us, and the forceful taking into the castle is that rescue. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: The night following the Lord stood by him, and said, Be of good cheer, Paul: for as thou hast testified of me in Jerusalem, so must thou bear witness also at Rome. Henry says divine consolations stood Paul in the most stead, and it is the will of Christ, that his servants who are faithful, should be always cheerful, and he might think he should never see Rome, but God tells him, even in that he should be gratified, since he desired to go there only for the honour of Christ, and to do good. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: When it was day, certain of the Jews banded together, and bound themselves under a curse, that they would neither eat nor drink till they had killed Paul. Henry says false religious principles, adopted by carnal men, urge on to such wickedness, as human nature would hardly be supposed capable of, and yet the Lord readily disappoints the best concerted schemes of iniquity, and the curse-bound band is that wickedness concerted. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: They were more than forty which had made this conspiracy. Henry's concerted scheme is more than forty strong, and the Lord readily disappoints the best concerted schemes of iniquity, whatever their number. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: They came to the chief priests and elders, and said, We have bound ourselves under a great curse, that we will eat nothing until we have slain Paul. Henry's false principles enlist the chief priests and elders: carnal men, urged by false religious principles to wickedness hardly supposed capable of human nature, bring their curse to the chief priests and elders for the council's aid. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Now therefore ye with the council signify to the chief captain that he bring him down unto you to morrow, as though ye would enquire something more perfectly concerning him: and we, or ever he come near, are ready to kill him. Henry's scheme to be disappointed is the signify-and-kill plan: the council is to signify for Paul to be brought down as though to enquire more perfectly, while the band waits to kill him before he comes near. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: When Paul's sister's son heard of their lying in wait, he went and entered into the castle, and told Paul. Henry says Paul knew that the divine providence acts by reasonable and prudent means, and that, if he neglected to use the means in his power, he could not expect God's providence to work on his behalf, for he who will not help himself according to his means and power has neither reason nor revelation to assure him that he shall receive help from God, and the nephew's hearing, entering the castle, and telling Paul are those prudent means. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Then Paul called one of the centurions unto him, and said, Bring this young man unto the chief captain: for he hath a certain thing to tell him. Henry's prudent means continue: Paul calls a centurion and sends the young man to the chief captain, using the means in his power rather than neglecting them. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: So he took him, and brought him to the chief captain, and said, Paul the prisoner called me unto him, and prayed me to bring this young man unto thee, who hath something to say unto thee. Henry's providence by means is the bringing: the centurion takes the young man and brings him to the chief captain, and divine providence acts by such reasonable and prudent means. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Then the chief captain took him by the hand, and went with him aside privately, and asked him, What is it that thou hast to tell me? Henry's private hearing is the captain's prudence: he takes the young man by the hand and goes aside privately to ask what he has to tell, and providence acts by such means. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: He said, The Jews have agreed to desire thee that thou wouldest bring down Paul to morrow into the council, as though they would enquire somewhat of him more perfectly. Henry's plot reported is the agreed desire: the Jews have agreed to desire Paul brought down to-morrow, as though to enquire more perfectly, which is the signify-and-kill scheme the nephew exposes. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: But do not thou yield unto them: for there lie in wait for him of them more than forty men, which have bound themselves with an oath, that they will neither eat nor drink till they have killed him: and now are they ready, looking for a promise from thee. Henry's keeping promise is the warning's end: believing in the Lord, we and ours shall be kept from every evil work, and kept to his kingdom, and the do-not-yield warning, with more than forty oath-bound men waiting, is the means of that keeping. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: So the chief captain then let the young man depart, and charged him, See thou tell no man that thou hast shewed these things to me. Henry's prudent means include secrecy: the captain lets the young man depart with the charge to tell no man that he showed these things, guarding the warning that disappoints the scheme. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: He called unto him two centurions, saying, Make ready two hundred soldiers to go to Caesarea, and horsemen threescore and ten, and spearmen two hundred, at the third hour of the night. Henry's providence by means is the escort: the captain calls two centurions and makes ready two hundred soldiers, seventy horsemen, and two hundred spearmen, at the third hour of the night, to carry Paul to Caesarea. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: And provide them beasts, that they may set Paul on, and bring him safe unto Felix the governor. Henry's keeping is the safe bringing: believing in the Lord, we and ours shall be kept from every evil work, and kept to his kingdom, and the beasts provided to set Paul on and bring him safe unto Felix are that keeping. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: He wrote a letter after this manner. Henry says God has instruments for every work, and the natural abilities and moral virtues of the heathens often have been employed to protect his persecuted servants, and the captain's letter is such an instrument. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: This man was taken of the Jews, and should have been killed of them: then came I with an army, and rescued him, having understood that he was a Roman. Henry says even the men of the world can discern between the conscientious conduct of upright believers, and the zeal of false professors, though they disregard or understand not their doctrinal principles, and Lysias's rescue, having understood Paul was a Roman, is that worldly discernment protecting an upright believer. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: When I would have known the cause wherefore they accused him, I brought him forth into their council. Henry's certainty sought is the council bringing: Lysias, when he would have known the cause wherefore they accused Paul, brought him forth into their council, using the means in his power to know it. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Whom I perceived to be accused of questions of their law, but to have nothing laid to his charge worthy of death or of bonds. Henry's worldly discernment is the perception: Lysias perceived Paul accused of questions of their law, with nothing laid to his charge worthy of death or of bonds, discerning upright conduct from false professors' zeal. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: When it was told me how that the Jews laid wait for the man, I sent straightway to thee, and gave commandment to his accusers also to say before thee what they had against him. Farewell. Henry says all hearts are in God's hand, and those are blessed who put their trust in him, and commit their ways unto him, and Lysias's straightway sending, with accusers commanded to speak before Felix, commits Paul's cause to the governor's hearing. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: Then the soldiers, as it was commanded them, took Paul, and brought him by night to Antipatris. Henry's escort by night carries the protection forward: the soldiers, as commanded, take Paul and bring him by night to Antipatris, on the way to bring him safe unto Felix. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: Who, when they came to Caesarea and delivered the epistle to the governor, presented Paul also before him. Henry's instruments complete their work: God has instruments for every work, and the horsemen, coming to Caesarea, deliver the epistle and present Paul also before the governor. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: I will hear thee, said he, when thine accusers are also come. And he commanded him to be kept in Herod's judgment hall. Henry says all hearts are in God's hand, and those are blessed who put their trust in him, and commit their ways unto him, and Felix's hearing when accusers are come, with Paul kept in Herod's judgment hall, is that committed way. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 26, 32, 34 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
