Study Notes — Acts

Chapter 25

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Now when Festus was come into the province, after three days he ascended from Caesarea to Jerusalem. Henry's restless malice awaits the new governor: see how restless malice is, and Festus, after three days, ascends to Jerusalem, where the malice will immediately inform against Paul. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Then the high priest and the chief of the Jews informed him against Paul, and besought him. Henry says persecutors deem it a peculiar favour to have their malice gratified, and the high priest and chief of the Jews informing against Paul and beseeching Festus are malice seeking that favour. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: And desired favour against him, that he would send for him to Jerusalem, laying wait in the way to kill him. Henry's restless malice is the desired favour itself: send for him to Jerusalem, laying wait in the way to kill him, and in suffering times the prudence of the Lord's people is tried, as well as their patience, and they need wisdom. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: But Festus answered, that Paul should be kept at Caesarea, and that he himself would depart shortly thither. Henry's prudence is Festus's answer: Paul should be kept at Caesarea, and Festus himself would depart shortly thither, refusing the Jerusalem transfer on which the ambush depended. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Let them therefore, said he, which among you are able, go down with me, and accuse this man, if there be any wickedness in him. Henry's justice procedure is the requirement: let those among them who are able go down with Festus and accuse Paul, if there be any wickedness in him, rather than gratifying malice by a Jerusalem transfer. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: When he had tarried among them more than ten days, he went down unto Caesarea, and the next day sitting on the judgment seat commanded Paul to be brought. Henry's slow providence begins at the judgment seat: Paul is neither released nor condemned, and it is an instance of the slow steps which Providence takes, by which we are often made ashamed, both of our hopes and of our fears, and are kept waiting on God. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: When he was come, the Jews which came down from Jerusalem stood round about, and laid many and grievous complaints against Paul, which they could not prove. Henry's unproved complaints are the grievous charges that could not be proved, and it becomes those who are innocent to insist upon their innocence, as Paul does in his answer. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: While he answered for himself, Neither against the law of the Jews, neither against the temple, nor yet against Caesar, have I offended any thing at all. Henry says preaching Christ, the end of the law, was no offence against the law, and it becomes those who are innocent to insist upon their innocence, and Paul, willing to abide by the rules of the law and to let that take its course, answers that he has offended neither law, temple, nor Caesar at all. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: But Festus, willing to do the Jews a pleasure, answered Paul, and said, Wilt thou go up to Jerusalem, and there be judged of these things before me? Henry's pleasure-seeking governor is the Jerusalem proposal: Festus, willing to do the Jews a pleasure, asks whether Paul will go up to Jerusalem to be judged there, and in suffering times the prudence of the Lord's people is tried, as well as their patience. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Then said Paul, I stand at Caesar's judgment seat, where I ought to be judged: to the Jews have I done no wrong, as thou very well knowest. Henry's proper court is Caesar's judgment seat: Paul stands where he ought to be judged, and to the Jews he has done no wrong, as Festus very well knows, and the innocent insist upon their innocence. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: For if I be an offender, or have committed any thing worthy of death, I refuse not to die: but if there be none of these things whereof these accuse me, no man can deliver me unto them. I appeal unto Caesar. Henry says Paul was willing to abide by the rules of the law, and to let that take its course, for if he deserved death, he would accept the punishment, but if none of the things whereof they accused him were true, no man could deliver him unto them, with justice, and the appeal unto Caesar is that justice claimed. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Then Festus, when he had conferred with the council, answered, Hast thou appealed unto Caesar? unto Caesar shalt thou go. Henry says Paul is neither released nor condemned, and it is an instance of the slow steps which Providence takes, by which we are often made ashamed, both of our hopes and of our fears, and are kept waiting on God, and the appeal's answer, unto Caesar shalt thou go, is that slow providence determining Rome. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: After certain days king Agrippa and Bernice came unto Caesarea to salute Festus. Henry's noble assembly gathers with the salute: Agrippa had the government of Galilee, and here was a noble assembly brought together to hear the truths of the gospel, though they only meant to gratify their curiosity by attending to the defence of a prisoner. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: When they had been there many days, Festus declared Paul's cause unto the king, saying, There is a certain man left in bonds by Felix. Henry's declaration is Festus laying Paul's cause before Agrippa after many days, seeking, as the chapter's end shows, some certain thing to write unto his lord. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: About whom, when I was at Jerusalem, the chief priests and the elders of the Jews informed me, desiring to have judgment against him. Henry's restless malice renews itself before the new hearers: the chief priests and elders informed Festus at Jerusalem, desiring to have judgment against Paul, the same malice seeking its favour again. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: To whom I answered, It is not the manner of the Romans to deliver any man to die, before that he which is accused have the accusers face to face, and have licence to answer for himself concerning the crime laid against him. Henry says how many unjust and hasty judgments the Roman maxim condemns: this heathen, guided only by the light of nature, followed law and custom exactly, yet how many Christians will not follow the rules of truth, justice, and charity, in judging their brethren. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Therefore, when they were come hither, without any delay on the morrow I sat on the judgment seat, and commanded the man to be brought forth. Henry's law and custom exactly followed is the without-delay hearing: on the morrow Festus sat on the judgment seat and commanded Paul to be brought forth, following the Roman manner. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Against whom when the accusers stood up, they brought none accusation of such things as I supposed. Henry's expected crimes fail: when the accusers stood up, they brought none accusation of such things as Festus supposed, and the supposed crimes give way to questions. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: But had certain questions against him of their own superstition, and of one Jesus, which was dead, whom Paul affirmed to be alive. Henry says the questions about God's worship, the way of salvation, and the truths of the gospel, may appear doubtful and without interest, to worldly men and mere politicians, and see how slightly this Roman speaks of Christ, and of the great controversy between the Jews and the Christians, but the day is at hand when Festus and the whole world will see, that all the concerns of the Roman empire were but trifles and of no consequence, compared with this question of Christ's resurrection, for Paul affirmed the dead Jesus to be alive. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: And because I doubted of such manner of questions, I asked him whether he would go to Jerusalem, and there be judged of these matters. Henry's doubted questions are Festus's Jerusalem proposal again: because he doubted of such manner of questions, he asked whether Paul would go to Jerusalem to be judged there, which Paul's appeal to Augustus answers. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: But when Paul had appealed to be reserved unto the hearing of Augustus, I commanded him to be kept till I might send him to Caesar. Henry's reserved hearing is the appeal's effect: Paul appealed to be reserved unto the hearing of Augustus, and Festus commanded him to be kept till he might send him to Caesar. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: Then Agrippa said unto Festus, I would also hear the man myself. To morrow, said he, thou shalt hear him. Henry's noble assembly's curiosity is Agrippa's wish: I would also hear the man myself, and to-morrow thou shalt hear him, bringing together the assembly to hear the truths of the gospel, though only to gratify curiosity. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: On the morrow, when Agrippa was come, and Bernice, with great pomp, and was entered into the place of hearing, with the chief captains, and principal men of the city, at Festus' commandment Paul was brought forth. Henry says here was a noble assembly brought together to hear the truths of the gospel, though they only meant to gratify their curiosity by attending to the defence of a prisoner, and many, even now, attend at the places of hearing the word of God with great pomp, and too often with no better motive than curiosity, and though ministers do not now stand as prisoners to make a defence for their lives, yet numbers affect to sit in judgment upon them, desirous to make them offenders for a word, rather than to learn from them the truth and will of God, for the salvation of their souls, but the pomp of this appearance was outshone by the real glory of the poor prisoner at the bar, for what was the honour of their fine appearance, compared with that of Paul's wisdom, and grace, and holiness, his courage and constancy in suffering for Christ. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Festus said, King Agrippa, and all men which are here present with us, ye see this man, about whom all the multitude of the Jews have dealt with me, both at Jerusalem, and also here, crying that he ought not to live any longer. Henry's multitude cry is the malice's voice before the pomp: all the multitude of the Jews dealt with Festus at Jerusalem and at Caesarea, crying that Paul ought not to live any longer, set against the prisoner's real glory at the bar. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: But when I found that he had committed nothing worthy of death, and that he himself hath appealed to Augustus, I have determined to send him. Henry says it is no small mercy to have God clear up our righteousness as the light, and our just dealing as the noon-day, to have nothing certain laid to our charge, and God makes even the enemies of his people to do them right, and Festus, finding nothing worthy of death, determines to send Paul to Augustus. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: Of whom I have no certain thing to write unto my lord. Wherefore I have brought him forth before you, and specially before thee, O king Agrippa, that, after examination had, I might have somewhat to write. Henry's cleared righteousness is Festus's dilemma: he has no certain thing to write unto his lord, and brings Paul forth before Agrippa specially, that after examination had, he might have somewhat to write, because nothing certain is laid to Paul's charge. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: For it seemeth to me unreasonable to send a prisoner, and not withal to signify the crimes laid against him. Henry's Roman procedure demands a certain charge: it seemeth unreasonable to send a prisoner, and not withal to signify the crimes laid against him, and the unreasonableness exposes that the gospel prisoner has no crime to signify. — Matthew Henry
