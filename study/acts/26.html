Study Notes — Acts

Chapter 26

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Then Agrippa said unto Paul, Thou art permitted to speak for thyself. Then Paul stretched forth the hand, and answered for himself. Henry says Christianity teaches us to give a reason of the hope that is in us, and also to give honour to whom honour is due, without flattery or fear of man, and Paul's stretched-forth hand answering for himself is that reason-giving. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: I think myself happy, king Agrippa, because I shall answer for myself this day before thee touching all the things whereof I am accused of the Jews. Henry says surely ministers may expect, when they preach the faith of Christ, to be heard patiently, and Paul counts himself happy to answer before Agrippa, who is expert in Jewish customs and questions. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Especially because I know thee to be expert in all customs and questions which are among the Jews: wherefore I beseech thee to hear me patiently. Henry says Agrippa was well versed in the Scriptures of the Old Testament, therefore could the better judge as to the controversy about Jesus being the Messiah, and Paul beseeches him to hear patiently on that ground. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: My manner of life from my youth, which was at the first among mine own nation at Jerusalem, know all the Jews. Henry says Paul professes that he still kept to all the good in which he was first educated and trained up, and see here what his religion was: he was a moralist, a man of virtue, and had not learned the arts of the crafty, covetous Pharisees, and he was not chargeable with any open vice and profaneness, and he was sound in the faith, with a holy regard for the ancient promise made of God unto the fathers, building his hope upon it. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Which knew me from the beginning, if they would testify, that after the most straitest sect of our religion I lived a Pharisee. Henry's straitest sect is Paul's Pharisee life, lived from the beginning and known to the Jews, yet Henry distinguishes his virtue from the arts of the crafty, covetous Pharisees, for Paul was a moralist, a man of virtue, not chargeable with open vice and profaneness. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: And now I stand and am judged for the hope of the promise made of God unto our fathers. Henry says he always had a holy regard for the ancient promise made of God unto the fathers, and built his hope upon it, and now he stands and is judged for the hope of that very promise. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Unto which promise our twelve tribes, instantly serving God day and night, hope to come. For which hope's sake, king Agrippa, I am accused of the Jews. Henry says the temple service, or continual course of religious duties, day and night, was kept up as the profession of faith in the promise of eternal life, and in expectation of it, and the prospect of eternal life should engage us to be diligent and stedfast in all religious exercises, and the twelve tribes instantly serving God day and night hope to come to that promise. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: Why should it be thought a thing incredible with you, that God should raise the dead? Henry says many things are thought to be beyond belief, only because the infinite nature and perfections of him that has revealed, performed, or promised them, are overlooked, and the resurrection is thought incredible only by overlooking the infinite God who raises the dead. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: I verily thought with myself, that I ought to do many things contrary to the name of Jesus of Nazareth. Henry says Paul acknowledged that while he continued a Pharisee, he was a bitter enemy to Christianity, and this was his character and manner of life in the beginning of his time, with every thing to hinder his being a Christian. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Which thing I also did in Jerusalem: and many of the saints did I shut up in prison, having received authority from the chief priests; and when they were put to death, I gave my voice against them. Henry's bitter enemy is the imprisoner: many of the saints did he shut up in prison, and when they were put to death, he gave his voice against them, which was his character in the beginning. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: I punished them oft in every synagogue, and compelled them to blaspheme; and being exceedingly mad against them, I persecuted them even unto strange cities. Henry says those who have been most strict in their conduct before conversion will afterwards see abundant reason for humbling themselves, even on account of things which they then thought ought to have been done, and Paul's punishing, compelling to blaspheme, and persecuting even unto strange cities, being exceedingly mad against them, is that strictness to be humbled for. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Whereupon as I went to Damascus with authority and commission from the chief priests. Henry says Paul was made a Christian by divine power, by a revelation of Christ both to him and in him, when in the full career of his sin, and the Damascus journey with authority and commission from the chief priests is that full career. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: At midday, O king, I saw in the way a light from heaven, above the brightness of the sun, shining round about me and them which journeyed with me. Henry's revelation in full career is the midday light: a light from heaven, above the brightness of the sun, shining round about Paul and them which journeyed with him, by which he was made a Christian by divine power. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: When we were all fallen to the earth, I heard a voice speaking unto me, and saying in the Hebrew tongue, Saul, Saul, why persecutest thou me? it is hard for thee to kick against the pricks. Henry's revelation to him is the voice: all fallen to the earth, Paul hears the voice speaking in the Hebrew tongue, Saul, Saul, why persecutest thou me, and it is hard for thee to kick against the pricks. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: I said, Who art thou, Lord? And he said, I am Jesus whom thou persecutest. Henry's revelation is Jesus himself: Paul was made a Christian by a revelation of Christ both to him and in him, and the voice names himself, I am Jesus whom thou persecutest. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: But rise, and stand upon thy feet: for I have appeared unto thee for this purpose, to make thee a minister and a witness of these things which thou hast seen, and of those things in the which I will appear unto thee. Henry says he was made a minister by divine authority: the same Jesus who appeared to him in that glorious light ordered him to preach the gospel to the Gentiles, and the purpose, to make thee a minister and a witness, is that authority. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Delivering thee from the people, and from the Gentiles, unto whom now I send thee. Henry's sending is the delivery and the mission together: delivering Paul from the people and from the Gentiles, unto whom now Christ sends him, by divine authority to preach the gospel to them. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: To open their eyes, and to turn them from darkness to light, and from the power of Satan unto God, that they may receive forgiveness of sins, and inheritance among them which are sanctified by faith that is in me. Henry says a world that sits in darkness must be enlightened, and those must be brought to know the things that belong to their everlasting peace, who are yet ignorant of them, and a world that lies in wickedness must be sanctified and reformed, for it is not enough for them to have their eyes opened, they must have their hearts renewed, not enough to be turned from darkness to light, but they must be turned from the power of Satan unto God, and all who are turned from sin to God are not only pardoned, but have a grant of a rich inheritance, for the forgiveness of sins makes way for this, and none can be happy who are not holy, and to be saints in heaven we must be first saints on earth, made holy and saved by faith in Christ. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Whereupon, O king Agrippa, I was not disobedient unto the heavenly vision. Henry's true convert is the obedient one: thus the true convert can give a reason of his hope, and a good account of the change manifest in him, and Paul was not disobedient unto the heavenly vision. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: But shewed first unto them of Damascus, and at Jerusalem, and throughout all the coasts of Judaea, and then to the Gentiles, that they should repent and turn to God, and do works meet for repentance. Henry's preaching is the repentance call: Paul showed, first to Damascus, then Jerusalem and Judea, then the Gentiles, that they should repent and turn to God, and do works meet for repentance, and yet for going about and calling on men thus to repent and to be converted, vast numbers have been blamed and persecuted. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: For these causes the Jews caught me in the temple, and went about to kill me. Henry's blamed and persecuted preacher is caught in the temple: for these causes, the repentance preaching, the Jews caught Paul in the temple, and went about to kill him. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: Having therefore obtained help of God, I continue unto this day, witnessing both to small and great, saying none other things than those which the prophets and Moses did say should come. Henry says the cross of Christ was a stumbling-block to the Jews, and they were in a rage at Paul's preaching the fulfilling of the Old Testament predictions, yet having obtained help of God, he continues unto this day, witnessing both to small and great, saying none other things than those which the prophets and Moses did say should come. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: That Christ should suffer, and that he should be the first that should rise from the dead, and should shew light unto the people, and to the Gentiles. Henry says Christ should be the first that should rise from the dead, the Head or principal One, and also, it was foretold by the prophets, that the Gentiles should be brought to the knowledge of God by the Messiah, and what in this could the Jews justly be displeased at? — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: As he thus spake for himself, Festus said with a loud voice, Paul, thou art beside thyself; much learning doth make thee mad. Henry says it becomes us, on all occasions, to speak the words of truth and soberness, and then we need not be troubled at the unjust censures of men, for active and laborious followers of the gospel often have been despised as dreamers or madmen, for believing such doctrines and such wonderful facts, and for attesting that the same faith and diligence, and an experience like their own, are necessary to all men, whatever their rank, in order to their salvation, but apostles and prophets, and the Son of God himself, were exposed to this charge. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: But he said, I am not mad, most noble Festus; but speak forth the words of truth and soberness. Henry's truth and soberness is Paul's answer: I am not mad, most noble Festus, but speak forth the words of truth and soberness, and speaking thus, we need not be troubled at the unjust censures of men. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: For the king knoweth of these things, before whom also I speak freely: for I am persuaded that none of these things are hidden from him; for this thing was not done in a corner. Henry's freely spoken things are known to the king: Agrippa was well versed in the Scriptures, and Paul speaks freely before him, persuaded that none of these things are hidden from him, for this thing was not done in a corner. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: King Agrippa, believest thou the prophets? I know that thou believest. Henry's convinced understanding is the question: King Agrippa, believest thou the prophets? I know that thou believest, and Agrippa's understanding and judgment were for the time convinced, though his heart was not changed. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: Then Agrippa said unto Paul, Almost thou persuadest me to be a Christian. Henry says Agrippa saw a great deal of reason for Christianity, and his understanding and judgment were for the time convinced, but his heart was not changed, and his conduct and temper were widely different from the humility and spirituality of the gospel, for many are almost persuaded to be religious, who are not quite persuaded, being under strong convictions of their duty, and of the excellence of the ways of God, yet do not pursue their convictions. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Paul said, I would to God, that not only thou, but also all that hear me this day, were both almost, and altogether such as I am, except these bonds. Henry says Paul urged that it was the concern of every one to become a true Christian, and that there is grace enough in Christ for all, and he expressed his full conviction of the truth of the gospel, the absolute necessity of faith in Christ in order to salvation, and such salvation from such bondage the gospel of Christ offers to the Gentiles, to a lost world, yet it is with much difficulty that any person can be persuaded he needs a work of grace on his heart, like that which was needful for the conversion of the Gentiles, so let us beware of fatal hesitation in our own conduct, and recollect how far the being almost persuaded to be a Christian is from being altogether such a one as every true believer is. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: When he had thus spoken, the king rose up, and the governor, and Bernice, and they that sat with them. Henry's conference rises to the verdict: the king rose up, and the governor, and Bernice, and they that sat with them, to talk between themselves of what they had heard. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: When they were gone aside, they talked between themselves, saying, This man doeth nothing worthy of death or of bonds. Henry says it is no small mercy to have God clear up our righteousness as the light, and our just dealing as the noon-day, to have nothing certain laid to our charge, and God makes even the enemies of his people to do them right, and the aside talk, This man doeth nothing worthy of death or of bonds, is that clearing. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: Then said Agrippa unto Festus, This man might have been set at liberty, if he had not appealed unto Caesar. Henry's liberty that might have been is the appeal's cost: this man might have been set at liberty, if he had not appealed unto Caesar, yet the appeal stands, and the slow providence that kept Paul waiting on God now carries him toward Rome. — Matthew Henry
