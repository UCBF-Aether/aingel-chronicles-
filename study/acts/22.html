Study Notes — Acts

Chapter 22

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Men, brethren, and fathers, hear ye my defence which I make now unto you. Henry says the apostle addressed the enraged multitude in the customary style of respect and good-will, and his defence opens with that respect. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: When they heard that he spake in the Hebrew tongue to them, they kept the more silence, and he saith. Henry's respectful address gains the hearing: the apostle addressed the enraged multitude in the customary style of respect and good-will, and speaking in the Hebrew tongue, they kept the more silence. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: I am verily a man which am a Jew, born in Tarsus, a city in Cilicia, yet brought up in this city at the feet of Gamaliel, and taught according to the perfect manner of the law of the fathers, and was zealous toward God, as ye all are this day. Henry says Paul relates the history of his early life very particularly, and notices that his conversion was wholly the act of God. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: I persecuted this way unto the death, binding and delivering into prisons both men and women. Henry's wholly-God conversion begins from this opposing life: the history Paul relates particularly is of a persecutor unto death, whose conversion was wholly the act of God. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: As also the high priest doth bear me witness, and all the estate of the elders: from whom also I received letters unto the brethren, and went to Damascus, to bring them which were there bound unto Jerusalem, for to be punished. Henry's particular history includes the witnesses: the high priest and all the estate of the elders bear witness to the persecuting commission, and Paul relates it particularly to show the conversion was wholly God's act. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: As I made my journey, and was come nigh unto Damascus about noon, suddenly there shined from heaven a great light round about me. Henry distinguishes the blindness: condemned sinners are struck blind by the power of darkness, and it is a lasting blindness, like that of the unbelieving Jews, but convinced sinners are struck blind as Paul was, not by darkness, but by light, and they are for a time brought to be at a loss within themselves, but it is in order to their being enlightened. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: I fell unto the ground, and heard a voice saying unto me, Saul, Saul, why persecutest thou me? Henry's light-blindness is the fall: convinced sinners are struck blind by light, not darkness, and are for a time at a loss within themselves, in order to their being enlightened, and Paul falls at the voice. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: I answered, Who art thou, Lord? And he said unto me, I am Jesus of Nazareth, whom thou persecutest. Henry's voice is the Just One himself: Christ is here called that Just One, for he is Jesus Christ the righteous, and the persecuted One names himself Jesus of Nazareth. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: I said, What shall I do, Lord? And the Lord said unto me, Arise, and go into Damascus; and there it shall be told thee of all things which are appointed for thee to do. Henry says a simple relation of the Lord's dealings with us, in bringing us, from opposing, to profess and promote his gospel, when delivered in a right spirit and manner, will sometimes make more impression than laboured speeches, even though it amounts not to the full proof of the truth, such as was shown in the change wrought in the apostle, and Paul's What shall I do, Lord is that relation's turning point. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: When I could not see for the glory of that light, being led by the hand of them that were with me, I came into Damascus. Henry's enlightenment order is the blindness first: convinced sinners are struck blind by light, and are for a time brought to be at a loss within themselves, but it is in order to their being enlightened, and Paul, blinded by glory, is led by the hand into Damascus. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: One Ananias, a devout man according to the law, having a good report of all the Jews which dwelt there, came unto me. Henry says the apostle goes on to relate how he was confirmed in the change he had made: the Lord having chosen the sinner, that he should know his will, he is humbled, enlightened, and brought to the knowledge of Christ and his blessed gospel, and Ananias, devout according to the law and of good report, is the confirmer. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Came unto me, and stood, and said unto me, Brother Saul, receive thy sight. And the same hour I looked up upon him. Henry's enlightenment after blindness is the same hour's sight: convinced sinners struck blind by light are for a time at a loss, in order to their being enlightened, and Paul receives his sight and looks up upon Ananias. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: He said, The God of our fathers hath chosen thee, that thou shouldest know his will, and see that Just One, and shouldest hear the voice of his mouth. Henry says the Lord having chosen the sinner, that he should know his will, he is humbled, enlightened, and brought to the knowledge of Christ and his blessed gospel, and Christ is here called that Just One, for he is Jesus Christ the righteous, and those whom God has chosen to know his will must look to Jesus, for by him God has made known his good-will to us. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: For thou shalt be his witness unto all men of what thou hast seen and heard. Henry's chosen witness is this commission: the Lord having chosen the sinner to know his will, he shall be his witness unto all men of what he has seen and heard. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: Now why tarriest thou? arise, and be baptized, and wash away thy sins, calling on the name of the Lord. Henry says the great gospel privilege, sealed to us by baptism, is the pardon of sins: be baptized, and wash away thy sins, that is, receive the comfort of the pardon of thy sins in and through Jesus Christ, and lay hold on his righteousness for that purpose, and receive power against sin, for the mortifying of thy corruptions, and be baptized, and rest not in the sign, but make sure of the thing signified, the putting away of the filth of sin, while the great gospel duty, to which by our baptism we are bound, is to seek for the pardon of our sins in Christ's name, and in dependence on him and his righteousness. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: It came to pass, that, when I was come again to Jerusalem, even while I prayed in the temple, I was in a trance. Henry's appointment point begins with the trance: God appoints his labourers their day and their place, and it is fit they should follow his appointment, though it may cross their own will, and the temple trance brings that appointment. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: And saw him saying unto me, Make haste, and get thee quickly out of Jerusalem: for they will not receive thy testimony concerning me. Henry says God appoints his labourers their day and their place, and it is fit they should follow his appointment, though it may cross their own will, and providence contrives better for us than we do for ourselves, so we must refer ourselves to God's guidance, and the command to get quickly out of Jerusalem crosses Paul's own will for his city. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: I said, Lord, they know that I imprisoned and beat in every synagogue them that believed on thee. Henry's crossed will is Paul's objection: he pleads that they know he imprisoned and beat in every synagogue those that believed, as if his past should make his testimony received, but God's appointment crosses his own will. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: When the blood of thy martyr Stephen was shed, I also was standing by, and consenting unto his death, and kept the raiment of them that slew him. Henry's crossed will continues in Stephen's blood: Paul, standing by and consenting, keeping the raiment of them that slew Stephen, pleads his past against the appointment, yet providence contrives better for us than we do for ourselves. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: He said unto me, Depart: for I will send thee far hence unto the Gentiles. Henry says God appoints his labourers their day and their place, and if Christ send any one, his Spirit shall go along with him, and give him to see the fruit of his labours, but nothing can reconcile man's heart to the gospel, except the special grace of God, and the sending far hence unto the Gentiles is that appointment. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: They gave him audience unto this word, and then lifted up their voices, and said, Away with such a fellow from the earth: for it is not fit that he should live. Henry says the Jews listened to Paul's account of his conversion, but the mention of his being sent to the Gentiles was so contrary to all their national prejudices that they would hear no more. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: As they cried out, and cast off their clothes, and threw dust into the air. Henry says their frantic conduct astonished the Roman officer, who supposed that Paul must have committed some great crime, and the crying, casting off clothes, and throwing dust are that frantic conduct. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: The chief captain commanded him to be brought into the castle, and bade that he should be examined by scourging; that he might know wherefore they cried so against him. Henry says their frantic conduct astonished the Roman officer, who supposed that Paul must have committed some great crime, and the examination by scourging is the officer's attempt to know wherefore they cried so against him. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: As they bound him with thongs, Paul said unto the centurion that stood by, Is it lawful for you to scourge a man that is a Roman, and uncondemned? Henry says Paul pleaded his privilege as a Roman citizen, by which he was exempted from all trials and punishments which might force him to confess himself guilty, and the manner of his speaking plainly shows what holy security and serenity of mind he enjoyed, for the apostle asks, simply, Is it lawful? He knew that the God whom he served would support him under all sufferings for his name's sake, but if it were not lawful, the apostle's religion directed him, if possible, to avoid it, for he never shrunk from a cross which his Divine Master laid upon his onward road, and he never stept aside out of that road to take one up. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: When the centurion heard that, he went and told the chief captain, saying, Take heed what thou doest: for this man is a Roman. Henry's stopping of the trouble begins with the centurion's warning: Take heed what thou doest, for this man is a Roman, and the privilege at once puts a stop to the examination. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: Then the chief captain came, and said unto him, Tell me, art thou a Roman? He said, Yea. Henry's simple claim is the Yea: the apostle asks, simply, Is it lawful? and answers, simply, Yea, with holy security and serenity of mind. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: The chief captain answered, With a great sum obtained I this freedom. And Paul said, But I was free born. Henry says as Paul was a Jew, in low circumstances, the Roman officer questioned how he obtained so valuable a distinction, but the apostle told him he was free born, and let us value that freedom to which all the children of God are born, which no sum of money, however large, can purchase for those who remain unregenerate. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Then straightway they departed from him which should have examined him: and the chief captain also was afraid, after he knew that he was a Roman, and because he had bound him. Henry says this at once put a stop to his trouble, and thus many are kept from evil practices by the fear of man, who would not be held back from them by the fear of God. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: On the morrow, because he would have known the certainty wherefore he was accused of the Jews, he loosed him from his bands, and commanded the chief priests and all their council to appear, and brought Paul down, and set him before them. Henry's certainty sought is the morrow's council: the scourging stopped, the captain looses Paul from his bands and commands the chief priests and all their council to appear, to know the certainty wherefore Paul was accused. — Matthew Henry
