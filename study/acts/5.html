Study Notes — Acts

Chapter 5

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: A certain man named Ananias, with Sapphira his wife, sold a possession. Henry diagnoses the root sin at once: they were ambitious of being thought eminent disciples when they were not true disciples, hypocrites who deny themselves in one instance with a prospect of finding their account in something else. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Ananias kept back part of the price, his wife also being privy to it, and brought a certain part to the apostles' feet. Henry adds the motives: they were covetous of the wealth of the world and distrustful of God and his providence, thinking they might serve both God and mammon and deceive the apostles. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Peter said, Ananias, why hath Satan filled thine heart to lie to the Holy Ghost? Henry notes the Spirit of God in Peter discerned the principle of unbelief reigning in Ananias's heart, yet Satan could not have filled his heart with this wickedness had he not been consenting, and the falsehood was an attempt to deceive the Spirit of truth who so manifestly spoke and acted by the apostles. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Whiles it remained, was it not thine own, and after it was sold, was it not in thine own power? Henry corrects a common misreading: the crime was not retaining part of the price, for Ananias might have kept it all had he pleased, but endeavouring to impose upon the apostles with an awful lie, from a desire to make a vain show, joined with covetousness. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Ananias hearing these words fell down and gave up the ghost, and great fear came on all them that heard. Henry says this punishment was in reality mercy to vast numbers: it would cause strict self-examination, prayer, and dread of hypocrisy, covetousness and vain-glory, and prevent the increase of false professors. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: Peter answered Sapphira, Tell me whether ye sold the land for so much, and she said, Yea, for so much. Henry's sad reflection fits her confirmation of the lie: relations who should quicken one another to that which is good were instead hardening one another in that which is evil. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Peter said, How is it that ye have agreed together to tempt the Spirit of the Lord? Henry's warning is that if we think to put a cheat upon God, we shall put a fatal cheat upon our own souls, and the agreed lie of husband and wife shows relations hardening one another in evil. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Then fell she down straightway at his feet and yielded up the ghost. Henry draws the standing lesson: learn how hateful falsehood is to the God of truth, and shun not only a direct lie but all advantages from doubtful expressions and double meaning in speech. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Great fear came upon all the church and upon as many as heard these things. Henry repeats the merciful purpose of the judgment: it would cause strict self-examination and dread of hypocrisy, and it should still do so in the church today. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: By the hands of the apostles were many signs and wonders wrought among the people, all with one accord in Solomon's porch. Henry says the separation of hypocrites by distinguishing judgments should make the sincere cleave closer to each other and to the gospel ministry, and whatever tends to the purity and reputation of the church promotes its enlargement. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Of the rest durst no man join himself to them, but the people magnified them. Henry reads this reserve as the wholesome effect of the judgment: hypocrites held back, while the sincere cleaved closer, and the church's purity advanced its reputation. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Believers were the more added to the Lord, multitudes both of men and women. Henry insists that only the power which wrought such miracles by the apostles can rescue sinners from the power of sin and Satan and add believers to his worshippers. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: They brought forth the sick into the streets on beds and couches, that at least the shadow of Peter passing by might overshadow some of them. Henry's general word for these healings is that Christ will work by all his faithful servants, and every one who applies to him shall be healed. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: There came also a multitude out of the cities round about unto Jerusalem, bringing sick folks and them vexed with unclean spirits, and they were healed every one. Henry's point stands: every one who applies to Christ shall be healed, and the same power that wrought the miracles adds believers to his worshippers. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Then the high priest rose up, and all they that were with him, being of the sect of the Sadducees, were filled with indignation. Henry calls them wretched in being vexed at the success of the gospel: they cannot but see that the word and power of the Lord are against them, and they tremble for the consequences, yet they will go on. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: They laid their hands on the apostles and put them in the common prison. Henry answers the prison with confidence: there is no prison so dark or so strong but God can visit his people in it, and, if he pleases, fetch them out. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: But the angel of the Lord by night opened the prison doors and brought them forth. Henry draws the purpose of such deliverances: recoveries from sickness and releases out of trouble are granted, not that we may enjoy the comforts of life, but that God may be honoured with the services of our life. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Go, stand and speak in the temple to the people all the words of this life. Henry presses the command: it is not for preachers of Christ's gospel to retire into corners while they can preach in the great congregation, and they must speak to the lowest, whose souls are as precious to Christ as the souls of the greatest, all the words of this heavenly, divine life, whereby we may be saved. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: When they heard that, they entered into the temple early in the morning and taught. Henry's description of such preaching fits their obedience: speak as those who resolve to stand to it, to live and die by it, speaking the words of life which the Holy Ghost puts into their mouths. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: When the high priest and the captain of the temple and the chief priests heard these things, they doubted of them whereunto this would grow. Henry sees the rulers' vexation at gospel success: they cannot but see the word and power of the Lord are against them, and they tremble for the consequences, yet they will go on. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: Then came one and told them, Behold, the men whom ye put in prison are standing in the temple and teaching the people. Henry's point is the rulers' helpless vexation: the word and power of the Lord are against them, yet, trembling for the consequences, they still go on opposing it. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: Did not we straitly command you that ye should not teach in this name, and behold ye have filled Jerusalem with your doctrine and intend to bring this man's blood upon us? Henry observes that many will do an evil thing with daring, yet cannot bear to hear of it afterward or to have it charged upon them. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Then Peter and the other apostles answered, We ought to obey God rather than men. Henry's principle behind the answer is that we cannot expect to be redeemed and healed by Christ unless we give up ourselves to be ruled by him, taking the Saviour in all his offices, who came not to save us in our sins but to save us from our sins. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: The God of our fathers raised up Jesus, whom ye slew and hanged on a tree. Henry's sermon summary begins here: the apostles testify that the Jesus the rulers slew is the one God raised, and that repentance and remission of sins are the blessings at issue. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: Him hath God exalted with his right hand to be a Prince and a Saviour, for to give repentance to Israel and forgiveness of sins. Henry stresses what the chief priests neither valued nor saw their need of: wherever repentance is wrought, remission is granted without fail, and none are freed from the guilt of sin but those freed from its power and dominion, turned from it and against it, by Christ's Spirit working with the word. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: And we are his witnesses of these things, and so is also the Holy Ghost, whom God hath given to them that obey him. Henry calls the giving of the Holy Ghost plain evidence that it is the will of God that Christ should be obeyed, and warns that he will surely destroy those who will not have him to reign over them. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: When they heard that, they were cut to the heart and took counsel to slay them. Henry's warning closes the scene: he will surely destroy those who will not have him to reign over them, and the rulers' murderous counsel shows that refusal hardening into action. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: Then stood there up one in the council, a Pharisee named Gamaliel, a doctor of the law, had in reputation among all the people. Henry sees the Lord's hand in this interruption: the Lord still has all hearts in his hands, and sometimes directs the prudence of the worldly wise so as to restrain the persecutors. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: Ye men of Israel, take heed to yourselves what ye intend to do as touching these men. Henry's gloss is common sense: be cautious, while experience and observation show that the success of frauds in matters of religion has been very short. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: For before these days rose up Theudas, boasting himself to be somebody, to whom a number of men joined, who was slain, and all were scattered and brought to nought. Henry uses the example as Gamaliel intended: the success of frauds in religion has been very short, and Theudas came to nought. — Matthew Henry

37 COMMENTARY: After this man rose up Judas of Galilee in the days of the taxing, and drew away much people after him, and he also perished, and all were dispersed. Henry's point repeats: experience shows religious frauds rise and fall quickly, and Judas of Galilee likewise perished with his following dispersed. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: And now I say unto you, Refrain from these men and let them alone, for if this counsel or this work be of men, it will come to nought. Henry commends the caution: common sense tells us to be cautious, and the short success of frauds is the prudence Gamaliel urges on the council. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: But if it be of God, ye cannot overthrow it, lest haply ye be found even to fight against God. Henry's frame for the counsel is sovereignty: the Lord has all hearts in his hands, and a work of God cannot be overthrown by the council's prudence or violence. — Matthew Henry

41 COMMENTARY: They departed from the presence of the council, rejoicing that they were counted worthy to suffer shame for his name. Henry calls reproach for Christ true preferment, as it makes us conformable to his pattern and serviceable to his interest, and says if we suffer ill for doing well, provided we suffer it well, we ought to rejoice in the grace which enabled us so to do. — Matthew Henry

42 COMMENTARY: Daily in the temple and in every house they ceased not to teach and preach Jesus Christ. Henry closes the chapter on the apostles' business: they did not preach themselves but Christ, and it ought to be the constant business of gospel ministers to preach Christ, and him crucified, Christ, and him glorified, nothing beside this but what has reference to it. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 6, 7, 22, 23, 26, 27, 40 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
