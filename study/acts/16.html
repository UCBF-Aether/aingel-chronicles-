Study Notes — Acts

Chapter 16

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Then came he to Derbe and Lystra, and behold, a certain disciple was there, named Timotheus, the son of a certain woman, which was a Jewess, and believed, but his father was a Greek. Henry says well may the church look for much service from youthful ministers who set out in the same spirit as Timothy. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Which was well reported of by the brethren that were at Lystra and Iconium. Henry's youthful-minister point is Timothy's report itself: a young man set out in Timothy's spirit is well reported of by the brethren, and the church may look for much service from him. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Him would Paul have to go forth with him, and took and circumcised him because of the Jews which were in those quarters, for they knew all that his father was a Greek. Henry contrasts the temper: when men will submit in nothing, and oblige in nothing, the first elements of the Christian temper seem to be wanting, and the doctrines and precepts of the gospel will not be successfully taught, but Timothy submits and obliges for the sake of the Jews among whom they will work. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: As they went through the cities, they delivered them the decrees for to keep, that were ordained of the apostles and elders which were at Jerusalem. Henry explains the decree's design: being to set aside the ceremonial law and its carnal ordinances, believers were confirmed in the Christian faith, because it set up a spiritual way of serving God, as suited to the nature both of God and man. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: So were the churches established in the faith, and increased in number daily. Henry's result is the increase: thus the church increased in numbers daily, established in the faith by the spiritual way of serving God the decree set up. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Now when they had gone throughout Phrygia and the region of Galatia, and were forbidden of the Holy Ghost to preach the word in Asia. Henry's direction rule is the forbidding: the removals of ministers, and the dispensing the means of grace by them, are in particular under divine conduct and direction, and we must follow Providence, and whatever we seek to do, if that suffer us not, we ought to submit and believe to be for the best. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: After they were come to Mysia, they assayed to go into Bithynia, but the Spirit suffered them not. Henry's submission repeats: we must follow Providence, and if that suffer us not, we ought to submit and believe to be for the best, and the Spirit's refusal at Bithynia is that Providence closing a door. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: A vision appeared to Paul in the night: There stood a man of Macedonia, and prayed him, saying, Come over into Macedonia, and help us. Henry says people greatly need help for their souls, it is their duty to look out for it, and to invite those among them who can help them, and God's calls must be complied with readily, and the Macedonian man's prayer is that invitation. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: After he had seen the vision, immediately we endeavoured to go into Macedonia, assuredly gathering that the Lord had called us for to preach the gospel unto them. Henry's readiness is the immediacy: God's calls must be complied with readily, and after the vision they immediately endeavoured to go. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: On the sabbath we went out of the city by a river side, where prayer was wont to be made, and we sat down, and spake unto the women which resorted thither. Henry says a solemn assembly the worshippers of God must have, if possible, upon the sabbath day, and if we have not synagogues, we must be thankful for more private places, and resort to them, not forsaking the assembling together, as our opportunities are. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: A certain woman named Lydia, a seller of purple, of the city of Thyatira, which worshipped God, heard us, whose heart the Lord opened, that she attended unto the things which were spoken of Paul. Henry praises her honest calling, which the historian notices to her praise, yet though she had a calling to mind, she found time to improve advantages for her soul, for it will not excuse us from religious duties to say we have a trade to mind, since we have also a God to serve and souls to look after, and religion directs us in our business, not calls us from it, while pride, prejudice and sin shut out the truths of God, till his grace makes way for them into the understanding and affections, and the Lord alone can open the heart to receive and believe his word. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: When she was baptized, and her household, she besought us, saying, If ye have judged me to be faithful to the Lord, come into my house, and abide there. And she constrained us. Henry's opened heart bears this fruit: the Lord alone can open the heart to receive and believe his word, and Lydia, whose heart was opened, is baptized with her household and constrains the missionaries to abide in her house. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: As we went to prayer, a certain damsel possessed with a spirit of divination met us, which brought her masters much gain by soothsaying. Henry's gain motive for the coming persecution is here: the damsel brought her masters much gain, and when that hope of gain is gone, the masters will seize Paul and Silas, for those who do good by drawing men from sin may expect to be reviled as troublers of the city. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: The same followed Paul and us, and cried, saying, These men are the servants of the most high God, which shew unto us the way of salvation. Henry warns that Satan, though the father of lies, will declare the most important truths when he can thereby serve his purposes, and much mischief is done to the real servants of Christ by unholy and false preachers of the gospel, who are confounded with them by careless observers. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: This did she many days. But Paul, being grieved, turned and said to the spirit, I command thee in the name of Jesus Christ to come out of her. And he came out the same hour. Henry's grieved turning is the refusal of false testimony: though the words were true, the unholy preacher's cry confounds the real servants of Christ with false ones in careless eyes, and Paul silences the spirit in Jesus' name. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: When her masters saw that the hope of their gains was gone, they caught Paul and Silas, and drew them into the marketplace unto the rulers. Henry's expectation is fulfilled: those who do good by drawing men from sin may expect to be reviled as troublers of the city, and the masters, their hope of gain gone, seize the apostles. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Brought them to the magistrates, saying, These men, being Jews, do exceedingly trouble our city. Henry says while they teach men to fear God, to believe in Christ, to forsake sin, and to live godly lives, they will be accused of teaching bad customs, and the troublers-of-the-city charge is that accusation. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: And teach customs, which are not lawful for us to receive, neither to observe, being Romans. Henry's bad-customs accusation is the charge itself: teaching men to fear God and forsake sin is accused as teaching customs unlawful for Romans to receive or observe. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: The multitude rose up together against them, and the magistrates rent off their clothes, and commanded to beat them. Henry's reviling turns to violence here: those who do good by drawing men from sin are reviled as troublers of the city, and the multitude rises and the magistrates command the beating. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: At midnight Paul and Silas prayed, and sang praises unto God, and the prisoners heard them. Henry says the consolations of God to his suffering servants are neither few nor small, and how much more happy are true Christians than their prosperous enemies: as in the dark, so out of the depths, we may cry unto God, no place, no time is amiss for prayer, if the heart be lifted up to God, and no trouble, however grievous, should hinder us from praise. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: Suddenly there was a great earthquake, so that the foundations of the prison were shaken, and immediately all the doors were opened, and every one's bands were loosed. Henry's midnight scene carries the shaking: the consolations of God to his suffering servants are neither few nor small, and the prayer and praise at midnight are followed by foundations shaken, doors opened, and bands loosed. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: The keeper of the prison awaking out of his sleep, and seeing the prison doors open, he drew his sword, and would have killed himself, supposing that the prisoners had been fled. Henry's self-harm warning begins in the keeper's despair: Christianity proves itself to be of God in that it obliges us to be just to our own lives, and the keeper, supposing the prisoners fled, would destroy himself before Paul's cry stops him. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: But Paul cried with a loud voice, saying, Do thyself no harm: for we are all here. Henry says Paul cried aloud to make the jailer hear, and to make him heed, saying, Do thyself no harm, and all the cautions of the word of God against sin, and all appearances of it, and approaches to it, have this tendency: man, woman, do not ruin thyself, hurt not thyself, and then none else can hurt thee, do not sin, for nothing but that can hurt thee. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Then he called for a light, and sprang in, and came trembling, and fell down before Paul and Silas. Henry's conviction picture is the trembling fall: the jailer's inquiry is serious, his salvation becomes his great concern, that lies nearest his heart which before was furthest from his thoughts, and it is his own precious soul he is concerned about. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: And brought them out, and said, Sirs, what must I do to be saved? Henry exclaims at the inquiry: how serious the jailer's inquiry! His salvation becomes his great concern, and those who are thoroughly convinced of sin, and truly concerned about their salvation, will give themselves up to Christ. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: They said, Believe on the Lord Jesus Christ, and thou shalt be saved, and thy house. Henry calls this the sum of the whole gospel, the covenant of grace in a few words: Believe in the Lord Jesus Christ, and thou shalt be saved, and thy house. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: They spake unto him the word of the Lord, and to all that were in his house. Henry's blessed word follows: the Lord so blessed the word that the jailer was at once softened and humbled, and the word spoken to him and to all his house is that blessing applied to the household. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: He took them the same hour of the night, and washed their stripes, and was baptized, he and all his, straightway. Henry says the Lord so blessed the word that the jailer was at once softened and humbled: he treated them with kindness and compassion, washing their stripes, and, professing faith in Christ, was baptized in that name, with his family, while the Spirit of grace worked such a strong faith in them as did away further doubt, and when sinners are thus converted, they will love and honour those whom they before despised and hated, and seek to lessen the suffering they before desired to increase. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: When he had brought them into his house, he set meat before them, and rejoiced, believing in God with all his house. Henry says when the fruits of faith begin to appear, terrors will be followed by confidence and joy in God, and the jailer's table and rejoicing, believing with all his house, are those fruits. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: When it was day, the magistrates sent the serjeants, saying, Let those men go. Henry's honourable dismissal begins with this order: Paul, though willing to suffer for the cause of Christ, and without any desire to avenge himself, did not choose to depart under the charge of having deserved wrongful punishment, and therefore required to be dismissed in an honourable manner. — Matthew Henry

37 COMMENTARY: But Paul said unto them, They have beaten us openly uncondemned, being Romans, and have cast us into prison, and now do they thrust us out privily? nay verily; but let them come themselves and fetch us out. Henry says it was not a mere point of honour that the apostle stood upon, but justice, and not to himself so much as to his cause. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: The serjeants told these words unto the magistrates, and they feared, when they heard that they were Romans. Henry's justice point lands: the magistrates feared when they heard the apostles were Romans, for the apostle stood not on personal honour but on justice, and to his cause. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: They came and besought them, and brought them out, and desired them to depart out of the city. Henry says when proper apology is made, Christians should never express personal anger, nor insist too strictly upon personal amends, and the magistrates' beseeching and bringing them out is that apology, received without anger. — Matthew Henry

40 COMMENTARY: They went out of the prison, and entered into the house of Lydia, and when they had seen the brethren, they comforted them, and departed. Henry closes on comfort: the Lord will make them more than conquerors in every conflict, and instead of being cast down by their sufferings, they will become comforters of their brethren, as Paul and Silas comfort the brethren in Lydia's house before departing. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 8, 11, 12, 23, 24, 36 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
