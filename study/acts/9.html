Study Notes — Acts

Chapter 9

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Saul, yet breathing out threatenings and slaughter against the disciples of the Lord, went unto the high priest. Henry says Saul was so ill informed that he thought he ought to do all he could against the name of Christ, and that he did God service thereby, seeming to breathe in this as in his element. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: He desired of him letters to Damascus to the synagogues, that if he found any of this way, whether men or women, he might bring them bound unto Jerusalem. Henry's mercy in the story begins here: it is a signal token of divine favour if God, by the inward working of his grace or the outward events of his providence, stops us from prosecuting or executing sinful purposes. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: As he journeyed, he came near Damascus, and suddenly there shined round about him a light from heaven. Henry notes Saul saw that Just One, and reflects how near to us is the unseen world: it is but for God to draw aside the veil, and objects are presented to the view compared with which whatever is most admired on earth is mean and contemptible. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: He fell to the earth and heard a voice saying unto him, Saul, Saul, why persecutest thou me? Henry says Christ's discoveries of himself to poor souls are humbling: they lay them very low, in mean thoughts of themselves. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Who art thou, Lord? And the Lord said, I am Jesus whom thou persecutest. Henry's humbling point stands: the discovery lays the soul low, and persecution of the disciples is shown to be persecution of Jesus himself. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Trembling and astonished, Saul said, Lord, what wilt thou have me to do? Henry calls this the beginning of the good work: Saul submitted without reserve, desirous to know what the Lord Jesus would have him to do, and a good work was begun in Saul when he was brought to Christ's feet with those words, for never did Christ leave any who were brought to that. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: Saul arose from the earth, and when his eyes were opened, he saw no man, but they led him by the hand and brought him into Damascus. Henry's account of the blindness begins here: his sins were now set in order before him, and he was in the dark concerning his own spiritual state, wounded in spirit for sin. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: He was three days without sight, and neither did eat nor drink. Henry says it pleased God to leave him for that time without relief, and when a sinner is brought to a proper sense of his own state and conduct, he will cast himself wholly on the mercy of the Saviour, asking what he would have him to do, and God will direct the humbled sinner, though not often without sorrows and distress of conscience, yet happy are those who sow in tears, for they shall reap in joy. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: The Lord said unto Ananias, Arise, and go into the street which is called Straight, and enquire in the house of Judas for one called Saul, of Tarsus: for, behold, he prayeth. Henry exclaims over the tidings: behold, the proud Pharisee, the unmerciful oppressor, the daring blasphemer, prayeth! Now he began to pray after another manner than he had done, for before he said his prayers, now he prayed them, and regenerating grace sets people on praying, as surely as a living man breathes. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Then Ananias answered, Lord, I have heard by many of this man, how much evil he hath done to thy saints at Jerusalem. Henry is gentle with the hesitation: even eminent disciples, like Ananias, sometimes stagger at the commands of the Lord. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: And here he hath authority from the chief priests to bind all that call on thy name. Henry gives the reason for Ananias's staggering in full: Saul came with authority to bind all who called on Christ's name, the very work that made the Lord's command seem perilous. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: But the Lord said unto him, Go thy way: for he is a chosen vessel unto me, to bear my name before the Gentiles, and kings, and the children of Israel. Henry answers the staggering with the Lord's glory: it is the Lord's glory to surpass our scanty expectations, and show that those are vessels of his mercy whom we are apt to consider as objects of his vengeance. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Ananias went his way, and entered into the house, and putting his hands on him said, Brother Saul, the Lord, even Jesus, that appeared unto thee in the way as thou camest, hath sent me, that thou mightest receive thy sight, and be filled with the Holy Ghost. Henry's point for the laying on of hands is that the teaching of the Holy Spirit takes away the scales of ignorance and pride from the understanding, and then the sinner becomes a new creature. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Immediately there fell from his eyes as it had been scales, and he received sight forthwith, and arose, and was baptized. Henry reads the scales literally as the figure for the understanding: the teaching of the Holy Spirit takes away the scales of ignorance and pride, and the sinner becomes a new creature. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Straightway he preached Christ in the synagogues, that he is the Son of God. Henry's fruit of the new creature is this: he endeavours to recommend the anointed Saviour, the Son of God, to his former companions, preaching in the very synagogues where he had meant to bind believers. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: But all that heard him were amazed, and said, Is not this he that destroyed them which called on this name in Jerusalem, and came hither for that intent, that he might bring them bound unto the chief priests? Henry's amazement matches theirs: behold the proud Pharisee, the unmerciful oppressor, the daring blasphemer, now preaching the name he destroyed. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: But Saul increased the more in strength, and confounded the Jews which dwelt at Damascus, proving that this is very Christ. Henry traces the increase to the same grace: the scales taken away, the new creature recommends the anointed Saviour, and Saul's strength grows as he proves Jesus is the Christ. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: After that many days were fulfilled, the Jews took counsel to kill him. Henry warns at the turn: when we enter into the way of God, we must look for trials, but the Lord knows how to deliver the godly, and will, with the temptation, also make a way to escape. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: But their laying await was known of Saul, and they watched the gates day and night to kill him. Henry's deliverance promise covers the watched gates: the Lord knows how to deliver the godly, and will make a way to escape with the temptation. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: Then the disciples took him by night, and let him down by the wall in a basket. Henry's way of escape is literally this basket: the Lord, with the temptation, makes a way to escape, and the disciples' night escape is that way provided. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: When Saul was come to Jerusalem, he assayed to join himself to the disciples, but they were all afraid of him, and believed not that he was a disciple. Henry cautions the church: believers are apt to be too suspicious of those against whom they have prejudices, and though the world is full of deceit and caution is necessary, true charity must still be exercised toward believers. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: But Barnabas took him, and brought him to the apostles, and declared unto them how he had seen the Lord in the way, and that he had spoken to him, and how he had preached boldly at Damascus in the name of Jesus. Henry's pattern for such cases is Barnabas: the Lord brings converts to his people, and often gives them opportunities of bearing testimony to his truth before those who once witnessed their hatred to it. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: He spake boldly in the name of the Lord Jesus, and disputed against the Grecians, but they went about to slay him. Henry's guard for witnesses fits: Christ's witnesses cannot be slain till they have finished their testimony, and Saul's bold dispute draws the same murderous intent he once carried. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: Which when the brethren knew, they brought him down to Caesarea, and sent him forth to Tarsus. Henry notes Christ now appeared to Saul and ordered him to go quickly out of Jerusalem, for he must be sent to the Gentiles, and his witnesses cannot be slain till they have finished their testimony. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: Then had the churches rest throughout all Judaea and Galilee and Samaria, and were edified, and walking in the fear of the Lord, and in the comfort of the Holy Ghost, were multiplied. Henry describes the rest: the persecutions were stayed, the professors walked uprightly and enjoyed much comfort from the Holy Ghost in the hope and peace of the gospel, and others were won over to them, living upon that comfort not only in trouble but in days of rest and prosperity. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: As Peter passed throughout all quarters, he came down also to the saints which dwelt at Lydda. Henry's definition opens the section: Christians are saints, or holy people, not only the eminent ones, as Saint Peter and Saint Paul, but every sincere professor of the faith of Christ. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: There he found a certain man named Aeneas, which had kept his bed eight years, and was sick of the palsy. Henry says Christ chose patients whose diseases were incurable in the course of nature, to show how desperate was the case of fallen mankind, and when we were wholly without strength, as this poor man, he sent his word to heal us. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: Peter said unto him, Aeneas, Jesus Christ maketh thee whole: arise, and make thy bed. And he arose immediately. Henry is precise about the agency: Peter does not pretend to heal by any power of his own, but directs Aeneas to look up to Christ for help, and adds the duty with the gift, for though Jesus Christ makes thee whole, yet thou must arise, and use the power he gives thee. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: All that dwelt at Lydda and Saron saw him, and turned to the Lord. Henry's fruit of the healing is the turning: Christ sent his word to heal the helpless, and those who saw the cure turned to the Lord who wrought it. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: Now there was at Joppa a certain disciple named Tabitha, which by interpretation is called Dorcas: this woman was full of good works and almsdeeds which she did. Henry contrasts her with the opposite sort: many are full of good words who are empty and barren in good works, but Tabitha was a great doer, no great talker, and Christians who have not property to give may yet do acts of charity, working with their hands or walking with their feet for the good of others. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: Then Peter arose and went with them. When he was come, they brought him into the upper chamber, and all the widows stood by him weeping, and shewing the coats and garments which Dorcas made, while she was with them. Henry says those are certainly best praised whose own works praise them, whether the words of others do so or not, and the widows showing her garments are that praise in visible form. — Matthew Henry

40 COMMENTARY: But Peter put them all forth, and kneeled down, and prayed, and turning him to the body said, Tabitha, arise. And she opened her eyes, and when she saw Peter, she sat up. Henry says power went along with the word, and Dorcas came to life, and thus in the raising of dead souls to spiritual life, the first sign of life is the opening of the eyes of the mind. — Matthew Henry

41 COMMENTARY: He gave her his hand, and lifted her up, and when he had called the saints and widows, presented her alive. Henry's comfort for every loss is here: the Lord can make up every loss, and overrules every event for the good of those who trust in him, and for the glory of his name. — Matthew Henry

42 COMMENTARY: It was known throughout all Joppa, and many believed in the Lord. Henry's fruit of the restoration is belief: the Lord overrules every event for the good of those who trust in him and for the glory of his name, and the news through Joppa brought many to believe. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 7, 10, 12, 16, 19, 28, 37, 38, 43 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
