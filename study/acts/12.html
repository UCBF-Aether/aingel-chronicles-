Study Notes — Acts

Chapter 12

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: About that time Herod the king stretched forth his hands to vex certain of the church. Henry warns that the way of persecution, as of other sins, is downhill: when men are in it, they cannot easily stop, and those who make it their business to please men make themselves an easy prey to Satan. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: He killed James the brother of John with the sword. Henry recalls Christ's word to the sons of Zebedee, that they should drink of the cup he was to drink of and be baptized with the baptism he was to be baptized with, and says now the words were made good in James, for if we suffer with Christ, we shall reign with him, and thus James finished his course. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Because he saw it pleased the Jews, he proceeded further to take Peter also, in the days of unleavened bread. Henry's downhill warning fits the pleasing of the Jews: Herod, having pleased men by killing James, cannot easily stop, and pleasing men makes a man an easy prey to Satan. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: When he had apprehended him, he put him in prison, and delivered him to four quaternions of soldiers to keep him, intending after Easter to bring him forth to the people. Henry's distinction is that Peter, being designed for further services, was safe, though he seemed now marked out for a speedy sacrifice. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Peter therefore was kept in prison, but prayer was made without ceasing of the church unto God for him. Henry marvels at the earnestness: we who live in a cold, prayerless generation can hardly form an idea of the earnestness of these holy men of old, but if the Lord should bring on the church an awful persecution like Herod's, the faithful would learn what soul-felt prayer is. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: When Herod would have brought him forth, the same night Peter was sleeping between two soldiers, bound with two chains, and the keepers before the door kept the prison. Henry says a peaceful conscience, a lively hope, and the consolations of the Holy Spirit can keep men calm in the full prospect of death, even those very persons who have been most distracted with terrors on that account. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Behold, the angel of the Lord came upon him, and a light shined in the prison, and he smote Peter on the side, and raised him up, saying, Arise up quickly. And his chains fell off from his hands. Henry's timing point is that God's time to help is when things are brought to the last extremity, and Peter was assured that the Lord would cause this trial to end in the way most for his glory. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: The angel said unto him, Gird thyself, and bind on thy sandals. And so he did. And he saith unto him, Cast thy garment about thee, and follow me. Henry says those delivered out of spiritual imprisonment must follow their Deliverer, like the Israelites when they went out of the house of bondage: they knew not whither they went, but knew whom they followed. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: He went out, and followed him, and wist not that it was true which was done by the angel, but thought he saw a vision. Henry observes that souls delivered out of spiritual bondage are not at first aware what God has wrought in them: many have the truth of grace, that want evidence of it. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: When they were past the first and the second ward, they came unto the iron gate that leadeth unto the city, which opened to them of his own accord, and they went out, and passed on through one street, and forthwith the angel departed from him. Henry says when God will work salvation for his people, all difficulties in their way will be overcome, even gates of iron are made to open of their own accord, and this deliverance represents our redemption by Christ, which not only proclaims liberty to the captives, but brings them out of the prison-house. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: When Peter was come to himself, he said, Now I know of a surety, that the Lord hath sent his angel, and hath delivered me out of the hand of Herod, and from all the expectation of the people of the Jews. Henry notes Peter, when he recollected himself, perceived what great things God had done for him, and so, when the Comforter comes, whom the Father will send, sooner or later he will let delivered souls know what a blessed change is wrought. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: When he had considered the thing, he came to the house of Mary the mother of John, whose surname was Mark, where many were gathered together praying. Henry says these Christians continued in prayer for Peter, for they were truly in earnest, and thus men ought always to pray, and not to faint, for as long as we are kept waiting for a mercy, we must continue praying for it. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: When she knew Peter's voice, she opened not the gate for gladness, but ran in, and told how Peter stood before the gate. Henry's human paradox begins here and lands in the next verse: sometimes that which we most earnestly wish for, we are most backward to believe, and Rhoda's gladness outruns the opening of the gate. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: They said unto her, Thou art mad. But she constantly affirmed that it was even so. Then said they, It is his angel. Henry states the paradox plainly: sometimes that which we most earnestly wish for, we are most backward to believe, and the praying company calls the bearer of their answered prayer mad. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: But Peter continued knocking, and when they had opened the door, and saw him, they were astonished. Henry's waiting rule and the paradox meet: as long as we are kept waiting for a mercy we must continue praying for it, yet when it arrives we are backward to believe it, and the company is astonished at the very Peter they prayed for. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Beckoning unto them with the hand to hold their peace, he declared unto them how the Lord had brought him out of the prison, and said, Go shew these things unto James, and to the brethren. And he departed, and went into another place. Henry adds the prudence point: God's providence leaves room for the use of our prudence, and the Christian law of self-denial and suffering for Christ has not done away the natural law of caring for our own safety by lawful means, for in times of public danger, all believers have God for their hiding-place, so secret that the world cannot find them. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Now as soon as it was day, there was no small stir among the soldiers, what was become of Peter. Henry warns the persecutors' side: the instruments of persecution are themselves exposed to danger, the wrath of God hangs over all that engage in this hateful work, and the range of persecutors often vents itself on all in its way. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: When Herod had sought for him, and found him not, he examined the keepers, and commanded that they should be put to death. And he went down from Judaea to Caesarea, and there abode. Henry's warning is fulfilled in the keepers: the instruments of persecution are themselves exposed to danger, and Herod's rage vents itself on all in its way. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Herod was highly displeased with them of Tyre and Sidon, but they came with one accord to him, and, having made Blastus the king's chamberlain their friend, desired peace, because their country was nourished by the king's country. Henry says we may learn wisdom from the people of Tyre and Sidon, for we have offended the Lord with our sins, and we depend on him for life, and breath, and all things, so it behoves us to humble ourselves before him, that through the appointed Mediator we may be reconciled to him. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Upon a set day Herod, arrayed in royal apparel, sat upon his throne, and made an oration unto them. Henry sets the scene for the blasphemy: many heathen princes claimed and received divine honours, but it was far more horrible impiety in Herod, who knew the word and worship of the living God, to accept such idolatrous honours without rebuking the blasphemy. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: The people gave a shout, saying, It is the voice of a god, and not of a man. Henry says such men as Herod, when puffed with pride and vanity, are ripening fast for signal vengeance, and to accept the shout without rebuking the blasphemy is that ripening. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: Immediately the angel of the Lord smote him, because he gave not God the glory, and he was eaten of worms, and gave up the ghost. Henry says God is very jealous for his own honour, and will be glorified upon those whom he is not glorified by, and we may learn from Herod what vile bodies we carry about with us, which have in them the seeds of their own dissolution, by which they will soon be destroyed, whenever God does but speak the word. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: But the word of God grew and multiplied. Henry's contrast for the chapter is the persecutor's end against the word's growth: Herod, ripened for signal vengeance, is smitten, while the word he tried to vex grows and multiplies. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 13, 25 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
