Study Notes — Acts

Chapter 15

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Certain men which came down from Judaea taught the brethren, and said, Except ye be circumcised after the manner of Moses, ye cannot be saved. Henry says some from Judea taught the Gentile converts at Antioch that they could not be saved unless they observed the whole ceremonial law as given by Moses, and thus they sought to destroy Christian liberty, with a strange proneness in us to think that all do wrong who do not just as we do, and their doctrine was very discouraging. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: When therefore Paul and Barnabas had no small dissension and disputation with them, they determined that Paul and Barnabas, and certain other of them, should go up to Jerusalem unto the apostles and elders about this question. Henry says wise and good men desire to avoid contests and disputes as far as they can, yet when false teachers oppose the main truths of the gospel, or bring in hurtful doctrines, we must not decline to oppose them. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: But there rose up certain of the sect of the Pharisees which believed, saying, That it was needful to circumcise them, and to command them to keep the law of Moses. Henry's discouraging doctrine returns in the council: the Pharisee believers would impose the whole ceremonial law, seeking to destroy the Christian liberty the Gentiles had received. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: The apostles and elders came together for to consider of this matter. Henry's opposition rule is the convening itself: wise and good men desire to avoid contests, yet when false teachers oppose the main truths of the gospel, they must not decline to oppose them, and the apostles and elders meet to do so. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: When there had been much disputing, Peter rose up, and said unto them, Men and brethren, ye know how that a good while ago God made choice among us, that the Gentiles by my mouth should hear the word of the gospel, and believe. Henry's frame for Peter's speech is that justification by faith, and sanctification by the Holy Ghost, cannot be separated, and both are the gift of God, and Peter's own first preaching to the Gentiles is the case in point. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: God, which knoweth the hearts, bare them witness, giving them the Holy Ghost, even as he did unto us. Henry says we may have that faith which the great Searcher of hearts approves, and attests by the seal of the Holy Spirit, and then our hearts and consciences will be purified from the guilt of sin, and freed from the burdens some try to lay upon the disciples of Christ. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: And put no difference between us and them, purifying their hearts by faith. Henry takes the very words purifying their hearts by faith as the proof: justification by faith and sanctification by the Holy Ghost cannot be separated, and both are the gift of God, with no difference put between Jew and Gentile. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Now therefore why tempt ye God, to put a yoke upon the neck of the disciples, which neither our fathers nor we were able to bear? Henry's burden point is the yoke: the disciples would be freed from the burdens some try to lay upon them, burdens that perplex the conscience but cannot purify or pacify it. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: But we believe that through the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ we shall be saved, even as they. Henry says we have great cause to bless God that we have heard the gospel, and may we have that faith which the great Searcher of hearts approves and attests by the seal of the Holy Spirit, for Jew and Gentile are saved the same way, through grace. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Then all the multitude kept silence, and gave audience to Barnabas and Paul, declaring what miracles and wonders God had wrought among the Gentiles by them. Henry says Paul and Barnabas showed by plain matters of fact that God owned the preaching of the pure gospel to the Gentiles without the law of Moses, and therefore to press that law upon them was to undo what God had done. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: After they had held their peace, James answered, saying, Men and brethren, hearken unto me. Henry's James opinion follows: the Gentile converts ought not to be troubled about Jewish rites, and James's answer is that sentence in the making. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Simeon hath declared how God at the first did visit the Gentiles, to take out of them a people for his name. Henry grounds James's sentence in Simeon's declaration: God at the first visited the Gentiles to take out a people for his name, and the facts, not theory, carry the argument. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: To this agree the words of the prophets, as it is written. Henry's agreement point is that the prophets agree with Simeon's declaration, and James cites them as concurring witnesses to God's visit to the Gentiles. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: That the residue of men might seek after the Lord, and all the Gentiles, upon whom my name is called, saith the Lord, who doeth all these things. Henry's prophetic agreement has this purpose: the residue of men and all the Gentiles upon whom God's name is called might seek after the Lord, which is exactly what Simeon declared God was doing. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Wherefore my sentence is, that we trouble not them, which from among the Gentiles are turned to God. Henry states James's opinion plainly: the Gentile converts ought not to be troubled about Jewish rites. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: But that we write unto them, that they abstain from pollutions of idols, and from fornication, and from things strangled, and from blood. Henry explains each abstention: from meats offered to idols, so that they might show their hatred of idolatry; against fornication, which was not abhorred by the Gentiles as it should be and even formed a part of some of their rites; and from things strangled and from blood, forbidden by the law of Moses and here from reverence to the blood of the sacrifices, which being then still offered, would needlessly grieve the Jewish converts and further prejudice the unconverted Jews, though as the reason has long ceased, we are left free in this as in like matters, with converts warned to avoid all appearances of the evils they formerly practised and to use Christian liberty with moderation and prudence. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: For Moses of old time hath in every city them that preach him, being read in the synagogues every sabbath day. Henry's reason for the abstentions is this weekly reading: Moses is preached in every city and read every sabbath, so the abstentions avoid needlessly grieving Jewish converts and prejudicing unconverted Jews who hear Moses read. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: Then pleased it the apostles and elders with the whole church, to send chosen men of their own company to Antioch with Paul and Barnabas, namely, Judas surnamed Barsabas, and Silas, chief men among the brethren. Henry's concord point for the letter is that, being warranted to declare themselves directed by the immediate influence of the Holy Ghost, the apostles and disciples were assured it seemed good unto God the Holy Spirit, as well as to them, to lay upon the converts no other burden than the things mentioned, and the whole church's pleasure in sending chosen men shows that accord. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Forasmuch as we have heard, that certain which went out from us have troubled you with words, subverting your souls, saying, Ye must be circumcised, and keep the law: to whom we gave no such commandment. Henry says it was a comfort to hear that carnal ordinances were no longer imposed on them, which perplexed the conscience but could not purify or pacify it, and that those who troubled their minds were silenced, so that the peace of the church was restored and that which threatened division was removed, and the letter's disowning of the troublers is that silencing. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: For it seemed good to the Holy Ghost, and to us, to lay upon you no greater burden than these necessary things. Henry says the apostles were warranted to declare themselves directed by the immediate influence of the Holy Ghost, and assured that it seemed good unto God the Holy Spirit, as well as to them, to lay upon the converts no other burden than the things mentioned, necessary either on their own account or from present circumstances. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: That ye abstain from meats offered to idols, and from blood, and from things strangled, and from fornication: from which if ye keep yourselves, ye shall do well. Fare ye well. Henry repeats the comfort: it was a comfort to hear that carnal ordinances were no longer imposed, which perplexed the conscience but could not purify or pacify it, and the necessary things alone are laid on, with a blessing on keeping them. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: Which when they had read, they rejoiced for the consolation. Henry says all this was consolation for which they blessed God: that carnal ordinances were no longer imposed, that those who troubled their minds were silenced, that the peace of the church was restored, and that which threatened division was removed. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: Judas and Silas, being prophets also themselves, exhorted the brethren with many words, and confirmed them. Henry's Antioch note fits the prophets' labour: where many labour in the word and doctrine, yet there may be opportunity for us, and the zeal and usefulness of others should stir us up, not lay us asleep. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: Paul also and Barnabas continued in Antioch, teaching and preaching the word of the Lord, with many others also. Henry says many others were at Antioch, and where many labour in the word and doctrine, yet there may be opportunity for us, and the zeal and usefulness of others should stir us up, not lay us asleep. — Matthew Henry

37 COMMENTARY: Barnabas determined to take with them John, whose surname was Mark. Henry warns from Barnabas's wish: his nephew John Mark was the one Barnabas wished to take, and we should suspect ourselves of being partial, and guard against this in putting our relations forward. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: But Paul thought not good to take him with them, who departed from them from Pamphylia, and went not with them to the work. Henry gives Paul's reason: Paul did not think him worthy of the honour, nor fit for the service, who had departed from them without their knowledge, or without their consent, referring to the departure at Perga. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: The contention was so sharp between them, that they departed asunder one from the other: and so Barnabas took Mark, and sailed unto Cyprus. Henry is frank about the quarrel: neither would yield, therefore there was no remedy but they must part, and the best of men are but men, subject to like passions as we are, with perhaps faults on both sides, as usual in such contentions, for Christ's example alone is a copy without a blot, and it will be so while we are in this imperfect state, for we shall never be all of one mind till we come to heaven, and what mischief the remainders of pride and passion found even in good men do in the world and in the church. — Matthew Henry

40 COMMENTARY: Paul chose Silas, and departed, being recommended by the brethren unto the grace of God. Henry's end to the quarrel is that it was made to end well: Paul speaks with esteem and affection both of Barnabas and Mark in his epistles written after this event, and believers must be constant in prayer that they may never be led by unholy tempers to hurt the cause they desire to serve, until all are thoroughly reconciled by that love which is not easily provoked and soon forgets and buries injuries. — Matthew Henry

41 COMMENTARY: He went through Syria and Cilicia, confirming the churches. Henry's work-goes-on point closes the chapter: the private quarrel between two ministers was yet made to end well, and Paul's confirming of the churches through Syria and Cilicia shows the cause served on, not stopped by the separation. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 3, 4, 16, 18, 23, 25, 26, 27, 30, 33, 34, 36 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
