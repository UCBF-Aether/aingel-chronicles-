Study Notes — Acts

Chapter 27

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: When it was determined that we should sail into Italy, they delivered Paul and certain other prisoners unto one named Julius, a centurion of Augustus' band. Henry says it was determined by the counsel of God, before it was determined by the counsel of Festus, that Paul should go to Rome, for God had work for him to do there, and God here encourages those who suffer for him to trust in him, for he can put it into the hearts of those to befriend them, from whom they least expect it. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Entering into a ship of Adramyttium, we launched, meaning to sail by the coasts of Asia; one Aristarchus, a Macedonian of Thessalonica, being with us. Henry says the course they steered, and the places they touched at, are here set down, and sailors must make the best of the wind, and so must we all in our passage over the ocean of this world, and the launch by the coasts of Asia is that course's beginning. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: The next day we touched at Sidon. And Julius courteously entreated Paul, and gave him liberty to go unto his friends to refresh himself. Henry says God encourages those who suffer for him to trust in him, for he can put it into the hearts of those to befriend them, from whom they least expect it, and Julius courteously entreating Paul at Sidon, giving him liberty to go unto his friends to refresh himself, is that unexpected befriending. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: When we had launched from thence, we sailed under Cyprus, because the winds were contrary. Henry says sailors must make the best of the wind, and so must we all in our passage over the ocean of this world, for when the winds are contrary, yet we must be getting forward as well as we can, and the sailing under Cyprus because the winds were contrary is that getting forward. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: When we had sailed slowly many days, and scarce were come over against Cnidus, the wind not suffering us, we sailed under Crete, over against Salmone. Henry says when the winds are contrary, yet we must be getting forward as well as we can, and many who are not driven backward by cross providences do not get forward by favourable providences, and many real Christians complain as to the concerns of their souls, that they have much ado to keep their ground, and the slow sailing scarce over against Cnidus is that much ado. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: Hardly passing it, came unto a place which is called The fair havens; nigh whereunto was the city of Lasea. Henry says every fair haven is not a safe haven, and the hardly-passing arrival at The fair havens, nigh unto Lasea, is the fair haven that proves unsafe. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Now when much time was spent, and when sailing was now dangerous, because the fast was now already past, Paul admonished them. Henry's dangerous sailing is the admonition's ground: much time was spent, and sailing was now dangerous, because the fast was now already past, and Paul admonished them of the hurt and damage the voyage would bring. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: And said unto them, Sirs, I perceive that this voyage will be with hurt and much damage, not only of the lading and ship, but also of our lives. Henry says many show respect to good ministers, who will not take their advice, but the event will convince sinners of the vanity of their hopes, and the folly of their conduct, and Paul's perception, that the voyage will be with hurt and much damage, not only of the lading and ship, but also of their lives, is the advice the event will vindicate. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Nevertheless the centurion believed the master and the owner of the ship, more than those things which were spoken by Paul. Henry says many show respect to good ministers, who will not take their advice, and the centurion believing the master and the owner of the ship, more than those things spoken by Paul, is that respect without advice-taking, which the event will convince of vanity and folly. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Because the haven was not commodious to winter in, the more part advised to depart thence also, if by any means they might attain to Phenice, and there to winter; which is an haven of Crete, and lieth toward the south west and north west. Henry says most people bring themselves into trouble, because they do not know when they are well off, for they come to harm and loss by aiming to mend their condition, often against advice, and the more part advising to depart the uncommodious haven for Phenice is that aiming to mend their condition against advice. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: When the south wind blew softly, supposing that they had obtained their purpose, loosing thence, they sailed close by Crete. Henry says those who launch forth on the ocean of this world, with a fair gale, know not what storms they may meet with, and therefore must not easily take it for granted that they have obtained their purpose, and the south wind blowing softly, supposing they had obtained their purpose, is that easy taking-for-granted. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: But not long after there arose against it a tempestuous wind, called Euroclydon. Henry says those who launch forth on the ocean of this world, with a fair gale, know not what storms they may meet with, and let us never expect to be quite safe till we enter heaven, and not long after the soft south wind, there arose against it the tempestuous wind called Euroclydon. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: When the ship was caught, and could not bear up into the wind, we let her drive. Henry's caught ship is the storm's capture: the ship was caught, and could not bear up into the wind, and they let her drive, and the means the sailors used did not succeed. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: Running under a certain island which is called Clauda, we had much work to come by the boat. Henry's much work is the sailors' means beginning: running under Clauda, they had much work to come by the boat, yet the means the sailors used did not succeed. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Which when they had taken up, they used helps, undergirding the ship; and, fearing lest they should fall into the quicksands, strake sail, and so were driven. Henry says the means the sailors used did not succeed: they used helps, undergirding the ship, and fearing lest they should fall into the quicksands, strake sail, and so were driven. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: And we being exceedingly tossed with a tempest, the next day they lightened the ship. Henry says see what the wealth of this world is: though coveted as a blessing, the time may come when it will be a burden, not only too heavy to be carried safely, but heavy enough to sink him that has it, and the children of this world can be prodigal of their goods for the saving their lives, yet are sparing of them in works of piety and charity, and in suffering for Christ, and the next day's lightening of the ship is that prodigality for life. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: The third day we cast out with our own hands the tackling of the ship. Henry says any man will rather make shipwreck of his goods than of his life, but many rather make shipwreck of faith and a good conscience, than of their goods, and the third day's casting out with their own hands the tackling of the ship is the shipwreck of goods for life. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: When neither sun nor stars in many days appeared, and no small tempest lay on us, all hope that we should be saved was then taken away. Henry says they saw neither sun nor stars for many days, and thus melancholy sometimes is the condition of the people of God as to their spiritual matters, for they walk in darkness, and have no light, but when sinners give up all hope of saving themselves, they are prepared to understand God's word, and to trust in his mercy through Jesus Christ. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: But after long abstinence Paul stood forth in the midst of them, and said, Sirs, ye should have hearkened unto me, and not have loosed from Crete, and to have gained this harm and loss. Henry says they did not hearken to the apostle when he warned them of their danger, yet if they acknowledge their folly, and repent of it, he will speak comfort and relief to them when in danger, and most people bring themselves into trouble, because they do not know when they are well off, for they come to harm and loss by aiming to mend their condition, often against advice, and the gained harm and loss is that trouble. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: And now I exhort you to be of good cheer: for there shall be no loss of any man's life among you, but of the ship. Henry's comfort and relief when in danger is the exhortation: now be of good cheer, for there shall be no loss of any man's life among you, but of the ship, and God is ever faithful, therefore let all who have an interest in his promises be ever cheerful. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: For there stood by me this night the angel of God, whose I am, and whom I serve. Henry says observe the solemn profession Paul made of relation to God, for no storms or tempests can hinder God's favour to his people, for he is a Help always at hand, and there stood by Paul this night the angel of God, whose he is, and whom he serves. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Saying, Fear not, Paul; thou must be brought before Caesar: and, lo, God hath given thee all them that sail with thee. Henry says it is a comfort to the faithful servants of God when in difficulties, that as long as the Lord has any work for them to do, their lives shall be prolonged, and if Paul had thrust himself needlessly into bad company, he might justly have been cast away with them, but God calling him into it, they are preserved with him, for they are given thee, and there is no greater satisfaction to a good man than to know he is a public blessing. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: Wherefore, sirs, be of good cheer: for I believe God, that it shall be even as it was told me. Henry says he comforts them with the same comforts wherewith he himself was comforted, and God is ever faithful, therefore let all who have an interest in his promises be ever cheerful, and as, with God, saying and doing are not two things, believing and enjoying should not be so with us, and Paul believes God, that it shall be even as it was told him. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: Howbeit we must be cast upon a certain island. Henry's cast-upon-an-island is the assurance's condition: they must be cast upon a certain island, yet hope is an anchor of the soul, sure and stedfast, entering into that within the veil, so let those who are in spiritual darkness hold fast by that, and think not of putting to sea again, but abide by Christ, and wait till the day break, and the shadows flee away. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: But when the fourteenth night was come, as we were driven up and down in Adria, about midnight the shipmen deemed that they drew near to some country. Henry's fourteenth night is the waiting till the day break: driven up and down in Adria, about midnight the shipmen deemed that they drew near to some country, and those in spiritual darkness hold fast and wait till the day break, and the shadows flee away. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Then fearing lest we should have fallen upon rocks, they cast four anchors out of the stern, and wished for the day. Henry says hope is an anchor of the soul, sure and stedfast, entering into that within the veil, and let those who are in spiritual darkness hold fast by that, and think not of putting to sea again, but abide by Christ, and wait till the day break, and the shadows flee away, and fearing lest they should have fallen upon rocks, they cast four anchors out of the stern, and wished for the day. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: As the shipmen were about to flee out of the ship, when they had let down the boat into the sea, under colour as though they would have cast anchors out of the foreship. Henry says God, who appointed the end, that they should be saved, appointed the means, that they should be saved by the help of these shipmen, but how selfish are men in general, often even ready to seek their own safety by the destruction of others, and the shipmen about to flee, letting down the boat under colour of casting anchors out of the foreship, is that selfish seeking. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: Paul said to the centurion and to the soldiers, Except these abide in the ship, ye cannot be saved. Henry says God, who appointed the end, that they should be saved, appointed the means, that they should be saved by the help of these shipmen, and duty is ours, events are God's, for we do not trust God, but tempt him, when we say we put ourselves under his protection, if we do not use proper means, such as are within our power, for our safety, and except these abide in the ship, ye cannot be saved. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: Then the soldiers cut off the ropes of the boat, and let her fall off. Henry's cut ropes are the means secured: the soldiers cut off the ropes of the boat, and let her fall off, keeping the shipmen whose help God appointed for the saving. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: While the day was coming on, Paul besought them all to take meat, saying, This day is the fourteenth day that ye have tarried and continued fasting, having taken nothing. Henry says happy those who have such a one as Paul in their company, who not only had intercourse with Heaven, but was of an enlivening spirit to those about him, and the sorrow of the world works death, while joy in God is life and peace in the greatest distresses and dangers, and Paul besought them all to take meat, this fourteenth day of fasting, as that enlivening spirit. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: Wherefore I pray you to take some meat: for this is for your health: for there shall not an hair fall from the head of any of you. Henry says the comfort of God's promises can only be ours by believing dependence on him, to fulfil his word to us, and the salvation he reveals must be waited for in use of the means he appoints, and Paul prays them to take some meat, for this is for their health, for there shall not an hair fall from the head of any of them. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: When he had thus spoken, he took bread, and gave thanks to God in presence of them all: and when he had broken it, he began to eat. Henry says it is an encouragement to people to commit themselves to Christ as their Saviour, when those who invite them, clearly show that they do so themselves, and Paul took bread, and gave thanks to God in presence of them all, and when he had broken it, he began to eat, showing that commitment himself. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: Then were they all of good cheer, and they also took some meat. Henry says the sorrow of the world works death, while joy in God is life and peace in the greatest distresses and dangers, and then were they all of good cheer, and they also took some meat, sharing Paul's enlivening spirit. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: When they had eaten enough, they lightened the ship, and cast out the wheat into the sea. Henry's cast wheat is the means used for the appointed salvation: when they had eaten enough, they lightened the ship, and cast out the wheat into the sea, and the salvation he reveals must be waited for in use of the means he appoints. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: When it was day, they knew not the land: but they discovered a certain creek with a shore, into the which they were minded, if it were possible, to thrust in the ship. Henry says they had the shore in view, yet suffered shipwreck in the harbour, and thus we are taught never to be secure, and when it was day, they knew not the land, but discovered a certain creek with a shore, into which they were minded to thrust in the ship. — Matthew Henry

40 COMMENTARY: When they had taken up the anchors, they committed themselves unto the sea, and loosed the rudder bands, and hoised up the mainsail to the wind, and made toward shore. Henry's toward-shore is the last means before the wreck: they took up the anchors, committed themselves unto the sea, loosed the rudder bands, hoisted up the mainsail to the wind, and made toward shore, with the shore in view, yet taught never to be secure. — Matthew Henry

41 COMMENTARY: Falling into a place where two seas met, they ran the ship aground; and the forepart stuck fast, and remained unmoveable, but the hinder part was broken with the violence of the waves. Henry says the ship that had weathered the storm in the open sea, where it had room, is dashed to pieces when it sticks fast, and thus, if the heart fixes in the world in affection, and cleaving to it, it is lost, for Satan's temptations beat against it, and it is gone, but as long as it keeps above the world, though tossed with cares and tumults, there is hope for it, and the forepart stuck fast and remained unmoveable, while the hinder part was broken with the violence of the waves. — Matthew Henry

42 COMMENTARY: The soldiers' counsel was to kill the prisoners, lest any of them should swim out, and escape. Henry's selfish safety is the soldiers' counsel: the soldiers' counsel was to kill the prisoners, lest any of them should swim out, and escape, seeking their own safety by the destruction of others. — Matthew Henry

43 COMMENTARY: But the centurion, willing to save Paul, kept them from their purpose; and commanded that they which could swim should cast themselves first into the sea, and get to land. Henry says God here encourages those who suffer for him to trust in him, for he can put it into the hearts of those to befriend them, from whom they least expect it, and the centurion, willing to save Paul, kept the soldiers from their purpose, and commanded that they which could swim should cast themselves first into the sea, and get to land. — Matthew Henry

44 COMMENTARY: And the rest, some on boards, and some on broken pieces of the ship. And so it came to pass, that they escaped all safe to land. Henry says though there is great difficulty in the way of the promised salvation, it shall, without fail, be brought to pass, and it will come to pass that whatever the trials and dangers may be, in due time all believers will get safely to heaven, for Lord Jesus, thou hast assured us that none of thine shall perish, and thou wilt bring them all safe to the heavenly shore, and what a pleasing landing will that be, and they escaped all safe to land. — Matthew Henry
