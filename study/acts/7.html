Study Notes — Acts

Chapter 7

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Then said the high priest, Are these things so? Henry frames Stephen's whole defence as answering the charge of blasphemy and apostasy by showing that he is a son of Abraham and values himself on it. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: The God of glory appeared unto our father Abraham when he was in Mesopotamia, before he dwelt in Charran. Henry reads Abraham's calling as showing the power and freeness of divine grace and the nature of conversion, and adds that outward forms and distinctions are as nothing compared with separation from the world and devotedness to God. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Get thee out of thy country and from thy kindred, and come into the land which I shall shew thee. Henry's lesson from the call is separation: outward forms are nothing beside leaving the world behind and being devoted to God. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Then came he out of the land of the Chaldaeans and dwelt in Charran, and from thence, when his father was dead, he removed him into the land of Canaan. Henry notes the slow steps by which the promise made to Abraham advanced toward performance, plainly showing it had a spiritual meaning and that the land intended was the heavenly. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: He gave him none inheritance in it, no, not so much as to set his foot on, yet he promised that he would give it to him for a possession, and to his seed after him. Henry's point is the same slow advance: the promise moved by steps that show its spiritual meaning, with the heavenly land in view. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: God spake on this wise, that his seed should sojourn in a strange land, and that they should bring them into bondage and entreat them evil four hundred years. Henry warns not to be discouraged at the slowness of the fulfilling of God's promises, for suffering times often are growing times with the church. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: The nation to whom they shall be in bondage will I judge, said God, and after that shall they come forth and serve me in this place. Henry adds that God is preparing for his people's deliverance when their day is darkest and their distress deepest. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: He gave him the covenant of circumcision, and so Abraham begat Isaac, and Isaac begat Jacob, and Jacob begat the twelve patriarchs. Henry notes it is well to recur to the first rise of usages or sentiments which have been perverted, and the covenant sign stands at the head of the line Stephen traces. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: The patriarchs, moved with envy, sold Joseph into Egypt, but God was with him. Henry reminds the Jews of the wickedness of the patriarchs of their tribes in envying their brother Joseph, and says the same spirit was still working in them toward Christ and his ministers, while God owned Joseph in his troubles and was with him by the power of his Spirit. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: And delivered him out of all his afflictions, and gave him favour and wisdom in the sight of Pharaoh king of Egypt, who made him governor over Egypt and all his house. Henry's gloss is that God was with Joseph by his Spirit, both on his own mind by giving him comfort, and on those he was concerned with, by giving him favour in their eyes. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: The faith of the patriarchs in desiring to be buried in the land of Canaan is Henry's point here: carried over into Sychem and laid in the sepulchre Abraham bought, they plainly showed they had regard to the heavenly country. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: But when the time of the promise drew nigh, which God had sworn to Abraham, the people grew and multiplied in Egypt. Henry's encouragement fits the growth in bondage: suffering times often are growing times with the church, and we should not be discouraged at the slowness of God's promises. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Till another king arose, which knew not Joseph. Henry's pattern is that God is preparing for his people's deliverance when their day is darkest, and a king who knew not Joseph marks that darkening. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: The same dealt subtilly with our kindred, and evil entreated our fathers, so that they cast out their young children, to the end they might not live. Henry says God prepares deliverance when distress is deepest, and this subtil cruelty is the depth from which deliverance would come. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: In which time Moses was born, and was exceeding fair, and nourished up in his father's house three months. Henry explains the fairness: Moses was exceeding fair, fair toward God, and it is the beauty of holiness which is in God's sight of great price, and he was wonderfully preserved in his infancy, for God takes special care of those of whom he designs to make special use. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: When he was cast out, Pharaoh's daughter took him up and nourished him for her own son. Henry's preservation point continues: God will take special care of those of whom he designs to make special use, and the rescue by Pharaoh's daughter is that care at work. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: For he supposed his brethren would have understood how that God by his hand would deliver them, but they understood not. Henry turns the misunderstanding toward Stephen's hearers: if they do not wilfully shut their eyes, they may understand that God will, by this Jesus, deliver them out of a worse slavery than that of Egypt, and though men prolong their own miseries, the Lord will effect his own designs of mercy. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: But he that did his neighbour wrong thrust him away, saying, Who made thee a ruler and a judge over us? Henry's later point anticipates this refusal: this Moses whom they refused, even this same has God advanced to be a Prince and Saviour, just as the Jesus they now refuse has been advanced. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: When forty years were expired, there appeared to him in the wilderness of mount Sina an angel of the Lord in a flame of fire in a bush. Henry says men deceive themselves if they think God cannot do what he sees to be good anywhere, for he can bring his people into a wilderness and there speak comfortably to them, and the bush not consumed represented Israel in the fire of affliction, yet not consumed, and may be a type of Christ taking human nature upon him. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: Saying, I am the God of thy fathers, the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob. Henry draws the resurrection proof from the name: the death of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob cannot break the covenant relation between God and them, for Abraham is dead, yet God is still his God, therefore Abraham is still alive, and this is the life and immortality brought to light by the gospel. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: I have seen, I have seen the affliction of my people which is in Egypt, and I have heard their groaning, and am come down to deliver them. Henry says God has compassion for the troubles of his church and the groans of his persecuted people, and their deliverance takes rise from his pity, and that deliverance was typical of what Christ did when, for us men and for our salvation, he came down from heaven. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: This Moses whom they refused, saying, Who made thee a ruler and a judge? the same did God send to be a ruler and a deliverer by the hand of the angel which appeared to him in the bush. Henry states the type plainly: Moses was an eminent type of Christ as Israel's deliverer, and this Jesus, whom they now refused as their fathers did Moses, even this same has God advanced to be a Prince and Saviour. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: He brought them out, after that he had shewed wonders and signs in the land of Egypt, and in the Red sea, and in the wilderness forty years. Henry traces the deliverance to its rise in God's pity for his persecuted people, a deliverance typical of Christ's coming down from heaven for our salvation. — Matthew Henry

37 COMMENTARY: This is that Moses, which said unto the children of Israel, A prophet shall the Lord your God raise up unto you of your brethren, like unto me; him shall ye hear. Henry defends Stephen against the blasphemy charge here: in asserting that Jesus should change the customs of the ceremonial law, Stephen was so far from blaspheming Moses that he honoured him, by showing how the prophecy of Moses was come to pass, and God who gave those customs by Moses might no doubt change the custom by his Son Jesus. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: To whom our fathers would not obey, but thrust him from them, and in their hearts turned back again into Egypt. Henry's application is direct: Israel thrust Moses from them and would have returned to their bondage, so men in general will not obey Jesus, because they love this present evil world and rejoice in their own works and devices. — Matthew Henry

40 COMMENTARY: Saying unto Aaron, Make us gods to go before us, for as for this Moses, which brought us out of the land of Egypt, we wot not what is become of him. Henry reads the demand for gods as the same turning back: they thrust Moses away and chose their own works and devices over obedience. — Matthew Henry

41 COMMENTARY: They made a calf in those days, and offered sacrifice unto the idol, and rejoiced in the works of their own hands. Henry joins this to the fathers' idolatry, to which God gave them up as a punishment for their early forsaking of him, and notes men rejoice in their own works and devices instead of obeying. — Matthew Henry

42 COMMENTARY: Then God turned, and gave them up to worship the host of heaven, as it is written in the book of the prophets. Henry's charge is that Stephen upbraids the Jews with the idolatry of their fathers, to which God gave them up as a punishment for their early forsaking him. — Matthew Henry

43 COMMENTARY: Yea, ye took up the tabernacle of Moloch, and the star of your god Remphan, figures which ye made to worship them, and I will carry you away beyond Babylon. Henry's point stands over the whole idolatry passage: God gave them up to the worship they chose, as punishment for forsaking him early. — Matthew Henry

44 COMMENTARY: Our fathers had the tabernacle of witness in the wilderness, as he had appointed, speaking unto Moses, that he should make it according to the fashion that he had seen. Henry's succession argument begins here: it was no dishonour but an honour to God that the tabernacle gave way to the temple, so it is now that the earthly temple gives way to the spiritual one. — Matthew Henry

45 COMMENTARY: Which also our fathers that came after brought in with Jesus into the possession of the Gentiles, whom God drave out before the face of our fathers, unto the days of David. Henry continues the succession: the tabernacle gave way to the temple without dishonour to God, as the earthly temple now gives way to the spiritual. — Matthew Henry

47 COMMENTARY: But Solomon built him an house. Henry's point is the succession itself: the tabernacle gave way to the temple, the earthly temple gives way to the spiritual one, and at last the spiritual shall give way to the eternal one. — Matthew Henry

48 COMMENTARY: Howbeit the most High dwelleth not in temples made with hands, as saith the prophet. Henry asks with Stephen: the whole world is God's temple, in which he is everywhere present and fills it with his glory, so what occasion has he for a temple to manifest himself in? — Matthew Henry

49 COMMENTARY: Heaven is my throne, and earth is my footstool: what house will ye build me? saith the Lord: or what is the place of my rest? Henry adds that as heaven is his throne and the earth his footstool, none of our services can profit him who made all things. — Matthew Henry

50 COMMENTARY: Hath not my hand made all these things? Henry closes the temple argument: none of our services can profit him who made all things, and next to the human nature of Christ, the broken and spiritual heart is his most valued temple. — Matthew Henry

51 COMMENTARY: Ye stiffnecked and uncircumcised in heart and ears, ye do always resist the Holy Ghost: as your fathers did, so do ye. Henry explains the break in Stephen's defence: perceiving they would not bear the argument about the temple, he broke off and by the Spirit of wisdom, courage and power sharply rebuked his persecutors, for there is in our sinful hearts that which always resists the Holy Ghost, though in God's elect this resistance is overcome when the fulness of time comes. — Matthew Henry

52 COMMENTARY: Which of the prophets have not your fathers persecuted? and they have slain them which shewed before of the coming of the Just One, of whom ye have been now the betrayers and murderers. Henry says their guilt stung them to the heart, and they sought relief in murdering their reprover instead of sorrow and supplication for mercy, and when plain truths provoke opposers of the gospel, they should be shown their guilt and danger. — Matthew Henry

53 COMMENTARY: Who have received the law by the disposition of angels, and have not kept it. Henry contrasts the manner of giving: the gospel was offered now, not by angels, but from the Holy Ghost, yet they did not embrace it, for they were resolved not to comply with God either in his law or in his gospel. — Matthew Henry

54 COMMENTARY: When they heard these things, they were cut to the heart, and they gnashed on him with their teeth. Henry's diagnosis is that their guilt stung them to the heart, and they sought relief in murdering their reprover instead of sorrow and supplication for mercy. — Matthew Henry

55 COMMENTARY: But he, being full of the Holy Ghost, looked up stedfastly into heaven, and saw the glory of God, and Jesus standing on the right hand of God. Henry says nothing is so comfortable to dying saints, or so encouraging to suffering saints, as to see Jesus at the right hand of God, and blessed be God, by faith we may see him there. — Matthew Henry

56 COMMENTARY: And said, Behold, I see the heavens opened, and the Son of man standing on the right hand of God. Henry repeats the comfort: to see Jesus at the right hand of God is the encouragement of suffering saints, and by faith believers may see him there. — Matthew Henry

59 COMMENTARY: And they stoned Stephen, calling upon God, and saying, Lord Jesus, receive my spirit. Henry notes Stephen offered up two short prayers in his dying moments, and that our Lord Jesus is God, to whom we are to seek and in whom we are to trust and comfort ourselves, living and dying. — Matthew Henry

60 COMMENTARY: And he kneeled down, and cried with a loud voice, Lord, lay not this sin to their charge. And when he had said this, he fell asleep. Henry calls this the prayer for his persecutors: though the sin was very great, yet if they would lay it to their hearts, God would not lay it to their charge, and Stephen died with as much composure as if he had been going to sleep, to awake again in the morning of the resurrection into the presence of the Lord, where is fulness of joy. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 22, 23, 24, 26, 28, 29, 31, 33, 38, 46, 57, 58 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
