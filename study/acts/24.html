Study Notes — Acts

Chapter 24

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: After five days Ananias the high priest descended with the elders, and with a certain orator named Tertullus, who informed the governor against Paul. Henry's flattery scene begins with the orator's arrival: see here the unhappiness of great men, and a great unhappiness it is, to have their services praised beyond measure, and never to be faithfully told of their faults, hereby they are hardened and encouraged in evil, like Felix, and Tertullus is the praiser. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: When he was called forth, Tertullus began to accuse him, saying, Seeing that by thee we enjoy great quietness, and that very worthy deeds are done unto this nation by thy providence. Henry says the selfish and evil passions of men urge them forward, and the graces and power of speech, too often have been used to mislead and prejudice men against the truth, and Tertullus's opening praise of Felix's quietness and worthy deeds is that speech used to prejudice. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: We accept it always, and in all places, most noble Felix, with all thankfulness. Henry's unmeasured praise is the always-and-everywhere thankfulness: great men, having their services praised beyond measure and never faithfully told of their faults, are hardened and encouraged in evil, like Felix. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: For we have found this man a pestilent fellow, and a mover of sedition among all the Jews throughout the world, and a ringleader of the sect of the Nazarenes. Henry says God's prophets were charged with being troublers of the land, and our Lord Jesus Christ, that he perverted the nation, and the very same charges were brought against Paul, and Christians should not value the applause, or be troubled at the revilings of ungodly men, who represent the vilest of the human race almost as gods, and the excellent of the earth as pestilences and movers of sedition. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Who also hath gone about to profane the temple: whom we took, and would have judged according to our law. Henry's same-charges point continues: as God's prophets were charged with troubling the land, and Christ with perverting the nation, so Paul is charged with profaning the temple, the same pattern of charge against the excellent of the earth. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: But the chief captain Lysias came upon us, and with great violence took him away out of our hands. Henry's accusers' version turns rescue into violence: the chief captain's taking of Paul, which in the last chapter was God's seasonable interposition for his servant's safety, is here charged as great violence taking him out of their hands. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: Commanding his accusers to come unto thee: by examining of whom thyself mayest take knowledge of all these things, whereof we accuse him. Henry's examination invitation is answered by Paul's defence: Tertullus invites Felix, by examining Paul, to take knowledge of all the charges, and Paul's just account of himself is that examination's answer. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Then Paul, after that the governor had beckoned unto him to speak, answered, Forasmuch as I know that thou hast been of many years a judge unto this nation, I do the more cheerfully answer for myself. Henry says Paul gives a just account of himself, which clears him from crime, and likewise shows the true reason of the violence against him. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Because that thou mayest understand, that there are yet but twelve days since I went up to Jerusalem for to worship. Henry's just account begins with the timeline: only twelve days since he went up to Jerusalem for to worship, which clears him from the sedition and profanation charges. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: They neither found me in the temple disputing with any man, neither raising up the people, neither in the synagogues, nor in the city. Henry's just account continues in the negative: they neither found him disputing in the temple, nor raising up the people in synagogues or city, which clears him from crime. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Neither can they prove the things whereof they now accuse me. Henry's just account ends in proof: Paul gives an account which clears him from crime, and the accusers cannot prove the things whereof they now accuse him. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: But this I confess unto thee, that after the way which they call heresy, so worship I the God of my fathers, believing all things which are written in the law and in the prophets. Henry says let us never be driven from any good way by its having an ill name, and it is very comfortable, in worshipping God, to look to him as the God of our fathers, and to set up no other rule of faith or practice but the Scriptures, and Paul's confession, after the way they call heresy, is that comfort. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: And have hope toward God, which they themselves also allow, that there shall be a resurrection of the dead, both of the just and unjust. Henry says this shows there will be a resurrection to a final judgment, and prophets and their doctrines were to be tried by their fruits, and Paul's hope, which his accusers themselves also allow, is that resurrection hope. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: And herein do I exercise myself, to have always a conscience void of offence toward God, and toward men. Henry says Paul's aim was to have a conscience void of offence, and his care and endeavour was to abstain from many things, and to abound in the exercises of religion at all times, both towards God and towards men. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Now after many years I came to bring alms to my nation, and offerings. Henry's just account includes the true purpose of his coming: after many years he came to bring alms to his nation, and offerings, not sedition or profanation, and the account clears him from crime. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Whereupon certain Jews from Asia found me purified in the temple, neither with multitude, nor with tumult. Henry's just account answers the temple charge directly: certain Jews from Asia found him purified in the temple, neither with multitude, nor with tumult, which is the opposite of profaning the temple or raising a tumult. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Who ought to have been here before thee, and object, if they had ought against me. Henry's proof point is the absent accusers: the Jews from Asia who found him ought to have been here before Felix to object, if they had ought against him, and their absence leaves the charges unproved. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Or else let these same here say, if they have found any evil doing in me, while I stood before the council. Henry's challenge to the present accusers is the council test: let these same here say, if they have found any evil doing in him while he stood before the council, and the just account stands on that challenge. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Except it be for this one voice, that I cried standing among them, Touching the resurrection of the dead I am called in question this day. Henry says Paul's just account likewise shows the true reason of the violence against him, and the one voice, touching the resurrection of the dead, is that true reason. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: When Felix heard these things, having more perfect knowledge of that way, he deferred them, and said, When Lysias the chief captain shall come down, I will know the uttermost of your matter. Henry's deferral begins the delay: Felix, having more perfect knowledge of that way, defers the matter until Lysias comes down, and in the affairs of our souls, delays are dangerous. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: He commanded a centurion to keep Paul, and to let him have liberty, and that he should forbid none of his acquaintance to minister or come unto him. Henry's keeping with liberty is the deferral's condition: Paul is kept, yet with liberty, and none of his acquaintance forbidden to minister or come unto him, while Felix defers. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: After certain days, when Felix came with his wife Drusilla, which was a Jewess, he sent for Paul, and heard him concerning the faith in Christ. Henry's reasoning scene begins with the hearing: Felix, with Drusilla, sends for Paul and hears him concerning the faith in Christ, and the apostle reasons concerning righteousness, temperance, and judgment to come, showing the oppressive judge and his profligate mistress their need of repentance, forgiveness, and the grace of the gospel. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: As he reasoned of righteousness, temperance, and judgment to come, Felix trembled, and answered, Go thy way for this time; when I have a convenient season, I will call for thee. Henry explains the reasoning: justice respects our conduct in life, particularly in reference to others, and temperance, the state and government of our souls, in reference to God, and he who does not exercise himself in these has neither the form nor the power of godliness, and must be overwhelmed with divine wrath in the day of God's appearing, so a prospect of the judgment to come is enough to make the stoutest heart tremble, yet Felix trembled, but that was all, for many are startled by the word of God who are not changed by it, and many fear the consequences of sin, yet continue in the love and practice of sin, and in the affairs of our souls, delays are dangerous, for Felix put off this matter to a more convenient season, but we do not find that the more convenient season ever came. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: He hoped also that money should have been given him of Paul, that he might loose him: wherefore he sent for him the oftener, and communed with him. Henry's delay has a corrupt motive: Felix hoped money should have been given him by Paul, that he might loose him, and therefore sent for him the oftener and communed with him, while the convenient season never came. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: But after two years Porcius Festus came into Felix' room: and Felix, willing to shew the Jews a pleasure, left Paul bound. Henry's delay ends without the season: after two years Festus came into Felix's room, and Felix, willing to show the Jews a pleasure, left Paul bound, for the more convenient season never came, and behold, now is the accepted time, while to-morrow may be too late. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 4, 9 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
