Study Notes — Nehemiah

Chapter 5

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: A great cry arose from the people and their wives against their brethren the Jews. Henry frames it as the tears of the oppressed brought before the governor whose office was to deliver the poor out of the hand of wicked oppressors. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Some complained that they, their sons, and their daughters were many, and must take up corn to eat and live. Henry notes the hardest pinch: the most numerous families were the most necessitous, with many mouths and no meat. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Others had mortgaged their lands, vineyards, and houses to buy corn in the dearth. Henry traces the dearth to God's chastisement for neglected dues, and notes the poor had come out of Babylon poor and spent heavily on houses before these burdens fell. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Others had borrowed money for the king's tribute upon their lands and vineyards. Henry observes the double squeeze: as corn grew dear, taxes stayed high, and the more mouths they had to maintain, the more they had to pay. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Their bitterest complaint was that their children were brought into bondage, some already, with no power to redeem them, while others held their lands and vineyards. Henry stresses the aggravation: their children were as dear as their brethren's, free-born Israelites bearing the covenant seal, yet made slaves. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Nehemiah was very angry when he heard their cry and these words. Henry notes he did not reject the complaint as unseasonable amid the wall-building, nor adjourn it, because a city could not be safe while such abuses were tolerated within it. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: He consulted with himself, then rebuked the nobles and rulers, saying they exacted usury every one of his brother, and set a great assembly against them. Henry notes his anger stayed within bounds: reproof must be given with consideration, or well-meant correction fails for want of management. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: He reasoned with them: they themselves had redeemed brethren sold to the heathen, so would they now sell their brethren to be sold to them? Henry calls this absurd: must the same trouble be spent redeeming them from their own rulers as from Babylon? They held their peace, finding nothing to answer. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: He pressed further: what they did was not good, for they ought to walk in the fear of God, especially because of the reproach of the heathen enemies. Henry notes nothing exposes religion more to its enemies' reproach than the worldliness and hard-heartedness of its professors. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Nehemiah added his own example: he, his brethren, and his servants might have exacted money and corn, but he urged them to leave off this usury. Henry observes that those who insist rigorously on their own rights will with ill grace persuade others to recede from theirs. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: He pressed them to restore the people's lands, vineyards, oliveyards, and houses, and the hundredth part of the money, corn, wine, and oil they had exacted. Henry notes the interest ran at twelve percent, a hundredth part every month, and that he urged them to give up mortgages, remit interest, and give time for the principal. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: They promised to restore and require nothing, and Nehemiah called the priests to take their oath to perform it. Henry notes he bound them while their convictions were strong, so that resolution would be kept, not merely felt. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Nehemiah shook his lap, saying God would likewise shake out and empty every man who did not perform this promise, and the congregation said Amen and praised the LORD. Henry notes they promised with joy, and better still, they did according to the promise, unlike ancestors who re-enslaved those they had freed. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: From the day he was appointed governor, in the twentieth year of Artaxerxes until the thirty-second, twelve years, neither Nehemiah nor his brethren ate the bread of the governor. Henry notes he recorded this not in pride but as an inducement to successors and magistrates to be tender of the people's ease. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Former governors had been chargeable, taking bread and wine besides forty shekels of silver, while their servants squeezed the people. Henry notes the contrast: Nehemiah did not do so, because of the fear of God, which restrained him from oppression purely for conscience' sake. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: He continued in the work of the wall, bought no land, and his servants were all gathered to the work. Henry observes he got nothing from office except the satisfaction of doing good, aiming not to feather his own nest. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: At his table sat a hundred and fifty Jews and rulers, besides those who came from the heathen round about. Henry notes he kept a constantly good table for honourable guests, using his place to do good rather than to enrich himself. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: The daily provision was an ox, six choice sheep, and fowls, with wine every ten days, yet he did not require the governor's bread because the bondage was heavy on the people. Henry's rule: in our demands we must consider not only their justice but the ability of those on whom we make them. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: He closed with prayer: "Think upon me, my God, for good, according to all that I have done for this people." Henry notes he mentioned his deeds to God not as merit owed, but because he looked for no recompense from men; if God but thought on him for good, he had enough. — Matthew Henry
