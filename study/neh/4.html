Study Notes — Nehemiah

Chapter 4

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: When Sanballat heard the wall was being built, he was very angry and greatly indignant, mocking the Jews. Henry notes the church's strength and safety are the grief of its enemies, who had hoped an unwalled Jerusalem could be swallowed up at pleasure. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Sanballat scorned the workmen before his brethren and Samaria's army: "these feeble Jews" — would they revive stones from rubbish heaps, offer sacrifice, and finish in a day? Henry observes the mockery backfired, since enemies who despised the work as foolish did not obstruct it until it was too late. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Tobiah added his jest: even if a fox went up on their stone wall, he would break it down. Henry notes profane scoffers sharpen one another, and many a good work has been looked on with contempt by proud scorners. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Instead of answering folly with folly, Nehemiah prayed: "Hear, O our God; for we are despised," asking God to turn their reproach on their own heads and give them as a prey in captivity. Henry notes God's people may appeal to a God who hears every slight put upon them. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: He further prayed that their iniquity not be covered nor their sin blotted out, since they had provoked God before the builders. Henry cautions that this was spoken by a spirit of prophecy rather than as a pattern for us, who are taught by Christ to pray for persecutors. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Despite the scoffs, the wall was joined to half its height, because the people had a mind to work. Henry's lesson: good work goes on well when people have a mind to it, and enemies' reproaches should quicken us to duty rather than drive us from it. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: When Sanballat, Tobiah, the Arabians, Ammonites, and Ashdodites heard the breaches were being stopped, they were very wroth. Henry notes they were angry both at the Jews for pushing the work and at themselves for being slow to oppose it. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: They conspired together to come and fight against Jerusalem and hinder the work. Henry stresses their unanimity, secrecy, and cruelty: men of different interests became one in opposing God's work, planning murder to make the building cease. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Nehemiah's answer was prayer joined with watchfulness: "we made our prayer unto our God, and set a watch against them day and night." Henry's rule is watch and pray together: prayer without watchfulness is slothful, watchfulness without prayer is proud. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Judah said the bearers' strength was decayed, there was much rubbish, and they were not able to build the wall. Henry notes that active leaders often have as much trouble grappling with their friends' fears as with their enemies' terrors. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: The adversaries plotted in secret: the builders would neither know nor see until their enemies came among them, slew them, and caused the work to cease. Henry observes they promised themselves success only through surprise. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Jews dwelling near the enemies came and warned Nehemiah ten times over, from every side, that attack was coming. Henry reads the repeated, breathless report as God's way of bringing hidden devices to light, even through feeble neighbours who served as spies. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Nehemiah therefore set the people in the lower places behind the wall and in higher places upon it, arranged by families, with swords, spears, and bows. Henry notes the family order: mutual relation would engage them to mutual assistance. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Seeing the nobles and people in consternation, he urged them not to be afraid but to remember the Lord, great and terrible, and to fight for their brethren, sons, daughters, wives, and houses. Henry's point: the reigning fear of God is the best antidote to the ensnaring fear of man. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: When the enemies heard their design was known, God brought their counsel to nought, and everyone returned to his work. Henry notes they returned with more cheerfulness, seeing plainly that God owned them in the doing of it. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: Still, half the servants worked while the other half held spears, shields, bows, and coats of mail, with rulers behind the house of Judah. Henry notes they kept their arms ready because the enemy was restless, and one failed design would be followed by another. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: The builders worked with one hand and held a weapon with the other, and the burden-bearers likewise laboured laden. Henry explains this cannot be literal, since work needs both hands; it means they were equally employed in building and in readiness to fight. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Every builder had his sword girded by his side as he built, and the trumpeter stood beside Nehemiah. Henry compares the sword to the word of God, the sword of the Spirit, which Christians should keep always at hand in labour and in conflict. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Nehemiah told the nobles, rulers, and people that the work was great and large, with builders separated far apart upon the wall. Henry notes that as workmen they had to be dispersed, but as soldiers they had to come together in close order at the alarm. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Wherever the trumpet sounded, they were to resort to Nehemiah there, for their God would fight for them. Henry stresses the assurance given with the alarm: unity against a common foe, under a God who fights for his people. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: So they laboured from morning until the stars appeared, half of them holding spears. Henry notes the spears were held up day and night, from twilight to twilight, as a terror to the enemy and a sign of constant guard. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: Nehemiah ordered the people and their servants to lodge within Jerusalem, so that at night they could guard and by day labour. Henry's maxim: a city's strength lies more in its hands than in its walls. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: Neither Nehemiah, his brethren, his servants, nor the guard put off their clothes, except for washing. Henry reads this as total intentness: they could not find time to dress and undress, resolving to be at all times ready for service. — Matthew Henry
