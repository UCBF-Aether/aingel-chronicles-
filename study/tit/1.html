Study Notes — Titus

Chapter 1

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Paul presents himself as God's servant and Christ's apostle, whose work exists to advance the faith of God's elect and a knowledge of truth that leads to godliness. JFB notes the apostleship is described by its purpose: faith and godly knowledge, not self-importance. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: That faith rests on the hope of eternal life, promised by God who cannot lie and purposed before time began. The promise was hidden in God's eternal purpose and is now made known in the gospel. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: In God's own appointed seasons he made his word known through preaching, a trust committed to Paul by God's command. Henry stresses that faith comes by hearing this preached word. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Titus is Paul's genuine son in the faith they share in common, a faith embracing Gentile and Jew alike. The greeting of grace, mercy, and peace traces every blessing to God's free favour and its fruit in pardon and peace. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Titus was left in Crete to finish unfinished work: setting things in order and appointing elders in every city, as Paul had directed. JFB notes elder and bishop name the same office, one of dignity, the other of oversight of the flock. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: An elder must be blameless, the husband of one wife, with believing children who are not accused of riot or disobedience. JFB stresses reputation: one who could not lead his own household in faith could hardly lead others. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: A bishop, as God's steward over God's household, must be blameless, and therefore not self-willed, quick-tempered, given to wine, violent, or greedy for gain. The greater the Master, JFB notes, the greater the character required in his servant. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Positively, he must love hospitality and love what is good, and be sober, just, holy, and self-controlled. Henry describes these as the spirit and practice fitting one who must be an example of good works. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: He must hold firmly to the faithful word he was taught, so that he can both encourage with sound doctrine and refute those who contradict it. Sound teaching, not novelty, is the elder's chief weapon. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Many in Crete were unruly, empty talkers and deceivers, especially from the circumcision party. Their talk ran against the faithful word of verse 9, and corrupted Judaism was the source of the evil. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Their mouths must be stopped, because they overturn whole households by teaching what they ought not, for shameful gain. Henry calls this serving a worldly interest under a pretence of religion. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Paul quotes a Cretan prophet of their own, Epimenides, who called Cretans always liars, evil beasts, and slow bellies. The witness stings precisely because it comes from one of themselves. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: This testimony is true, so Titus must rebuke them sharply, not from anger but so that they may become sound in the faith. Henry adds that even the sharpest reproof must aim at the good of the reproved. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Soundness means not giving heed to Jewish fables and human commandments that turn people away from the truth. Such teaching was profitless and tended away from godliness, not toward it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: To the pure all things are pure, because purity lies not in outward things themselves but in the heart of the user. To the defiled and unbelieving, by contrast, nothing is pure, for even mind and conscience are defiled. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: They profess to know God but deny him by their works, being detestable, disobedient, and unfit for any good work. Henry warns against being quick to fix this charge on others while careless that it might apply to ourselves. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
