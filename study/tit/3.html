Study Notes — Titus

Chapter 3

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Believers are to be reminded to submit to rulers and authorities, obey magistrates, and be ready for every good work. Spiritual privilege does not cancel civil duty; it confirms it, and Cretan proneness to riot made the reminder necessary. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: They must speak evil of no one, avoid quarrelling, and be gentle, showing all meekness to all people. Such conduct toward all follows from the universality of God's grace toward all. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: We too were once foolish, disobedient, deceived, enslaved to various lusts, living in malice and envy, hateful and hating one another. Remembering our own past sins should make us lenient toward others, for grace alone remedied our folly. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: But then the kindness and love of God our Saviour toward mankind appeared. Salvation is owed to God's benign goodness, not to any merit in us that would let us despise others. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: He saved us not because of righteous works we had done, but according to his mercy, by the washing of regeneration and renewing of the Holy Spirit. Henry stresses that works are found in the saved but are not among the causes of salvation. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: That Spirit he poured out on us richly through Jesus Christ our Saviour, the channel and Mediator of the gift. The pouring out covers grace received before, in, and after baptism. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: The purpose was that, being justified by his grace, we should become heirs according to the hope of eternal life. Justification is free forgiveness through Christ's righteousness received by faith, not anything merited by the sinner. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: This is a faithful saying, and Titus must affirm it constantly, so that believers will be careful to maintain good works, which are good and profitable to people. Declared grace presses the necessity of good works rather than excusing their absence. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Foolish questions, genealogies, contentions, and strivings about the law must be avoided, for they are unprofitable and vain. They yield no moral fruit and distract from the practical duties grace teaches. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: A divisive, self-willed heretic is to be rejected after a first and second admonition. Rejection here means having nothing more to do with him in admonition or intercourse, not yet formal excommunication. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Such a person is perverted and sins wilfully against knowledge, being condemned by himself. After repeated warning he cannot claim that no one told him better. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: When Paul sends Artemas or Tychicus to take Titus's place in Crete, Titus is to come diligently to Paul at Nicopolis, where Paul has determined to winter. Tychicus was well qualified, having twice carried Paul's letters from Rome. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Titus is to help Zenas the lawyer and Apollos forward on their journey diligently, so that nothing is lacking to them. Supplying travellers' needs was a concrete form of the hospitality and good works urged throughout the letter. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: And our people also must learn to maintain good works for necessary uses, so that they are not unfruitful. Christianity obliges believers to honest labour and to meeting the real needs of fellow workers. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: All with Paul salute Titus, who is to greet those who love them in the faith, and grace is wished to them all. Grace, Henry notes, is the chief thing to be wished and prayed for, being all good. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
