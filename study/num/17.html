Study Notes — Numbers

Chapter 17

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

2 COMMENTARY: Speak to Israel, and take of every one of them a rod according to the house of their fathers, of all their princes, twelve rods, writing every man's name upon his rod. JFB explains the princes were chosen because, as oldest sons and tribal heads, they could advance the best claims to a shared priesthood, and the name-inscription was a practice borrowed from the Egyptians, giving every tribe a direct interest in the settlement. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Write Aaron's name upon the rod of Levi, for one rod shall be for the head of the house of their fathers. JFB notes Aaron's name rather than Levi's was used, since Levi's name would have opened controversy among the Levites, and one rod for Aaron determined him as head of the tribe and of the priestly branch within it. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Lay them up in the tabernacle of the congregation before the testimony, where I will meet with you. JFB notes the rods lay close to the ark, where a divine token was promised that would for all time terminate the dispute about the priesthood. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: It shall come to pass that the man's rod whom I shall choose shall blossom, and I will make to cease from me the murmurings of Israel, whereby they murmur against you. Henry calls this an instance of grace: having worked divers miracles to punish sin, God would work one more to prevent it. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Moses spoke to Israel, and every prince gave him a rod apiece, for each prince one, twelve rods, and the rod of Aaron was among their rods. JFB notes the rods were dry sticks, probably old wands transmitted from head to head of each family, and Aaron's was either one of the twelve or, as many suppose, a thirteenth in the midst. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Moses laid up the rods before the Lord in the tabernacle of witness. Moses did not object that the matter was already settled, Henry notes, nor undertake to determine it himself, but left the case before the Lord. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: On the morrow Moses went into the tabernacle of witness, and behold, the rod of Aaron for the house of Levi was budded, and brought forth buds, and bloomed blossoms, and yielded almonds. JFB notes, from Josephus, that it was an almond stick bearing fruit in three stages at once, and Henry adds that while all other rods remained as they were, Aaron's became a living branch, with fruitfulness as the best evidence of a divine call. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Moses brought out all the rods from before the Lord to all Israel, and they looked upon them, and took every man his rod. The inspection was public: every man saw his own dry rod beside Aaron's living one, and took it back as his own witness to the decision. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Bring Aaron's rod again before the testimony, to be kept for a token against the rebels, to quite take away their murmurings from me, that they die not. JFB warns that if, after all admonitions, judgments and miracles, the people still rebelled, they would certainly pay the penalty by death, and Henry adds God's design in all his providences and memorials is to take away sin. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Moses did so: as the Lord commanded him, so did he. The obedience is recorded without comment, closing the controversy exactly as God ordered it: the rod returned to the testimony, and the case was left there. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Israel spoke to Moses, saying, behold, we die, we perish, we all perish! JFB reads this as an exclamation of fear, from remembrance of former judgments and apprehension of future relapses into murmuring, while Henry hears a repining people quarrelling with judgments their own pride and obstinacy had brought on themselves. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Whosoever comes anything near to the tabernacle of the Lord shall die: shall we be consumed with dying? JFB explains nearer than he ought to be, an error into which many may fall, and asks whether stern justice overtakes every slight offence, though some regard the cry as a symptom of new discontent rather than reverential submission. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

No note for verse(s) 1 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
