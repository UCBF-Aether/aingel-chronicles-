Study Notes — Numbers

Chapter 6

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

2 COMMENTARY: When a man or woman vows the vow of a Nazarite, to separate themselves to the Lord, the word Nazarite itself signifies separation. Henry notes the vow was in general voluntary, for a limited time, and open to any person who pleased, though some, like Samson and John the Baptist, were appointed Nazarites from before birth for all their days. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: The Nazarite separated himself from wine and strong drink, drinking no vinegar of wine or of strong drink, nor any liquor of grapes. Henry's rule is that those who separate themselves to God must not gratify the desires of the body but keep it under, and Christians should be very moderate in wine, lest its love get the mastery. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: All the days of his separation he ate nothing made of the vine, from kernel to husk. Henry draws the wider lesson: the utmost care to avoid sin, and all that borders on it, leads to it, or may tempt us, is taught by this total abstinence from the vine's fruit. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: No razor came upon his head all the days of his separation, until the days were fulfilled. Henry notes the uncut hair was the Nazarite's mark, as with Samson, signifying neglect of the body and of its ease and ornament for God's sake. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: All the days he separated himself to the Lord he came at no dead body. The Nazarite kept his conscience pure from dead works and touched no unclean thing, since contact with death broke the consecration he lived under. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: He might not make himself unclean even for father, mother, brother or sister at their death, because his consecration was upon his head. Henry's principle is that devotion to God moderates even natural affections: the vow to the Lord outranked the nearest family claim. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: All the days of his separation he was holy to the Lord. Henry insists this was the meaning of all the outward observances, and without inward holiness they were of no account. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: If any man died very suddenly beside him, defiling the head of his consecration, he shaved his head on the day of his cleansing, the seventh day. Henry distinguishes this case: no sacrifice was appointed for wilful breach of the vow, but those who did not sin wilfully were relieved, and the defiled Nazarite was restored by cleansing. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: On the eighth day he brought two turtles or two young pigeons to the priest at the tabernacle door. The eighth day, after a full week of cleansing, marked his return to the sanctuary he had been cut off from by defilement. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: The priest offered one bird for a sin offering and the other for a burnt offering, making atonement for him, and he hallowed his head that same day. Atonement came first, and only then was the consecrated head hallowed anew, since the old consecration had been broken. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: He consecrated to the Lord the days of his separation anew and brought a lamb of the first year for a trespass offering, while the days before were lost. Henry's rule is plain: the interrupted vow did not resume where it stopped, but began again, and the loss of the former days was part of the cost of defilement. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: This is the law of the Nazarite when the days of his separation are fulfilled: he was brought to the door of the tabernacle. The completion was a public act at the sanctuary door, not a private decision that the vow was over. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: He offered to the Lord a he lamb of the first year without blemish for a burnt offering, a ewe lamb for a sin offering, and a ram without blemish for peace offerings. The threefold offering at completion covered every side of the vow: dedication, sin, and fellowship. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: With the offerings came a basket of unleavened bread, cakes of fine flour mingled with oil and wafers anointed with oil, with their meat and drink offerings. The unleavened bread kept the completion feast free from the leaven of corruption, matching the purity of the separation itself. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: The priest brought them before the Lord and offered his sin offering and his burnt offering. The priest, not the Nazarite, presented the offerings, since acceptance with God came through the appointed mediator, not through the vow itself. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: The priest offered the ram for a peace offering to the Lord, with the basket of unleavened bread, and the priest's own meat and drink offerings. The peace offering closed the vow in fellowship: after separation, the Nazarite shared a meal of peace before God. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: The Nazarite shaved the head of his separation at the tabernacle door and put the hair in the fire under the peace offering. The hair, the visible mark of the whole vow, was given up at the end, consumed with the offering that sealed his return to ordinary life. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: The priest took the sodden shoulder of the ram, one unleavened cake and one wafer, and put them on the Nazarite's hands after he had shaved. The portion placed in his own hands showed the completed vow was now his to carry back into common life as a finished service. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: The priest waved them as a wave offering before the Lord, and they were holy for the priest, with the wave breast and heave shoulder. The waved portion belonged to the priest, a reminder that even a voluntary vow ended in provision for God's ministers. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: This is the law of the Nazarite who vowed, and of his offering for his separation, besides whatever else his hand could get, according to the vow he vowed. Henry's closing lesson is to vow and pay to the Lord our God, for he has no pleasure in fools who vow and do not perform. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: Aaron and his sons were told: on this wise you shall bless the children of Israel, saying to them. The blessing was given in a set form, not invented by the priests, so that the people heard God's own words of blessing from his appointed mouths. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: The Lord bless you and keep you: to be under the almighty protection of God our Saviour is the first part of the blessing. Henry calls protection and favour the substance of this blessing and the sum total of all blessings, beside which worldly joys are not worthy to be mentioned. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: The Lord make his face shine upon you and be gracious unto you. Henry pictures God's favour as the smile of a loving Father, or the cheering beams of the sun, mercifully forgiving sins, supplying wants, and consoling the heart. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: The Lord lift up his countenance upon you and give you peace. The lifted countenance is God's attentive, favourable regard, and its gift is peace: not merely the absence of trouble, but the settled favour that prepares us by grace for eternal glory. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: They shall put my name upon the children of Israel, and I will bless them. The priests put the name on the people, but God himself blesses: Henry notes the name Jehovah is three times repeated, and Christians read in it the blessing from the grace of Christ, the love of the Father, and the communion of the Holy Ghost. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 1, 22 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
