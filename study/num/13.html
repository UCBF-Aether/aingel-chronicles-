Study Notes — Numbers

Chapter 13

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

2 COMMENTARY: Moses was told to send men to search the land of Canaan, which God gave to Israel. JFB notes from Deuteronomy that the proposal came from the people who petitioned for it, and God granted it at once as a trial and a punishment of their distrust, while Henry adds they had a better opinion of their own policy than of God's wisdom. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Moses sent them from the wilderness of Paran by the Lord's commandment, all rulers of Israel. JFB clarifies these were heads and leading men, though not the first-rank princes named in chapter 10, a distinction that matters when their report later carries the people's trust. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Of Judah, Caleb son of Jephunneh was sent. Caleb is named here among the twelve, but Henry already singles him out with Joshua as the only two who possessed the strong faith from which courage in such an undertaking can spring. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: These are the names of the men Moses sent to spy out the land, and Moses called Oshea son of Nun, Jehoshua. JFB explains Oshea means a desire of salvation, while Jehoshua, by prefixing God's name, means divinely appointed, head of salvation, Saviour, the same as Jesus. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Moses sent them to spy out Canaan, saying, get up southward and go up into the mountain, and be of good courage. JFB identifies the route over Mount Seir, northeast across Paran into the southern promised land, and Henry stresses the charge: courage in a great undertaking and a great trust can only spring from strong faith. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: They were to see the land, what it was, and the people dwelling in it, whether strong or weak, few or many. The commission asked for honest observation, not for a verdict on whether God could give the land, a distinction the ten spies would soon forget. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: They were to see what land the people dwelt in, whether good or bad, and what cities, whether in tents or strong holds. The questions about dwellings measured how the land could be taken and held, information for a campaign, not an excuse for retreat. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: They were to see whether the land was fat or lean, whether wood was in it, and to bring of its fruit, for the time was the time of the firstripe grapes. JFB dates the season to August for first clusters, with second in September and third in October, and the forty days' absence fixes their grapes to the second gathering. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: They went up and searched the land from the wilderness of Zin to Rehob, as men come to Hamath. JFB traces the route south to north along the Jordan, with the return by the western border through Sidonian and Philistine territory, and describes Zin as a long sandy plain continuing the Jordan valley from the Dead Sea to the Gulf of Akaba. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: They ascended by the south and came to Hebron, where Ahiman, Sheshai and Talmai, children of Anak, were. JFB describes Hebron, in the heart of Judah's mountains, as one of the oldest cities in the world, its Anakite chiefs ruling distinct townships, and notes its building seven years before Zoan in Egypt. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: They came to the brook of Eshcol and cut down a branch with one cluster of grapes, borne between two on a staff, with pomegranates and figs. JFB notes the grapes of that valley are still magnificent, a cluster sometimes weighing ten or twelve pounds, and the two-man carrying preserved the specimen entire for a people familiar only with Egypt's scanty vines. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: The place was called the brook Eshcol, because of the cluster of grapes the children of Israel cut down there. JFB renders Eshcol as the torrent of the cluster, a little southwest of Hebron, whose sloping hills are still covered with vineyards matching its ancient celebrity. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: They returned from searching the land after forty days. Henry wonders that Israel waited forty days for the spies when ready to enter under every assurance of success, and reads the waiting itself as distrust of God's power and promise. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: They came to Moses, Aaron and all the congregation in the wilderness of Paran, to Kadesh, and brought back word and showed them the land's fruit. JFB notes Kadesh was an important encampment, though its exact site is uncertain, supposed to be Ain-el-Weibeh or Petra. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: They told him, we came to the land you sent us to, and surely it flows with milk and honey, and this is its fruit. JFB observes the report was given publicly and artfully arranged to begin with commendations of the country's fertility, so that their subsequent slanders might more readily receive credit. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Nevertheless the people are strong that dwell in the land, and the cities are walled and very great, and we saw the children of Anak there. Henry's diagnosis is that though they found the land as good as God said, they would not believe it as sure as he said, but despaired of having it though Eternal Truth had engaged it. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: The Amalekites dwell in the south, the Hittites, Jebusites and Amorites in the mountains, and the Canaanites by the sea and by Jordan. JFB maps them: Amalek between the Dead and Red Seas skirting Canaan, the mountain peoples in southern Palestine, and the Canaanite remnant divided between the Jordan side and the Mediterranean coast. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: Caleb stilled the people before Moses and said, let us go up at once and possess it, for we are well able to overcome it. Henry notes Caleb does not say, let us go up and conquer it, but let us go and possess it: difficulties in the way of salvation dwindle and vanish before a lively, active faith in God's power and promise. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: But the men who went up with him said, we are not able to go up against the people, for they are stronger than we. Henry's rule is that unbelief overlooks God's promises and power, magnifies every danger and difficulty, and fills the heart with discouragement. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: They brought up an evil report of the land, saying it eats up its inhabitants, and all the people we saw in it are men of great stature. JFB calls this a false and exaggerated report, taking what was true of a few as descriptive of all, and explains the eating-up charge as an unhealthy climate, with Jewish writers noting many funerals from plague or hornet in God's providence at that time. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: There we saw the giants, sons of Anak, who come of the giants, and we were in our own sight as grasshoppers, and so we were in their sight. JFB traces Anak to Arba, a great man whose name suggests a splendid collar, and reads the Anakim as a distinguished family or select body of warriors chosen for size, with grasshoppers as an Oriental exaggeration by treacherous spies. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

No note for verse(s) 1, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
