Study Notes — Numbers

Chapter 1

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Thirteen months had passed since the exodus, most of them camped at Sinai, and the tabernacle had just been set up. JFB notes the census came now because Israel, newly formed as a nation under God as King, had to be put in good order before marching toward the promised land. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: The census was taken by tribe, family and household, so that an exact register was formed, each tribe's strength known, and the order of camp and march settled. Henry adds that numbering showed God's faithfulness in increasing Jacob's seed, and trained the people for the wars and the division of Canaan ahead. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Only men twenty years old and upward, able to go to war, were counted, and they were numbered by their armies or companies. JFB explains that from the wilderness onward Israel marched in four great divisions rather than the five companies of the departure from Egypt. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: One man from each tribe, the head of his father's house, was to stand with Moses and Aaron. JFB notes the tribal head was a hereditary dignity, like the chiefs of nomad tribes, with lesser hereditary heads under him among the branches of the tribe. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Each prince is named with his father's name, after the clan custom by which a people are called the sons of their ancestor. JFB compares this to the Macs of Scotland, the Aps of Wales, and the O's and Fitzes of Ireland. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: These princes were the renowned, literally the called, of the congregation, summoned by name. JFB notes they entered on the survey the very day the order was given. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Moses and Aaron took the men expressly named for the work, acting at once on the command. The naming matters: the census was entrusted to known, responsible heads, not to anonymous officials. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: The people declared their pedigrees and were counted by their polls, that is, individually, one by one. Henry stresses the care of the record: every man was known by family and name, not merely estimated in bulk. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Moses numbered them exactly as the Lord commanded, in the wilderness of Sinai. JFB contrasts this with David's later census: numbering was not sinful in itself when done by divine appointment, but David incurred guilt by doing it without God's authority. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: The tribal register begins with Reuben, Israel's eldest son, and JFB observes from it that Judah appears the most numerous, as Jacob had assigned it pre-eminence, while Ephraim outnumbered Manasseh, as predicted. The relative increase of every tribe is traced to God's special blessing, working through ordinary causes such as the fruitfulness of each tribe's locality. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

44 COMMENTARY: Moses and Aaron, with the twelve princes, numbered all Israel together. Henry calls this the sum total, the point at which the whole strength of the people could be seen at once. — Matthew Henry

45 COMMENTARY: The count covered every man of Israel from twenty years upward by his father's house. JFB marvels at the increase from about seventy-five persons who went down to Egypt some two hundred and fifteen years before, despite great privation and hardship. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

46 COMMENTARY: The total was six hundred and three thousand five hundred and fifty fighting men. Henry asks how much was required to maintain such a host in the wilderness, and answers that God provided for them every day, so that his past faithfulness gives courage for promises yet unfulfilled. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

47 COMMENTARY: The Levites were not numbered among the fighting men, but kept a register of their own. JFB explains that the priestly office was customarily exempt from military service, and in Israel exempt by God's express authority, so the census itself separated Levi from those fit for war. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

49 COMMENTARY: Levi was deliberately left out of the military sum, not overlooked. Henry reads this as honour, not slight: singular services are recompensed by singular honours, and Levi's service was the tabernacle itself. — Matthew Henry

50 COMMENTARY: The Levites were appointed over the tabernacle of testimony and all its vessels. Henry notes it was to Levi's honour that the care of the tabernacle and its treasures, in camp and on march, was committed to them alone. — Matthew Henry

51 COMMENTARY: When the tabernacle moved, the Levites took it down and set it up, and no stranger might come near on pain of death. JFB stresses how exclusive this custody was: not even an Israelite of another tribe might approach the holy things. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

52 COMMENTARY: Every other Israelite pitched by his own camp and standard, in his appointed place around the sanctuary. The arrangement kept the whole host ordered, with Levi's camp forming the inner ring about the tabernacle. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

53 COMMENTARY: The Levites pitched round about the tabernacle so that no wrath would fall on the congregation. Henry's rule is that great care must be taken to prevent sin, for preventing sin is preventing wrath, and holiness about holy things guards the whole people. — Matthew Henry

54 COMMENTARY: Israel did according to all the Lord commanded Moses. Henry closes with the practical point: let every believer seek to do what the Lord has commanded, as Israel here did in the ordering of the camp. — Matthew Henry
