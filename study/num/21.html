Study Notes — Numbers

Chapter 21

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: When king Arad the Canaanite, who dwelt in the south, heard Israel came by the way of the spies, he fought against Israel and took some prisoners. JFB identifies Arad as an ancient town on Palestine's southernmost border near Kadesh, and notes the discomfiture was permitted to teach Israel to expect Canaan's conquest not from their own wisdom and valour, but solely from God's favour and help. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Israel vowed a vow to the Lord: if you will indeed deliver this people into my hand, then I will utterly destroy their cities. JFB notes, made to feel their weakness, they implored Heaven's aid and devoted the king's cities to future destruction, and this vow gave the place the name Hormah, slaughter and destruction, though it was not accomplished till after the Jordan. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: The Lord hearkened to Israel's voice, delivered up the Canaanites, and they utterly destroyed them and their cities, and he called the name of the place Hormah. The vow and its fulfilment are paired: Israel learned to look thoroughly to the Lord before the march round Edom even began, as Henry notes. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: They journeyed from mount Hor by the way of the Red Sea, to compass the land of Edom, and the soul of the people was much discouraged because of the way. JFB traces the retrograde route through the Arabah to Elath and up through the mountains to Edom's eastern desert, and explains the discouragement: disappointment at being so near the land without entering it, vexation at Edom's refusal, and dread of a long circuit through the worst sandy desert. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: The people spoke against God and against Moses: wherefore have you brought us up out of Egypt to die in the wilderness? For there is no bread, neither is there any water, and our soul loathes this light bread. JFB notes light bread means bread without substance, a calumny refuted by forty years of toilsome journeys on its strength, while Henry asks what will please those whom manna will not please, since it is the bread of life to those who feed on it by faith. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: The Lord sent fiery serpents among the people, and they bit the people, and much people of Israel died. JFB locates the plague near the head of the Gulf of Akaba, greatly infested with venomous reptiles, and explains fiery as either their bright vivid colour or the violent inflammation their bite occasioned. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Therefore the people came to Moses, saying, we have sinned, for we have spoken against the Lord and against you: pray to the Lord that he take away the serpents from us, and Moses prayed for the people. JFB notes the severity and mortality brought them to a sense of sin, and Henry fears they would not have owned the sin if they had not felt the smart, but they relented under the rod. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: The Lord said to Moses, make a fiery serpent, and set it upon a pole, and it shall come to pass that everyone bitten, when he looks upon it, shall live. JFB explains the method was designed to show the cure was God's power and grace, not nature or art, and to be a type of faith in Christ healing all who look to him for their sins. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Moses made a serpent of brass, put it upon a pole, and it came to pass that if a serpent had bitten any man, when he beheld the serpent of brass, he lived. Henry notes the Jews themselves said it was not the sight of the serpent that cured, but in looking up to it they looked up to God as the Lord who healed them, and whosoever looked, however desperate his case, feeble his sight, or distant his place, was certainly and perfectly cured. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: The children of Israel set forward and pitched in Oboth. The march resumed along the eastern frontier of Edom, encamping in various stations, with the serpent's healing behind them and the land ahead. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: They journeyed from Oboth and pitched at Ije-abarim, in the wilderness before Moab, toward the sunrising. The stations trace the circuit round Edom's east side, turning the host northward toward Moab's border. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: From thence they removed and pitched in the valley of Zared. JFB describes Zared as a woody brook-valley, a torrent rising east of Moab and flowing west into the Dead Sea, with Ije-abarim supposed to be its ford. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: From thence they removed and pitched on the other side of Arnon, which is in the wilderness that comes out of the coasts of the Amorites, for Arnon is the border of Moab, between Moab and the Amorites. JFB identifies Arnon, now El-Mojib, as a deep, broad, rapid stream dividing the Moabites' and Amorites' dominions. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Wherefore it is said in the book of the wars of the Lord, what he did in the Red Sea, and in the brooks of Arnon. JFB notes a fragment is quoted here from a poem or history of Israel's wars, principally to decide the position of Arnon, and Henry adds that in every stage of life we should distinctly remember what God wrought at such a time and in such a place. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: And at the stream of the brooks that goes down to the dwelling of Ar, and lies upon the border of Moab. JFB identifies Ar as the capital of Moab, the point where the Arnon brooks marked the kingdom's edge. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: And from thence they went to Beer, that is the well whereof the Lord spoke to Moses, gather the people together, and I will give them water. JFB notes Beer means a well, its name probably given afterwards, since it is not mentioned in the itinerary of chapter 33. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Then Israel sang this song, Spring up, O well, sing you unto it. JFB notes the little song suits the wants of travelling caravans in the East, where water is an occasion both of prayer and thanksgiving, and Henry adds they received the water with joy and thankfulness, which made the mercy doubly sweet. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: The princes dug the well, the nobles of the people dug it, by the direction of the lawgiver, with their staves. JFB notes the princes used only their official rods, not spades, suggesting a well concealed by brushwood or sand, as many Idumean wells still are, discovered by God's special interposition, and Henry adds God promised water, but they must open the ground: favours are expected in the use of means within our power, though the power is only God's. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Israel sent messengers to Sihon king of the Amorites, saying, let me pass through your land. JFB notes the rejection of this respectful and pacific message was resented, Sihon was discomfited in battle, and Israel obtained the whole Amorite dominions by right of conquest. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Let me pass through your land: we will not turn into the fields or vineyards, neither will we drink of the waters of the well, but we will go along by the king's high way, until we are past your borders. The terms repeated Edom's offer exactly: highway only, no fields, no wells, no deviation until the border was passed. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Sihon would not suffer Israel to pass through his border, but gathered all his people together, went out against Israel into the wilderness, and fought against Israel at Jahaz. Henry notes Sihon went with his forces against Israel out of his own borders without provocation, and so ran upon his own ruin, as God's church's enemies often perish by the counsels they think most wisely taken. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Israel smote him with the edge of the sword, and possessed his land from Arnon to Jabbok, even to the children of Ammon, for the border of Ammon was strong. JFB notes Arnon and Jabbok, now the Zurka, formed the southern and northern boundaries of Sihon's usurped territory, and Ammon's strong border explains why his invasion had stopped there. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Israel took all these cities, and dwelt in all the cities of the Amorites, in Heshbon and in all its villages. JFB notes this followed the extermination of inhabitants previously doomed, so that dwelling in the cities was possession after judgment, not mere occupation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: For Heshbon was the city of Sihon king of the Amorites, who had fought against the former king of Moab and taken all his land out of his hand, even to Arnon. JFB notes Heshbon lay sixteen miles north of the Arnon, and its ruins show it was a large city, the capital from which Sihon had already conquered Moab's north. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Wherefore they that speak in proverbs say, Come into Heshbon, let the city of Sihon be built and prepared. JFB notes this is an extract from an Amorite song exultingly anticipating an extension of their conquests to Arnon, quoted through verse 28, with the following verses as Israel's exposure of the usurpers' impotence. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: For there is a fire gone out of Heshbon, a flame from the city of Sihon: it has consumed Ar of Moab, and the lords of the high places of Arnon. The Amorite boast recalled Sihon's own fire against Moab, a conquest song now turned, by quotation, into evidence against its singers. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Woe to you, Moab! You are undone, O people of Chemosh: he has given his sons that escaped, and his daughters, into captivity to Sihon king of the Amorites. JFB identifies Chemosh as the Moabite idol, and reads the line as their god surrendering his worshippers to Sihon's victorious arms. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: We have shot at them: Heshbon is perished even to Dibon, and we have laid them waste even to Nophah, which reaches to Medeba. These verses are Israel's answer strain: the cities the Amorite song boasted of are now perished, and the usurpers' empire is laid waste by the very people they displaced. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: Thus Israel dwelt in the land of the Amorites. The summary closes the conquest: Israel did not merely pass through Sihon's land but dwelt in it, from Arnon to Jabbok, as its new possessors. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: Moses sent to spy out Jaazer, and they took its villages and drove out the Amorites that were there. The advance continued by reconnaissance: Jaazer, like Heshbon, was taken village by village before the host moved on. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: They turned and went up by the way of Bashan, and Og king of Bashan went out against them, he and all his people, to the battle at Edrei. JFB describes Bashan, from its rich soil, as the hilly region east of Jordan between Hermon and Gilead, and Og as a giant Amorite prince who opposed Israel's progress. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: The Lord said to Moses, fear him not, for I have delivered him into your hand, and you shall do to him as you did to Sihon king of the Amorites. JFB notes the encouragement was necessary, for Og's gigantic stature was calculated to inspire terror, and the promise repeated Sihon's pattern as the guarantee for Bashan. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: So they smote him, and his sons, and all his people, until there was none left him alive, and they possessed his land. The victory was total, as with Sihon: Og's house left no survivor, and Bashan passed to Israel entire. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

No note for verse(s) 19, 20 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
