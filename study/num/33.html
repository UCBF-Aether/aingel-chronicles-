Study Notes — Numbers

Chapter 33

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: These are the journeys of Israel, which went forth out of the land of Egypt with their armies, under the hand of Moses and Aaron. JFB notes this chapter winds up the history of Israel's wilderness travels, and the itinerary probably lists only the most important stations, those where they formed prolonged encampments and dispersed flocks to pasture until the herbage was exhausted, extending from departure out of Egypt to arrival on the plains of Moab, while Henry adds they were continually on the remove, led to and fro yet all the while under the pillar of cloud and fire, God leading them about yet leading them the right way. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Moses wrote their goings out according to their journeys by the commandment of the Lord: these are their journeys according to their goings out. JFB notes the wisdom of this divine order: partly to establish the truth of the history, partly to preserve a memorial of God's marvellous interpositions for Israel, and partly to confirm their faith for the difficult enterprise ahead, the invasion of Canaan. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: They departed from Rameses in the first month, on the fifteenth day of the first month; on the morrow after the passover Israel went out with a high hand in the sight of all the Egyptians. JFB identifies Rameses, probably the capital of Goshen, as the place of general rendezvous previous to departure, and the departure on the morrow after passover, with a high hand before all Egypt, marked the exodus itself. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: For the Egyptians buried all their firstborn, which the Lord had smitten among them: upon their gods also the Lord executed judgments. JFB notes upon their gods denotes either their rulers, the firstborn of king and princes, or the idolatrous objects of Egyptian worship, so that Egypt buried its dead while its gods were judged. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Israel removed from Rameses, and pitched in Succoth. JFB notes Succoth means booths, a place of no note except as a temporary halting place, the first pitch after leaving Egypt. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: They departed from Succoth, and pitched in Etham, which is in the edge of the wilderness. JFB notes Etham was the edge or border of that part of Arabia Petraea contiguous to Egypt, known by the general name of Shur, the last station before the turn back toward the sea. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: They removed from Etham, and turned again to Pi-hahiroth, which is before Baal-zephon, and they pitched before Migdol. The turn back to Pi-hahiroth before Baal-zephon set the trap for Pharaoh, placing Israel between Migdol and the sea. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: They departed from before Pi-hahiroth, and passed through the midst of the sea into the wilderness, and went three days' journey in the wilderness of Etham, and pitched in Marah. JFB identifies Marah, thought to be Ain Howarah, by its position and the three days it would take so great a host with children and flocks to march there from the waters of Ayun Musa, and the sea passage stands at the head of the itinerary's wonders. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: They removed from Marah, and came to Elim, and in Elim were twelve fountains of water, and threescore and ten palm trees, and they pitched there. JFB supposes Elim to be Wady Ghurundel, where twelve fountains and seventy palms answered Marah's bitterness with abundance. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: They removed from Elim, and encamped by the Red sea. JFB notes the road from Wady Ghurundel leads inland behind a high continuous ridge, then after about three days' march opens again on a plain along the Red Sea's margin, and the narrative's minute accuracy is shown in describing Israel as proceeding by the only practicable route, this plain being the Desert of Sin. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: They removed from the Red sea, and encamped in the wilderness of Sin. The wilderness of Sin, where the manna would fall, was the next encampment after the Red Sea shore, the road leading Israel deeper into dependence on daily bread. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: They took their journey out of the wilderness of Sin, and encamped in Dophkah. JFB groups Dophkah with Alush and Rephidim as three stations in the great valleys of El Sheikh and Feiran, equivalent to four days' journey for so great a host. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: They departed from Dophkah, and encamped in Alush. Alush was the middle station of the three in the valleys of El Sheikh and Feiran, on the road from the wilderness of Sin toward Rephidim and Sinai. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: They removed from Alush, and encamped at Rephidim, where was no water for the people to drink. JFB notes Rephidim was in Horeb, the burnt region, a generic name for a hot mountainous country, and the station is remembered for its lack of water, which the smitten rock supplied. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: They departed from Rephidim, and pitched in the wilderness of Sinai. JFB identifies the wilderness of Sinai with Wady Er-Raheh, the plain before the mountain where the law would be given. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: They removed from the desert of Sinai, and pitched at Kibroth-hattaavah. JFB notes Kibroth-hattaavah means the graves of lust, and the route on breaking up Sinai's encampment led down Wady Sheikh and across Jebel-et-Tih into Wady Zalaka, pitching at two brief though memorable stations before Hazeroth. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: They departed from Kibroth-hattaavah, and encamped at Hazeroth. JFB notes Hazeroth means unwalled villages, supposed to be at Ain-Hadera, the station of Miriam's leprosy and the people's next halt after the graves of lust. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: They departed from Hazeroth, and pitched in Rithmah. JFB notes Rithmah means the place of the broom, a station possibly in some wady extending westward of the Ghor, the first of the eighteen stations between Hazeroth and Kadesh that belong to the long wandering interval. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: They removed from Ezion-geber, and pitched in the wilderness of Zin, which is Kadesh. JFB notes the arrival at Kadesh in this catalogue corresponds to the second sojourn there, in the first month, while between the two visits to Kadesh there intervened thirty-eight years of wandering hither and thither through El-Tih, often returning to the same spots as their flocks' pasture required, and the intermediate stations belong to that long interval. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

37 COMMENTARY: They removed from Kadesh, and pitched in mount Hor, in the edge of the land of Edom. From Kadesh the host moved to mount Hor on Edom's edge, where the second generation's march toward Canaan would turn northward around Edom. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

38 COMMENTARY: Aaron the priest went up into mount Hor at the commandment of the Lord, and died there, in the fortieth year after Israel was come out of the land of Egypt, in the first day of the fifth month. Aaron's death on mount Hor, at the Lord's commandment in the fortieth year, closed the high priest's wilderness ministry within sight of the land. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

39 COMMENTARY: Aaron was a hundred and twenty and three years old when he died in mount Hor. Aaron died at one hundred and twenty-three, three years older than the age at which Moses would die, having served as priest from the exodus to the fortieth year. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

40 COMMENTARY: And king Arad the Canaanite, which dwelt in the south in the land of Canaan, heard of the coming of Israel. Arad, the southern Canaanite king, heard of Israel's coming from mount Hor, the first Canaanite to take note of the host now turning toward his land. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

47 COMMENTARY: They removed from Almondiblathaim, and pitched in the mountains of Abarim, before Nebo. JFB notes Abarim was a ridge of rugged hills northwest of the Arnon, whose part called Nebo was one of its highest peaks, opposite Jericho, and from these mountains Moses would soon view the land. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

48 COMMENTARY: They departed from the mountains of Abarim, and pitched in the plains of Moab by Jordan near Jericho. The descent from Abarim brought Israel to the plains of Moab by Jordan near Jericho, the final staging ground before the crossing. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

49 COMMENTARY: They pitched by Jordan, from Beth-jesimoth even to Abel-shittim in the plains of Moab. The final camp stretched along Jordan from Beth-jesimoth to Abel-shittim, a broad encampment in the plains of Moab facing Jericho, where the book's remaining events would unfold. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

51 COMMENTARY: Speak to Israel, and say to them, when you are passed over Jordan into the land of Canaan. The itinerary closed with a command for the crossing: what Israel was to do once passed over Jordan into Canaan. — Matthew Henry

52 COMMENTARY: Then you shall drive out all the inhabitants of the land from before you, and destroy all their pictures, and destroy all their molten images, and quite pluck down all their high places. JFB notes drive out here means not mere expulsion but extermination, and pictures means obelisks for idolatrous worship, while molten images and high places stand by metonymy for all groves, altars and materials of worship on hilltops. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

53 COMMENTARY: You shall dispossess the inhabitants of the land, and dwell therein: for I have given you the land to possess it. Dispossession and dwelling went together: the land was given to possess, not merely to conquer, and possession required inhabiting it. — Matthew Henry

54 COMMENTARY: You shall divide the land by lot for an inheritance among your families: and to the more you shall give the more inheritance, and to the fewer you shall give the fewer inheritance: every man's inheritance shall be in the place where his lot falls, according to the tribes of your fathers shall you inherit. JFB notes the particular locality of each tribe was determined by lot, while a line was used in measuring the proportion, and Henry's equity rule from chapter 26 applied again: many received more, fewer less. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

55 COMMENTARY: But if you will not drive out the inhabitants of the land from before you, then it shall come to pass, that those which you let remain of them shall be pricks in your eyes, and thorns in your sides, and shall vex you in the land wherein you dwell. JFB warns no associations were to be formed with the inhabitants, otherwise the remnant would prove troublesome and dangerous neighbours, enticing to idolatry and depriving Israel of divine favour, and Henry adds if we do not drive out sin, sin will drive us out. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

56 COMMENTARY: Moreover it shall come to pass, that I shall do to you, as I thought to do to them. The final warning reversed the sentence: if Israel learned the Canaanites' wicked ways, Israel too would be put out of the land, as the Canaanites were to be. — Matthew Henry
