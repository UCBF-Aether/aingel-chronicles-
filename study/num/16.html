Study Notes — Numbers

Chapter 16

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Now Korah son of Izhar, son of Kohath, son of Levi, and Dathan and Abiram sons of Eliab, and On son of Peleth, sons of Reuben, took men. JFB traces the conspiracy's roots: Korah, of Izhar the second son of Kohath, had been supplanted as Kohathite prince by a descendant of the fourth son, while the Reubenites claimed Moses had arbitrarily taken away primogeniture's priesthood and given it to his own house, and resented Judah's advancement to tribal leadership, with On afterwards withdrawing from the conspiracy. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: They rose up before Moses with two hundred and fifty princes of the assembly, famous in the congregation, men of renown. The rebellion was no rabble rising but a movement of the recognised leadership, which made its charge against Moses and Aaron a public challenge to God's settlement. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: They gathered against Moses and Aaron, saying, you take too much upon you, seeing all the congregation are holy, every one of them, and the Lord is among them: wherefore then do you lift yourselves above the congregation? JFB notes their ground: the whole people, separated to divine service, were equally qualified to offer, and God's presence by tabernacle and cloud showed readiness to receive sacrifices from any hand, while Henry adds small reason they had to boast of the people's purity, so lately polluted with sin. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: When Moses heard it, he fell upon his face. JFB reads the prostration as humble and earnest desire that God would interpose to free him from a false and odious imputation, and a strong sense of the daring sin involved, from a devout man who adopted the prudential course heavenly wisdom dictated. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: He spoke to Korah and all his company: even tomorrow the Lord will show who are his, and who is holy, and will cause him to come near to him, even him whom he has chosen. JFB notes Korah's company was addressed first, as Moses' own cousin's party stationed near the tabernacle, and the test would afford decisive evidence by some visible or miraculous token of approval. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: This do: take censers, Korah and all his company, and put fire and incense in them before the Lord tomorrow, and it shall be that the man whom the Lord chooses shall be holy. JFB notes the magnanimity: since they aspired to the priesthood, let them perform its highest function, offering incense, though Moses warned them they were making a perilous experiment. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: And put fire therein, and put incense in them before the Lord tomorrow: and it shall be that the man whom the Lord does choose, he shall be holy: you take too much upon you, you sons of Levi. The test turned their own charge back on them: the question was not who claimed holiness, but whom the Lord would show to be his. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Moses said to Korah, hear, I pray you, you sons of Levi. The expostulation carried weight precisely because Moses was himself a Levite excluded with his family from the priesthood, so he argued not as an outsider defending a privilege but as one who shared their station. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Does it seem but a small thing to you that the God of Israel has separated you from the congregation to bring you near to himself, to do the service of the tabernacle and stand before the congregation to minister? Henry notes Moses showed them their privileges as Levites and convicted them of undervaluing them: duly considering how many are below us helps keep us from envying those above. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: He has brought you near to him, and all your brethren the sons of Levi with you, and do you seek the priesthood also? The grievance was not exclusion but appetite: having been brought near, they demanded the one office still above them. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: For which cause both you and all your company are gathered together against the Lord: and what is Aaron, that you murmur against him? The gathering, Moses insists, was against the Lord who settled the priesthood, not merely against Aaron who received it. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Moses sent to call Dathan and Abiram, who said, we will not come up. JFB notes the separate interview because their ground differed: Korah murmured against the priesthood's appropriation, while they opposed Moses' civil supremacy, and their refusal rested on the pretext that their desert stay was prolonged for the leader's secret selfish purposes. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Is it a small thing that you have brought us up out of a land that flows with milk and honey, to kill us in the wilderness, except you make yourself altogether a prince over us? Henry calls these very false charges: those often fall under the heaviest censures who in truth deserve the highest praise, and Egypt is mockingly called a land of milk and honey by men who had fled its bondage. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Moreover you have not brought us into a land flowing with milk and honey, nor given us inheritance of fields and vineyards: will you put out the eyes of these men? We will not come up. The charge turned God's promise into Moses' personal failure, as if the wilderness sentence were one man's broken pledge rather than God's judgment on the whole generation. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Moses was very wroth, and said to the Lord, respect not their offering: I have not taken one ass from them, neither have I hurt one of them. JFB notes even the meekest of men could not restrain indignation at unjust and groundless charges, and his brief exclamation mixed prayer with an impassioned assertion of integrity, calling the censer test their offering because it was the united appeal of all the mutineers. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Moses said to Korah, be you and all your company before the Lord, you, and they, and Aaron, tomorrow. JFB notes the trial was set at the tabernacle door so the assembled people might witness the experiment and be properly impressed by its issue. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Take every man his censer, put incense in them, and bring before the Lord every man his censer, two hundred and fifty censers, you also and Aaron each his censer. JFB notes the censers were probably the small platters common in Egyptian families for household incense, among the precious things borrowed at the departure. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: They took every man his censer, put fire in them, laid incense thereon, and stood in the door of the tabernacle with Moses and Aaron. The challengers accepted the test in public, standing where all could see, with the very instruments of the priesthood they claimed. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Korah gathered all the congregation against them to the door of the tabernacle, and the glory of the Lord appeared to all the congregation. Henry notes the same glory that appeared to place Aaron in office at first now appeared to confirm him, and nothing is more terrible to the consciously guilty than the appearance of the divine glory. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Separate yourselves from among this congregation, that I may consume them in a moment. JFB explains curiosity had drawn a vast concourse, and the popular mind had been incited to evil by the mutineers' clamours, so that after his glory appeared for Aaron's confirmation, God bade Moses and Aaron withdraw for the assembly's consumption. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: They fell on their faces and said, O God, the God of the spirits of all flesh, shall one man sin, and will you be wroth with all the congregation? JFB notes the benevolent importunity of the prayer was the more remarkable because the intercession was made for their enemies, and Henry adds that though the people had treacherously deserted them, Moses and Aaron approved themselves faithful shepherds. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Speak to the congregation, saying, get up from about the tabernacle of Korah, Dathan, and Abiram. JFB notes Moses was attended by the elders, and the united and urgent entreaties of so many dignified persons convinced the people of their crime and withdrew them from doomed men, lest, partaking in their sins, they perish with them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Moses rose up and went to Dathan and Abiram, and the elders of Israel followed him. Henry's rule is that it is our duty to countenance and support lawful authority when opposed, and the elders' visible following gave the people a lead out of the rebels' company. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: He spoke to the congregation, saying, depart, I pray you, from the tents of these wicked men, and touch nothing of theirs, lest you be consumed in all their sins. Henry's lesson is that those who would not perish with sinners must come out from among them and be separate, and grace to separate from evildoers accompanies salvation. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: So they got up from the tabernacle of Korah, Dathan, and Abiram on every side, and Dathan and Abiram came out and stood in the door of their tents, with their wives, sons and little children. JFB notes Korah, a Kohathite, could not have had his tent in the Reubenite camp and does not appear on this spot himself, while Dathan and Abiram's defiant stance showed a character equally regardless of God and man. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Moses said, hereby you shall know that the Lord has sent me to do all these works, for I have not done them of my own mind. JFB notes the earthquake that followed, as predicted, gave divine attestation to Moses' mission and struck the spectators with solemn awe. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: If these men die the common death of all men, or if they be visited after the visitation of all men, then the Lord has not sent me. The test was staked on ordinariness itself: a common death would have left Moses an impostor by his own terms. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: But if the Lord makes a new thing, and the earth opens her mouth and swallows them up, with all that appertain to them, and they go down quick into the pit, then you shall understand that these men have provoked the Lord. The new thing was unprecedented judgment, fitted to an unprecedented rebellion against God's settled order. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: As he made an end of speaking all these words, the ground clave asunder that was under them. Henry stresses the immediacy: as soon as Moses had spoken, God caused the earth to open, leaving no interval for doubt about whose word it was. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: The earth opened her mouth and swallowed them up, and their houses, and all the men that appertained to Korah, and all their goods. The judgment took households and goods together, so that nothing of the rebellion's establishment remained above ground. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: They, and all that appertained to them, went down alive into the pit, and the earth closed upon them, and they perished from among the congregation. Henry calls it altogether miraculous and very significant: considering how the earth is loaded with man's sins, we have reason to wonder it does not now sink under its load, and the ruin of others should be our warning. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: All Israel round about them fled at their cry, saying, lest the earth swallow us up also. The cry of the swallowed became the warning of the survivors, and fear did in a moment what argument had not done: it separated the people from the rebels. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: There came out a fire from the Lord and consumed the two hundred and fifty men that offered incense. JFB notes the fire came from the cloud and describes the destruction of Korah and the Levites who aspired to priesthood, while Aaron, who stood with them, was preserved alive, since God is jealous of his institutions' honour and will not have them invaded. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

37 COMMENTARY: Speak to Eleazar son of Aaron the priest, that he take up the censers out of the burning, and scatter the fire yonder, for they are hallowed. JFB notes Eleazar was selected lest the high priest contract defilement from going among the dead carcasses, a practical care even in the midst of judgment. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

38 COMMENTARY: The censers of these sinners against their own souls, let them make them broad plates for a covering of the altar, for they offered them before the Lord, therefore they are hallowed, and they shall be a sign to Israel. Henry notes the censers, as devoted things, must be made serviceable to God's glory, and the covering would remind Israel of this event, that others might hear and fear and do no more presumptuously. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: Eleazar the priest took the brazen censers, wherewith they that were burnt had offered, and they were made broad plates for a covering of the altar. JFB notes the altar, wood covered with brass, was by this additional covering rendered doubly secure against fire, and served as a warning beacon against future invasions of the priesthood. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

40 COMMENTARY: To be a memorial to Israel, that no stranger, not of the seed of Aaron, come near to offer incense before the Lord, that he be not as Korah and as his company, as the Lord said to him by Moses. The memorial's purpose was preventive: every future sight of the altar's plates preached the boundary of the priesthood. — Matthew Henry

41 COMMENTARY: But on the morrow all the congregation murmured against Moses and Aaron, saying, you have killed the people of the Lord. JFB calls this a strange exhibition of popular prejudice, blaming the leaders for saving the rebels, and Henry notes the gaping earth was scarcely closed before the same sins were again committed and all warnings slighted. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

42 COMMENTARY: When the congregation was gathered against Moses and Aaron, they looked toward the tabernacle, and behold, the cloud covered it, and the glory of the Lord appeared. The glory appeared a second time in the same controversy, again shielding the leaders the congregation had gathered against. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

43 COMMENTARY: Moses and Aaron came before the tabernacle of the congregation. The leaders' response to a second gathering against them was the same as to the first: they went to the place of God's presence rather than to the crowd's tribunal. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

45 COMMENTARY: Get up from among this congregation, that I may consume them as in a moment, and they fell upon their faces. The second sentence repeated the first, and the second intercession repeated the first: Moses and Aaron fell on their faces for a people gathered against them. — Matthew Henry

46 COMMENTARY: Moses said to Aaron, take a censer, put fire therein from off the altar, put on incense, go quickly to the congregation and make an atonement for them, for wrath is gone out from the Lord, and the plague is begun. Henry notes Aaron burned incense not to purify the air but to pacify an offended God, and as one tender of every Israelite's life, he made all possible speed. — Matthew Henry

47 COMMENTARY: Aaron took as Moses commanded, and ran into the midst of the congregation, and behold, the plague was begun among the people, and he put on incense and made an atonement for the people. JFB notes Moses and Aaron interceded for the people, the high priest perilling his own life in doing good to that perverse race. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

48 COMMENTARY: He stood between the dead and the living, and the plague was stayed. JFB notes the plague seems to have begun in the camp's extremities, and Aaron in this act was a type of Christ, who enters the defiled and dying camp and stands between the eternal Judge and souls under condemnation. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

49 COMMENTARY: Now they that died in the plague were fourteen thousand and seven hundred, besides them that died about the matter of Korah. The number measures the second judgment: thousands died for murmuring against the judgment on the rebels, on top of those who died in the rebellion itself. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

50 COMMENTARY: Aaron returned to Moses at the door of the tabernacle, and the plague was stayed. The return to the tabernacle door closed the mediation: the plague stopped where intercession stood, and Aaron came back alive from between the dead and the living. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 20, 23, 36, 44 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
