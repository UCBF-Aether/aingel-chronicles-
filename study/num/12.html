Study Notes — Numbers

Chapter 12

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Miriam and Aaron spoke against Moses because of the Ethiopian woman he had married. JFB explains the Hebrew is a Cushite woman, Arabia being called the land of Cush, and the real occasion was the great change of government by the seventy rulers: jealous of the relatives through whose influence the innovation came, and feeling overlooked, they disparaged his wife, with Miriam, named first, as chief instigator. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: They asked, has the Lord indeed spoken only by Moses? Has he not spoken also by us? JFB notes the prophetical name belonged to Aaron and Miriam too, so they treated Moses' exclusive authority as an encroachment on their rights, while Henry sees the deeper motive: pride hurt and envy stirred by his superior authority. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Now the man Moses was very meek, above all men on the face of the earth. JFB explains this accounts for Moses taking no notice of their angry reproaches and for God's speedy vindication, and notes the verse may be a parenthesis inserted as a gloss by Ezra or a later prophet, with some rendering the word as very afflicted rather than very meek. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The Lord spoke suddenly to Moses, Aaron and Miriam: come out, you three, to the tabernacle of the congregation, and they came out. JFB notes the divine interposition was made openly and immediately in order to suppress the sedition and prevent its spreading among the people. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: The Lord came down in the pillar of cloud, stood in the tabernacle door, and called Aaron and Miriam, and they both came forth. JFB notes the public exclusion was itself a token of displeasure: Aaron was denied the admission that was usually his privilege, and granted to no other man or woman. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: God said, hear my words: if there is a prophet among you, I the Lord will make myself known to him in a vision, and speak to him in a dream. JFB stresses the difference of degree in gifts and authority even among divinely commissioned prophets: Moses, set over all God's house, was invested with supremacy over Miriam and Aaron also. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: My servant Moses is not so, who is faithful in all my house. Faithfulness over the whole house, God's church and people, is the ground of Moses' supremacy: he was not one prophet among others, but the steward set over all. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: With Moses God speaks mouth to mouth, even apparently, and not in dark speeches, and he beholds the similitude of the Lord. JFB explains: immediately, not by an interpreter nor by visionary symbols, plainly and surely, not in parables, and the similitude is not God's invisible face or essence but some unmistakable evidence of his glorious presence. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: The anger of the Lord was kindled against them, and he departed. Henry warns that God's presence is the surest token of his favour, and his departure the saddest token of displeasure: woe to us if he depart, for he never departs till by sin and folly we drive him from us. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: When the cloud departed from the tabernacle, Miriam became leprous, white as snow. JFB notes the malignant form and sudden appearance proved a divine judgment, and she was made the victim either because of her extreme violence or because leprosy on Aaron would have interrupted and dishonoured the holy service, while Henry adds her foul tongue was justly punished with a foul face. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Aaron said to Moses, alas, my lord, lay not the sin upon us, wherein we have done foolishly and sinned. JFB calls this humble and penitential submission, on which Moses interceded for both offenders, and Henry notes Aaron, as priest and judge of leprosy, could not pronounce her leprous without trembling, knowing himself equally guilty. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Aaron pleaded, let her not be as one dead, of whom the flesh is half consumed when he comes out of his mother's womb. The image of a stillborn child, half consumed, measures the horror of the leprosy: Miriam's living body already bore the mark of death. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Moses cried to the Lord, saying, heal her now, O God, I beseech you. Henry notes Moses made it appear that he forgave the injury done him, a pattern with our Saviour's Father, forgive them, to which all who are wronged must conform. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: The Lord answered, if her father had but spit in her face, should she not be ashamed seven days? Let her be shut out of the camp seven days, and after that received in again. JFB explains the eastern abhorrence of spitting: a parent's spit separated a child as unclean from society for seven days, so seven days was the fitting, not excessive, measure of her shame. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Miriam was shut out from the camp seven days, and the people journeyed not till she was brought in again. JFB suggests the restraint was either not to crush her with overwhelming severity or not to expose a prophetess to popular contempt, and Henry adds the wider lesson: her sin hindered the whole people's march toward Canaan, and nothing so hinders us on the way to heaven as sin. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Afterward the people removed from Hazeroth and pitched in the wilderness of Paran. JFB identifies the station of encampments as Rithmah, and the move marks the end of the Hazeroth delay: only when Miriam was restored did the host advance again. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
