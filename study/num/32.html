Study Notes — Numbers

Chapter 32

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Now Reuben and Gad had a very great multitude of cattle, and when they saw the land of Jazer, and the land of Gilead, that, behold, the place was a place for cattle. JFB notes a complete conquest had been made east of Jordan, Jazer the southern district between Arnon and Jabbok and Gilead the middle region between Jabbok and Jarmouk including Bashan, a region always famous for rich and extensive pastures, still the Bedouin shepherds' favourite resort, and Reuben and Gad, pre-eminently pastoral and placed under the same standard, united in preferring a request for this trans-jordanic region so well suited to pastoral habits. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: The children of Gad and the children of Reuben came and spoke to Moses, and to Eleazar the priest, and to the princes of the congregation, saying. The two tribes brought their request jointly to Moses, Eleazar and the princes, the public authorities who could grant or refuse a tribal inheritance. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Ataroth, and Dibon, and Jazer, and Nimrah, and Heshbon, and Elealeh, and Shebam, and Nebo, and Beon. The tribes named the conquered cities one by one, the Amorite towns whose pasture lands they had surveyed and now sought as their portion. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Even the country which the Lord smote before the congregation of Israel, is a land for cattle, and your servants have cattle. Their argument was fitness: the country the Lord had already smitten before Israel was a land for cattle, and they were cattlemen. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Wherefore, said they, if we have found grace in your sight, let this land be given to your servants for a possession, and bring us not over Jordan. Henry notes two common things in the world might lead these tribes to this choice, the lust of the eye and the pride of life, and there was much amiss in the principle they went upon, consulting their own private convenience more than the public good, as many still seek their own things more than the things of Christ and are led by worldly interests to take up short of the heavenly Canaan. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Moses said to Gad and to Reuben, shall your brethren go to war, and shall you sit here? JFB notes their language was ambiguous, and Moses, suspicious the proposal was an act of unbelief, a scheme of self-policy and indolence to escape the perils of warfare and live in ease and safety, addressed a reproachful and passionate remonstrance, adding that if they truly meant to co-operate, they ought to have been more explicit at first. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: And wherefore discourage you the heart of Israel from going over into the land which the Lord has given them? Henry notes the proposal showed disregard to Canaan, distrust of the Lord's promise, and unwillingness to encounter the difficulties and dangers of conquering and driving out its inhabitants. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: Thus did your fathers, when I sent them from Kadesh-barnea to see the land. Moses reached back to the spies' mission from Kadesh-barnea, the last time Israel stood ready to enter and turned back. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: For when they went up to the valley of Eshcol, and saw the land, they discouraged the heart of Israel, that they should not go into the land which the Lord had given them. The fathers' sight of the land produced discouragement rather than courage, and Moses warned the sons against repeating it. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: And the Lord's anger was kindled the same time, and he swore, saying, surely none of the men that came up out of Egypt, from twenty years old and upward, shall see the land which I swore to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, because they have not wholly followed me. The oath that followed the spies' discouragement barred the whole generation from the land, because they had not wholly followed the Lord. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Surely none of the men that came up out of Egypt, from twenty years old and upward, shall see the land which I swore to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, because they have not wholly followed me. The sentence fell on fighting men, twenty and upward, the same measure by which both censuses had counted Israel. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Save Caleb son of Jephunneh the Kenezite, and Joshua son of Nun: for they have wholly followed the Lord. Two men escaped the sentence, Caleb and Joshua, because they had wholly followed the Lord when the rest had not. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: And the Lord's anger was kindled against Israel, and he made them wander in the wilderness forty years, until all the generation, that had done evil in the sight of the Lord, was consumed. The wandering lasted forty years, until the evil generation was consumed, a sentence the second census had just proved fulfilled. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: And, behold, you are risen up in your fathers' stead, an increase of sinful men, to augment yet the fierce anger of the Lord toward Israel. Moses charged the sons with rising in their fathers' stead as an increase of sinful men, whose discouragement would augment the very anger that had consumed their fathers. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: For if you turn away from after him, he will yet again leave them in the wilderness, and you shall destroy all this people. The warning was corporate: turning away would leave not only the two tribes but all Israel in the wilderness, and destroy the whole people. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: And they came near to him, and said, we will build sheepfolds here for our cattle, and cities for our little ones. JFB notes the suppliants had shrunk back, dreading refusal from their leader's undisguised emotions, but perceiving his objection rested only on the supposition they would not cross Jordan to help their brethren, they grew emboldened to approach with assurances of goodwill, and build here means rebuild or repair, since within two months new cities could not have been founded, the Amorite cities not being absolutely demolished but consisting only of mud-built or dry-stone walls. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: But we ourselves will go ready armed before Israel, until we have brought them to their place, and our little ones shall dwell in the fenced cities because of the inhabitants of the land. JFB notes good policy in leaving a sufficient force to protect the conquered region against reprisals, since only forty thousand of Reuben, Gad and half Manasseh passed over Jordan, leaving seventy thousand five hundred and eighty men besides women and children under twenty for the new possessions' security, and ready armed means in a collective body, or as many as deemed necessary, while the rest remained to sustain and protect families and flocks. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: We will not return to our houses, until Israel have inherited every man his inheritance. The pledge was total: no return home until every man of Israel had inherited, not merely until the war was won. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: For we will not inherit with them on yonder side Jordan, or forward, because our inheritance is fallen to us on this side Jordan eastward. Their choice was now stated plainly: no inheritance west of Jordan, because theirs had fallen eastward, and the condition for it was the pledge just made. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Moses said to them, if you will do this thing, if you will go armed before the Lord to war. JFB notes go before the Lord alludes to the order of march in which Reuben and Gad immediately preceded the ark, or to the Jordan passage where the ark stood mid-channel while all tribes marched by, so that literally they passed before the Lord and before the rest of Israel, or generally denotes an expedition blessed with God's presence and destined to promote his glory, and Moses' displeasure disappeared on their solemn assurances, though a lurking suspicion still kept him in an admonitory strain. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: And will go all of you armed over Jordan before the Lord, until he has driven out his enemies from before him. The condition was armed passage over Jordan before the Lord, until his enemies were driven out, a war fought under God's banner rather than the tribes' convenience. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: And the land be subdued before the Lord: then afterward you shall return, and be guiltless before the Lord, and before Israel, and this land shall be your possession before the Lord. Only when the land was subdued before the Lord would return leave them guiltless before God and Israel, and only then would the east bank be their possession before the Lord. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: But if you will not do so, behold, you have sinned against the Lord: and be sure your sin will find you out. Henry warns sin will surely find out the sinner sooner or later, and it concerns us now to find our sins out, that we may repent and forsake them, lest they find us out to our ruin. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Build you cities for your little ones, and folds for your sheep, and do that which has proceeded out of your mouth. Moses granted the request on the pledge's terms: build the cities and folds, and do what your mouth has spoken, the pledge now binding as a vow. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: And Gad and Reuben spoke to Moses, saying, your servants will do as my lord commands. The tribes answered in the language of servants to their lord, accepting Moses' terms without dispute, which Henry calls the good effect of plain dealing. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: Our little ones, our wives, our flocks, and all our cattle, shall be there in the cities of Gilead. Families, wives, flocks and cattle would remain in Gilead's cities while the fighting men crossed Jordan, the division of labour the pledge required. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: But your servants will pass over, every man armed for war, before the Lord to battle, as my lord says. The pledge was repeated in full: every man armed for war, before the Lord to battle, as Moses had said. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: So concerning them Moses commanded Eleazar the priest, and Joshua son of Nun, and the chief fathers of the tribes of Israel. The arrangement, with its express terms, was announced to the public authorities, Eleazar, Joshua and the chief fathers, so that the conditional grant stood as public law. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Moses said to them, if Gad and Reuben will pass with you over Jordan, every man armed to battle, before the Lord, and the land shall be subdued before you, then you shall give them the land of Gilead for a possession. The grant was stated to the authorities in conditional form: armed passage and a subdued land, then Gilead for a possession. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: But if they will not pass over with you armed, they shall have possessions among you in the land of Canaan. Failure meant forfeiture: they would be compelled to cross Jordan and fight for a settlement like the rest of their brethren, with possessions among them in Canaan instead of Gilead. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: And Gad and Reuben answered, saying, as the Lord has said to your servants, so will we do. The tribes answered in the Lord's name, and JFB notes their final reply showed that now all was right, whether Moses' expostulation had driven them from an original withdrawal or they had always meant to co-operate. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: We will pass over armed before the Lord into the land of Canaan, that the possession of our inheritance on this side Jordan may be ours. The final pledge tied the crossing to the possession: armed passage into Canaan was the price of keeping the inheritance east of Jordan. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: Moses gave to them, even to Gad, and to Reuben, and to half the tribe of Manasseh son of Joseph, the kingdom of Sihon king of the Amorites, and the kingdom of Og king of Bashan, the land, with the cities thereof in the coasts, even the cities of the country round about. JFB notes it is nowhere explained how half Manasseh was incorporated with the two tribes or what broke the tribe in two, since they were not mentioned in the application nor in Moses' first directions, but as a pastoral people with immense flocks like the others, Moses invited half of them to remain, the region being more than sufficient for the others, perhaps preferring them for valorous conduct against the Amorites. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: And Gad built Dibon, and Ataroth, and Aroer. JFB identifies Dibon with Dheban, now in ruins an hour from the Arnon, Ataroth, Hebrew for crowns, a town of that name not identified in Gad, and Aroer with Arair, standing on a precipice on the Arnon's north bank, while Henry notes built means repaired, not founded anew. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: And Atroth, Shophan, and Jaazer, and Jogbehah. JFB notes Jaazer stood near a famed fountain whose waters flow into the Wady Schaib about fifteen miles from Heshbon, and the Gadite cities together formed the repaired strongholds of the southern east bank. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: And Beth-nimrah, and Beth-haran, fenced cities, and folds for sheep. Beth-nimrah, now Nimrin, and Beth-haran were fenced cities with folds for sheep, the pastoral infrastructure of Gad's inheritance. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

37 COMMENTARY: And Reuben built Heshbon, and Elealeh, and Kirjathaim. JFB identifies Heshbon with Hesban, Elealeh, Hebrew for the high, with Elaal, and Kirjathaim, Hebrew for the double city, as Reuben's repaired cities in the conquered Amorite country. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

38 COMMENTARY: And Nebo, and Baal-meon, (their names being changed,) and Shibmah, and gave other names to the cities which they built. JFB notes Nebo is now Neba near the mountain of that name, Baal-meon now Myoun in ruins where a temple of Baal stood, and Shibmah, or Shebam, near Heshbon famous for vines, while the names were changed either by the general custom of conquerors or, more likely, because the prohibition on mentioning other gods' names made it expedient to obliterate all remembrance of those idols on Israel's first settlement. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

39 COMMENTARY: And the children of Machir son of Manasseh went to Gilead, and took it, and dispossessed the Amorites which were therein. Machir's sons conquered Gilead itself, dispossessing the Amorites who remained there, a conquest within the conquest. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

40 COMMENTARY: Moses gave Gilead to Machir son of Manasseh, and he dwelt therein. Gilead, now Jelud, was given to Machir, and he dwelt in the land his sons had taken. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

41 COMMENTARY: And Jair son of Manasseh went and took the small towns thereof, and called them Havoth-jair. Havoth-jair means tent-villages, and Jair, who captured them, was a descendant of Manasseh on his mother's side. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

42 COMMENTARY: And Nobah went and took Kenath, and the villages thereof, and called it Nobah, after his own name. Nobah, a distinguished person of eastern Manasseh, took Kenath and its villages and renamed the town after himself, as Jair had done with his. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
