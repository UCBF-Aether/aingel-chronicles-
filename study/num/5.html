Study Notes — Numbers

Chapter 5

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

2 COMMENTARY: Israel was commanded to put out of the camp every leper, everyone with an issue, and everyone defiled by the dead. Henry notes the camp was newly modelled and put in order, and cleansing completed the reformation: the purity of the church must be kept as carefully as its peace and order. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Both male and female were put outside the camp, so that they would not defile the camps in the midst of which God dwelt. The rule admits no exception of sex or standing: where God dwells, defilement must be kept far from his tabernacle. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Israel did so at once, putting them out without the camp as the Lord spoke to Moses. The immediate execution matters: the order was not debated or delayed once the camp had been ordered and the tabernacle fixed in its midst. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: When a man or woman commits any sin that men commit, doing a trespass against the Lord, that person is guilty. Henry stresses that overreaching or defrauding a brother is a trespass against the Lord himself, who strictly charges us to do justly. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: The offender must confess his sin and recompense the trespass with its principal, adding a fifth part, to the one wronged. Henry insists confession must be to God and to the neighbour, with shame taken to oneself, and restitution alone is not enough without faith and repentance. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: If the wronged man has no kinsman to receive recompense, the trespass is recompensed to the Lord, that is, to the priest, besides the ram of atonement. Satisfaction was thus made both for the loss sustained by the neighbour and for the offence done to God. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Every offering of the holy things of Israel, brought to the priest, shall be his. The priesthood lived by the holy things, a standing provision that freed them for the sanctuary's service. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Every man's hallowed things shall be his: whatever any man gives the priest, it shall be his. Henry's group comment covers both verses: the offerings belonged to the priests as God's appointed portion, not as a gift at the giver's later disposal. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: If a man's wife goes aside and commits a trespass against him, the law of jealousy applies. Henry calls adultery an exceedingly sinful sin: going aside from God and virtue, robbing the husband of honour, alienating his right, and violating her covenant with him. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: The case supposes a man lying with her carnally, hidden from her husband's eyes, kept close, with no witness against her and she not taken in the act. Henry notes the sin is taken to be one sinners take great care to conceal, since it would not covet secrecy if it were not shameful. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: The spirit of jealousy may come on a husband whether his wife is defiled or not defiled. Henry quotes Solomon: jealousy is the rage of a man, and cruel as the grave, which is why the law had to govern it rather than leave it to private revenge. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: The man must bring his wife to the priest, with her offering, a tenth of an ephah of barley meal, without oil or frankincense. Henry explains the coarse offering, an offering of memorial, suited the afflicted state of the family, whether the jealousy had cause or not, and marked the trial as a religious appeal to God's omniscience and justice. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: The priest brought her near and set her before the Lord. The trial was not a private ordeal but a solemn public appeal to a heart-searching God, in whose presence the truth would be declared. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: The priest took holy water in an earthen vessel, with dust from the floor of the tabernacle in it. Henry explains the plain earthen vessel suited the occasion's coarseness, the dust signified the reproach she lay under and the shame she ought to take to herself, and dust from the tabernacle floor honoured the place God had chosen for his name. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: The priest set the woman before the Lord, uncovered her head, and put the jealousy offering in her hands. The uncovered head and the offering in her hands made the trial public and solemn: she stood exposed before God, holding the memorial of the charge against her. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: The priest charged her by oath: if no man had lain with her and she had not gone aside to uncleanness, she would be free from the bitter water. The oath first offered innocence its clearing, so that a guiltless wife was not condemned by suspicion alone. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: But if she had gone aside to another instead of her husband and been defiled, the curse would apply. The conditional form is the heart of the trial: the same water and the same oath would clear the innocent and condemn the guilty. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: The priest charged her with an oath of cursing: the Lord would make her thigh rot and her belly swell. The curse was pronounced in advance and in detail, so that no guilty woman could afterwards claim she had not understood what she invoked. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: The water that causes the curse would go into her bowels, to make her belly swell and her thigh rot, and the woman answered, Amen, amen. Henry observes no guilty woman could say Amen to the adjuration and drink afterwards, unless she disbelieved God's truth or defied his justice. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: The priest wrote the curses in a book and blotted them out with the bitter water. Henry notes the priest first urged confession, warning her not to let the great name described in the sacred ceremony be blotted out with the bitter water, so the writing joined her fate to the water she would drink. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: The priest caused the woman to drink the bitter water that causes the curse, and the water entered into her. The water is called bitter because it caused the curse, and Henry draws the moral: sin is an evil and bitter thing, and forbidden pleasures will be bitterness in the latter end. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: The priest took the jealousy offering out of the woman's hand and waved it before the Lord. The memorial offering, waved after the drinking, kept the whole trial in the realm of worship: the case had been laid before God, not settled by the husband's hand. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: The priest took a handful of the offering, its memorial, burned it on the altar, and afterward caused the woman to drink the water. The burning of the memorial sealed the appeal: God had been called to remember the case, and the water would now declare it. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: If she was defiled, the water that caused the curse entered her, her belly swelled, her thigh rotted, and she became a curse among her people. Henry's lesson is that secret sins are known to God and sometimes strangely brought to light in this life, and that whoremongers and adulterers God will surely judge. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: If the woman was not defiled but clean, she was free and would conceive seed. The same providence that hurt the guilty cleared the innocent, and Henry notes God will manifest the innocency of the innocent, answering the purposes he intends. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: This is the law of jealousies, when a wife goes aside to another instead of her husband and is defiled. The summary gathers the whole trial into a standing law, so that no future case of suspicion needed a new rule. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: The same law applied when the spirit of jealousy came on a man over his wife, whether she was defiled or not. The law thus covered both outcomes in advance, restraining the husband from cruel treatment on mere suspicion while still leaving guilt no escape. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: Then the man was guiltless from iniquity, and the woman bore her iniquity. Gill explains the husband, having proceeded as the law directed on just ground of suspicion, was chargeable with no sin or blame whatever the outcome, while the guilty woman bore the punishment the bitter waters brought on her. — Matthew Henry; John Gill

No note for verse(s) 1, 5, 11 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
