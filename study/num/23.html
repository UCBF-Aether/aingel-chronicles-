Study Notes — Numbers

Chapter 23

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Balaam said to Balak, build me here seven altars, and prepare me here seven bullocks and seven rams. JFB notes Balak, a heathen, would suppose the altars honoured Baal, though they were prepared for the true God's worship, while Balaam blended superstition with divine worship by choosing Baal's high places and many altars instead of the one God appointed, and by trading on the heathen mystery attached to seven. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Balak did as Balaam had spoken, and Balak and Balaam offered on every altar a bullock and a ram. The king and the prophet sacrificed together, fourteen animals on seven altars, in the full view of Israel's camps, as if magnitude of offering could purchase a curse. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Balaam said to Balak, stand by your burnt offering, and I will go, peradventure the Lord will come to meet me, and whatsoever he shows me I will tell you, and he went to a high place. JFB notes standing by the offering was the posture of one expecting an important favour, and the high place apart was where Balaam practised rites to obtain an oracle's response. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: God met Balaam, and he said to him, I have prepared seven altars, and I have offered upon every altar a bullock and a ram. JFB stresses God met him not in compliance with his incantations, but to frustrate his wicked designs and compel him, contrary to his desires and interests, to pronounce a blessing. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: The Lord put a word in Balaam's mouth, and said, return to Balak, and thus you shall speak. Henry notes the miracle was as great as opening the ass's mouth: he who opened the beast's mouth caused this wicked man's mouth to speak words as contrary to his heart's desire as the ass's were to brute powers. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: He returned to him, and lo, he stood by his burnt sacrifice, he, and all the princes of Moab. Balak waited where Balaam had left him, with all his princes, expecting a curse and about to hear the opposite. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: He took up his parable, and said, Balak king of Moab has brought me from Aram, out of the mountains of the east, saying, come, curse me Jacob, and come, defy Israel. JFB notes a parable means speech under inspiration in a highly poetical, figurative, oracular style, and Aram with the eastern mountains denotes upper Mesopotamia east of Moab, a region infamous for magicians and soothsayers. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: How shall I curse, whom God has not cursed? Or how shall I defy, whom the Lord has not defied? JFB notes a divine blessing had been pronounced over Jacob's posterity, so whatever prodigies charms could achieve, all magical skill and human power were utterly impotent to counteract God's decree. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: For from the top of the rocks I see him, and from the hills I behold him: lo, the people shall dwell alone, and shall not be reckoned among the nations. JFB notes the bare place on the rocks was chosen because seeing the people was deemed necessary to devote them to destruction, yet Israel's destiny was to be a distinct, peculiar people, separated in government, religion, customs and divine protection. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Who can count the dust of Jacob, and the number of the fourth part of Israel? Let me die the death of the righteous, and let my last end be like his! JFB notes the dust is an Oriental hyperbole for a populous nation as promised to Jacob, and the fourth part means the camp's four divisions, each formidable in numbers, while the death of the righteous, Hebrew Jeshurun, means a people happy in life and at death through knowledge of the true God and hope through his grace. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Balak said to Balaam, what have you done to me? I took you to curse my enemies, and behold, you have blessed them altogether. Henry notes a confession of God's overruling power was thus extorted from a wicked prophet to the confusion of a wicked prince, and the curse was turned into blessing by God's overruling power in love to Israel. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: He answered and said, must I not take heed to speak that which the Lord has put in my mouth? The prophet's defence was compulsion itself: the word put in his mouth was not his to edit, and heeding it was the only faithfulness left to a man hired to do the opposite. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Balak said to him, come, I pray you, with me to another place, from whence you may see them: you shall see but the utmost part of them, and shall not see them all, and curse me them from thence. JFB notes Balak, surprised and disappointed at the eulogy, hoped a different point of observation would produce different feelings, as if a partial view could change God's verdict. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: He brought him into the field of Zophim, to the top of Pisgah, and built seven altars, and offered a bullock and a ram on every altar. JFB describes Zophim as a flat surface on the mountain summit, cultivated land, or, in another rendering, the field of sentinels, where Balak's guards were posted to give signals. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: He said to Balak, stand here by your burnt offering, while I meet the Lord yonder. The second attempt repeated the first exactly: the king by his offering, the prophet apart to meet the Lord, as if repetition could succeed where the first rite had failed. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: The Lord met Balaam, put a word in his mouth, and said, go again to Balak, and say thus. The second meeting matched the first: God met the prophet again, and again the word given was not the word Balak had paid for. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: When he came to him, behold, he stood by his burnt offering, and the princes of Moab with him, and Balak said to him, what has the Lord spoken? Balak waited a second time with his princes, and his question assumed the Lord might at last have changed his answer. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: He took up his parable, and said, rise up, Balak, and hear, hearken to me, you son of Zippor. JFB notes rise up, since Balak was already standing, means now attend to me, and the counsel and promises of God respecting Israel are unchangeable, so no attempt to prevail on him to reverse them will succeed as it might with a man. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: God is not a man, that he should lie, neither the son of man, that he should repent: has he said, and shall he not do it? Or has he spoken, and shall he not make it good? Henry notes men change their minds and break their words, but God never changes his mind and therefore never recalls his promise, and when Scripture says he repents, it means a change of his way, not of his mind. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Behold, I have received commandment to bless, and he has blessed, and I cannot reverse it. The commandment to bless was itself the reversal Balak feared: what God had blessed, no prophet's word could unbless. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: He has not beheld iniquity in Jacob, neither has he seen perverseness in Israel: the Lord his God is with him, and the shout of a king is among them. JFB notes many sins were observed and punished in this people, but no universal and hopeless apostasy had yet appeared to induce God to abandon or destroy them, and the shout of a king is the joyful acclamation of a people rejoicing in a victorious prince's presence. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: God brought them out of Egypt; he has as it were the strength of a unicorn. JFB notes Israel was no longer the poor, feeble, spiritless horde of the exodus, but powerful and invincible as a reem, that is, a rhinoceros. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Surely there is no enchantment against Jacob, neither is there any divination against Israel: according to this time it shall be said of Jacob and of Israel, what has God wrought! JFB notes no art can prevail against a people under the shield of Omnipotence, for whom miracles have been and yet shall be performed, a theme of admiration in succeeding ages. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Behold, the people shall rise up as a great lion, and lift up himself as a young lion: he shall not lie down until he eat of the prey, and drink the blood of the slain. Henry notes this second blessing was both larger and stronger than the former: Israel rises like a lion that does not rest until its prey is eaten, a picture of conquest still to come. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: Balak said to Balaam, neither curse them at all, nor bless them at all. Since Balaam could not say what Balak would have him say, Balak wished him to say nothing, yet, as Henry notes, though there are many devices in man's heart, God's counsels shall stand. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: Balaam answered Balak, told not I you, saying, all that the Lord speaks, that I must do? JFB calls this a remarkable confession: Balaam was divinely constrained to utter words different from what it was his purpose and inclination to do. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Balak said to Balaam, come, I pray you, I will bring you to another place; peradventure it will please God that you may curse me them from thence. Henry notes they resolved on another attempt, though they had no promise on which to build their hopes, unlike those who have a promise that the vision at the end shall speak and not lie. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: Balak brought Balaam to the top of Peor, that looks toward Jeshimon. JFB identifies Peor, or Beth-peor, as the eminence on which a temple of Baal stood, looking toward Jeshimon, the desert tract in the south of Palestine on both sides of the Dead Sea. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Balaam said to Balak, build me here seven altars, and prepare me here seven bullocks and seven rams. The third set of altars repeated the first two exactly: seven altars, seven bullocks, seven rams, as if the third repetition could move a God who had spoken twice. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: Balak did as Balaam had said, and offered a bullock and a ram on every altar. The king obeyed a third time, and the sacrifices stood ready a third time, for the oracle that would bless Israel a third time. — Matthew Henry
