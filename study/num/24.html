Study Notes — Numbers

Chapter 24

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: When Balaam saw that it pleased the Lord to bless Israel, he went not, as at other times, to seek for enchantments, but set his face toward the wilderness. JFB notes his experience on the two former occasions had taught him these superstitious accompaniments were useless, so he now simply looked toward Israel's camp, either with a secret design to curse or to await the divine afflatus. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Balaam lifted up his eyes and saw Israel abiding in his tents according to their tribes, and the Spirit of God came upon him. JFB notes the tents lay in the orderly distribution of chapter 2, and before the regular ministry of prophets was instituted, God used various persons as instruments to reveal his will, and Balaam was one of them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: He took up his parable, saying, Balaam son of Beor has said, and the man whose eyes are open has said. JFB explains the man whose eyes are open is a seer, a prophet to whom the visioned future was disclosed, sometimes in sleep and frequently in a trance. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: He has said, which heard the words of God, which saw the vision of the Almighty, falling into a trance, but having his eyes open. Henry warns many have their eyes open who have not their hearts open: they are enlightened but not sanctified, and knowledge that puffs men up with pride will but light them to hell, whither many go with their eyes open. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: How goodly are your tents, O Jacob, and your tabernacles, O Israel! JFB calls this a fine burst of admiration in highly poetical strains, noting travellers describe the beauty of a circular area of Bedouin tents in the desert, and how much more impressive Israel's immense camp over the plains must have looked from the heights of Abarim. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: As the valleys are they spread forth, as gardens by the river's side, as the trees of lign aloes which the Lord has planted, and as cedar trees beside the waters. JFB notes valleys means the brooks or watercourses of the mountains, and lign aloes, an aromatic shrub on his native Euphrates whose conical form resembles a tent, while the redundant imagery depicts Israel's humble origin, rapid progress and prosperity. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: He shall pour the water out of his buckets, and his seed shall be in many waters, and his king shall be higher than Agag, and his kingdom shall be exalted. JFB notes Agag was a title common to Amalekite kings, then the most powerful of the desert tribes, and Henry reads the verse as Israel's fruitfulness, increase, honour and advancement. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: God brought him forth out of Egypt; he has as it were the strength of a unicorn: he shall eat up the nations his enemies, and shall break their bones, and pierce them through with his arrows. Henry notes Balaam looks back on what had been done for them: their power and victory were already proved by the exodus and the conquests that followed. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: He couched, he lay down as a lion, and as a great lion: who shall stir him up? Blessed is he that blesses you, and cursed is he that curses you. Henry notes the righteous are bold as a lion, not when assaulting others but when at rest, because God makes them dwell in safety, and God takes what is done to Israel, good or evil, as done to himself. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Balak's anger was kindled against Balaam, and he smote his hands together, and said, I called you to curse my enemies, and behold, you have altogether blessed them these three times. JFB notes smiting the hands together is, among Oriental peoples, an indication of the most violent rage and of ignominious dismissal. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Therefore now flee to your place: I thought to promote you to great honour, but lo, the Lord has kept you back from honour. Balak's dismissal turned the promised honour into a reproach: the prophet who came for promotion left in disgrace, because the Lord, not Balak, controlled his words. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Balaam said to Balak, spoke I not also to your messengers which you sent to me, saying, if Balak would give me his house full of silver and gold, I cannot go beyond the commandment of the Lord? Balaam's excuse was that he had warned the messengers from the start: no fee, however great, could move him beyond God's word. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: If Balak would give me his house full of silver and gold, I cannot go beyond the commandment of the Lord, to do either good or bad of my own mind; but what the Lord says, that will I speak. Henry calls this a very full excuse: God restrained him from saying what he would have said, and constrained him to say what he would not have uttered. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: And now, behold, I go to my people: come therefore, and I will advertise you what this people shall do to your people in the latter days. Before departing, Balaam offered a final advertisement, a prophecy of latter days, which would prove more dreadful to Balak than any curse he had sought. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: He took up his parable, and said, Balaam son of Beor has said, and the man whose eyes are open has said. The fourth parable opens with the same seer formula, now in a poetical prophecy style, marking a new and farther-reaching oracle. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: He has said, which heard the words of God, and knew the knowledge of the most High, which saw the vision of the Almighty, falling into a trance, but having his eyes open. Henry notes a man may be full of the knowledge of God yet utterly destitute of his grace, expressing great respect to God as Most High and Almighty while having no true fear of him, love to him, nor faith in him, and so go far toward heaven yet come short at last. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: I shall see him, but not now: I shall behold him, but not nigh: there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel, and shall smite the corners of Moab, and destroy all the children of Sheth. JFB renders the sight as prophetic, I do see or I have seen, like Abraham's sight, and explains the Star as glory and lustre and the Sceptre as power and authority, denoting primarily David but secondarily and pre-eminently the Messiah, with corners meaning the whole border country. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: And Edom shall be a possession, Seir also shall be a possession for his enemies, and Israel shall do valiantly. JFB notes this prophecy was accomplished by David, and Seir, seen in the south, is poetically used for Edom, with the double conquest of Moab and Edom in view. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Out of Jacob shall come he that shall have dominion, and shall destroy him that remains of the city. JFB identifies the ruler as David, and particularly Christ, and the remnant of the city as those who flee from the field to fortified places. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: When he looked on Amalek, he took up his parable, and said, Amalek was the first of the nations, but his latter end shall be that he perish for ever. JFB notes Amalek's territory lay at the desert's remote extremity, and its latter end, perishing for ever, was fulfilled in its destruction under Saul and after. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: He looked on the Kenites, and took up his parable, and said, strong is your dwelling place, and you put your nest in a rock. JFB notes the Kenites were securely established among the clefts of the high rocks of En-gedi toward the west, yet Henry warns even a nest in a rock will not be a lasting security. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Nevertheless the Kenite shall be wasted, until Asshur shall carry you away captive. The security of the rock would fail by degrees: a succession of enemies would reduce the Kenites until the Assyrian invader carried them into captivity. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: He took up his parable, and said, alas, who shall live when God does this! JFB notes few shall escape the desolation that would send a Nebuchadnezzar to scourge all those regions, and Henry adds the revolutions of states and kingdoms are the Lord's doing, and those who live then will be as brands plucked out of the fire. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: And ships shall come from the coast of Chittim, and shall afflict Asshur, and shall afflict Eber, and he also shall perish for ever. JFB identifies Chittim with the Mediterranean countries, particularly Greece and Italy, whose powers overthrew Assyria, first the Greeks under Alexander and his successors, then the Romans, while Eber means the Hebrews' posterity, and the conqueror himself would also perish. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Balaam rose up, and went and returned to his place, and Balak also went his way. JFB notes Balaam's place was Mesopotamia, to which, however, he did not return, since he would die among the Midianites, and Henry closes the vain attempt: instead of cursing the church, Balaam cursed Amalek the first and Rome the last enemy of the church. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
