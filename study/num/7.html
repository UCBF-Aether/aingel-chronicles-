Study Notes — Numbers

Chapter 7

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: On the day Moses had fully set up the tabernacle and anointed it, the princes' offerings began. JFB notes day is used loosely here for a period, since several days were occupied, fixing this ceremonial about a month after the tabernacle's completion, after the Levites' separation and the people's numbering. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: The princes of Israel, heads of their fathers' houses, who had overseen the numbering, brought their offering before the Lord. JFB describes a spontaneous yet concerted movement: the leading men, not content with general rejoicing, distinguished themselves by providing carriage for the migratory tabernacle, a need the divine pattern had left to voluntary liberality. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Their offering was six covered wagons and twelve oxen, two princes partners in a wagon, each furnishing an ox. The gift was judicious and public, evidence of the pious and patriotic feeling the finished sanctuary had stirred throughout Israel. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The Lord spoke to Moses, saying, take the offering for the service of the tabernacle. JFB notes Moses may have doubted whether he could accept it without orders, given his strict injunctions to follow the divine model exactly, and God relieved him by declaring his acceptance and giving the distribution. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: The wagons and oxen were given to the Levites, to every man according to his service. JFB draws the principle: while great matters of worship must follow the revealed rule, minor arrangements may lawfully be made according to the means and convenience of God's people, as with a church bell for which no express warrant exists. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Moses took the wagons and oxen and gave them to the Levites. JFB explains the wagons were covered wheel carriages, each drawn by two oxen, like the covered wagons of the East, some brought from Egypt and others built by the tabernacle's skilled artisans. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: To the Gershonites Moses gave two wagons and four oxen, according to their service. Their burden was the light but precious coverings and hangings, so they needed fewer wagons than Merari, whose burden was heavy. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: To the Merarites Moses gave four wagons and eight oxen, according to their service under Ithamar. JFB notes the double portion was natural: Merari transported the heavy boards, bars, pillars and sockets, and the weight of metal alone makes four wagons seem barely sufficient. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: To the Kohathites Moses gave none, because the service of the sanctuary belonging to them was to be borne on their shoulders. The sacred vessels demanded the more honourable mode of conveyance, and JFB notes the later breach of this rule brought holy displeasure on David's first attempt to move the ark. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: The princes offered for dedicating the altar, bringing their offering before it. JFB explains altar here covers both the altar of burnt offering and the altar of incense, and this was an additional dedication, the first offerings made for particular persons or tribes, after Moses and Aaron's earlier dedication. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Each prince was to offer on his own day, one after another. JFB compares the scene to eastern princes presenting tribute singly and with display before their king, and notes the order followed camp rank, east, then south, then west, then north, as the sun runs its course, preventing disorder and hurry. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: He that offered the first day was Nahshon, son of Amminadab, prince of Judah. Judah had the precedence in the camp, so its prince was first admitted to offer as his tribe's representative, and Henry notes the princes, the great men, were most forward in God's service, an example to all in authority. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Nahshon's offering was a silver charger of a hundred and thirty shekels, a silver bowl of seventy shekels, both full of fine flour mingled with oil for a meat offering. JFB notes these silver vessels were for the altar of burnt offering or the court, not the holy place, whose furniture was gold, and the offering was likely furnished by the tribe's general contributions, not the prince's private means. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: He offered one spoon of gold of ten shekels, full of incense. JFB explains the spoon was a hollow cup shaped like a hand, with which priests lifted incense to the altar fire, and its golden contents show its destination was the altar of incense. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: For a burnt offering he offered one young bullock, one ram, one lamb of the first year. The animals for sacrifice served the ritual of the day, while the vessels were for permanent sanctuary use, so each day's offering joined present worship with lasting provision. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: For a sin offering he offered one kid of the goats. Henry notes that even in this time of joy and rejoicing, in the midst of their sacrifices, there was a sin offering, because in our best services we are conscious of sin, and there should be repentance even in our most joyful services. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: For a sacrifice of peace offerings he offered two oxen, five rams, five he goats, five lambs of the first year. JFB notes the peace offerings were by far the most numerous, since the princes and some of the people joined with the priests afterwards in festive rejoicing, and the feast of dedication became an anniversary festival. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: On the second day Nethaneel, prince of Issachar, offered. JFB explains the succession: Issachar stood on Judah's right side and offered next, then Zebulun on the left, and so on, every tribe making the same offering in the same amount, to show each was under equal obligation and rendered an equal tribute. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: On the third day Eliab, prince of Zebulun, offered. The separate notice given to each identical offering, on its own day, conferred equal honour on each tribe, so that none appeared overlooked or slighted, and posterity reading the record would feel a livelier interest in the national worship. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: On the fourth day Elizur, prince of Reuben, offered. The order now turned to the south camp, following the camp's own arrangement, so that the sequence of offerings traced the very order of Israel's tents around the tabernacle. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: On the fifth day Shelumiel, prince of Simeon, offered. Though each offering repeated the last in quantity and quality, its separate day and separate record marked it as a distinct act of dedication by a distinct tribe, not a mere copy. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

42 COMMENTARY: On the sixth day Eliasaph, prince of Gad, offered. The south camp's three tribes having offered in turn, the dedication moved on in the same orderly succession, God's work not done confusedly or in a hurry, as Henry puts it, but day by day. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

48 COMMENTARY: On the seventh day Elishama, prince of Ephraim, offered. JFB addresses the surprise that presentation continued on the seventh day: assuming it was a Sabbath, the work was directly religious in character and perfectly in accordance with the design of the sacred day. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

54 COMMENTARY: On the eighth day Gamaliel, prince of Manasseh, offered. The west camp now began its offerings, the order still following the camp, so that the dedication circled the tabernacle as the tribes themselves encircled it. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

60 COMMENTARY: On the ninth day Abidan, prince of Benjamin, offered. The repetition across twelve days was no burden to be resented, Henry notes: if services run twelve days together, we must not call it a task, and taking time means the work is done better. — Matthew Henry

66 COMMENTARY: On the tenth day Ahiezer, prince of Dan, offered. The north camp, which brought up the rear of the march, now brought its first offering, closing the circle of the camps in the same order in which they marched. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

72 COMMENTARY: On the eleventh day Pagiel, prince of Asher, offered. Asher, camped with Dan on the north, followed its camp leader, preserving the camp order to the end of the dedication. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

78 COMMENTARY: On the twelfth day Ahira, prince of Naphtali, offered. The last tribe's offering completed the dedication: all Israel had an equal share in the altar and an equal interest in the sacrifices, as Henry stresses, since all their offerings were the same. — Matthew Henry

84 COMMENTARY: This was the dedication of the altar, in the day when it was anointed, by the princes of Israel. JFB sums the separate items: twelve silver chargers and twelve bowls, twelve golden spoons, and two hundred and forty animals in all, a collection proving both Israel's large flocks and the rich pastures of the Sinaitic valleys. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

85 COMMENTARY: Each silver charger weighed a hundred and thirty shekels, each bowl seventy, and all the silver vessels together weighed two thousand and four hundred shekels. JFB computes the silver at over thirteen hundred ounces, worth hundreds of pounds, a costly permanent provision for the altar's service. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

86 COMMENTARY: The golden spoons were twelve, each of ten shekels, and all the gold of the spoons was a hundred and twenty shekels. The gold, reserved for incense service, marked the spoons as vessels of the inner worship, distinct from the silver of the court. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

87 COMMENTARY: All the oxen for the burnt offering were twelve, the rams twelve, the lambs twelve, with their meat offering, and the kids of the goats for sin offering twelve. The burnt and sin offerings, repeated twelve times over, kept atonement at the centre of even this joyful dedication. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

88 COMMENTARY: All the oxen for the sacrifice of the peace offerings were twenty and four, the rams sixty, the he goats sixty, the lambs sixty. The great number of peace offerings shows the dedication ended in shared feasting, the tribes rejoicing together with the priests before God. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

89 COMMENTARY: When Moses went into the tabernacle to speak with him, he heard the voice of one speaking to him from off the mercy seat, from between the two cherubims. JFB notes that as a king gives private audience to his minister, so Moses, though not a priest, was admitted into the sanctuary to receive instructions from his heavenly King. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
