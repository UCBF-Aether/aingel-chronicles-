Study Notes — Numbers

Chapter 15

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

2 COMMENTARY: When you come into the land of your habitation which I give you, and will make an offering by fire to the Lord, the law of proportions applies. Henry notes the opening is very encouraging after chapter 14: it plainly intimates God would secure the promised land to their seed, despite the sentence on that generation. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: The offering by fire might be a burnt offering, a sacrifice in performing a vow, a freewill offering, or in solemn feasts, to make a sweet savour to the Lord. Henry explains these sacrifices of acknowledgment were intended as the food of God's table, requiring a constant supply of bread, oil and wine whatever the flesh meat was. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: He that offers his offering shall bring a meat offering of a tenth deal of flour mingled with a fourth part of a hin of oil. The intent of the law, Henry says, is to direct the proportions: every flesh offering carried its measured bread and oil, so that God's table was furnished in full. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: The fourth part of a hin of wine for a drink offering was prepared with the burnt offering or sacrifice, for one lamb. The drink offering matched the meat offering in scale, a quarter hin for the smallest animal, rising with the size of the sacrifice. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: For a ram, the meat offering was two tenth deals of flour mingled with oil. The proportion doubled for the ram, keeping the bread in step with the flesh, neither stinted nor excessive. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: For a drink offering with the ram, the third part of a hin of wine was offered for a sweet savour. Wine, like flour and oil, rose by fixed steps: a fourth for a lamb, a third for a ram, a half for a bullock. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: When a bullock was prepared for a burnt offering, or a sacrifice in performing a vow, or peace offerings, the same law of proportions governed it. The largest animal carried the largest accompaniment, so that no offering came to God's table bare. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: With a bullock he brought a meat offering of three tenth deals of flour mingled with half a hin of oil. The three tenths and half hin complete the ascending scale, lamb, ram, bullock, each with its appointed measure. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: For a drink offering with the bullock, half a hin of wine was brought, for an offering made by fire of a sweet savour to the Lord. The half hin crowned the scale, and the sweet savour belonged to the whole offering, flesh, bread, oil and wine together. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Thus it was done for one bullock, or one ram, or a lamb, or a kid. The rule was universal across the kinds: no animal was offered without its proportioned bread and wine. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: According to the number prepared, so it was done to every one according to their number. The proportions multiplied with the offerings: ten lambs meant ten measures, not one measure stretched over ten, so that God's table was furnished for every sacrifice. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: All that are born of the country shall do these things after this manner, in offering an offering made by fire of a sweet savour to the Lord. The native Israelite was bound by the same measures as everyone else: birth in the land gave no exemption from the law of the table. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: If a stranger sojourns with you, or whoever is among you in your generations, and will offer an offering made by fire of a sweet savour to the Lord, he shall do as you do. The stranger who worshipped Israel's God worshipped by Israel's measures, a happy forewarning, Henry says, of the Gentiles' calling and admission into the church. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: One ordinance shall be both for you of the congregation and for the stranger that sojourns with you, an ordinance for ever in your generations: as you are, so shall the stranger be before the Lord. The permanence matters: the equality was not a temporary concession but a standing ordinance. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: One law and one manner shall be for you, and for the stranger that sojourns with you. Henry draws the gospel conclusion: if the law made so little difference between Jew and Gentile, much less would the gospel, which broke down the partition wall and reconciled both to God. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Speak to Israel: when you come into the land whither I bring you, the law of the dough offering will apply. Like the offering law of verse 2, this law was given in the wilderness for a land not yet entered, a promise in itself that Israel would eat Canaan's bread. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Then it shall be, that when you eat of the bread of the land, you shall offer up a heave offering to the Lord. The heave offering began at the eating itself: the first use of Canaan's harvest was God's, before Israel's own tables were spread. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: You shall offer up a cake of the first of your dough for a heave offering, as you do the heave offering of the threshing floor. The first cake, like the first sheaf, acknowledged that the whole harvest came from God, and the first portion sanctified the rest. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Of the first of your dough you shall give to the Lord a heave offering in your generations. The gift was perpetual, generation after generation, so that every household's baking remembered the Giver. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: If you have erred and not observed all these commandments which the Lord has spoken to Moses, even all he commanded by Moses from the day he gave commandment onward, the law of ignorance applies. Henry notes ignorance will in a degree excuse, but will not justify those who might have known their Lord's will yet did it not, and David prayed to be cleansed from secret faults he was not aware of. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: Even all that the Lord commanded you by the hand of Moses, from the day the Lord gave commandment and onward throughout your generations, is covered by this law when missed through ignorance. The scope is total: no commandment was too small for atonement, and none too great to be missed unknowingly without remedy. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: If anything was committed by ignorance without the knowledge of the congregation, the whole congregation offered one young bullock for a burnt offering with its meat and drink offering, and one kid of the goats for a sin offering. The congregation's ignorance was atoned corporately, with burnt offering for dedication and sin offering for guilt. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: The priest made an atonement for all the congregation of Israel, and it was forgiven them, for it was ignorance. Henry notes sins committed ignorantly are forgiven through Christ the great Sacrifice, who on the cross seemed to explain one part of his offering's intention in praying, Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: It was forgiven to all the congregation and to the stranger that sojourned among them, seeing all the people were in ignorance. Henry observes it looked favourably upon the Gentiles that this law of atoning for sins of ignorance was expressly made to extend to strangers to Israel. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: If any soul sinned through ignorance, he brought a she goat of the first year for a sin offering. The individual's ignorance had its own offering, smaller than the congregation's, but of the same kind: a sin offering for a sin not intended. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: The priest made an atonement for the soul that sinned ignorantly, when he sinned by ignorance before the Lord, and it was forgiven him. Atonement was personal as well as corporate: the ignorant soul, not only the ignorant congregation, was forgiven. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: You shall have one law for him that sins through ignorance, both for him born among Israel and for the stranger that sojourns among them. The same one law that governed offerings governed ignorance: native and stranger stood equal before atonement as before the table. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: But the soul that does anything presumptuously, whether born in the land or a stranger, reproaches the Lord, and that soul shall be cut off from among his people. Henry defines the presumptuous sinner as one who sins designedly against God's will and glory, whose sins are exceedingly sinful because he reproaches the Lord himself. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: Because he has despised the word of the Lord and broken his commandment, that soul shall utterly be cut off, and his iniquity shall be upon him. Henry adds the presumptuous despise the word, thinking themselves too great, too good, and too wise to be ruled by it, so the guilt remains on them without atonement. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: While Israel was in the wilderness, they found a man gathering sticks on the Sabbath day. Henry gives the particular instance of presumption: gathering sticks to make a fire, whereas the people were to bake and seethe what they needed the day before, was done as an affront both to the law and to the Lawgiver. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: Those who found him gathering sticks brought him to Moses and Aaron and to all the congregation. The case was brought to the appointed judges rather than settled by the finders: God was the Judge, and before him they brought this cause. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: They put him in ward, because it was not declared what should be done to him. The delay was judicial, not negligent: the sentence for this new case had not yet been declared, so the man was held until God spoke. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: The Lord said to Moses, the man shall surely be put to death: all the congregation shall stone him with stones without the camp. Henry notes the sentence adjudged him a Sabbath-breaker according to the law's intent, and the most terrible death was appointed to show how great the crime was and how displeasing to God, that others might hear and fear. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: All the congregation brought him without the camp and stoned him with stones, and he died, as the Lord commanded Moses. Henry notes as many as could were employed in the execution, that those at least who threw a stone might be afraid of breaking the Sabbath, and God intended the exemplary punishment as a standing warning to make conscience of keeping the Sabbath holy. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: Israel was bid to make fringes in the borders of their garments throughout their generations, and put upon the fringe a ribband of blue. Henry explains the Jews were distinguished from neighbours in dress as in diet, taught not to be conformed to heathen ways, proclaiming themselves Jews wherever they were, as not ashamed of God and his law, and the fringes were memorandums to prevent sin through forgetfulness. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: The fringe was to be looked upon, to remember all the Lord's commandments and do them, and not to seek after your own heart and eyes, after which you go a whoring. Henry notes the fringes were not for trimming or adorning clothes, but to stir up minds by remembrance, and if tempted to sin, the fringe would warn them not to break God's commandments. — Matthew Henry

40 COMMENTARY: That you may remember and do all my commandments, and be holy unto your God. Henry's summary is that we should use every means of refreshing our memories with God's truths and precepts, to strengthen and quicken obedience and arm our minds against temptation. — Matthew Henry

41 COMMENTARY: I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, to be your God: I am the Lord your God. Henry notes this great reason for all the commandments is again and again repeated, and holiness means cleansed from sin and sincerely devoted to his service. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 1, 17, 37 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
