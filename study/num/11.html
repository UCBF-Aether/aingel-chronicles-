Study Notes — Numbers

Chapter 11

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: When the people complained, it displeased the Lord, and his fire burned among them and consumed those in the uttermost parts of the camp. JFB notes the discontent seems confined to the camp's extremities, where the mixed multitude likely stood, and Henry adds that God knows secret frettings of the heart, though Moses did not, and his judgments came gradually, that they might take warning. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: The people cried to Moses, and when Moses prayed, the fire was quenched. The judgment stopped at intercession, showing, as Henry says, that God delights not in punishing: when he begins, he is soon prevailed with to let it fall. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: He called the place Taberah, because the fire of the Lord burnt among them. The name remained a monument of national sin and punishment, as JFB notes, just as Kibroth-hattaavah would later name the graves of lust. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The mixed multitude among them fell a lusting, and Israel wept again, saying, who shall give us flesh to eat? JFB identifies the multitude as Egyptians, whose appetite for animal food became a disease of imagination, and Israel, incited by their clamours, joined the complaint against the manna. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: They remembered the fish eaten freely in Egypt, the cucumbers, melons, leeks, onions and garlic. JFB details the Egyptian diet: fish from Nile lakes and canals in April and May, the smooth foot-long cucumber, juicy watermelons, sweet onions that fed the labouring classes, and garlic once abundant, all grateful food in warm countries. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Now their soul was dried away, with nothing at all beside this manna before their eyes. JFB notes daily familiarity had disgusted them with the monotonous food, and Henry answers their scorn: the manna cost no money or care and little labour, yet they talked of Egypt's cheapness as if it had cost nothing, when they paid dearly for it with hard service. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: The manna was like coriander seed, its colour like bdellium. JFB explains the resemblance was in size and figure, not colour, and bdellium, a drop of white gum or a white pearl, helps picture it, while its taste in the raw state was like fresh oil, and when ground and baked, like wafers made with honey. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: The people went about gathering it, ground it in mills or beat it in mortars, baked it in pans, and made cakes of it. JFB notes this proves it was not the natural manna of the Arabian desert, which is too gummy to be ground into meal, and the minute description shows the sinfulness of dissatisfaction with such excellent food, furnished plentifully and gratuitously. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: When the dew fell on the camp at night, the manna fell upon it. The nightly provision, arriving with the dew, was a daily, unearned gift, which makes the people's contempt for it, in Henry's words, the fault-finding of peevish minds with that which has no fault but that it is too good for them. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Moses heard the people weep throughout their families, every man at his tent door, and the anger of the Lord was kindled greatly, and Moses was displeased. The weeping was universal, family by family, and it pressed on Moses as a public crisis, not a private grumble. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Moses asked the Lord why he had afflicted his servant, and why he had not found favour, that the burden of all this people was laid on him. JFB sympathises with Moses' distress, though the tone cannot be justified: he had a mighty multitude under his care with no means of satisfying their clamorous demands, and felt the undivided responsibility of his office. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Moses asked, have I conceived all this people, have I begotten them, that you should say, carry them in your bosom as a nursing father carries a sucking child? Henry notes Moses magnified his own performances and undervalued the honour God had put on him, speaking as if the people were his own burden rather than God's charge. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Moses asked, whence should I have flesh to give all this people, for they weep to me, saying, give us flesh that we may eat? The question exposes his helplessness: the demand was real, the people were clamorous, and Moses had no store from which to answer them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Moses said, I am not able to bear all this people alone, because it is too heavy for me. Henry's lesson stands over the verse: had the work been much less, Moses could not have done it in his own strength, but had it been much greater, through God strengthening him he might have done it. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Moses prayed, if you deal thus with me, kill me out of hand, if I have found favour, and let me not see my wretchedness. Henry judges the prayer as expressed otherwise than became him, yet it reveals a leader agonised and almost overwhelmed, and teaches us to pray, lead us not into temptation. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: The Lord told Moses to gather seventy men of the elders of Israel, whom he knew to be elders and officers, and bring them to the tabernacle to stand with him. JFB notes the order of seventy, whether selected or new, was empowered to assist by collective wisdom and experience, and Jewish writers call it the origin of the Sanhedrin, though it was likely only a temporary expedient. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: God would come down and talk with Moses there, and take of the spirit upon him and put it on them, that they might bear the burden with him. JFB explains the spirit means the gifts and influences of the Spirit, especially prophecy, an extraordinary penetration in discovering hidden things, and taking it did not impair Moses at all. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Moses was to tell the people to sanctify themselves against tomorrow, for they would eat flesh. JFB notes prepare yourselves by repentance and submission, but the tenor implied a severe rebuke: the blessing promised would prove a curse. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: They would not eat one day, nor two, nor five, nor ten, nor twenty days, but a whole month. Henry's moral is the vanity of all delights of sense: they will cloy but not satisfy, while spiritual pleasures alone satisfy and last. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: They would eat a whole month, until it came out at their nostrils and became loathsome to them, because they despised the Lord who was among them and wept before him, saying, why came we out of Egypt? The loathing was the judgment itself: the desired flesh would sicken them, because they had despised the God who gave them manna. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Moses objected that the people among whom he stood were six hundred thousand footmen, yet God said, shall flesh be given them for a whole month? JFB notes the great leader, struck by so astonishing a promise for more than two million people in a desert, betrayed a momentary incredulous spirit, uttered only to himself, sharply reproved but not punished. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Moses asked, shall the flocks and herds be slain for them to suffice them, or shall all the fish of the sea be gathered together for them? Henry notes even true and great believers sometimes find it hard to trust God under the discouragements of second causes, and against hope to believe in hope. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: The Lord answered, is the Lord's hand waxed short? You shall see now whether my word shall come to pass to you or not. Henry notes God brings Moses to this point: the Lord God is Almighty, and puts the proof upon the issue, for if he speaks, it is done. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Moses went out and told the people the Lord's words, and gathered the seventy elders and set them round about the tabernacle. The tabernacle was chosen for the convocation because there God manifested himself, there his Spirit would be imparted, and the elders' office invested with reverence in the people's eyes. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: The Lord came down in a cloud, spoke to Moses, took of the spirit on him and gave it to the seventy elders, and when it rested on them they prophesied and did not cease. JFB explains their prophesying as the performance of civil and sacred duties by extraordinary endowment, continued either uninterruptedly that first day or as permanent gifts qualifying them as magistrates. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Two men remained in the camp, Eldad and Medad, who were written among the elders but did not go out to the tabernacle, and the spirit rested on them and they prophesied in the camp. JFB suggests modesty or ceremonial defilement kept them back, yet they received the Spirit as fully as their brethren, and Henry adds the Spirit is not confined to the tabernacle but blows where he wills. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: A young man ran and told Moses that Eldad and Medad prophesied in the camp. The report reached Moses at once, because prophesying outside the appointed place looked like a breach of order, and the camp, not the tabernacle, was where it was happening. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Joshua, Moses' servant, answered, my lord Moses, forbid them. Henry notes Joshua did not desire punishment, only restraint for the future, out of zeal for what he thought was the unity of the church, lest they occasion schism or rival Moses. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Moses answered, enviest thou for my sake? Would God that all the Lord's people were prophets, and that the Lord would put his Spirit upon them! Henry draws the church lesson: shall we reject those whom Christ has owned, or restrain any from doing good because they are not in everything of our mind? — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: Moses got him into the camp, he and the elders of Israel. The convocation ended with the elders returning to the camp, now endowed and authorised to share the burden Moses had carried alone. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: A wind from the Lord brought quails from the sea and let them fall by the camp, about a day's journey on either side, two cubits high on the face of the earth. JFB explains the migratory birds were on their journey from Egypt when an east wind forced them over the Red Sea to Israel, in countless numbers covering a vast space, flying low enough to be easily caught. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: The people stood up all that day, all that night, and all the next day, gathering quails, he that gathered least gathering ten homers, and they spread them abroad round the camp. JFB notes they rose in eager haste, gathering not for immediate use only but for the future, distrustful of God's goodness, and salted and dried the quails as they had been accustomed in Egypt. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: While the flesh was yet between their teeth, before it was chewed, the Lord's wrath was kindled and he smote them with a very great plague. JFB notes their stomachs, long inured to light manna, were unprepared for sudden heavy animal food taken intemperately, and their sin, proceeding not from necessity but wanton lust, was made to carry its own punishment. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: He called the place Kibroth-hattaavah, because there they buried the people that lusted. The name means the graves of lust, and JFB notes it proves the mortality was confined to those who had indulged inordinately. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: The people journeyed from Kibroth-hattaavah to Hazeroth and abode there. JFB identifies Hazeroth as the extreme southern station of this route, a watering-place in a spacious plain, now Ain-Haderah, where the host rested after the plague. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
