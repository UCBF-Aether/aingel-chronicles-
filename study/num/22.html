Study Notes — Numbers

Chapter 22

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Israel set forward and pitched in the plains of Moab on this side Jordan by Jericho. The host now lay across the Jordan from Jericho, within sight of the land, and Moab, watching from its plains, saw the conquerors of Sihon and Og encamped on its border. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Balak son of Zippor saw all that Israel had done to the Amorites. The sight of two Amorite kingdoms fallen was Balak's motive: Moab's king measured Israel by what it had done to his neighbours, and feared he would be next. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Moab was sore afraid of the people, because they were many, and Moab was distressed because of the children of Israel. Fear, not grievance, drove Moab: Israel had done Moab no wrong, but its numbers alone distressed a smaller kingdom. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Moab said to the elders of Midian, now shall this company lick up all that are round about us, as the ox licks up the grass of the field. The image is of total consumption: Moab joined with Midian, its old neighbour, to find a weapon against a host it pictured as devouring everything around it. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: He sent messengers to Balaam son of Beor, to Pethor by the river of the land of the children of his people, to call him. Balak sent a great way for the prophet of greatest reputation, since his plan needed not an army but a curse: to set God against a people for whom God had hitherto fought. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Come now therefore, I pray you, curse me this people, for they are too mighty for me: peradventure I shall prevail, that we may smite them, and drive them out of the land, for I know that he whom you bless is blessed, and he whom you curse is cursed. Henry notes Balak's false notion: that if a prophet prayed evil on Israel and blessing on Moab's forces, he could then deal with them, as if God's favour could be hired by a curse. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: The elders of Moab and Midian departed with the rewards of divination in their hand, and came to Balaam and spoke Balak's words to him. The wages of unrighteousness travelled with the message: Balak paid in advance for the curse he wanted, and the elders carried the fee openly. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: He said to them, lodge here this night, and I will bring you word again, as the Lord shall speak to me, and the princes of Moab abode with Balaam. Henry notes Balaam ought to have answered at once that he would never curse a people God had blessed, but he took a night to consider, and when we parley with temptations we are in great danger of being overcome. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: God came to Balaam and said, what men are these with you? The question exposed the embassy: God asked not for information but to bring Balaam to own the company he was keeping and the errand he was entertaining. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Balaam said to God, Balak son of Zippor, king of Moab, has sent to me, saying, behold, there is a people come out of Egypt, which covers the face of the earth. Balaam reported the embassy faithfully in its facts, yet without the refusal that knowledge of Israel's cause should have produced at once. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Behold, there is a people come out of Egypt, which covers the face of the earth: come now, curse me them, peradventure I shall be able to overcome them and drive them out. The message as Balaam relayed it made Israel's multitude the very reason for cursing: a people covering the earth was, to Balak, a people to be shrunk by a prophet's word. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: God said to Balaam, you shall not go with them, you shall not curse the people, for they are blessed. The restraint was absolute and grounded in blessing: a people God had blessed could not be cursed, whatever fee was offered or embassy sent. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Balaam rose up in the morning and said to Balak's princes, get into your land, for the Lord refuses to give me leave to go with you. Henry notes Balaam was not faithful in returning God's answer: he lessened the divine restraint, as if going against God's law were only going without his leave, instead of reporting the flat prohibition and the blessing. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: The princes of Moab rose up, went to Balak, and said, Balaam refuses to come with us. The messengers were likewise unfaithful, reporting a refusal rather than God's prohibition, so that Balak was abused by flattery and prevented from seeing the fault in his own design. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Balak sent yet again princes, more, and more honourable than they. The second embassy was grander than the first, as if honour could succeed where a fee had failed, and Henry notes it were well if we were as earnest in a good work, notwithstanding disappointments, as Balak was in this evil one. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: They came to Balaam and said, thus says Balak son of Zippor, let nothing, I pray you, hinder you from coming to me. The urgency grew with the embassy: Balak would let nothing hinder, neither God's refusal nor Balaam's reluctance, and pressed the prophet as if refusal were merely a matter of persuasion. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: For I will promote you to very great honour, and I will do whatsoever you say to me: come therefore, I pray you, curse me this people. Henry notes Balak laid a bait not only for covetousness but for pride and ambition, and sinners stick at no pains, spare no cost, and care not how low they stoop, to gratify malice. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Balaam answered the servants of Balak, if Balak would give me his house full of silver and gold, I cannot go beyond the word of the Lord my God, to do less or more. Henry notes no man could have spoken better, yet words do not judge a man: Balaam's convictions charged him to keep God's command, while his corruptions inclined him to go contrary to it. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Now therefore, I pray you, tarry you also here this night, that I may know what the Lord will speak to me more. Henry's verdict is sharp: he seemed to refuse the temptation but expressed no abhorrence of it, having a strong desire to accept and hoping God might give him leave, and it is certain evidence of corruption ruling the heart to beg leave to sin after already being told God's will. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: God came to Balaam at night and said, if the men come to call you, rise up and go with them, but yet the word which I shall say to you, that shall you do. Henry explains God gave Balaam up to his own heart's lusts: as God sometimes denies his people's prayers in love, so sometimes he grants the wicked's desires in wrath. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Balaam rose up in the morning, saddled his ass, and went with the princes of Moab. The eagerness of the rising and saddling shows which way his heart already leaned: permission was taken as encouragement, and the journey began at once. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: God's anger was kindled because he went, and the angel of the Lord stood in the way for an adversary against him, while he rode upon his ass with two servants. Henry warns we must not think God's providence in not restraining men from sin means he approves it: the holy angels oppose sin, and this angel was an adversary to Balaam precisely because Balaam counted him an adversary. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: The ass saw the angel of the Lord standing in the way, his sword drawn in his hand, and the ass turned aside out of the way and went into the field, and Balaam smote the ass to turn her into the way. The beast saw what the prophet could not: the drawn sword in the road, and her turning was the first mercy that stopped his progress. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: But the angel of the Lord stood in a path of the vineyards, a wall on this side and a wall on that side. The second standing narrowed the way: where the open field had allowed escape, the vineyard walls shut the prophet in with the angel. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: When the ass saw the angel of the Lord, she thrust herself to the wall and crushed Balaam's foot against the wall, and he smote her again. The crush was the second warning, costing Balaam pain, yet he answered it with a second beating, pushing on violently through the difficulties Providence laid in his way. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: The angel of the Lord went further and stood in a narrow place, where there was no way to turn either to the right hand or to the left. The third standing left no escape at all: the prophet's road was now a corridor with the angel at its end, and the beast could go neither way. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: When the ass saw the angel of the Lord, she fell down under Balaam, and Balaam's anger was kindled, and he smote the ass with a staff. The falling was the beast's last resort, and Balaam's anger, kindled against the creature that had saved him three times, shows a heart fully set on evil. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: The Lord opened the mouth of the ass, and she said to Balaam, what have I done to you, that you have smitten me these three times? Henry notes this great miracle was wrought by God's power: he who made man speak could, when he pleased, make the ass speak with man's voice, and the ass complained of Balaam's cruelty. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Balaam said to the ass, because you have mocked me: I would there were a sword in my hand, for now would I kill you. The prophet, who could not see the angel's sword, wished for a sword of his own to kill the beast that had seen it, a blindness Henry holds up as the picture of a hard heart. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: The ass said to Balaam, am not I your ass, upon which you have ridden ever since I was yours to this day? Was I ever wont to do so to you? And he said, nay. Henry notes the righteous God does not allow the meanest or weakest to be abused, but they shall speak in their own defence, or he will somehow speak for them, and the beast's faithful record condemned its rider. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: Then the Lord opened the eyes of Balaam, and he saw the angel of the Lord standing in the way, and his sword drawn in his hand, and he bowed down his head and fell flat on his face. Henry notes God has many ways to bring down the hard and unhumbled heart, and when our eyes are opened we see the danger of sinful ways and how much it was for our advantage to be crossed. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: The angel of the Lord said to him, wherefore have you smitten your ass these three times? Behold, I went out to withstand you, because your way is perverse before me. The three smitings were now answered by three turnings: the beast had turned from the angel three times, and each turning had been punished by its rider. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: And the ass saw me, and turned from me these three times: unless she had turned from me, surely now also I had slain you, and saved her alive. The angel's sentence reversed Balaam's values: the beast he would have killed had saved his life, and the prophet he counted himself stood condemned. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: Balaam said to the angel of the Lord, I have sinned, for I knew not that you stood in the way against me: now therefore, if it displease you, I will get me back again. Henry notes Balaam seemed to relent, but it does not appear he was sensible of his heart's wickedness: if he cannot go forward, he will be content to go back, as many leave their sins only because their sins have left them. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: The angel of the Lord said to Balaam, go with the men, but only the word that I shall speak to you, that you shall speak, and Balaam went with the princes of Balak. The permission continued, but with a sterner condition: he should not only be unable to curse Israel, but forced to bless them, more for God's glory and his own confusion than if he had turned back. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: When Balak heard that Balaam was come, he went out to meet him to a city of Moab, which is in the border of Arnon, at the utmost coast. Balak travelled to his own border to meet the prophet, honouring the man he had hired before a single word had been spoken. — Matthew Henry

37 COMMENTARY: Balak said to Balaam, did I not earnestly send to you to call you? Wherefore came you not to me? Am I not able indeed to promote you to honour? Balak's complaint was only that Balaam had not come sooner: the king who had been refused by God now reproached the prophet for delay, as if honour were the only question. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: Balaam said to Balak, lo, I am come to you: have I now any power at all to say anything? The word that God puts in my mouth, that shall I speak. Henry notes Balaam bids Balak not depend too much on him, speaking with seeming vexation, yet really as desirous to please Balak as ever he had pretended to be to please God. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: Balaam went with Balak, and they came to Kirjath-huzoth. The prophet travelled on with the king to the city of streets, where the sacrifices of welcome would prepare the way for the attempted curse. — Matthew Henry

40 COMMENTARY: Balak offered oxen and sheep, and sent to Balaam, and to the princes that were with him. The offerings feasted prophet and princes together, binding Balaam to Balak's table before he was taken to the high places to look on Israel. — Matthew Henry

41 COMMENTARY: It came to pass on the morrow, that Balak took Balaam, and brought him up into the high places of Baal, that thence he might see the utmost part of the people. The high places of Baal were chosen for the view: from there Balaam could see Israel's utmost part, and the curse was to be aimed at a people spread before his eyes. — Matthew Henry
