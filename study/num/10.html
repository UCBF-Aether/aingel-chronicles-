Study Notes — Numbers

Chapter 10

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

2 COMMENTARY: Moses was to make two trumpets of silver, of a whole piece. JFB describes them as straight, a cubit or more long, of solid silver for a shrill, distinct sound, unlike the curved Egyptian trumpets, and two in number probably because Aaron had two sons. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: When both trumpets were blown, all the assembly gathered at the tabernacle door. JFB explains the signals were distinguished by loudness and variety of notes: a uniform sound by both trumpets summoned the general assembly, a single trumpet the princes, and other notes sounded alarms for journeying or war. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: If but one trumpet was blown, the princes, heads of the thousands of Israel, gathered to Moses. The distinct sound matched the persons addressed, as Henry stresses: the gospel trumpet likewise must give a distinct sound according to the persons addressed or the end proposed. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: When an alarm was blown, the camps on the east went forward. One alarm was the recognised signal for Judah's eastern division, the van of the host, to march. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: When an alarm was blown the second time, the camps on the south took their journey. JFB adds that the Septuagint supplies the rest of the sequence: three alarms for the west, four for the north, so that no military march could be better regulated. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: When the congregation was to be gathered, they blew, but did not sound an alarm. Gathering and marching had different sounds, so that the people were never left to guess whether they were summoned to assemble or to move. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: The sons of Aaron, the priests, blew the trumpets, an ordinance forever. JFB stresses no Levite or common Israelite could hold this office: it was reserved to the priests as the Lord's ministers, and blown from the tabernacle, the visible residence of Israel's King, as alarm trumpets in Persia sounded from the sovereign's tent. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: If Israel went to war against an oppressor, the priests blew an alarm and Israel would be remembered before the Lord and saved. JFB calls this an act of devout confidence, later acted on in battle, whose deeper meaning is that God would, as it were, be aroused by the trumpet to bless with his presence and aid. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Also in the day of gladness, solemn days and beginnings of months, the trumpets sounded over burnt and peace offerings, as a memorial before God. JFB notes festive occasions were ushered in with trumpets to intimate the joyous feelings with which Israel engaged in God's service. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: On the twentieth day of the second month of the second year, the cloud was taken up from the tabernacle of testimony. JFB reckons Israel had lain at Sinai eleven months and twenty-nine days, an interval of repose after Egyptian oppression and of moral education, fitting them for the warlike peoples of Canaan. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Israel took their journeys out of the wilderness of Sinai, and the cloud rested in the wilderness of Paran. JFB describes Paran as stretching from the Sinaitic group over a plateau to the southwestern borders of Palestine, and Henry warns that all our removes are but from one wilderness to another, until heaven. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: They first took their journey according to the Lord's commandment by the hand of Moses. JFB pictures Moses stationed on an eminence watching the ranks defile in order, with extraordinary care for the safe conveyance of the holy things in the actual migration. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: In the first place went the standard of Judah's camp according to its armies. Judah, with Issachar and Zebulun, led the van, and behind it followed Gershon and Merari with the heavy materials of the tabernacle. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Then the tabernacle was taken down, and the Gershonites and Merarites bore it, setting forward after Judah. Their place behind the van meant the framework could be reared at the new encampment before the sacred vessels arrived. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Then the standard of Reuben's camp set forward according to its armies. Reuben's division formed the second great body on the march, following the tabernacle's framework bearers. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Then the Kohathites set forward, bearing the sanctuary, while the others set up the tabernacle before they came. The Kohathites occupied the centre of the moving mass, so far behind Gershon and Merari that the framework stood ready to receive the holy things on arrival. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Then the standard of Ephraim's camp set forward according to its armies. Ephraim's division followed the sanctuary itself, guarding the sacred centre from behind as Judah had guarded it from before. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Then the standard of Dan's camp set forward, the rereward of all the camps throughout their hosts. Dan, with Asher and Naphtali, brought up the rear of the immense caravan, closing the host as Judah had opened it. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Thus were the journeyings of Israel according to their armies, when they set forward. The summary seals the chapter's order: every tribe marched under its prince and rallied around its own standard, in the order appointed at Sinai. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Moses said to Hobab, son of Raguel the Midianite, Moses' father in law, that they were journeying to the place God promised, and asked him to come and do them good. JFB identifies Raguel with Reuel, that is Jethro, making Hobab Moses' brother-in-law, who had sojourned with Israel throughout the Sinai encampment. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: Hobab answered, I will not go, but will depart to my own land and kindred. His first answer was refusal: the ties of home and clan pulled against the invitation, and Moses' urging had to overcome a settled purpose to return. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: Moses urged, leave us not, for you know how we are to encamp in the wilderness, and you may be to us instead of eyes. JFB answers the surprise at Moses' importunity despite the cloud: the cloud showed the general route, not the minute places of pasture, shade and water hidden by shifting sands, and Hobab, a prince of a powerful clan, could serve detachments sent from the main body. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: If Hobab went with them, whatever goodness the Lord showed to Israel, they would do the same to him. The inducement was shared blessing, yet JFB notes it seems not to have changed the young man's purpose, for he departed and settled in his own district. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: They departed from the mount of the Lord three days' journey, and the ark of the covenant went before them to search out a resting place. JFB notes the first day's progress was small, about eighteen or twenty miles, and the cloudy pillar, which rested on the ark while stationary, preceded them in the march. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: The cloud of the Lord was upon them by day when they went out of the camp. The visible sign did not stay behind at Sinai: it travelled with the host, covering the march as it had covered the encampment. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: When the ark set forward, Moses said, Rise up, Lord, and let thine enemies be scattered, and those that hate thee flee before thee. JFB notes Moses, as the people's organ, uttered a prayer at the commencement and end of each journey, so that all the journeys were sanctified by devotion, and Henry adds that God's arising alone suffices to scatter his enemies. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: When the ark rested, Moses said, Return, O Lord, unto the many thousands of Israel. Henry notes the welfare of Israel consisted in God's continual presence among them: their safety lay not in numbers but in God's favour and gracious return to rest with them. — Matthew Henry

No note for verse(s) 1, 15, 16, 19, 20, 23, 24, 26, 27 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
