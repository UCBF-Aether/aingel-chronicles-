Study Notes — 2 Kings

Chapter 14

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: In the second year of Joash son of Jehoahaz king of Israel, Amaziah son of Joash king of Judah began to reign. JFB's section "Amaziah's good reign over Judah" opens with this accession. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Amaziah was twenty-five when he began to reign and reigned twenty-nine years in Jerusalem, his mother Jehoaddan of Jerusalem. JFB's section frames the beginning of his reign as excellent, acting as a constitutional king according to God's law. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Amaziah did right in the LORD's sight, yet not like David his father, doing according to all his father Joash did. JFB notes his reign began excellently yet not with perfect sincerity of heart, and his early promise was belied by his later devious course and the public irregularities he tolerated. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Howbeit the high places were not taken away, and the people still sacrificed and burnt incense on them. JFB cites these tolerated irregularities as part of the failure of Amaziah's later course. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: As soon as the kingdom was confirmed in his hand, Amaziah slew his servants who had slain his father the king. JFB calls this an act of justice and filial piety, noting the assassins must have had considerable weight, since the king dared not proceed against them until his power was fully consolidated. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: But the children of the murderers he slew not, according to the law of Moses that fathers shall not be put to death for children, nor children for fathers. JFB praises this moderation, inspired by the Mosaic law, as directly contrary to ancient custom, under which all connected with criminals were doomed to unsparing destruction. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Amaziah slew ten thousand Edomites in the valley of salt, took Selah by war, and called it Joktheel to this day. JFB explains the Edomites had revolted under Joram, and Amaziah, determined to reduce them, routed their army and took their capital; the valley of salt was the salt plain south of the Dead Sea, Selah was "the rock," generally thought to be Petra, and Joktheel meant "given or conquered by God." — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Amaziah sent messengers to Jehoash king of Israel, saying, "Come, let us look one another in the face." JFB calls this bold, haughty challenge most probably stimulated by desire for satisfaction over discharged Israelite auxiliaries' outrages and revenge for Jehu's massacre of his ancestors, springing from pride inspired by the Edomite victory. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Jehoash answered with the fable of the thistle in Lebanon that sent to the cedar seeking marriage for his son, until a wild beast passed by and trode down the thistle. JFB explains Eastern speakers often used parables for unwelcome truths: the thistle represented Amaziah, a petty prince, the cedar the powerful king of Israel, and the wild beast the overwhelming army that could desolate Judah. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Jehoash warned that Amaziah's heart was lifted up by smiting Edom, and he should glory in that and tarry at home rather than meddle to his hurt and Judah's fall. JFB notes the moral of the fable is contained here: pride towering beyond its natural sphere is sure to fall with a sudden, ruinous crash. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: But Amaziah would not hear, so Jehoash went up, and the two kings faced each other at Bethshemesh in Judah. JFB explains the sarcastic reply incited Amaziah the more, for in judicial blindness he was immovably determined on war, but Joash's superior energy surprised him before his preparations were complete. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Judah was put to the worse before Israel, and every man fled to his tent. JFB records Joash routing Amaziah's army in pitched battle and taking him prisoner. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Jehoash took Amaziah at Bethshemesh, came to Jerusalem, broke down its wall from the Ephraim gate to the corner gate, four hundred cubits. JFB notes Joash marched to Jerusalem, demolished part of its walls, and plundered palace and temple treasures. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Jehoash took all the gold, silver, and vessels found in the LORD's house and the king's house, with hostages, and returned to Samaria. JFB notes he took hostages to prevent further molestation from Judah, then returned with all speed, his forces being needed against Syrian incursions. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Amaziah son of Joash king of Judah lived fifteen years after the death of Jehoash son of Jehoahaz king of Israel. JFB's section "He is slain by a conspiracy" opens with this interval before Amaziah's fall. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: A conspiracy was made against Amaziah in Jerusalem, and he fled to Lachish, but they sent after him to Lachish and slew him there. JFB explains his apostasy was followed by maladministration, and the ruin of Jerusalem, temple plunder, and hostage children lost him the respect of grandees and subjects alike, so he fled in terror to the Philistine frontier town, where he was traced and murdered. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: They brought Amaziah on horses and buried him at Jerusalem with his fathers in the city of David. JFB notes his friends had his corpse brought without pomp in a chariot to Jerusalem, where he was interred among his royal ancestors. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: All the people of Judah took Azariah, sixteen years old, and made him king instead of his father Amaziah. JFB notes Azariah, or Uzziah, succeeded because popular opposition was directed against Amaziah personally, not extended to his family or heir. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Azariah built Elath and restored it to Judah after his father slept with his fathers. JFB explains Elath was a fortified seaport that had revolted with Edom but was now recovered, completing the Edomite conquest his father had left unfinished. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: In the fifteenth year of Amaziah, Jeroboam son of Joash king of Israel began to reign in Samaria for forty-one years. JFB identifies him as Jeroboam II, who, on regaining lost territory, raised the kingdom to great political power. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Jeroboam did evil in the LORD's sight, departing not from all the sins of Jeroboam son of Nebat. JFB notes he adhered to the favourite religious policy of Israel's sovereigns even while God granted national prosperity. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Jeroboam restored Israel's coast from the entering of Hamath to the sea of the plain, according to the LORD's word by Jonah son of Amittai. JFB notes this regaining of lost territory raised the kingdom to great political power. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: The LORD saw Israel's affliction was very bitter, with none shut up or left, and no helper for Israel. JFB notes the reason for God's granting such prosperity is expressly stated in these verses. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: The LORD had not said he would blot out Israel's name from under heaven, but saved them by Jeroboam son of Joash. JFB explains the divine covenant's purposes forbade as yet the overthrow of the ten tribes' kingdom. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

No note for verse(s) 15, 16, 18, 28, 29 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
