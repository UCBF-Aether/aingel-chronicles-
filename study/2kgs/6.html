Study Notes — 2 Kings

Chapter 6

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The sons of the prophets told Elisha the place where they dwelt with him was too strait for them. Henry notes the pleasantness of God's servants' converse, and that even the sons of the prophets must not be unwilling to labour. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

2 COMMENTARY: They proposed going to Jordan, each man taking a beam, to make a larger dwelling there, and Elisha answered, "Go ye." Henry observes no man should think honest employment a burden or disgrace, and labour of the head is often harder than labour with the hands. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

3 COMMENTARY: One asked Elisha to be content to go with his servants, and he answered, "I will go." Henry's passage shows the prophet sharing the labour of his disciples. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

4 COMMENTARY: Elisha went with them, and when they came to Jordan they cut down wood. Henry notes the sons of the prophets undertook the work themselves rather than shunning labour. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

5 COMMENTARY: As one was felling a beam, the axe head fell into the water, and he cried, "Alas, master! for it was borrowed." Henry stresses we ought to be careful of what is borrowed as of our own, and to the honest mind the sorest grievance of poverty is being unable to pay just debts. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

6 COMMENTARY: The man of God asked where it fell, and when shown the place, cut down a stick and cast it in, and the iron swam. Henry notes the LORD cares for his people in their smallest concerns, and God's grace can raise the stony iron heart sunk in this world's mud. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

7 COMMENTARY: Elisha said, "Take it up to thee," and the man put out his hand and took it. Henry presents this small miracle as proof of God's care in the smallest concerns of his people. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

8 COMMENTARY: The king of Syria warred against Israel and took counsel with his servants, saying his camp would be in such and such a place. Henry's account turns to the Syrian counsels Elisha would disclose. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

9 COMMENTARY: The man of God sent to the king of Israel, warning him not to pass such a place, for the Syrians had come down there. Henry notes the king of Israel regarded Elisha's warnings of danger from the Syrians, but would not heed warnings of danger from his sins. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

10 COMMENTARY: The king of Israel sent to the place Elisha warned him of and saved himself there, not once or twice. Henry observes such warnings are little heeded by most: they would save themselves from death but will not from hell. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

11 COMMENTARY: The heart of the king of Syria was sore troubled, and he called his servants to ask who among them was for the king of Israel. Henry notes nothing done, said, or thought by any person, in any place, at any time, is out of God's knowledge. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

12 COMMENTARY: A servant answered that none was a traitor, but Elisha the prophet in Israel told the king of Israel the very words the Syrian king spoke in his bedchamber. Henry's point is God's perfect knowledge, disclosed through his prophet. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

13 COMMENTARY: The Syrian king ordered his men to spy where Elisha was, that he might send and fetch him, and was told Elisha was in Dothan. Henry's passage shows the enemy turning from the army to the prophet himself. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

14 COMMENTARY: The king sent horses, chariots, and a great host by night, and they compassed the city. Henry notes Elisha's word to his servant applies to all faithful servants of God when without are fightings and within are fears. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

15 COMMENTARY: When Elisha's servant rose early and saw the host compassing the city with horses and chariots, he cried, "Alas, my master! how shall we do?" Henry observes the servant's bodily eyes were open and saw the danger; we must pray, "Lord, open the eyes of our faith, that we may see thy protecting hand." — Matthew Henry (Concise)

16 COMMENTARY: Elisha answered, "Fear not: for they that be with us are more than they that be with them." Henry stresses that those with us to protect us are more than those against us to destroy us, and the clearer our sight of Heaven's sovereignty, the less we fear earth's troubles. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

17 COMMENTARY: Elisha prayed the LORD to open the servant's eyes, and the LORD opened them, and he saw the mountain full of horses and chariots of fire round about Elisha. Henry notes Satan blinds men's eyes to their ruin, but when God enlightens them they see their true condition. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

18 COMMENTARY: When the Syrians came down to Elisha, he prayed the LORD to smite them with blindness, and he smote them according to Elisha's word. Henry presents this as divine power joined to divine goodness. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

19 COMMENTARY: Elisha told the blinded Syrians this was not the way nor the city, and bade them follow him, and he would bring them to the man they sought, and he led them to Samaria. Henry's lesson is to overcome evil with good rather than be overcome by evil. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

20 COMMENTARY: When they came into Samaria, Elisha prayed the LORD to open their eyes, and they saw they were in the midst of Samaria. Henry notes the Syrians then saw it was to no purpose to assault so great and so good a man. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

21 COMMENTARY: The king of Israel asked Elisha, "My father, shall I smite them? shall I smite them?" Henry's passage shows the king eager for vengeance on captives now at his mercy. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

22 COMMENTARY: Elisha answered he should not smite them, asking whether he would smite captives taken with his own sword and bow, and bade him set bread and water before them and send them to their master. Henry stresses Elisha made it appear he was influenced by divine goodness as well as divine power. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

23 COMMENTARY: The king prepared great provision, and when the Syrians had eaten and drunk he sent them away to their master, and the bands of Syria came no more into Israel. Henry concludes that the Syrians saw it was to no purpose to try to assault so great and so good a man. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

24 COMMENTARY: After this Benhadad king of Syria gathered all his host and besieged Samaria. Henry turns to the famine that followed, urging readers to learn to value plenty and be thankful for it. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

25 COMMENTARY: There was a great famine in Samaria under siege, until an ass's head sold for fourscore pieces of silver and a cab of dove's dung for five. Henry notes how contemptible money becomes when in famine it is freely parted with for anything eatable. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

26 COMMENTARY: As the king of Israel passed upon the wall, a woman cried to him, "Help, my lord, O king." Henry's passage frames the horror that follows as God's threatened judgment fulfilled. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

27 COMMENTARY: The king answered that if the LORD did not help her, whence could he help her, from barnfloor or winepress? Henry notes the king's language may be the language of despair. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

28 COMMENTARY: When the king asked what ailed her, she answered that another woman had said, "Give thy son, that we may eat him to day," and they would eat her son the next day. Henry cites the threatened judgment that Israel would eat the flesh of their own children, and calls the truth and awful justice of God displayed in this horrible transaction. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

29 COMMENTARY: She said they had boiled her son and eaten him, but when she asked for the other woman's son the next day, the woman had hid her son. Henry exclaims at what miseries sin has brought upon the world. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

30 COMMENTARY: When the king heard the woman's words he rent his clothes, and as he passed by upon the wall the people saw sackcloth within upon his flesh. Henry warns that rending clothes without a broken and contrite heart, or wearing sackcloth without renewal of spirit, will not avail. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

31 COMMENTARY: The king swore God would do so and more to him if Elisha's head stood on him that day. Henry notes wicked men will blame anyone as the cause of their troubles rather than themselves, and will not leave their sins. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

32 COMMENTARY: Elisha sat in his house with the elders, and the king sent a man before him, but before the messenger came Elisha told the elders the king's son of a murderer had sent to take away his head, and bade them shut the door and hold the messenger. Henry's theme is the foolishness of man perverting his way, then fretting against the LORD. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

33 COMMENTARY: While Elisha yet talked, the messenger came down and said this evil was of the LORD, asking why he should wait for the LORD any longer. Henry closes praying that God's whole word would increase in us reverent fear and holy hope, making us stedfast and immovable in the LORD's work. — Matthew Henry (Concise)
