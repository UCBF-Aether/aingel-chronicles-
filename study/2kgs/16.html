Study Notes — 2 Kings

Chapter 16

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: In the seventeenth year of Pekah son of Remaliah, Ahaz son of Jotham king of Judah began to reign. JFB's section "Ahaz' wicked reign over Judah" opens with this accession, noting Isaiah prophesied in this period of religious degeneracy. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Ahaz was twenty when he began to reign, reigned sixteen years in Jerusalem, and did not what was right in the LORD's sight like David his father. JFB notes the voluptuousness and religious degeneracy of all classes under Ahaz, as worldly wealth and luxury from Azariah's and Jotham's reigns bore fruit in idolatry throughout the kingdom. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Ahaz walked in the way of Israel's kings, even making his son pass through the fire, after the abominations of the heathen the LORD cast out before Israel. JFB explains this describes his early reign, when he patronized symbolic worship by images before going further into gross idolatry, and the fire-passing was a lustration between Moloch's red-hot hands, sometimes burning children to death, strongly prohibited in the law. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Ahaz sacrificed and burnt incense in the high places, on the hills, and under every green tree. JFB notes idolatrous practices of every kind prevailed in all parts of the kingdom under his influence. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Rezin king of Syria and Pekah son of Remaliah king of Israel came up to Jerusalem to war but could not prevail against it. JFB notes that despite great efforts and military preparations they failed to take the city, and, disappointed, raised the siege and returned home. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: At that time Rezin recovered Elath to Syria, driving the Jews from Elath, and the Syrians came and dwelt there to this day. JFB notes Elath was what Azariah had gained, and prefers the Septuagint's "Edomites" here, following judicious commentators and travellers. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Ahaz sent messengers to Tiglathpileser king of Assyria, saying, "I am thy servant and thy son: come up, and save me out of the hand of Syria's king and Israel's king." JFB notes Ahaz did this despite Isaiah's assurances by two signs that the confederate kings would not prevail, seeking Assyrian aid instead. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Ahaz took the silver and gold found in the LORD's house and the king's treasures and sent it as a present to Assyria's king. JFB notes he purchased Assyrian aid with the treasures of palace and temple. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Assyria's king hearkened, went up against Damascus, took it, slew Rezin, and carried Damascus captive to Kir. JFB notes Tiglathpileser marched against Damascus, slew its king, and carried its people to Kir, thought to be Karine in Media. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Ahaz went to Damascus to meet Tiglathpileser, saw an altar there, and sent its fashion and pattern to Urijah the priest. JFB explains this was a visit of respect and perhaps gratitude, during which Ahaz, captivated by a heathen altar, sent a sketch to Jerusalem ordering Urijah to build one on the Damascus model to supersede the old temple altar. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Urijah the priest built an altar according to all King Ahaz had sent from Damascus, before the king came from Damascus. JFB notes Urijah, with culpable complaisance, acted according to his instructions, and the sin lay in meddling with temple altars whose patterns had been furnished by divine authority. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: When the king came from Damascus and saw the altar, he approached it and offered upon it. JFB's account of the Damascus altar covers this first use by the king himself. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Ahaz burnt his burnt offering, meat offering, and drink offering, sprinkling the blood of his peace offerings upon the altar. JFB presents this as the king's adoption of the heathen-modelled altar for regular worship. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: He also brought the brasen altar before the LORD from the forefront of the house, between the new altar and the LORD's house, and put it on the north side of his altar. JFB notes the new altar was to supersede the old one in the temple, displacing the divinely patterned altar. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Ahaz commanded Urijah that upon the great altar should be burnt the morning and evening offerings, the king's offerings, and all the people's offerings, with the brasen altar reserved for his inquiry. JFB's exposition of the altar's replacement covers this transfer of all regular worship to the Damascus model. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Urijah the priest did according to all King Ahaz commanded. JFB notes Urijah, one of Isaiah's witnesses against Syria and Israel, acted with culpable complaisance throughout. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Ahaz cut off the borders of the bases, removed the laver from them, and took down the sea from the brasen oxen, setting it on a pavement of stones. JFB's section "He spoils the temple" explains he did this, it is thought, to use the elaborate sculpture in adorning his palace. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Ahaz took away the covert for the sabbath built in the house and the king's entry without, turning them to the king of Assyria. JFB explains the covert was the portico through which priests entered on the sabbath, and the king's entry a private external entrance, both removed into the temple from fear of Assyria's king, to secure the temple entrance in case of siege. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Ahaz slept with his fathers, was buried with them in the city of David, and Hezekiah his son reigned in his stead. JFB's chapter closes with this succession. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

No note for verse(s) 19 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
