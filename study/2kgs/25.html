Study Notes — 2 Kings

Chapter 25

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: In the ninth year of Zedekiah's reign, in the tenth month, on the tenth day, Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon came with all his host against Jerusalem, pitched against it, and built forts round about. JFB explains that, incensed by Zedekiah's revolt, the despot determined to end Judah's perfidious monarchy in his third and last invasion, conducted in person with an immense army, having taken almost all the fenced cities before marching direct to invest Jerusalem, and building lines of circumvallation with a ditch and engines for throwing missiles. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: The city was besieged until the eleventh year of King Zedekiah. JFB notes that, with a brief interruption when Nebuchadnezzar marched against relieving Egyptians who retreated without fighting, the siege lasted a year and a half, the long resistance owing not to Jewish skill or valour but to the city's fortifications, on which the king too confidently relied. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: On the ninth day of the fourth month the famine prevailed in the city, with no bread for the land's people. JFB explains that under the close, protracted blockade the inhabitants were reduced to dreadful extremities, perpetrating inhuman atrocities under hunger's maddening influence, fulfilling prophetic denunciations against the chosen people's apostasy. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The city was broken up, and all the men of war fled by night by the gate between two walls by the king's garden, while the Chaldees were round about the city, and the king went toward the plain. JFB explains a breach was effected in the lower city's wall, and the king's garden lay at Siloam's pool at the Tyropoeon valley's mouth, where the besiegers had probably overlooked this pass. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: The Chaldees' army pursued the king and overtook him in Jericho's plains, and all his army was scattered from him. JFB's section on Zedekiah's capture covers this pursuit and scattering. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: They took the king and brought him up to Babylon's king at Riblah, where they gave judgment upon him. JFB explains Nebuchadnezzar, having left the siege to oppose Pharaohhophra, stationed himself at Riblah in Hamath, where his council condemned Zedekiah as a seditious vassal for violating his oath and neglecting Jeremiah's announcements of God's will. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: They slew Zedekiah's sons before his eyes, put out his eyes, bound him with fetters of brass, and carried him to Babylon. JFB notes his sons and the nobles who joined his flight were slain before his eyes, and, in Eastern ideas deeming a blind man incapable of ruling, his eyes were put out and he was chained to perpetual imprisonment in Babylon, which, as Ezekiel foretold, he came to but did not see. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: In the fifth month, on the seventh day, in Nebuchadnezzar's nineteenth year, Nebuzaradan, captain of the guard and servant of Babylon's king, came to Jerusalem. JFB explains Nebuzaradan set out on the seventh but may not have arrived or executed orders until the tenth, and his office as captain of the guard called him to execute justice, so he was dispatched to rase the city, plunder the temple, lay both in ruins, demolish fortifications, and transport the inhabitants. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Nebuzaradan burnt the LORD's house, the king's house, and all Jerusalem's houses, burning every great man's house with fire. JFB notes he was dispatched to rase the city and plunder the temple, laying both in ruins. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: All the Chaldees' army with the captain of the guard broke down Jerusalem's walls round about. JFB includes demolishing the fortifications among Nebuzaradan's orders. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Nebuzaradan carried away the rest of the people left in the city, the fugitives who had fallen to Babylon's king, and the remnant of the multitude. JFB notes he was dispatched to transport the inhabitants to Babylon. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: But the captain of the guard left some of the poor of the land to be vinedressers and husbandmen. JFB notes those permitted to remain were only the peasantry who could till the land and dress the vineyards, besides the king's daughters and a few attendants too insignificant to remove. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: The captain of the guard took Seraiah the chief priest, Zephaniah the second priest, and the three keepers of the door. JFB notes the most eminent captives were taken to the king at Riblah and executed as instigators of rebellion, among them Seraiah the high priest, grandfather of Ezra, and his deputy, while the three door-keepers were not mere porters but officers of high trust among the Levites. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Out of the city he took an officer over the men of war, five men of the king's presence, the principal scribe of the host who mustered the land's people, and threescore men of the land's people found in the city. JFB explains the five men of the king's presence belonged to the royal retinue, probably five at first with two more found afterwards. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Nebuzaradan took these and brought them to Babylon's king at Riblah. JFB notes the most eminent captives were taken to the king at Riblah. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Babylon's king smote them and slew them at Riblah in Hamath, and so Judah was carried away out of their land. JFB notes they were executed as instigators and abettors of the rebellion or otherwise obnoxious to the Assyrian government. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: As for the people remaining in Judah's land, whom Nebuchadnezzar had left, he made Gedaliah son of Ahikam, son of Shaphan, ruler over them. JFB notes Gedaliah was Jeremiah's friend, who, by the prophet's counsel, probably fled the city as abandoned of God, surrendered to the conqueror, and, promoted to Judea's government, fixed his court at Mizpeh, well qualified to rule at such a crisis. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: When all the captains of the armies and their men heard Babylon's king had made Gedaliah governor, they came to Gedaliah at Mizpah, with Ishmael, Johanan, Seraiah, and Jaazaniah. JFB notes many fugitive Jews and Zedekiah's soldiers left their retreats and flocked around the governor. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Gedaliah swore to them and their men, saying, "Fear not to be the servants of the Chaldees: dwell in the land, and serve Babylon's king, and it shall be well with you." JFB notes Gedaliah counselled submission, promising on oath that, complying, they would retain their possessions and enjoy their land's produce. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: In the seventh month Ishmael son of Nethaniah, of the seed royal, came with ten men and smote Gedaliah, so that he died, along with the Jews and Chaldees who were with him at Mizpah. JFB explains Ishmael had found refuge with Baalis king of the Ammonites, returning with a bad design, either from envy of a governor not descended from David or bribed by Baalis, and the generous governor, though warned, refused to credit the report or sanction cutting Ishmael off, so he was murdered while entertaining Ishmael in his own house. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: All the people, small and great, with the captains of the armies, arose and came to Egypt, for fear of the Chaldees. JFB notes they settled in various Egyptian cities despite Jeremiah's dissuasions. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: In the thirty-seventh year of Jehoiachin's captivity, in the twelfth month, on the twenty-seventh day, Evilmerodach king of Babylon, in the year he began to reign, lifted up Jehoiachin king of Judah's head out of prison. JFB notes this year corresponded with Nebuchadnezzar's death and Evilmerodach's accession. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Evilmerodach spoke kindly to Jehoiachin and set his throne above the throne of the kings with him in Babylon. JFB explains he gave him liberty upon parole, a kindly feeling said to have originated in prison acquaintance, since Evilmerodach had lain in prison till his father's death, but doubtless traced to the overruling providence and grace of God, who still cherished purposes of love to David's house. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Jehoiachin changed his prison garments and ate bread continually before the king all the days of his life. JFB notes that by ancient Eastern court usage he had a seat at the royal table on great days and a stated provision for his exiled court's maintenance. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: His allowance was a continual allowance given him by the king, a daily rate for every day, all the days of his life. JFB's note on his continual eating before the king covers this stated daily provision. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

No note for verse(s) 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
