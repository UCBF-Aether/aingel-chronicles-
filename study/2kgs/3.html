Study Notes — 2 Kings

Chapter 3

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Jehoram son of Ahab began to reign over Israel in Samaria in Jehoshaphat's eighteenth year, reigning twelve years. Henry notes he took warning by God's judgment on his house, yet his reform would prove partial. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

2 COMMENTARY: Jehoram wrought evil, but not like his father and mother, for he put away Baal's image. Henry observes that those who only part with the sins they lose by, while continuing to love sins they think to gain by, do not truly repent or reform. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

3 COMMENTARY: Nevertheless Jehoram cleaved to the sins of Jeroboam son of Nebat and departed not from them. Henry's point is that keeping the calf worship showed his reform stopped short of true repentance. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

4 COMMENTARY: Mesha king of Moab, a sheepmaster, had rendered Israel a hundred thousand lambs and a hundred thousand rams with wool. Henry's outline treats this tribute as the bond Moab now broke. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

5 COMMENTARY: When Ahab died, the king of Moab rebelled against the king of Israel. Henry's earlier chapter theme applies: rebellion against God is often punished by the rebellion of subjects. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

6 COMMENTARY: King Jehoram went out of Samaria and numbered all Israel for the campaign. Henry's account shows the king gathering force before seeking counsel from God. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

7 COMMENTARY: Jehoram sent to Jehoshaphat king of Judah, asking him to go with him to battle against Moab. Henry notes it was well Jehoshaphat was consulted, though inquiring of the LORD beforehand would have been much better. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

8 COMMENTARY: Jehoshaphat agreed to go, as his people and horses would be as Jehoram's. Henry's lesson is that good men sometimes neglect their duty until necessity and affliction drive them to it. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

9 COMMENTARY: They chose the way through the wilderness of Edom. Henry's narrative follows the allied kings into the desert march that would soon distress them. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

10 COMMENTARY: The kings of Israel, Judah, and Edom fetched a compass of seven days' journey, and there was no water for the host or their cattle. Henry notes the king of Israel then lamented their distress and charged it upon Providence, though man's foolishness perverts his way and then his heart frets against the LORD. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

11 COMMENTARY: The king of Israel cried that the LORD had called the three kings together to deliver them into Moab's hand. Henry cites the proverb that the foolishness of man perverts his way, and then his heart frets against the LORD. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

12 COMMENTARY: Jehoshaphat asked whether there was a prophet of the LORD to inquire by, and a servant answered that Elisha, who poured water on Elijah's hands, was there. Henry notes it was well Jehoshaphat inquired of the LORD now, and that wicked people often fare the better for the friendship of the godly. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

13 COMMENTARY: Jehoshaphat said the word of the LORD was with Elisha, so the three kings went down to him. Henry presents this as the blessing of company with one who has power with God and can prevail by prayer. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

14 COMMENTARY: Elisha asked the king of Israel what he had to do with him, bidding him go to his father's and mother's prophets, but Jehoram pleaded that the LORD had called the three kings together to deliver them to Moab. Henry's theme is that the wicked king had no claim on the prophet apart from Jehoshaphat's presence. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

15 COMMENTARY: Elisha swore that were it not for his regard for Jehoshaphat king of Judah, he would not look toward Jehoram or see him. Henry notes a kingdom may be upheld and prosper through the fervent prayers of those dear to God. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

16 COMMENTARY: Elisha called for a minstrel, and when the minstrel played, the hand of the LORD came upon him. Henry's passage treats this as the prophet seeking the LORD's word for the distressed kings. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

17 COMMENTARY: Elisha said, "Thus saith the LORD, Make this valley full of ditches." Henry explains this as a trial of faith and obedience: those who expect God's blessings must dig pools for the rain to fill, making even the valley of Baca a well. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

18 COMMENTARY: Though they would see neither wind nor rain, the valley would be filled with water for them, their cattle, and their beasts. Henry adds we need not inquire whence the water came, for God is not tied to second causes, and those who sincerely seek the dew of his grace shall have it. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

19 COMMENTARY: Elisha added that delivering the Moabites into their hand was but a light thing to the LORD. Henry's lesson is that those who seek God's grace shall by it be made more than conquerors. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

20 COMMENTARY: They were to smite every fenced and choice city, fell every good tree, stop every well, and mar every good piece of land with stones. Henry's outline presents this as the promised victory over Moab. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

21 COMMENTARY: In the morning, when the meat offering was offered, water came by the way of Edom and filled the country. Henry treats this unseen supply as God's answer to the prayer of his people, without wind or rain. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

22 COMMENTARY: When the Moabites heard the kings had come to fight them, they gathered all who could bear armour and stood on the border. Henry's narrative turns to the Moabites' fatal misreading of the scene. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

23 COMMENTARY: Rising early, the Moabites saw the sun shining on the water and took it for blood, red as blood on the other side. Henry notes that when sinners say "Peace, peace," destruction comes upon them, and despair follows mad presumption. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

24 COMMENTARY: The Moabites cried, "This is blood: the kings are surely slain and have smitten one another; now therefore, Moab, to the spoil." Henry observes their presumption rushed them into the trap. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

25 COMMENTARY: When the Moabites came to Israel's camp, the Israelites rose and smote them so they fled, and Israel pursued into Moab. Henry presents this as the fulfilment of Elisha's promise of deliverance. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

26 COMMENTARY: Israel beat down the cities, filled good land with stones, stopped the wells, and felled the good trees, leaving only Kirharaseth with its stones, which slingers beset and smote. Henry's account shows the victory executed as Elisha had foretold. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

27 COMMENTARY: The king of Moab, seeing the battle too sore for him, took seven hundred swordsmen to break through to the king of Edom, but could not. Henry notes it is well not to urge the worst of men to extremities, but rather leave them to God's judgment. — Matthew Henry (Concise)
