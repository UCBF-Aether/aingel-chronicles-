Study Notes — 2 Kings

Chapter 8

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Elisha had spoken to the Shunammite, telling her to arise and sojourn wherever she could, for the LORD had called for a famine of seven years. JFB notes this direction probably preceded the events of chapters 5 and 6, and that such calamities are chastisements from God's hand, this famine being double Elijah's in length because Israel remained obdurate under Elisha's ministry. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: The woman sojourned in the land of the Philistines seven years. JFB notes that territory was recommended by its closeness to her home, its reduced state lessening the risk of idolatry's seductions, and many Jews and Israelites residing there. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: At the seven years' end she returned and cried to the king for her house and land. JFB explains that during her long absence her possessions had been occupied by kindred or confiscated by the crown, an innovation with no statute in Moses' law ordaining such alienation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: The king was talking with Gehazi, asking him to tell all Elisha's great things. JFB notes ceremonial pollution came by contact alone, so a conference with the leper at a distance, perhaps at the gate or in a royal garden, was possible, and providence had led Jehoram to inquire with great interest into Elisha's deeds. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Just as Gehazi was telling the king how Elisha had restored a dead body to life, the very woman whose son he had restored appeared to cry for her house. JFB stresses providence ordained this timing, so the king was pleased to grant her request. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: When the king asked the woman, she told him, and he appointed an officer to restore all that was hers with all the fruits of the field since she left. JFB notes a state officer was charged to afford her every facility in recovering her family possession from its occupier. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Elisha came to Damascus while Benhadad king of Syria was sick, and the king was told the man of God had come. JFB explains Elisha was directed there by the Spirit in pursuance of the mission given to Elijah at Horeb, to anoint Hazael king of Syria. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: The king told Hazael to take a present and meet the man of God, inquiring of the LORD whether he would recover from his disease. JFB notes Benhadad, following heathen practice in consulting soothsayers, ordered a liberal present in remuneration. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Hazael went with a present of every good thing of Damascus, forty camels' burden, calling Benhadad Elisha's son and asking whether he would recover. JFB notes the present was liberal and magnificent, though Orientals fond of display would lay on forty beasts what four could bear, and "son" was the established usage of calling the prophet "father." — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Elisha told Hazael to say to Benhadad, "Thou mayest certainly recover," yet added that the LORD had showed him Benhadad would surely die. JFB explains there was no contradiction: the first part answered Benhadad's inquiry, while the second was intended for Hazael, who artfully reported only what suited his views. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Elisha settled his countenance stedfastly until Hazael was ashamed, and the man of God wept. JFB explains the prophet's penetrating look convinced Hazael his secret designs were known, and Elisha's deep emotion was justified by the horrible atrocities the usurper would commit in Israel. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: When Hazael asked why his lord wept, Elisha answered because he knew the evil Hazael would do to Israel's children: dashing their children, ripping up their women with child, and burning their strong holds. JFB links this grief to the atrocities, too common in ancient warfare, that Hazael as usurper would commit. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Hazael protested, "What, is thy servant a dog, that he should do this great thing?" and Elisha answered the LORD had showed him Hazael would be king over Syria. JFB notes the prophet's steadfast look had already convinced Hazael that his secret designs were known. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Hazael returned to his master, who asked what Elisha had said, and Hazael answered that Elisha had said he would certainly recover. JFB notes Hazael, like an artful and ambitious courtier, reported only as much of the prophet's statement as suited his own views. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: On the morrow Hazael took a thick cloth, dipped it in water, and spread it on Benhadad's face, so that he died, and Hazael reigned in his stead. JFB explains the coverlet was a thick quilt whose weight when steeped in water could accomplish murder without marks of violence, though some suppose Hazael may have spread it as a refreshing remedy, and the king's rapid death and burial favoured Hazael's instant elevation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: In the fifth year of Joram son of Ahab king of Israel, Jehoram son of Jehoshaphat king of Judah began to reign. JFB notes his father had resigned the throne to him two years before his own death. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Jehoram walked in the way of the kings of Israel, as did the house of Ahab, for Ahab's daughter was his wife, and he did evil in the LORD's sight. JFB identifies the daughter as Athaliah, through whose influence Jehoram introduced Baal worship and many other evils into Judah. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Yet the LORD would not destroy Judah for David his servant's sake, as he had promised him a light always for his children. JFB notes this apostasy would have led to the total extinction of Judah's royal family had it not been for the divine promise to David. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: In Jehoram's days Edom revolted from under Judah's hand and made a king over themselves. JFB explains Edom, hitherto governed by a tributary ruler, now erected the standard of independence, a national chastisement on Judah. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Joram went over to Zair with all his chariots, rose by night, and smote the Edomites who compassed him and the captains of the chariots, though the people fled to their tents. JFB's account of the Edomite revolt frames this night action as Judah's failed attempt to recover its tributary. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Yet Edom revolted from under Judah's hand to this day, and Libnah revolted at the same time. JFB presents this lasting independence as the chastisement inflicted on Judah for Jehoram's apostasy. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Joram slept with his fathers and was buried with them in the city of David, and Ahaziah his son reigned in his stead. JFB heads this section "Ahaziah succeeds him," marking the succession that closes Jehoram's wicked reign. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

No note for verse(s) 17, 23, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
