Study Notes — 2 Kings

Chapter 19

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: When Hezekiah heard Rabshakeh's words, he rent his clothes, covered himself with sackcloth, and went into the LORD's house. JFB explains the rending expressed horror at the daring blasphemy, the sackcloth his mental distress, and his entrance into the temple the refuge of a pious man in affliction. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Hezekiah sent Eliakim over the household, Shebna the scribe, and the elders of the priests, covered with sackcloth, to Isaiah the prophet. JFB notes forwarding an account of the Assyrian's speech to Isaiah was to obtain the prophet's counsel and comfort. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: They told Isaiah that Hezekiah said this was a day of trouble, rebuke, and blasphemy, for the children were come to the birth with no strength to bring forth. JFB explains this figure described the kingdom's desperate condition and its inability to help itself. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: They hoped the LORD his God would hear Rabshakeh's words, sent to reproach the living God, and would reprove them, so Isaiah should lift up prayer for the remnant left. JFB notes "the living God" is significant against the senseless deities Rabshakeh boasted could not resist Assyria, and the message intimated hope that this blasphemous defiance might lead to direct interposition for God's honour. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: So Hezekiah's servants came to Isaiah. JFB's section "Hezekiah in deep affliction" carries this embassy to the prophet. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Isaiah told them to say to their master, "Thus saith the LORD, Be not afraid of the words thou hast heard, with which the servants of Assyria's king have blasphemed me." JFB calls the prophet's answer most cheering, holding out speedy deliverance from the invader. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: The LORD would send a blast upon Sennacherib, who would hear a rumour, return to his own land, and fall by the sword there. JFB notes this brief prediction was soon fulfilled in all three particulars: the alarm hastening his retreat, the destruction of his army, and the violent death ending his career. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Rabshakeh returned and found Assyria's king warring against Libnah, having heard he had departed from Lachish. JFB notes whether Lachish had fallen is not said, but Sennacherib had transferred his battering-rams against the neighbouring fortress of Libnah. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: When Sennacherib heard that Tirhakah king of Ethiopia had come out to fight him, he sent messengers to Hezekiah. JFB identifies this as the "rumour" Isaiah had foretold, noting Tirhakah reigned in Upper Egypt while So ruled Lower Egypt, and rage at Hezekiah's Egyptian alliance, or hope of meeting the southern attack, moved Sennacherib to send a menacing letter forcing immediate surrender. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: The messengers were to tell Hezekiah not to let his God, in whom he trusted, deceive him by saying Jerusalem would not be delivered to Assyria's king. JFB notes the letter, couched in the same vaunting style as Rabshakeh's speech, exceeded it in blasphemy. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: The letter reminded Hezekiah what Assyria's kings had done to all lands, destroying them utterly, and asked how he could be delivered. JFB notes the letter contained a larger enumeration of conquered places, to terrify Hezekiah and show the hopelessness of resistance. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: It asked whether the gods of the nations his fathers destroyed — Gozan, Haran, Rezeph, and Eden's children in Telassar — had delivered them. JFB presents this catalogue as the letter's terrifying enumeration of conquered places. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: It asked where were the kings of Hamath, Arpad, Sepharvaim's city, Hena, and Ivah. JFB notes this further enumeration aimed to show the utter hopelessness of all attempts at resistance. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Hezekiah received the letter, read it, went up into the LORD's house, and spread it before the LORD. JFB explains that after reading it, Hezekiah hastened into the temple and spread it in childlike confidence of faith before the LORD, as containing taunts deeply affecting the divine honour. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Hezekiah prayed, "O LORD God of Israel, which dwellest between the cherubims, thou art the God, even thou alone, of all the kingdoms of the earth; thou hast made heaven and earth." JFB praises the prayer's recognition of God's majesty, strikingly contrasted with the Assyrian fancy of his merely local power. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Hezekiah prayed the LORD to bow down his ear, open his eyes, and hear Sennacherib's words sent to reproach the living God. JFB notes the prayer implored deliverance from this proud defier of God and man. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Hezekiah acknowledged that of a truth Assyria's kings had destroyed the nations and their lands. JFB notes the prayer acknowledged the conquests obtained over other lands. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Hezekiah noted Assyria had cast the nations' gods into the fire, for they were no gods but the work of men's hands, wood and stone. JFB explains that, according to Assyrian practice, wooden idols were committed to the flames because their tutelary deities were no gods. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Hezekiah besought the LORD to save them from Sennacherib's hand, that all earth's kingdoms might know the LORD alone was God. JFB notes the prayer's object was that all kingdoms might know the LORD was the only God, an attitude worthy of a pious theocratic king. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Isaiah sent to Hezekiah saying the LORD God of Israel had heard his prayer against Sennacherib. JFB explains that a revelation having been made to Isaiah, the prophet announced the prayer was heard, in a message of three portions. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: The LORD's word against Sennacherib began: the virgin daughter of Zion had despised him and laughed him to scorn, shaking her head behind him. JFB describes this first portion as a highly poetical apostrophe to Sennacherib, descriptive of his turgid vanity, haughty pretensions, and presumptuous impiety. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: The LORD asked whom Sennacherib had reproached and blasphemed, and against whom he had exalted his voice and lifted up his eyes on high, even against the Holy One of Israel. JFB's description of the apostrophe covers this charge of impiety against the despot. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: By his messengers Sennacherib had reproached the LORD, boasting he had come up with his multitude of chariots to the mountains' sides and Lebanon, cutting down its tall cedars and choice firs, and lodging in its utmost border. JFB presents these boasts as the haughty pretensions the apostrophe answers. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Sennacherib boasted he had digged and drunk strange waters, and with his feet's sole dried up all the rivers of besieged places. JFB's account of the vaunting despot covers these claims of effortless conquest. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: The LORD answered that Sennacherib had surely heard long ago how God himself had done it and formed it in ancient times, and now had brought it to pass to lay waste fenced cities into ruinous heaps. JFB's exposition presents this as God's claim that Assyria was merely his instrument. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Therefore the nations' inhabitants were of small power, dismayed and confounded, like grass on housetops, blasted before it is grown. JFB's section on the apostrophe covers this picture of Assyria's victims as powerless before God's ordained judgment. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: The LORD declared he knew Sennacherib's abode, going out, coming in, and rage against him. JFB notes the apostrophe's strain of divine omniscience over the despot's movements. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Because Sennacherib's rage and tumult had come up into God's ears, God would put his hook in his nose, his bridle in his lips, and turn him back by the way he came. JFB's exposition of the apostrophe covers this humbling of the presumptuous invader. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: The sign to Hezekiah was that they would eat this year what grew of itself, the second year what sprang of the same, and the third year they would sow, reap, plant vineyards, and eat their fruit. JFB explains the second portion addressed to Hezekiah gave this sign: for two years the enemy would interrupt husbandry, but in the third year the people would till and reap as formerly. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: The remnant escaped of Judah's house would again take root downward and bear fruit upward. JFB's second portion covers this promise of the remnant's recovery. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: Out of Jerusalem would go forth a remnant, and out of Mount Zion those who escape, by the zeal of the LORD of hosts. JFB notes this remnant promise formed part of the sign of deliverance. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: The LORD declared Assyria's king would not come into this city, nor shoot an arrow there, nor come before it with shield, nor cast a bank against it. JFB's third portion announced the invasion's issue, and its note explains Sennacherib would be permitted none of ancient siege warfare's principal modes: no arrow even from the most powerful engine, no fence or mantelet, no raised bank overtopping the walls. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: By the way Sennacherib came, by the same he would return, and not come into the city, said the LORD. JFB notes the army under Rabshakeh encamped at a little distance intending blockade, but delayed laying siege, waiting for the king's detachment, and only divine interposition could have saved Jerusalem. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: The LORD would defend this city to save it, for his own sake and for his servant David's sake. JFB's third portion closes with this promise of defence, confirmed, as Rawlinson noted, in its most important features by Sennacherib's own Annals. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: That night the angel of the LORD went out and smote in the Assyrian camp a hundred fourscore and five thousand, and in the morning they were all dead corpses. JFB calls this the miraculous interposition of the Almighty defending Jerusalem, most probably effected by a hot south wind, the simoon, which envelops and destroys whole caravans, the camp's discipline being relaxed in full security. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: Sennacherib king of Assyria departed, went and returned, and dwelt at Nineveh. JFB notes he returned the same way he came, and his dwelling at Nineveh implies a considerable period, his Annals carrying his history at least five years after the disastrous campaign, recording victories alone. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

37 COMMENTARY: As Sennacherib worshipped in the house of Nisroch his god, Adrammelech and Sharezer his sons smote him with the sword, and they escaped to Armenia, while Esarhaddon his son reigned in his stead. JFB identifies Nisroch with Asshur, head of the Assyrian pantheon, represented as a winged figure in a circle, and notes Sennacherib's savage cruelty at last led his two sons, whom he intended to sacrifice, to assassinate him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
