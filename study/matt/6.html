Study Notes — Matthew

Chapter 6

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Take heed that ye do not your alms — better read “your righteousness” — before men, to be seen of them, otherwise ye have no reward of your Father in heaven. This verse is the general heading for the whole section: unostentatiousness in all deeds of righteousness, almsgiving, prayer and fasting being selected examples. It is consistent with letting light shine before men (5:16), for that forbids making a display of righteousness for self-glorification. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: When thou doest thine alms, do not sound a trumpet before thee, figuratively blazoning it, as the hypocrites do in the synagogues and streets, places of religious and secular resort. “Hypocrite” means one who acts a part, pretending to be what he is not; they have their reward — all they wanted was human applause, and with it all they will ever get. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: When thou doest alms, let not thy left hand know what thy right hand doeth. So far from making a display of it, dwell not on it even in thine own thoughts, lest it minister to spiritual pride; vain-glory creeps into what we do before we are aware. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: That thine alms may be in secret, and thy Father which seeth in secret shall reward thee openly. When we take least notice of our good deeds ourselves, God takes most notice of them, and he rewards not as a master paying earned wages, but as a Father giving abundantly to his serving son. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: When thou prayest, thou shalt not be as the hypocrites, who love to pray standing in the synagogues and corners of the streets that they may be seen of men. The standing posture was ancient practice, but it opened the way for the ostentatious; the scribes and Pharisees were guilty of vain-glory and vain repetitions, and if praise of men is all we seek in prayer, it is just that it should be all our reward. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: But thou, when thou prayest, enter into thy closet, shut thy door, and pray to thy Father which is in secret. It is not simple publicity that is condemned — prayer may be offered openly if not prompted by ostentation — but the retiring character of true prayer is taught; not a secret, sudden breathing after God escapes his observation. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: When ye pray, use not vain repetitions — “babble not,” a senseless multiplication of words, as the heathen do, thinking they shall be heard for much speaking. Praying much, and at times using the same words, is not condemned, having our Lord's own example; but God is not wrought upon by length of words, and the most powerful intercessions are made with groanings that cannot be uttered. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Be not ye therefore like unto them, for your Father knoweth what things ye have need of before ye ask him — he needs neither to be informed of our wants nor roused to attend to them. Yet he will have his children pray, linking all promised supplies to their petitions, that asking they may receive, seeking find, knocking have it opened. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: After this manner therefore pray ye — “Thus,” as both model and form, an actual prayer compressing much real prayer into fewest words, yet to be used with understanding and not needlessly repeated. “Our Father which art in heaven” joins nearness and distance: holy, loving familiarity with awful reverence, raising our souls to the Majesty where he dwells. Hallowed be thy name — be held in reverence, regarded and treated as holy; God's name means himself as revealed and manifested, by which he marks off the faith, love, reverence and obedience he will have from men. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Thy kingdom come: the moral and spiritual kingdom God is setting up in this fallen world under Messiah its Head, at hand when he appeared, founded at his death, gloriously coming at Pentecost, and still to come until the last subject is brought in. Thy will be done in earth as it is in heaven — as cheerfully, as constantly, as perfectly. The renewed soul's spontaneous longing is to see the whole inhabited earth in entire conformity to God's will, asking not whether it ever shall be, only breathing forth the holy yearning. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Give us this day our daily bread — the bread which this day's necessities require, the staff of life. Asking for bread teaches sobriety, honesty and industry, and constant dependence on Providence; we beg God to give it, not sell or lend it, and to give it us teaches compassion for the poor and prayer with our families. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Forgive us our debts: sin viewed as an offence against God demanding reparation to his dishonoured claims, the sinner as debtor in the creditor's hand, forgiveness being the wiping out of entries against us in his book of remembrance. As we forgive our debtors: no one can reasonably imagine himself forgiven who is deliberately and habitually unforgiving, and to ask God for what we refuse men is to insult him. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Lead us not into temptation — best taken as a prayer against being drawn or sucked, of our own will, into temptation, as Peter pressed into the high priest's hall and fell. But deliver us from evil — from all evil of whatever kind, not only sin but all its consequences, fully and finally, as Paul's assurance echoes: the Lord shall deliver me from every evil work and preserve me unto his heavenly kingdom. The doxology, wanting in the most ancient manuscripts, is probably no part of the original text. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: For if ye forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you. This is the one point in the prayer our Lord comes back upon, solemnly assuring us the divine procedure in forgiveness will be exactly what our own is; we must forgive as we hope to be forgiven. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: But if ye forgive not men their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses. Those who desire mercy with God must show mercy to their brethren; Christ came as the great Peacemaker, to reconcile us to God and to one another. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Moreover, when ye fast, be not as the hypocrites, of a sad countenance, for they disfigure their faces — literally “make unseen” — with slovenly appearance and ashes, that they may appear unto men to fast. It was not the deed but reputation for the deed they sought, and they multiplied their fasts for it; they have their reward. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: But thou, when thou fastest, anoint thine head and wash thy face, as the Jews did except when mourning: appear as usual, so as to attract no notice. Fasting is the humbling of the soul, and that inside of the duty is the principal care; as to the outside, covet not to let it be seen. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: That thou appear not unto men to fast, but unto thy Father which is in secret, and thy Father which seeth in secret shall reward thee openly. God sees in secret and will reward openly, though “openly” here seems a later addition from verses 4 and 7. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Lay not up treasures upon earth, where moth — the clothes-moth consuming costly stored dresses — and rust, any eating or wear and tear, doth corrupt, and where thieves break through and steal. By moth and rust our Lord teaches how perishable, and by thieves how precarious, such treasures are. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: But lay up treasures in heaven, where neither moth nor rust doth corrupt, and where thieves do not break through nor steal — treasures imperishable and unassailable. It is wisdom to make our title to eternal life sure through Jesus Christ, and to be content with nothing short of an inheritance incorruptible. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: For where your treasure is, that which ye value most, there will your heart be also. What a man loves, that is his God: he carries it in his heart night and day. Laying up is not in itself sinful, but multitudes flatter themselves all is right while their closest attention, anxiety, zeal and time are exhausted upon earthly pursuits. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: The light of the body is the eye — rather, the lamp: if thine eye be single, simple and clear, not looking two ways, thy whole body shall be full of light. A simple, persistent purpose to serve and please God in everything makes the whole character consistent and bright. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: If thine eye be evil, distempered, thy whole body shall be full of darkness; if the light that is in thee be darkness, how great is that darkness! A mind divided between heaven and earth is all dark, for the inward purpose and aim determine character, and what must the whole man be but a mass of darkness when they are distorted and double? — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: No man can serve two masters — “serve” meaning belong wholly and be entirely under command to — for either he will hate the one and love the other, or hold to the one and despise the other. Ye cannot serve God and mammon, riches considered as an idol master, “what one trusts in”: a man may do some service to two masters, but devote himself to no more than one, for God requires the whole heart. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Therefore take no thought — be not solicitous, anxiously concerned from unbelieving doubts — for your life, what ye shall eat or drink, nor for your body, what ye shall put on. Is not life more than meat, and the body than raiment? If God gives and keeps up the greater, will he withhold the less? Lawful care is a duty, but must not be carried into distracting, distrustful care. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Behold the fowls of the air: they sow not, neither reap, nor gather into barns, yet your heavenly Father feedeth them. Are ye not much better than they — nobler in yourselves and dearer to God? Brute creatures incapable of sowing and storing are sustained without those processes; will God leave his own children, using all reason's means and looking up to him at every step, to starve? — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Which of you by taking thought can add one cubit unto his stature? “Stature” is best taken in its primary sense of age: which of you, however anxiously you vex yourselves, can add so much as a step to the length of your life's journey? We cannot alter the disposals of Providence, therefore we must submit and resign ourselves to them. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Why take ye thought for raiment? Consider the lilies of the field, how they grow: they toil not, as men planting flax, neither do they spin, as women. Thoughtfulness for our souls is the best cure of thoughtfulness for the world. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Even Solomon in all his glory was not arrayed like one of these. What incomparable teaching, best left in its own transparent clearness and rich simplicity. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: If God so clothe the grass of the field, which today is and tomorrow is cast into the oven — wild flowers cut with the grass, withering and used for fuel — shall he not much more clothe you, O ye of little faith? The rebuke gently chides the spirit of unbelief natural even to the best, surrounded by a world of sense, and kindles desire to shake it off. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: Therefore take no thought, saying, What shall we eat? or, What shall we drink? or, Wherewithal shall we be clothed? The disquieting care condemned here insnares the poor as much as love of wealth does the rich. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: For after all these things do the Gentiles seek — pursue, knowing nothing beyond the present life to engage their supreme attention. But your heavenly Father knoweth that ye have need of all these things: food and raiment are pronounced needful to God's children, and will not that suffice you, O needy ones of the household of faith? — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: Seek ye first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things shall be added unto you. This golden verse strikes the two keynotes of the sermon, kingdom and righteousness, as the grand objects of supreme pursuit; seeking them first means before and above all else, and the rest follows as gracious reward for not seeking it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: Take therefore no thought for the morrow, for the morrow shall take thought for the things of itself — it shall have its own causes of anxiety. Sufficient unto the day is the evil thereof: every day brings its own cares, and to anticipate is only to double them. By daily prayers we get strength for daily troubles, and then let none of these things move us. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
