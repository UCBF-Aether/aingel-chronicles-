Study Notes — Matthew

Chapter 1

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The opening title covers only the first seventeen verses: “the book of the generation” is a Jewish table of genealogy. Henry and JFB stress the two names that matter most to a Jewish reader, son of David and son of Abraham, the two promised starting-points of the Messiah's line. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Of Jacob's sons only Judah is named here, because it was from Judah's line that the Messiah was to spring, as promised in Genesis 49:10. The genealogy is selective by design, tracing the promised line rather than every branch. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Tamar is the first of four women named in the genealogy, three of whom carry a blot in the Old Testament record. JFB reads this as grace on display from the outset, reaching even to those with stained histories. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Salmon's marriage to Rahab, and Boaz's to Ruth, bring two Gentiles by birth into the Messiah's line. JFB notes the fitness of this in a Gospel written by Matthew the publican: grace that fetches in “them that are afar off.” — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: David is twice emphatically called “David the king,” the first of the royal line from which the Messiah descends and the king whose name the promised throne bears. Solomon follows by Bathsheba, named only as her who had been the wife of Uriah, another stained name in the line. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: The royal succession runs through Solomon, Rehoboam, Abijah and Asa, kings who matter here chiefly as links joining the first king of the line to its last. JFB notes the intermediate kings are otherwise sunk, their importance being the connection they preserve. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Between Joram and Uzziah three kings are omitted — Ahaziah, Joash and Amaziah — so the whole may be compressed into three fourteens. JFB traces the omission to religious considerations, likely their connection with the house of Ahab. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Jeconiah was Josiah's grandson, Jehoiakim being sunk as a mere puppet of Egypt, and his “brethren” are his uncles, chiefly Zedekiah. The “carrying away to Babylon” is softened to a migration, respecting the national feeling about a bitter memory. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: After the migration, Jeconiah begets Salathiel and Salathiel Zerubbabel, though Zerubbabel was strictly Salathiel's grandson through the omitted Pedaiah. Jeremiah's “childless” for Coniah means no reigning child on David's throne, not no seed at all. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: None of the post-exilic names from Zerubbabel onward appear in the Old Testament, but JFB notes they were doubtless taken from the public or family registers the Jews carefully kept, whose accuracy was never challenged. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: The chain ends with Jacob begetting Joseph, the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born — making plain this is Joseph's genealogy, not Mary's. Joseph is proclaimed as legal father only, never natural father, so that Jesus' Davidic descent stands unquestioned while his virgin birth is preserved. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: The whole is arranged in three fourteens, each embracing a marked era and ending with a notable event — aids to memory familiar to Jewish readers. Counting David twice, as first fourteen's end and second's beginning, gives the last division its full fourteen as well. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Mary, betrothed to Joseph — betrothal being valid marriage under Jewish law — was found with child of the Holy Ghost before they came together. JFB notes only the fact was discovered; the explanation is the Evangelist's own, implying the Spirit as a living, conscious Person. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Joseph, being a just man, would not expose Mary publicly, and planned a private divorcement before two or three witnesses with no cause assigned, rather than dragging her before a magistrate. Henry adds that it is the thoughtful, not the unthinking, whom God guides. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: While Joseph brooded alone over the painful step, the angel came in a dream at the last extremity, addressing him as “son of David” to remind him of the Davidic hope. The message clears the cloud: what is conceived in her is of the Holy Ghost, and the relationship is unsullied still. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: The child is to be called Jesus, from the Hebrew meaning “Jehovah the Saviour,” the same name as Joshua. He himself, personally and by his own acts, shall save his people from their sins — from guilt by his death and from power by the Spirit's grace. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: All this was done that the word spoken by the Lord through the prophet might be fulfilled, Isaiah 7:14. Matthew's Gospel dwells on fulfilled prophecy because, to Jewish readers, fulfilment was strong evidence for the Messiah. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: The prophecy speaks of “the virgin,” that particular virgin destined to this unparalleled distinction. Emmanuel is not given as a proper name like Jesus, but as his character: God manifested in the flesh, the living bond of fellowship between God and men. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Joseph, raised from sleep with his difficulties removed, did as the angel bade him, speedily and without dispute, and took Mary as his wife. Henry notes the pattern for all of life's great turns: take direction from God's written word, and the way proves safe and comfortable. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Joseph knew her not till she brought forth her first-born son, and called his name Jesus as directed. JFB cautions that “till” does not necessarily imply a different footing afterwards, nor does “first-born” decide whether Mary had later children, since the law regarded only that none were born before. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

No note for verse(s) 4, 9, 10, 14, 15 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
