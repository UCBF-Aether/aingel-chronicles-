Study Notes — Matthew

Chapter 3

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: After Malachi there was no prophet until John the Baptist came, about six months before his Master, preaching in the wilderness of Judea — the thinly peopled Jordan valley, not an uninhabited desert. No place is so remote as to shut us out from the visits of divine grace. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: “Repent ye” denotes a total alteration of mind — judgment, disposition and affections given another and better bias — issuing in a changed way. The kingdom of heaven, a phrase used nearly thirty times in this peculiarly Jewish Gospel and drawn from Daniel 7, is at hand, and repentance is its proper preparation because it is essentially spiritual. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: This is he spoken of by Isaiah: the voice of one crying in the wilderness, “Prepare ye the way of the Lord, make his paths straight.” Quoted in all four Gospels, it makes the forerunner the connecting link between the old and new economies, calling for every obstruction, public or personal, to be removed so all flesh may see God's salvation. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: John's raiment of camel's hair with a leathern girdle was the prophetic dress of Elijah, and his meat of locusts and wild honey the food of the poor. Dress, diet and shrill wilderness cry recalled the stern days of Elijah, and those who call others to mourn for sin ought themselves to live a serious, self-denying life. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Then went out to him Jerusalem and all Judea and all the region round about Jordan, from the metropolitan centre to the extremities of the province. Many came to John's baptism, Henry warns, but few kept the profession they made; curiosity may bring forward hearers who are never subject to the power of the word. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: They were baptized in Jordan, confessing their sins, probably aloud — a public seal of felt need of deliverance, of expectation of the coming Deliverer, and of readiness to welcome him. Jews were familiar with baptizing proselytes from heathenism, but this baptism of Jews themselves was quite new and strange. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Seeing many Pharisees and Sadducees come to his baptism, John cried, “O generation of vipers” — viper brood, charging both mutually antagonistic sects alike with poisoning the nation's religious principles. He suspected popularity rather than spiritual anxiety had drawn them, and asked who had warned them to flee from the wrath to come, God's righteous displeasure against sin, already merited yet wholly to come at judgment. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: Bring forth therefore fruit meet for repentance — fruit befitting a true penitent, not mere profession. Penitents, Henry adds, are humble in their own eyes, thankful for the least mercy, patient under affliction, watchful against sin, abounding in duty and charitable in judging others. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Think not to say, “We have Abraham to our father” — that pillow on which the nation fatally reposed, the rock on which it split. God is able of these stones to raise up children to Abraham: he stands in no need of them to make good his promise, and the saying points to Gentiles, stone-dead in sin, called into the room of unbelieving Israel. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Now also the axe lieth at the root of the trees, ready to strike — an expressive figure of impending judgment. Every tree not bringing forth good fruit is hewn down and cast into the fire; the fire, called unquenchable elsewhere, is the future torment of the impenitent, everlasting punishment, and “cast” carries a strength of just indignation. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: John baptized with water unto repentance, but he that cometh after him is mightier, whose sandals — tied and borne by the meanest servants — John is not worthy to bear. He alone shall baptize with the Holy Ghost and with fire: not a separate baptism of hell-fire, but the fiery, searching, refining character of the Spirit's own purifying work on the soul. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: His fan is in his hand, ready for use — the preaching of the Gospel itself, separating solid wheat from worthless chaff. He will throughly purge his floor, the visible church, gather his wheat, his true-hearted saints, into the garner, and burn the chaff, empty professors void of solid principle, with unquenchable fire. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Then cometh Jesus from Galilee to Jordan to be baptized of John, after nearly thirty years spent in privacy at Nazareth, calmly awaiting the Father's appointed time. Unlike Moses, who rashly anticipated his call, he waited until all other applicants that day had been disposed of, that he might not seem merely one of the crowd — separate from sinners even in baptism. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: John was in the act of hindering him: “I have need to be baptized of thee, and comest thou to me?” The emphasis lies in the pronouns — shall the Master come to the servant, the sinless Saviour to a sinner? John regarded Jesus as needing no purification but qualified to impart it, yet bore his testimony in a beautiful spirit, like Peter's “Lord, dost thou wash my feet?” — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: “Suffer it to be so now” — let it pass for the present, startling as the seeming incongruity is. “Thus it becometh us to fulfil all righteousness”: Christ owns every divine institution and, as JFB weighs it, symbolically pledges himself to the whole righteousness of the law, entering on and engaging to fulfil it all. Then John suffered him, yielding to higher authority than his own impressions. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Jesus went up straightway from the water, Luke adding that he was praying — for the work before him, the Spirit to rest upon him, and the Father's glory. The heavens were opened, Mark says cleaving, and he saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove in bodily shape and lighting, indeed abiding, upon him: chaste purity, harmlessness, meekness and beauteousness marking the holy, harmless, undefiled One. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: A voice from heaven said, “This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased” — Mark and Luke give it directly, “Thou art.” The aorist expresses absolute complacency once and for ever felt, best rendered “I delight,” echoing Isaiah 42:1, “Mine Elect, in whom my soul delighteth,” with the Spirit put upon him to bring forth judgment to the Gentiles. At this baptism the three Persons are manifested: the Father confirming the Son as Mediator, the Son entering his work, the Spirit descending to be communicated through him. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
