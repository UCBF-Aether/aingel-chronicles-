Study Notes — Matthew

Chapter 15

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Then came to Jesus scribes and Pharisees, which were of Jerusalem. A deputation probably sent from the capital expressly to watch him; as he had not come to them at the last Passover, which they had reckoned on, they now come to him. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Why do thy disciples transgress the tradition of the elders? for they wash not their hands when they eat bread. Mark explains: the Pharisees, and all the Jews, except they wash their hands oft — literally with the fist, washing one hand by the use of the other — eat not, holding the tradition of the elders, with washings of cups, pots, brazen vessels and couches at meals. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: But he answered and said unto them, Why do ye also transgress the commandment of God by your tradition? The charge is retorted with startling power: the tradition they transgress is but man's, and is itself the occasion of heavy transgression, undermining the authority of God's law. Additions to God's laws reflect upon his wisdom, as if he had left out something man could supply, and in one way or other always lead men to disobey God. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: For God commanded, saying, Honour thy father and mother: and, He that curseth father or mother, let him die the death. Our Lord points out one instance where the Pharisees' transgression was very clear, that of their transgressing the fifth commandment. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: But ye say, Whosoever shall say to his father or his mother, It is a gift, by whatsoever thou mightest be profited by me. In Mark it is “Corban!” — an oblation, any unbloody offering or gift dedicated to sacred uses. When a parent's wants called for assistance, they pleaded they had devoted to the temple all they could spare, even though they did not part with it, and therefore their parents must expect nothing from them. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: And honour not his father or his mother, he shall be free. Thus have ye made the commandment of God of none effect by your tradition. To dedicate property to God is indeed lawful and laudable, but not at the expense of filial duty; Mark adds, “ye suffer him no more to do aught for his father or his mother.” — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Ye hypocrites, well did Esaias prophesy of you, saying — Isaiah 29:13. Our blessed Lord spoke of their traditions as inventions of their own, and named them hypocrites to their faces. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: This people draweth nigh unto me with their mouth, and honoureth me with their lips; but their heart is far from me. By putting the commandments of men on a level with the divine requirements, their whole worship was rendered vain — a principle of deep moment in the service of God. Mark adds, “laying aside the commandment of God, ye hold the tradition of men, as the washing of pots and cups; and many other such like things ye do.” The drivelling nature of their multitudinous observances is here pointedly exposed, in contrast with the manly observance of the commandment of God. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: But in vain they do worship me, teaching for doctrines the commandments of men. The doom of hypocrites is put in a little compass: “In vain do they worship me.” It will neither please God nor profit themselves; they trust in vanity, and vanity will be their recompence. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: And he called the multitude, and said unto them, Hear, and understand. The foregoing dialogue, though in the people's hearing, was between Jesus and the pharisaic cavillers, whose object was to disparage him with the people. But Jesus, having put them down, turns to the multitude, who at this time were prepared to drink in everything he said, and with admirable plainness, strength and brevity lays down the great principle of real pollution. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Not that which goeth into the mouth defileth a man; but that which cometh out of the mouth, this defileth a man. This is expressed even more emphatically in Mark, with “If any man have ears to hear, let him hear” added; as in Matthew 13:9, this oft-repeated saying seems designed to call attention to the fundamental and universal character of the truth it refers to. The defilement they ought to fear was not from what entered their mouths as food, but from what came out, which showed the wickedness of their hearts. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Then came his disciples, and said unto him, Knowest thou that the Pharisees were offended, after they heard this saying? They had given vent to their irritation, and perhaps threats, not to our Lord himself, from whom they seem to have slunk away, but to some of the disciples, who report it to their Master. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: But he answered and said, Every plant, which my heavenly Father hath not planted, shall be rooted up. They are offended, are they? Heed it not: their corrupt teaching is already doomed; the garden of the Lord upon earth, too long cumbered with their presence, shall yet be purged of them and their accursed system; yea, and whatsoever is not of the planting of my heavenly Father, the great Husbandman, shall share the same fate. Nothing will last in the soul but the regenerating graces of the Holy Spirit, and nothing should be admitted into the church but what is from above. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Let them alone: they be blind leaders of the blind. And if the blind lead the blind, both shall fall into the ditch. Striking expression of the ruinous effects of erroneous teaching; whoever is offended by a plain, seasonable declaration of the truth, we should not be troubled at it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Then answered Peter and said unto him, Declare unto us this parable — when he was entered into the house from the people, Mark adds. The disciples ask to be better taught as to this matter; where a weak head doubts concerning any word of Christ, an upright heart and a willing mind seek for instruction. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: And Jesus said, Are ye also yet without understanding? Slowness of spiritual apprehension in his genuine disciples grieves the Saviour: from others he expects no better. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Do not ye yet understand, that whatsoever entereth in at the mouth goeth into the belly, and is cast out into the draught? Familiar though these sayings have now become, what freedom from bondage to outward things do they proclaim; nothing which enters from without can really defile us. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: But those things which proceed out of the mouth come forth from the heart; and they defile the man. It is the heart that is desperately wicked, for there is no sin in word or deed which was not first in the heart; when Christ teaches, he will show men the deceitfulness and wickedness of their own hearts, and teach them to humble themselves and seek to be cleansed in the Fountain opened for sin and uncleanness. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: For out of the heart proceed evil thoughts — “evil reasonings,” referring here more immediately to those corrupt reasonings which stealthily introduced and gradually reared up that hideous fabric of tradition which at length practically nullified the unchangeable principles of the moral law. But the statement is far broader: the first shape evil in the heart takes, when it begins actively to stir, is “considerations” or “reasonings” on suggested actions. Then follow murders, adulteries, fornications, thefts, false witness, blasphemies — detractions, whether against God or man, here seemingly the latter. Mark adds covetousnesses, wickednesses, deceit, lasciviousness, an evil eye, pride, foolishness — in the Old Testament sense of criminal senselessness, the folly of the heart. How appalling is this black catalogue! — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: These are the things which defile a man: but to eat with unwashen hands defileth not a man. Thus does our Lord sum up this whole searching discourse. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Then Jesus went thence, and departed into the coasts of Tyre and Sidon. The dark corners of the country, the most remote, shall share Christ's influences; afterwards the ends of the earth shall see his salvation. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: And, behold, a woman of Canaan came out of the same coasts, and cried unto him, saying, Have mercy on me, O Lord, thou Son of David; my daughter is grievously vexed with a devil. The distress and trouble of her family brought a woman to Christ; though it is need that drives us to Christ, yet we shall not therefore be driven from him. She did not limit Christ to any particular instance of mercy, but mercy, mercy is what she begged for: she pleads not merit, but depends upon mercy. It is the duty of parents to pray for their children, and to be earnest in prayer for them, especially for their souls. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: But he answered her not a word. And his disciples came and besought him, saying, Send her away; for she crieth after us. Many methods of Christ's providence, especially of his grace, in dealing with his people, which are dark and perplexing, may be explained by this story, which teaches that there may be love in Christ's heart while there are frowns in his face; and it encourages us, though he seems ready to slay us, yet to trust in him. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: But he answered and said, I am not sent but unto the lost sheep of the house of Israel. His mission's first order was to Israel, yet the Gentile woman's persistence would show that mercy was already overflowing its appointed channel. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Then came she and worshipped him, saying, Lord, help me. Those whom Christ intends most to honour, he humbles to feel their own unworthiness; a proud, unhumbled heart would not have borne this trial, but she presses on in worship and short prayer. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: But he answered and said, It is not meet to take the children's bread, and to cast it to dogs. The saying sounds harsh, yet it was the common Jewish figure for Gentiles, spoken here to draw out her faith rather than to dismiss her. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: And she said, Truth, Lord: yet the dogs eat of the crumbs which fall from their masters' table. She turned the seeming refusal into an argument to support her request: the least of Christ is precious to a believer, even the very crumbs of the Bread of life. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: Then Jesus answered and said unto her, O woman, great is thy faith: be it unto thee even as thou wilt. And her daughter was made whole from that very hour. Of all graces, faith honours Christ most; therefore of all graces Christ honours faith most. He cured her daughter. He spake, and it was done. From hence let such as seek help from the Lord, and receive no gracious answer, learn to turn even their unworthiness and discouragements into pleas for mercy. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: And Jesus departed from thence, and came nigh unto the sea of Galilee; and went up into a mountain, and sat down there. Whatever our case is, the only way to find ease and relief is to lay it at Christ's feet, to submit it to him and refer it to his disposal. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: And great multitudes came unto him, having with them those that were lame, blind, dumb, maimed, and many others, and cast them down at Jesus' feet; and he healed them. See what work sin has made; what various diseases human bodies are subject to, such as fancy could neither guess the cause nor the cure of, yet these were subject to the command of Christ. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: Insomuch that the multitude wondered, when they saw the dumb to speak, the maimed to be whole, the lame to walk, and the blind to see: and they glorified the God of Israel. The spiritual cures Christ works are wonderful: when blind souls are made to see by faith, the dumb to speak in prayer, the maimed and lame to walk in holy obedience, it is to be wondered at. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: Then Jesus called his disciples unto him, and said, I have compassion on the multitude, because they continue with me now three days, and have nothing to eat: and I will not send them away fasting, lest they faint in the way. His power was also shown to the multitude in the plentiful provision he made for them; in seeking the welfare of men's souls, we should have compassion on their bodies likewise. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: And his disciples say unto him, Whence should we have so much bread in the wilderness, as to fill so great a multitude? The disciples, fresh from the five thousand, still ask whence bread shall come — a measure of how slowly faith learns. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: And Jesus saith unto them, How many loaves have ye? And they said, Seven, and a few little fishes. The manner is much the same as before: small store in his hands becomes abundance. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: And he commanded the multitude to sit down on the ground. Order precedes the meal; the multitude is arranged as guests at a table Christ himself spreads in the wilderness. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: And he took the seven loaves and the fishes, and gave thanks, and brake them, and gave to his disciples, and the disciples to the multitude. Let us always crave a blessing on our meals; the thanksgiving before the breaking is part of the pattern, and the manner is much the same as before. — Matthew Henry

37 COMMENTARY: And they did all eat, and were filled: and they took up of the broken meat that was left seven baskets full. Those whom Christ feeds, he fills. With Christ there is bread enough, and to spare; supplies of grace for more than seek it, and for those that seek for more. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: And they that did eat were four thousand men, beside women and children. A second great feeding, distinct from the five thousand, witnessed by a different count and a different number of baskets. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: And he sent away the multitude, and took ship, and came into the coasts of Magdala. Christ sent away the people. Though he had fed them twice, they must not look for miracles to find their daily bread; let them go home to their callings and their own tables. Lord, increase our faith, and pardon our unbelief, teaching us to live upon thy fulness and bounty, for all things pertaining to this life, and that which is to come. — Matthew Henry
