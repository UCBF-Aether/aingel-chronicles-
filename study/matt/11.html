Study Notes — Matthew

Chapter 11

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: When Jesus had made an end of commanding his twelve disciples, he departed thence to teach and to preach in their cities — scarcely a fourth circuit, but visits to places not reached or too rapidly passed, filling the time till the Twelve returned. Our Divine Redeemer never was weary of his labour of love, and we should not be weary of well-doing, for in due season we shall reap if we faint not. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: When John had heard in the prison the works of Christ, he sent two of his disciples. For the account of this imprisonment, see Mark 6:17-20; JFB treats the whole passage at Luke 7:18-35, so the notes that follow rest on Henry's concise exposition of Matthew's text. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Art thou he that should come, or do we look for another? Some think John sent for his own satisfaction: where there is true faith there may yet be a mixture of unbelief, and the remaining unbelief of good men may in an hour of temptation call in question the most important truths. Others think he sent his disciples for their satisfaction, that their faith might be strengthened and confirmed. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Jesus answered and said unto them, Go and shew John again those things which ye do hear and see. Christ points them to what they heard and saw, that things seen and heard, compared with the Scriptures, direct in what way salvation is to be found. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: The blind receive their sight, and the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, and the deaf hear, the dead are raised up, and the poor have the gospel preached to them. Christ's gracious condescensions and compassions to the poor show it was he that should bring to the world the tender mercies of our God. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Blessed is he, whosoever shall not be offended in me. It is difficult to conquer prejudices, and dangerous not to conquer them; but those who believe in Christ, their faith will be found so much the more to praise, honour and glory. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: As they departed, Jesus began to say unto the multitudes concerning John, What went ye out into the wilderness to see? A reed shaken with the wind? What Christ said concerning John was not only for his praise but for the people's profit; those who attend on the word will be called to give account of their improvements, and when the sermon is done, the greatest of the care begins. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: But what went ye out for to see? A man clothed in soft raiment? Behold, they that wear soft clothing are in kings' houses. John was a self-denying man, dead to all pomps of the world and pleasures of sense; it becomes people, in all appearances, to be consistent with their character and situation. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: But what went ye out for to see? A prophet? yea, I say unto you, and more than a prophet. John was a great and good man, yet not perfect; therefore he came short of glorified saints, but as forerunner he stands above the prophets, the connecting link between the old and new economies. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: For this is he, of whom it is written, Behold, I send my messenger before thy face, which shall prepare thy way before thee. The prophecy of Malachi is fulfilled in John, the messenger preparing the way of the Lord. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Verily I say unto you, Among them that are born of women there hath not risen a greater than John the Baptist: notwithstanding he that is least in the kingdom of heaven is greater than he. The least in heaven knows, loves and does more in praising God and receives more from him than the greatest in this world; but by the kingdom of heaven here is rather meant the kingdom of grace, the gospel dispensation in its power and purity, under whose advantages our lot is thankfully cast. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: From the days of John the Baptist until now the kingdom of heaven suffereth violence, and the violent take it by force. Multitudes were wrought upon by John's ministry and became his disciples, and those strove for a place in the kingdom that one would think had no right or title to it; it shows what fervency and zeal are required of all, for those who will have an interest in the great salvation will have it upon any terms. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: For all the prophets and the law prophesied until John. Self must be denied, and the bent, frame and temper of the mind altered; God requires no more from us than the right use of the faculties he has given us, and people are ignorant because they will not learn. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: And if ye will receive it, this is Elias, which was for to come. John came in the spirit and power of Elijah, as the predicted forerunner, if they would receive the identification. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: He that hath ears to hear, let him hear. The things of God are of great and common concern, and hearing aright is the duty laid on every hearer. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: But whereunto shall I liken this generation? It is like unto children sitting in the markets, and calling unto their fellows. Christ reflects on the scribes and Pharisees, who had a proud conceit of themselves, likening their behaviour to children's play, out of temper without reason, quarrelling with all attempts of their fellows to please them. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: And saying, We have piped unto you, and ye have not danced; we have mourned unto you, and ye have not lamented. The cavils of worldly men are often very trifling and show great malice; something they have to urge against every one, however excellent and holy. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: For John came neither eating nor drinking, and they say, He hath a devil. The austere forerunner, self-denying and dead to sense, was charged with demonic possession by those who would receive neither his mourning nor Christ's piping. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: The Son of man came eating and drinking, and they say, Behold a man gluttonous, and a winebibber, a friend of publicans and sinners. But wisdom is justified of her children. Christ, undefiled and separate from sinners, is here represented as in league with them and polluted by them; the most unspotted innocence will not always be a defence against reproach, but wisdom is vindicated by those who receive her. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Then began he to upbraid the cities wherein most of his mighty works were done, because they repented not. This outburst of feeling was suggested by the result of his labours in Galilee, where the most favoured region responded with impenitence. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Woe unto thee, Chorazin! woe unto thee, Bethsaida! for if the mighty works, which were done in you, had been done in Tyre and Sidon, they would have repented long ago in sackcloth and ashes. Chorazin, not elsewhere mentioned, must have lain near Capernaum; Bethsaida, “fishing-house” on the western side of the lake, birthplace of Andrew, Peter and Philip. The two cities denote the whole region favoured above every other with his presence, teaching and works; Tyre and Sidon, wealthy commercial cities whose luxury bred irreligion, had done less violence to conscience and so were less criminal. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: But I say unto you, It shall be more tolerable for Tyre and Sidon at the day of judgment, than for you. Christ knew the Jews' hearts were more bitter and hardened against his miracles and doctrines than Tyre and Sidon's would have been, therefore their condemnation would be greater; yet the Lord punishes none more than they deserve, and never withholds truth from those who long after it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: And thou, Capernaum, which art exalted unto heaven, shalt be brought down to hell: for if the mighty works, which have been done in thee, had been done in Sodom, it would have remained until this day. Capernaum, where Jesus had his stated abode through his Galilean public life, was the most favoured spot on earth, the most exalted in privilege; Sodom, destroyed for its pollutions, had done no such violence to conscience and incurred speakably less guilt. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: But I say unto you, That it shall be more tolerable for the land of Sodom in the day of judgment, than for thee. Stanley notes it has been more tolerable even in earthly judgment for Sodom, whose name and perhaps remains are still found by the Dead Sea, while Capernaum's name on the lake has been utterly lost; but the judgment here spoken of is still future, on responsible inhabitants, final and irretrievable. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: At that time Jesus answered and said, I thank thee, O Father, Lord of heaven and earth, because thou hast hid these things from the wise and prudent, and hast revealed them unto babes. “Answered” refers to the melancholy results just lamented: yes, but there is a brighter side — the pride of rejecters' hearts blinded them, while thirsting souls drew water with joy from salvation's wells. “I thank thee” conveys full, cordial concurrence, adoring acquiescence in the divine procedure. The wise are men proud of speculative attainments, the prudent men of worldly shrewdness; to bring our own wisdom to questions of recovery is impertinent, and if truth be hid from us it is fitting retribution. Babes are men of unassuming docility, conscious they know nothing, determined simply to hear what God the Lord will speak. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Even so, Father: for so it seemed good in thy sight. This is the emphatic term for any object of divine complacency, whether Christ himself or God's gracious eternal arrangements; a sublime echo, as if Jesus paused to reflect on the glory of God's absolute will that so it should be. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: All things are delivered unto me of my Father: and no man knoweth the Son, but the Father; neither knoweth any man the Father, save the Son, and he to whomsoever the Son will reveal him. “Delivered over” means the whole administration of the kingdom of grace, including all power necessary to execute it. That Father and Son are mutually and exclusively known to each other is a claim to equality with the Father beyond which nothing higher can be conceived; yet this sovereignty of gracious will is revealed not to paralyse souls in despair but to enhance the glory of grace once imparted, as what follows shows. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. Incomparable, ravishing sounds in this weary, groaning world: “Hither to Me”; in “all ye that toil and are burdened” the universal wretchedness of man is depicted in both its forms, active and passive. In some senses all men are so: worldly men burdened with fruitless cares for wealth and honours, the gay and sensual labouring after pleasures, the slave of Satan and his lusts the merest drudge, those labouring to establish their own righteousness labouring in vain, the convinced sinner heavy-laden with guilt and terror, the tempted believer with labours and burdens. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart: and ye shall find rest unto your souls. As Christ's willingness to empty himself to the uttermost of his Father's requirements was the spring of ineffable repose to his own Spirit, so in the same track he invites all to follow him with assurance of the same experience. In coming they must take his yoke and submit to his authority, learning of him all things as to comfort and obedience; he accepts the willing servant, however imperfect the services. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light. Matchless paradox: the rest the soul experiences once safe under Christ's wing makes all yokes easy, all burdens light. His commandments are holy, just and good; the yoke requires self-denial and exposes to difficulties, but is abundantly repaid even in this world by inward peace and joy — a yoke lined with love, and the way of duty is the way of rest. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
