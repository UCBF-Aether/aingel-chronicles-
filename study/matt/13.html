Study Notes — Matthew

Chapter 13

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The same day went Jesus out of the house, and sat by the sea side — the same day on which the solemn discourse of chapter 12 was delivered and his kindred thought him beside himself, retiring to the seashore for coolness and rest, only to begin without waiting to rest a new course of teaching. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Great multitudes were gathered together unto him, so that he went into a ship, and sat; and the whole multitude stood on the shore — a graphic picture, no doubt from an eye-witness. He entered a boat that he might be less pressed and better heard, teaching us in worship's outward circumstances not to covet stateliness but to make the best of the conveniences Providence allots. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: He spake many things unto them in parables, saying, Behold, a sower went forth to sow. These parables are seven in number: the first four spoken to the mixed multitude, the remaining three to the Twelve in private, the first, the Sower, serving as introduction to the whole, and the rest in three pairs setting forth the same truths under diverse aspects. To the parables of our Lord there is nothing in all language to be compared for simplicity, grace, fulness and variety of spiritual teaching. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: When he sowed, some seeds fell by the way side, and the fowls came and devoured them up. Careless, trifling hearers are an easy prey to Satan, who as the great murderer of souls is also the great thief of sermons, and will be sure to rob us of the word if we take not care to keep it. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Some fell upon stony places, where they had not much earth: and forthwith they sprung up, because they had no deepness of earth. Hypocrites, like stony ground, often get the start of true Christians in shows of profession, glad to hear of free salvation and heaven's happiness without any change of heart or abiding conviction of depravity and need of a Saviour. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: And when the sun was up, they were scorched; and because they had no root, they withered away. When some heavy trial threatens, or some sinful advantage may be had, they give up or disguise their profession, or turn to some easier system; many are glad to hear a good sermon who do not profit by it. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Some fell among thorns; and the thorns sprung up, and choked them. Worldly cares are fitly compared to thorns, for they came in with sin and are a fruit of the curse; good in their place to stop a gap, but entangling, vexing, scratching, and their end is to be burned. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: But other fell into good ground, and brought forth fruit, some an hundredfold, some sixtyfold, some thirtyfold. What distinguished the good ground was fruitfulness, by which true Christians are distinguished from hypocrites; Christ does not say this ground has no stones or thorns, but none that could hinder its fruitfulness. All are not alike; we should aim at the highest, to bring forth most fruit. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Who hath ears to hear, let him hear. The sense of hearing cannot be better employed than in hearing God's word, and we must look to ourselves to know what sort of hearers we are. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: The disciples came, and said unto him, Why speakest thou unto them in parables? Though he had couched some things in parabolic form before for vivid illustration, it would appear he now, for the first time, formally employed this method of teaching, and the disciples asked why when they were alone with him. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: He answered: Because it is given unto you to know the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven, but to them it is not given. “Mysteries” in Scripture means things of purely divine revelation, darkly announced under the ancient economy and fully published under the Gospel. Parables serve the double purpose of revealing and concealing: presenting the mysteries in a new and attractive light to those who know and relish them, but to the insensible yielding only temporary entertainment, as so many tales. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: For whosoever hath, to him shall be given, and he shall have more abundance: but whosoever hath not, from him shall be taken away even that he hath. “Hath” means keeps, as a thing he values, and he will be rewarded by increase of what he prizes; “hath not” means lets it go unused, as a thing on which he sets no value, and even what he seemeth to have shall be taken. This is a principle of immense importance, operating under the general law of habit, here viewed as a divine ordination and judicial retribution in continual operation. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Therefore speak I to them in parables: because they seeing see not; and hearing they hear not, neither do they understand. Our Lord did not begin to speak in parables till his miracles were malignantly ascribed to Satan. They saw, for light shone on them as never before, but they saw not, for they closed their eyes; they heard him who spake as never man spake, but took nothing in. By a fixed law of divine administration, the duty men voluntarily refuse and do not do, they at length become morally incapable of doing. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: And in them is fulfilled — rather, is fulfilling — the prophecy of Esaias, which saith, By hearing ye shall hear, and shall not understand; and seeing ye shall see, and shall not perceive. They were thus judicially sealed up under the darkness and obduracy they deliberately preferred to the light and healing Jesus brought nigh to them. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: For this people's heart is waxed gross, and their ears are dull of hearing, and their eyes they have closed; lest at any time they should see with their eyes, and hear with their ears, and should understand with their heart, and should be converted, and I should heal them. The things of God were made more plain and easy to those willing to be taught, and at the same time more difficult and obscure to those willingly ignorant. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: But blessed are your eyes, for they see: and your ears, for they hear. Happy ye, whose eyes and ears, voluntarily and gladly opened, are drinking in the light divine. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: For verily I say unto you, That many prophets and righteous men have desired to see those things which ye see, and have not seen them; and to hear those things which ye hear, and have not heard them. The disciples were blessed above the blinded, and favoured above the most honoured under the old economy, who had but glimpses of the new kingdom, sufficient to kindle desires not to be fulfilled in their day. In Luke the words are “many prophets and kings,” for several Old Testament saints were kings. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Hear ye therefore the parable of the sower. The seed sown is the word of God, the sower our Lord Jesus Christ, by himself or by his ministers; preaching to a multitude is sowing the corn, and we know not where it will light. Some sort of ground, though we take ever so much pains with it, brings forth no fruit to purpose, while the good soil brings forth plentifully. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: When any one heareth the word of the kingdom, and understandeth it not, then cometh the wicked one, and catcheth away that which was sown in his heart. This is he which received seed by the way side. So it is with the hearts of men, whose different characters are described by four sorts of ground, and the wayside hearer's word is stolen before it can take root. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: But he that received the seed into stony places, the same is he that heareth the word, and anon with joy receiveth it. Without any change of heart, without abiding conviction of depravity, need of a Saviour, or excellence of holiness, they soon profess an unwarranted assurance. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Yet hath he not root in himself, but dureth for a while: for when tribulation or persecution ariseth because of the word, by and by he is offended. The stony-ground hearer gives up or disguises his profession when trial threatens or sinful advantage beckons. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: He also that received seed among the thorns is he that heareth the word; and the care of this world, and the deceitfulness of riches, choke the word, and he becometh unfruitful. Worldly cares are great hinderances to profiting by the word; the deceitfulness of riches does the mischief, for they cannot be said to deceive us unless we put our trust in them, and then they choke the good seed. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: But he that received seed into the good ground is he that heareth the word, and understandeth it; which also beareth fruit, and bringeth forth, some an hundredfold, some sixty, some thirty. The good ground's distinction is fruitfulness, and though not all alike, we should aim at bringing forth most fruit. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Another parable put he forth unto them, saying, The kingdom of heaven is likened unto a man which sowed good seed in his field. Happily these exquisite parables are, with charming simplicity and clearness, expounded by the Great Preacher himself, and the second and seventh parables form a pair teaching the mixed character of the kingdom in its present state and the final absolute separation of the two classes. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: But while men slept, his enemy came and sowed tares among the wheat, and went his way. This parable represents the present and future state of the gospel church: Christ's care of it, the devil's enmity against it, the mixture of good and bad in this world, and the separation in the other world. So prone is fallen man to sin that if the enemy sow tares, he may go his way and they will spring up and do hurt, whereas good seed must be tended, watered and fenced. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: But when the blade was sprung up, and brought forth fruit, then appeared the tares also — the growth in both cases running parallel, as antagonistic principles are seen to do. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: So the servants of the householder came and said unto him, Sir, didst not thou sow good seed in thy field? from whence then hath it tares? This well expresses the surprise, disappointment and anxiety of Christ's faithful servants at the discovery of false brethren among church members. No doubt he did sow good seed; whatever is amiss in the church, we are sure it is not from Christ. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: He said unto them, An enemy hath done this. Kind words from a good Husbandman, honourably clearing his faithful servants of the wrong done to his field. The servants said unto him, Wilt thou then that we go and gather them up? Compare James and John's question about commanding fire from heaven: in this kind of zeal there is usually a large mixture of carnal heat. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: But he said, Nay; lest while ye gather up the tares, ye root up also the wheat with them. Nothing could more clearly teach the difficulty of distinguishing the two classes and the probability they will be confounded in the attempt. Though gross transgressors and open opposers of the gospel ought to be separated from the faithful, no human skill can make an exact separation; those who oppose must not be cut off but instructed, and that with meekness. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: Let both grow together until the harvest: and in the time of harvest I will say to the reapers, Gather ye together first the tares, and bind them in bundles to burn them: but gather the wheat into my barn. Let both grow together in the visible church until the one ripen for full salvation and the other for destruction; Christ as Judge will separate the two classes, and it is observed the tares are burned before the wheat is housed, as in the exposition and in Matthew 25:46. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: Another parable put he forth unto them, saying, The kingdom of heaven is like to a grain of mustard seed, which a man took, and sowed in his field. The scope is to show the beginnings of the gospel would be small but its latter end greatly increase, and so the work of grace in the heart would be carried on: in the soul where grace truly is, it will grow really, though at first not discerned, until at last it comes to great strength and usefulness. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: Which indeed is the least of all seeds, but when it is grown, it is the greatest among herbs, and becometh a tree, so that the birds of the air come and lodge in the branches thereof. “Least” is popular and proverbial, not absolute, as in “faith as a grain of mustard seed”; “greatest among herbs” is in relation to the seed's small size, and in warm latitudes proverbially great. The seed's hot, fiery vigour, giving its best virtues when bruised, and grateful to birds attracted for shelter and food, may further illustrate the shelter, repose and blessedness the kingdom is destined to afford the nations. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: Another parable spake he unto them; The kingdom of heaven is like unto leaven, which a woman took, and hid in three measures of meal, till the whole was leavened. While the mustard seed points chiefly to outward growth, the leaven holds forth rather the inward growth of the kingdom: the preaching of the gospel works like leaven in hearts receiving it, certainly yet gradually, silently and without being seen yet strongly, without noise for so is the way of the Spirit, but without fail. In the world the apostles hid a handful of leaven in the great mass of mankind, made powerful by the Spirit whom none can hinder; in the heart the gospel works a thorough change, spreading into all powers and faculties of the soul. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: All these things spake Jesus unto the multitude in parables; and without a parable spake he not unto them — that is, on this occasion, refraining not only from all naked discourse but even from all interpretation of these parables to the mixed multitude. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: That it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the prophet, saying, I will open my mouth in parables, and will utter things which have been kept secret from the foundation of the world (Psalm 78:2, nearly as in the Septuagint). Though the Psalm seems only a summary of Israelite history, the Psalmist himself calls it “a parable” and “dark sayings from of old,” containing underneath the history truths for all time, not fully brought to light till the Gospel day. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: Then Jesus sent the multitude away, and went into the house: and his disciples came unto him, saying, Declare unto us the parable of the tares of the field. In the Sower, the seed is the word of God; but here that word, received into the heart, has converted the receiver into a new creature, a child of the kingdom, as James says, “Of his own will begat he us with the word of truth, that we should be a kind of firstfruits of his creatures.” This vast field of the world is Christ's own field. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

37 COMMENTARY: He that soweth the good seed is the Son of man; the field is the world; the good seed are the children of the kingdom; but the tares are the children of the wicked one. The good seed, received into hearts, become children of the kingdom, while the tares, children of the wicked one, are deposited within the territory of the visible church. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: The enemy that sowed them is the devil — emphatically “his enemy.” By “tares” is meant not our husbandry's tare but some noxious plant, probably darnel; as the tares resemble the children of the kingdom, so they are produced by a similar process of sowing, the seeds of evil lodging in hearts upon which falls the seed of the world. The enemy, after sowing, “went his way,” his dark work soon done but taking time to develop its true character. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

39 COMMENTARY: The harvest is the end of the world, the period of Christ's second coming and of the judicial separation of the righteous and wicked. Till then no attempt is to be made to effect such separation, though stretching this to justify allowing openly scandalous persons to remain in church communion wrests the parable against apostolic injunctions. And the reapers are the angels — the Son of man's angels, subject unto him. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

40 COMMENTARY: As therefore the tares are gathered and burned in the fire; so shall it be in the end of this world. Though good and bad are together in this world, yet at the great day they shall be parted, and then the righteous and wicked shall be plainly known, where here it is sometimes hard to distinguish them. — Matthew Henry

41 COMMENTARY: The Son of man shall send forth his angels, and they shall gather out of his kingdom all things that offend, and them which do iniquity. The offenders never really belonged to his kingdom; they usurped place, name and outward privileges, but “the ungodly shall not stand in the judgment, nor sinners in the congregation of the righteous.” Those proving a stumbling-block to others are mentioned first, as the worst. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

42 COMMENTARY: And shall cast them into a furnace of fire: there shall be wailing and gnashing of teeth. What terrific strength of language: the “casting” expressive of indignation, abhorrence and contempt; the furnace, the fierceness of torment; the wailing, the anguish it causes; the gnashing of teeth, the despair in which its remedilessness issues. Let us, knowing the terrors of the Lord, not do iniquity. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

43 COMMENTARY: Then shall the righteous shine forth as the sun in the kingdom of their Father. As if they had been under a cloud during the present association with ungodly pretenders, claimants of their privileges and obstructors of their course, at death believers shall shine forth to themselves, and at the great day before all the world, by reflection with light borrowed from the Fountain of light, their sanctification perfected and justification published. Who hath ears to hear, let him hear. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

44 COMMENTARY: Again, the kingdom of heaven is like unto treasure hid in a field; the which when a man hath found, he hideth, and for joy thereof goeth and selleth all that he hath, and buyeth that field. Treasure hidden in a field was no uncommon thing in unsettled countries, where there was no other way to secure it from neighbours or marauders. The kingdom here is found without seeking, unexpectedly; on perceiving a treasure surpassing all he possessed, the finder sells all and buys the field, in which case by Jewish law the treasure becomes his own. Many slight the gospel because they look only upon the surface of the field, but all who search the Scriptures so as to find Christ and eternal life will discover treasure making it unspeakably valuable, and make it their own upon any terms; though nothing can be given as a price for salvation, yet much must be given up for its sake. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

45 COMMENTARY: Again, the kingdom of heaven is like unto a merchant man, seeking goodly pearls. The fifth and sixth parables form a pair on the priceless value of the kingdom's blessings: the treasure is found without seeking, the pearl is sought and found. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

46 COMMENTARY: Who, when he had found one pearl of great price, went and sold all that he had, and bought it. The pearl, found by one whose business is seeking such treasures, is alike recognized as surpassing in value, and in both cases all is parted with for it. All the children of men are busy — one would be rich, another honourable, another learned — but most are deceived and take up counterfeits for pearls; Jesus Christ is the Pearl of great price, in having whom we have enough to make us happy here and for ever. A man may buy gold too dear, but not this Pearl; when the convinced sinner sees Christ as gracious Saviour, all things else become worthless to his thoughts. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

47 COMMENTARY: Again, the kingdom of heaven is like unto a net, that was cast into the sea, and gathered of every kind. The net is a large drag-net, drawing everything after it and suffering nothing to escape, denoting the far-reaching efficacy of the Gospel; it gathered every variety of character. The world is a vast sea, men in their natural state like fishes, and preaching the gospel is casting a net into this sea to catch something out of it for his glory who has sovereignty of this sea. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

48 COMMENTARY: Which, when it was full, they drew to shore, and sat down, and gathered the good into vessels, but cast the bad away. The separation will not be made till the number of the elect is accomplished, and “sat down” expresses the deliberateness with which the judicial separation will at length be made; the bad are literally the rotten, here the foul or worthless fish, corresponding to the tares. Hypocrites and true Christians shall be parted, and miserable is the condition of those then cast away. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

49 COMMENTARY: So shall it be at the end of the world: the angels shall come forth, and sever the wicked from among the just, and shall cast them into the furnace of fire: there shall be wailing and gnashing of teeth. The diversity between the tares and net parables is this: in the former the bad are vile seed sown among wheat by the enemy of souls, in the latter foul fish drawn out of the sea of humanity by the Gospel net itself — both important truths, that the Gospel draws into the visible church multitudes who are Christians only in name, and that the injury done to the church is traced to the wicked one; while the former gives prominence to the present mixture, the latter to the future separation. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

50 COMMENTARY: And shall cast them into the furnace of fire: there shall be wailing and gnashing of teeth. As with the tares, the furnace denotes fierceness of torment, the wailing anguish, and the gnashing despair at remedilessness; the same awful language recurs because the same judgment is in view. — Matthew Henry

51 COMMENTARY: Jesus saith unto them, Have ye understood all these things? They say unto him, Yea, Lord. He had spoken the first four parables to the mixed multitude, and reserved the last three till, on the multitude's dismissal, he and the Twelve were alone in the house. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

52 COMMENTARY: Then said he unto them, Therefore every scribe which is instructed unto the kingdom of heaven is like unto a man that is an householder, which bringeth forth out of his treasure things new and old. A scribe here is a Christian teacher, himself taught in the mysteries of the Gospel he must teach others; a skilful, faithful minister is a householder dealing out from his store of divine truth old truths in ever new forms, aspects, applications and illustrations, with abundance and variety — old experiences and new observations all having their use. Our place is at Christ's feet, daily learning old lessons over again and new ones also. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

53 COMMENTARY: And it came to pass, that when Jesus had finished these parables, he departed thence. The parable discourse at the seaside closes, and the scene shifts to his own country. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

54 COMMENTARY: When he was come into his own country, that is, Nazareth, he taught them in their synagogue, insomuch that they were astonished, and said, Whence hath this man this wisdom, and these mighty works? This, in JFB's view, was his first and only visit to Nazareth during his public ministry, the majority of harmonists counting it the second; the questions are not those of people who had before proceeded from astonishment to rage at the brow of the hill, but of townsmen newly confronted with wisdom and miracles transcending human ability. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

55 COMMENTARY: Is not this the carpenter's son? is not his mother called Mary? and his brethren, James, and Joses, and Simon, and Judas? In Mark the question is, “Is not this the carpenter?” — in all likelihood our Lord, under his legal father's roof, wrought along with him. These particulars are valuable testimony, first, to his true humanity, for during thirty years his townsmen discovered nothing in him different from other men; second, to the divine character of his mission, for the Nazarenes themselves proclaim his teaching unparalleled and his miracles real and glorious; third, to his wonderful humility, in that being such as they now saw, he yet gave no indications of it for thirty years, because his hour was not yet come. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

56 COMMENTARY: And his sisters, are they not all with us? Whence then hath this man all these things? Here arises the difficult question of what the “brethren” and “sisters” were to Jesus — full brothers and sisters, step-children of Joseph by a former marriage, or cousins after Jewish collateral usage. JFB leaves the question dubiously: the first opinion suits the text best wherever the parties are certainly referred to, yet many best interpreters prefer cousins, thinking it improbable the Lord would commit his mother to John from the cross if full brothers were alive, though he might have good reasons for preferring the beloved disciple. James here, called the Lord's brother, may or may not be James son of Alphaeus; Joses must not be confused with Joseph Barsabas Justus; Simon is not Simon the Zealot; Judas can hardly be the apostle of that name. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

57 COMMENTARY: And they were offended in him. But Jesus said unto them, A prophet is not without honour, save in his own country, and in his own house. Christ repeats his offer to those who have repulsed him, and they upbraid him: Is not this the carpenter's son? Yes, it is true he was reputed so, and no disgrace to be the son of an honest tradesman; they should have respected him the more because he was one of themselves, but therefore they despised him. — Matthew Henry

58 COMMENTARY: And he did not many mighty works there, because of their unbelief — save that he laid his hands on a few sick folk and healed them, Mark adds. Unbelief is the great hinderance to Christ's favours; let us keep faithful to him as the Saviour who has made our peace with God. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
