Study Notes — Matthew

Chapter 9

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: He entered into a ship, and passed over, and came into his own city, Capernaum, returning from the country of the Gadarenes to the scene of his Galilean ministry. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Behold, they brought to him a man sick of the palsy, lying on a bed: and Jesus seeing their faith said unto the sick of the palsy, Son, be of good cheer; thy sins be forgiven thee. The friends' faith was strong, regarding no obstacles in pressing after Christ, humble in bringing him to attend on Christ, and active; sin may be pardoned yet sickness remain, and sickness removed yet sin not pardoned, but peace with God joined to recovery makes the healing a mercy indeed. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Behold, certain of the scribes said within themselves, This man blasphemeth. Our Lord Jesus has perfect knowledge of all we say within ourselves, and there is a great deal of evil in sinful thoughts, very offensive to him. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Jesus knowing their thoughts said, Wherefore think ye evil in your hearts? He turned from disputing with the scribes and spake healing to the sick man, designing to show his great errand was to save his people from their sins. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: For whether is easier, to say, Thy sins be forgiven thee; or to say, Arise, and walk? The question exposes the scribes: forgiveness is the harder, unseen claim, and the visible cure will prove it. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: But that ye may know that the Son of man hath power on earth to forgive sins, then saith he to the sick of the palsy, Arise, take up thy bed, and go unto thine house. This is no encouragement to sin: if thou bring thy sins to Jesus as thy malady to be cured of, it is well, but to come with them as darlings to retain while receiving him is a gross mistake. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: He arose, and departed to his house. Not only had he no more need to be carried upon his bed, but he had strength to carry it; God must be glorified in all the power given to do good. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: When the multitudes saw it, they marvelled, and glorified God, which had given such power unto men. The great intention of the blessed Jesus in the redemption he wrought is to separate our hearts from sin, and the crowd's glorifying of God answers it. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: As Jesus passed forth from thence, he saw a man, named Matthew, sitting at the receipt of custom — the writer of this Gospel, who with singular modesty and brevity relates his own calling. In Mark and Luke he is Levi, his family name, while Matthew is his disciple name; son of Alphaeus, sitting at the toll booth by the seaside, perhaps collecting the ferry tax. “Follow me”: witching words, never employed without resistless efficacy; he left all, arose and followed him. Christ comes with his calls to the employed, as Satan comes with temptations to the idle, and Matthew was never more found at his sinful gain. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: As Jesus sat at meat in the house — Luke says Levi made him a great feast in his own house, while Matthew merely says “in the house.” The feast probably came not in the novelty of discipleship but after Matthew was somewhat established, when compassion for old friends of his own calling led him to gather them to hear the gracious words of his Master. Behold, many publicans and sinners came and sat down — reclined — with him and his disciples. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: When the Pharisees — and scribes, add Mark and Luke — saw it, they said, murmured, unto his disciples, not venturing to put their question to Jesus himself: Why eateth your Master with publicans and sinners? It is easy and too common to put the worst constructions upon the best words and actions, and it may justly be suspected those have not the grace of God themselves who are not pleased with others' obtaining it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: When Jesus heard that, he said unto them: They that be whole need not a physician, but they that are sick — that is, “Ye deem yourselves whole; my mission therefore is not to you: the physician's business is with the sick; therefore eat I with publicans and sinners.” Those who suppose their souls without disease will not welcome the spiritual Physician; the Pharisees despised Christ because they thought themselves whole, but poor publicans and sinners felt they wanted instruction and amendment. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: But go ye and learn what that meaneth, I will have mercy, and not sacrifice (Hosea 6:6): sacrifice, the chief part of the ceremonial law, stands for a religion of literal adherence to mere rules, while mercy expresses compassion for the fallen seeking to lift them up. To keep aloof from the polluted in the sense of no fellowship with unfruitful works of darkness is obvious, but to read it as prohibiting intercourse necessary to their recovery is abuse — the very thing these religionists did. For I am not come to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance: the righteous are the whole, sinners the sick, and Christ's call to the latter was to heal their spiritual maladies. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Then came to him the disciples of John, saying, Why do we and the Pharisees fast oft, but thy disciples fast not? John was at this time in prison, and his circumstances, character and message led those peculiarly attached to him to keep frequent fasts. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Jesus said unto them, Can the children of the bridechamber mourn, as long as the bridegroom is with them? but the days will come, when the bridegroom shall be taken from them, and then shall they fast. Christ referred them to John's testimony of him (John 3:29); though Jesus and his disciples lived sparely and frugally, it would be improper to fast while they had the comfort of his presence, for the presence of the sun makes day and its absence night. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: No man putteth a piece of new cloth unto an old garment, for that which is put in to fill it up taketh from the garment, and the rent is made worse. It was not usual to join rough, unprepared wool to a soft old garment; great caution and prudence are necessary that young converts may not receive gloomy and forbidding ideas of the Lord's service, but duties are urged as they are able to bear them. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Neither do men put new wine into old bottles: else the bottles break, and the wine runneth out, and the bottles perish: but they put new wine into new bottles, and both are preserved. Old leathern bottles, going to decay, would burst from the fermenting wine; the new wine of the gospel needs new vessels. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: While he spake these things unto them, behold, there came a certain ruler, and worshipped him, saying, My daughter is even now dead: but come and lay thy hand upon her, and she shall live. The death of our relations should drive us to Christ, who is our life; it is high honour to the greatest rulers to attend on the Lord Jesus, and those who would receive mercy must honour him. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Jesus arose, and followed him, and so did his disciples. The variety of methods Christ took in working his miracles perhaps suited the different frames and tempers of those who came to him, which he who searches the heart perfectly knew. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Behold, a woman, which was diseased with an issue of blood twelve years, came behind him, and touched the hem of his garment. A poor woman applied herself to Christ and received mercy by the way; if we do but touch, as it were, the hem of Christ's garment by living faith, our worst evils will be healed. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: For she said within herself, If I may but touch his garment, I shall be whole. There is no other real cure, nor need we fear his knowing things which are a grief and burden to us but which we would not tell to any earthly friend. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: The woman received mercy by the way, and Henry's point is that if we but touch the hem of Christ's garment by living faith, our worst evils are healed; there is no other real cure, nor need we fear his knowing the griefs we would tell no earthly friend. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: When Jesus came into the ruler's house, and saw the minstrels and the people making a noise, he said, Give place. Sometimes, when the sorrow of the world prevails, it is difficult for Christ and his comforts to enter. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: For the maid is not dead, but sleepeth. And they laughed him to scorn. The ruler's daughter was really dead, but not so to Christ; the death of the righteous is in a special manner to be looked on as only a sleep. The words and works of Christ may not at first be understood, yet they are not therefore to be despised. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: But when the people were put forth, he went in, and took her by the hand, and the maid arose. Scorners who laugh at what they do not understand are not proper witnesses of Christ's wonderful works; dead souls are not raised to spiritual life unless Christ take them by the hand, in the day of his power. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: The fame hereof went abroad into all that land. If this single instance of Christ's raising one newly dead so increased his fame, what will be his glory when all in their graves shall hear his voice and come forth, those that have done good to the resurrection of life, and those that have done evil to the resurrection of damnation! — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: When Jesus departed thence, two blind men followed him, crying, and saying, Thou son of David, have mercy on us. At this time the Jews expected Messiah would appear; these blind men knew and proclaimed in the streets of Capernaum that he was come, and Jesus was he. Their faith fastened on such Messianic promises as “Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened” (Isaiah 35:5), appealing to him as the Consolation of Israel to do his predicted office. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: When he was come into the house, the blind men came to him: and Jesus saith unto them, Believe ye that I am able to do this? They said unto him, Yea, Lord. To try their faith and patience he seems to have made them no answer at first; his question was designed not only to test their faith but to deepen it and raise their expectation, and their touchingly simple acknowledgment shows the object was gained. They followed him crying aloud, but the great question is, Do ye believe? Nature may make us earnest, but only grace can work faith. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: Then touched he their eyes, saying, According to your faith be it unto you. Not a cure proportioned to faith, but a cure granted to faith, which they would carry in their restored vision as a gracious seal of the faith that drew it from their compassionate Lord. Christ gives sight to blind souls by his grace going with his word, and puts the cure upon their faith. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: Their eyes were opened; and Jesus straitly charged them, with great earnestness, saying, See that no man know it. Christ sometimes concealed his miracles because he would not indulge the conceit that their Messiah should be a temporal prince, nor give occasion to tumults and seditions. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: But they, when they were departed, spread abroad his fame in all that country — as with the leper at 8:4, an over-zealous, though natural and not very culpable, infringement of the injunction to keep the matter quiet. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: As they went out, behold, they brought to him a dumb man possessed with a devil. The dumbness was not natural, but was the effect of the possession; Christ's cures strike at the root, removing the effect by taking away the cause, opening lips by breaking Satan's power in the soul. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: When the devil was cast out, the dumb spake: and the multitudes marvelled, saying, It was never so seen in Israel. The particulars are not given; the object is the instantaneous restoration of natural faculties on removal of the malignant oppression, and the popular astonishment, probably referring to all those miraculous displays promising a new era, and hinting their inclination to regard him as the promised Messiah. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: But the Pharisees said, He casteth out devils through the prince of the devils. This seems the first muttering of a theory soon becoming a fixed mode of calumniating his miracles — a theory ridiculous if it were not melancholy as an outburst of the darkest malignity; nothing can convince those under pride's power, who will believe anything false or absurd rather than the Scriptures. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: Jesus went about all the cities and villages, teaching in their synagogues, and preaching the gospel of the kingdom, and healing every sickness and every disease among the people. The language is so identical with the first circuit (4:23) that the work was much the same: a further preparation of the soil and fresh sowing of precious seed. He visited not only great and wealthy cities but poor, obscure villages, for the souls of the meanest are as precious to Christ as those of the greatest. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: When he saw the multitudes, he was moved with compassion on them, because they fainted — the true reading doubtless “were harassed” — and were scattered abroad, rather “lying about, abandoned, neglected,” as sheep having no shepherd. There were priests, Levites and scribes all over the land, but they were idol shepherds; the people's weariness under bodily fatigue was but a faint picture of their wretchedness as victims of Pharisaic guidance, souls uncared for yet hanging upon him. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

37 COMMENTARY: Then saith he unto his disciples, The harvest truly is plenteous, but the labourers are few. His eye rested immediately on the Jewish field, but he saw it widening into the vast field of the world, teeming with souls to be gathered to him; the multitudes desirous of instruction formed a plenteous harvest needing many active labourers. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

38 COMMENTARY: Pray ye therefore the Lord of the harvest, that he will send forth labourers into his harvest. “Send forth” properly means thrust forth, as the Lord and Proprietor of all; it is a sign God is about to bestow some special mercy on a people when he stirs them up to pray for it, and commissions given in answer to prayer are most likely to be successful. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
