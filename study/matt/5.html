Study Notes — Matthew

Chapter 5

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Seeing the multitudes, he went up into a mountain near the Sea of Galilee, and when he was set, his disciples came unto him — a large circle attracted by his preaching and miracles, besides the smaller band of devoted adherents. None will find happiness in this world or the next who do not seek it from Christ by the rule of his word. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: He opened his mouth, a solemn way of arousing attention for something weighty, and taught them. He taught what was the evil they should abhor and what the good they should seek and abound in. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: “Blessed” here points to inward happiness in a lofty sense. The poor in spirit are those who in their deepest consciousness realize their entire need — self-emptying conviction that before God we are void of everything — and theirs already is the kingdom of heaven, for the very sense of their poverty is begun riches. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: They that mourn are the complement of the poor in spirit: poverty of spirit says “I am undone,” mourning breaks forth, “Woe is me, for I am undone.” These are mourners in Zion, and they shall be comforted — even now with beauty for ashes, and at last when God wipes away all tears. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: The meek, of lowly and gentle carriage, quietly submitting to God, bearing insult and returning a soft answer, shall inherit the earth — the promise of Psalm 37:11, the land as the Old Testament evidence of God's favour. The meek are the only rightful occupants of a foot of ground here, and heirs of all coming things. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: They which hunger and thirst after righteousness crave, with the keenest of appetites, spiritual and entire conformity to God's law, the possession of which is true saintship. They shall be filled — saturated — not merely supplied, though the full satisfaction awaits the waking in his likeness. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: The merciful shall obtain mercy, by what Bengel calls the gracious requital of the kingdom. The Christian stands between a mercy received and a mercy yet needed: forgiven ten thousand talents, he forgives his brother's hundred pence, and the foretastes of recompense here give way to perfection at the great white throne. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: The pure in heart shall see God. Heart purity begins in a conscience purged from dead works and a heart sprinkled from an evil conscience; with light within, the inner man looks on God with complacency, while the full beatific vision — seeing him as he is — is reserved for glory. Without holiness no man shall see the Lord. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: The peacemakers not only study peace but diffuse it, and shall be called the children of God. This beatitude alone could hardly find its ground in the Old Testament, for God as the God of peace was fully revealed only when Christ made peace by the blood of the cross; peace-receivers become peace-diffusers, and by the family likeness are recognized as his children. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: They persecuted for righteousness' sake are blessed — the eighth beatitude is supplementary, denoting merely the possessors of the seven preceding features, on account of which they are persecuted. The seven characters run counter to the spirit of the world at every point, and theirs is the kingdom of heaven, the same promise as the first. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Blessed are ye when men revile you to your face, persecute you, and say all manner of evil against you falsely, for my sake. Where he before said “for righteousness' sake,” he now identifies himself and his cause with righteousness — language no mere prophet could use, but fitting for Righteousness incarnate. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Rejoice and be exceeding glad — exult, even leap — for great is your reward in heaven, for so persecuted they the prophets before you. You do but serve yourselves heirs to their character and sufferings, and the reward will be common. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Ye are the salt of the earth, to preserve it from corruption, season its insipidity, and freshen it. Mankind under unrestrained evil nature is entirely corrupt, and the remedy is the active presence of disciples among their fellows; but if the salt lose its savour, wherewith shall it be salted? It is thenceforth good for nothing but to be cast out and trodden under foot — indignant exclusion and contempt for a profession that begets expectation and disappoints it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Ye are the light of the world — his own distinctive title, applied to disciples only as they shine with his light by his Spirit dwelling in them. Salt operates internally in the mass; sunlight operates externally, irradiating all it reaches. A city set on a hill cannot be hid, nor was it built except to be seen. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Neither do men light a lamp and put it under a bushel, a dry measure, but on a lampstand, that it may shine unto all in the house. The familiar household utensils make the point plain: light is lit to be conspicuous, not covered. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Let your light so shine before men that they may see your good works and glorify your Father in heaven. What is between God and our souls is kept to ourselves, but what is open to men's sight must suit our profession; men seeing the life of Christ's disciples glorify the Father for redeeming and transforming earth's sinful children. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Think not I am come to destroy the law or the prophets — the authority and principles of the Old Testament. He came not to subvert or annul, but to fulfil: to establish them, unfold them, embody them in living form, and enshrine them in the reverence, affection and character of men. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: Verily I say unto you — here first occurs that august expression of supreme legislative authority — till heaven and earth pass, one jot, the smallest Hebrew letter, and one tittle, the little stroke distinguishing letters, shall in no wise pass from the law till all be fulfilled. The enduring stability of the great moral and spiritual truths of the Old Testament is thus expressed. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Whosoever shall break — dissolve or annul — one of the least commandments and teach men so shall be called least in the kingdom of heaven: not exclusion, but a degraded position in the present stage of the kingdom, retributively reduced to the dishonour to which his system brought God's law. Whoever does and teaches them shall be called great, raised to the honour to which he exalts the law. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Except your righteousness exceed the righteousness of the scribes and Pharisees, ye shall in no case enter the kingdom of heaven. The superiority required is in kind, not degree: outward, formal righteousness must give way to inward, vital, spiritual righteousness, for entrance depends solely on having the character God demands. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: Ye have heard it was said by them of old time, “Thou shalt not kill,” with whosoever kills in danger of the judgment — the inferior local courts. Thus the commandment was reduced from a holy law of the heart-searching God to a mere criminal statute taking cognizance only of outward actions, as the Jewish teachers taught that nothing but actual murder was forbidden. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: But I say unto you, in the authoritative tone of the Lawgiver himself: causeless anger is heart-murder, hatred in the bud, and “Raca,” a scornful word from pride, and “Thou fool,” a spiteful word from hatred, express degrees of contempt answerable at the divine tribunal. The three punishments — judgment, council, hell-fire, alluding to the valley of Hinnom — are gradations of divine retribution, not temporal penalties. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Therefore, if thou bring thy gift to the altar and there remember thy brother hath aught of just complaint against thee, the application follows: Christian love and peace must be preserved, and a quarrel confessed and made up, for until this is done we are unfit for communion with God in holy ordinances. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Leave thy gift before the altar, go thy way, first be reconciled to thy brother — get him to dismiss his grudge against thee — and then come and offer thy gift. Even before the offering is presented, reconciliation is to be sought; the early church accordingly made up differences before communion. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: Agree with thine adversary quickly, whiles thou art in the way with him to the magistrate, lest he deliver thee to the judge and the judge to the officer. While we are alive we are in the way to God's judgment-seat; after death it will be too late, so seek peace with God without delay. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Verily, thou shalt by no means come out thence till thou hast paid the uttermost farthing, a fractional Roman coin. The language is drawn from human legal procedure but deals with a higher Adversary, a higher tribunal and a higher sentence: the offender has another party to deal with besides the man wronged on earth. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Ye have heard, “Thou shalt not commit adultery” — but the traditional perverters restricted the breach to acts of criminal intercourse between or with married persons exclusively. Christ now dissipates such delusions, showing the full spiritual meaning of the commandment. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Whosoever looketh on a woman to lust after her, with full consent of will to feed unholy desire, hath committed adultery with her already in his heart. The range extends to all forms of impurity, for the counsels that follow were intended for all, married or unmarried. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: If thy right eye, the readier and dearer, offend thee — be a trap-spring or occasion of stumbling — pluck it out with indignant promptitude, heedless of cost. It is not execution upon the bodily organ, for putting out the eyes would not quench the lust; we are to strike at the root of unholy dispositions and cut off the occasions that stimulate them, lest the whole body be cast into hell. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: If thy right hand, the organ of action to which the eye excites, offend thee, cut it off and cast it from thee, for the same reason. The repetition in identical terms of such stern truths is characteristic of our Lord's teaching; victory over the heart's desires must be attended with painful exertions, but it must be done. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: It hath been said, whosoever shall put away his wife, let him give her a writing of divorcement — a legal check on reckless separation, quoted from Deuteronomy 24. The one legitimate ground in that enactment was “some uncleanness,” conjugal infidelity; Shammai read it so, but Hillel stretched it to anything offensive to the husband, a view fitted to minister to caprice. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: But I say, whosoever puts away his wife, saving for fornication, causeth her to commit adultery, driving her into it if she marries again; and whosoever marries her that is divorced committeth adultery, for if the commandment is broken by one party it must be by the other also. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: Again ye have heard, thou shalt not forswear thyself, but perform unto the Lord thine oaths — not the precise words of Exodus 20:7, but what it was currently understood to condemn, false swearing. Solemn oaths in court or on proper occasions, taken with due reverence, are not hereby condemned; it is swearing in common intercourse and on frivolous occasions that is meant, for every oath carries an appeal to God whether his name is uttered or not. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: Swear not at all, neither by heaven, for it is God's throne (Isaiah 66:1). God's name is profaned quite as really by swearing by his throne as if his name had been uttered, because it is instantly suggested by the mention of it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

35 COMMENTARY: Nor by the earth, for it is his footstool, neither by Jerusalem, for it is the city of the great King (Psalm 48:2). The worse men are, the less they are bound by oaths; the better they are, the less need there is for them. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

36 COMMENTARY: Neither shalt thou swear by thy head, because thou canst not make one hair white or black. In swearing by our own head the objection lies in its being beyond our control, profanely assumed to have a stability which it has not. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

37 COMMENTARY: Let your communication be Yea, yea; Nay, nay — a simple Yes and No sufficing in ordinary intercourse. Whatsoever is more cometh of evil: the habit of confirming words by oath springs from, and aggravates, the vicious root of untruthfulness, while a disciple's plain Yes and No soon come to be relied on more than others' solemn asseverations. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

38 COMMENTARY: Ye have heard, an eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth (Exodus 21, Leviticus 24, Deuteronomy 19). This law of retribution was designed to take vengeance out of private hands and commit it to the magistrate, but was abused in the opposite way to the Decalogue: a judicial regulation was held as warrant for taking redress into one's own hands, contrary to Proverbs 20:22 and 24:29. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

39 COMMENTARY: But I say, resist not evil: whosoever smites thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also. Christ's own meek yet dignified bearing when smitten (John 18) is the best comment: not literally inviting another blow, but a preparedness to submit meekly to a further indignity without retaliation. Suffer any injury that can be borne, for the sake of peace. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

40 COMMENTARY: If any man will sue thee at law and take away thy coat, the inner garment taken in pledge for debt, let him have thy cloak also, the outer and costlier garment which could not be retained overnight from the poor because they used it for a bed covering. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

41 COMMENTARY: Whosoever shall compel thee to go a mile, go with him twain — an allusion to Roman practice of obliging people to furnish attendance for government despatches, often at great inconvenience. The demand is readiness to submit to unreasonable demands of whatever kind rather than raise quarrels with all their resulting evils. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

42 COMMENTARY: Give to him that asketh thee, the sense of unreasonable asking being implied, and from him that would borrow of thee turn not thou away — a graphic expression of unfeeling refusal to relieve a brother in extremity. Simple borrowing is meant, usury being forbidden among the Jews, and “owe no man anything” is not absolute, else the commendations of lending to a necessitous brother would have no application. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

43 COMMENTARY: Ye have heard, thou shalt love thy neighbour (Leviticus 19:18) — to which the corrupt teachers added, “and hate thine enemy,” as if a legitimate inference, instead of a detestable gloss. The Jewish teachers understood “neighbour” only as those of their own country, nation and religion. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

44 COMMENTARY: But I say, love your enemies — moral love, the benignant, compassionate outgoings of desire for another's good — bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you and persecute you. The best commentary is the bright example of him who gave them; yet he is here only the incomparable Interpreter of the law in force from the beginning. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

45 COMMENTARY: That ye may be the children of your Father in heaven — that ye may show yourselves to be such by resembling him — for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust. Henry adds that we must render good for evil, which speaks a nobler principle than most men act by. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

46 COMMENTARY: If ye love them which love you, what reward have ye? Do not even the publicans the same? Henry's group comment presses the point: while many render good for good, Christians must render good for evil, and more is expected from the followers of Christ than from others. — Matthew Henry

47 COMMENTARY: If ye salute your brethren only, what do ye more than others? Do not even the publicans so? Others salute their own party, way and opinion, but Christians must not so confine their respect; they must do all the real kindness they can to all, especially to their souls. — Matthew Henry

48 COMMENTARY: Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect. It is the duty of Christians to desire, aim at, and press towards perfection in grace and holiness, conforming themselves to the example of their heavenly Father — more being expected from, and found in, the followers of Christ than in others. — Matthew Henry
