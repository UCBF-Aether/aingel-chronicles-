Study Notes — Song of Songs

Chapter 5

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: I am come into my garden, my sister, my spouse; I have gathered my myrrh with my spice, eaten my honeycomb with my honey, drunk my wine with my milk: eat, O friends, drink abundantly, O beloved. See how ready Christ is to accept the invitations of his people, Henry says: what little good there is in us would be lost if he did not preserve it to himself, and he invites his beloved to eat and drink abundantly in the ordinances, which are means of grace. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

2 COMMENTARY: I sleep, but my heart wakes; it is the voice of my beloved that knocks, saying, open to me, my sister, my love, my dove, my undefiled, for my head is filled with dew. Churches and believers, by carelessness and security, provoke Christ to withdraw, Henry warns, and Christ knocks to awaken us by his word and Spirit, by afflictions, and by our consciences. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

3 COMMENTARY: I have put off my coat; how shall I put it on? I have washed my feet; how shall I defile them? Careless souls put slights on Jesus Christ, Henry says: making excuses is making light of Christ, and those put contempt upon him who cannot bear a cold blast or leave a warm bed for him. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

4 COMMENTARY: My beloved put in his hand by the hole of the door, and my bowels were moved for him. This betokens a work of the Spirit upon the soul, Henry explains, as one weary of waiting puts in his hand to unbolt the door. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

5 COMMENTARY: I rose up to open to my beloved, and my hands dropped with myrrh upon the handles of the lock. The believer's rising above self-indulgence, seeking by prayer the consolations of Christ and removing every hindrance to communion, Henry says, is represented by hands dropping sweet-smelling myrrh upon the handles. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

6 COMMENTARY: I opened to my beloved, but my beloved had withdrawn himself and was gone; my soul failed when he spoke; I sought him, but I could not find him; I called him, but he gave me no answer. By absenting himself, Henry says, Christ teaches his people to value his gracious visits more highly, yet every desertion is not despair: the soul still calls Christ her Beloved. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

7 COMMENTARY: The watchmen that went about the city found me, they smote me, they wounded me; the keepers of the walls took away my veil from me. Some refer this to those who misapply the word to awakened consciences, Henry notes, and awakened souls are more sensible of Christ's withdrawings than of any other trouble. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

8 COMMENTARY: I charge you, O daughters of Jerusalem, if ye find my beloved, that ye tell him that I am sick of love. This charge, Henry says, seems to mean the distressed believer's desire for the prayers of even the feeblest Christian. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

9 COMMENTARY: What is thy beloved more than another beloved, O thou fairest among women, that thou dost so charge us? Even those who have little acquaintance with Christ cannot but see amiable beauty in others who bear his image, Henry notes, and there are hopes of those who begin to inquire concerning Christ and his perfections. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

10 COMMENTARY: My beloved is white and ruddy, the chiefest among ten thousand. He is white in the spotless innocence of his life, Henry says, ruddy in the bleeding sufferings he went through at his death, and divine glory makes him truly lovely in the eyes of all enlightened to discern spiritual things. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

11 COMMENTARY: His head is as the most fine gold, his locks are bushy and black as a raven. This description, Henry notes, would form in the figurative language of those times a portrait of beauty of person and grace of manners, though the aptness of some allusions may not appear to us. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

12 COMMENTARY: His eyes are as the eyes of doves by the rivers of waters, washed with milk, and fitly set. The portrait continues to set forth the Beloved's beauty of person and grace, Henry says, as one truly lovely to all who discern spiritual things. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

13 COMMENTARY: His cheeks are as a bed of spices, as sweet flowers; his lips like lilies, dropping sweet smelling myrrh. These images belong to the same figurative portrait of the Beloved's loveliness, Henry notes, whose aptness in some allusions may not appear to us today. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

14 COMMENTARY: His hands are as gold rings set with the beryl; his belly is as bright ivory overlaid with sapphires. The portrait of beauty and grace continues, Henry says, pointing to him who shall come to be glorified in his saints and admired in all that believe. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

15 COMMENTARY: His legs are as pillars of marble, set upon sockets of fine gold; his countenance is as Lebanon, excellent as the cedars. The Beloved's portrait closes in strength and majesty, Henry notes, as one altogether lovely. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

16 COMMENTARY: His mouth is most sweet; yea, he is altogether lovely. This is my beloved, and this is my friend, O daughters of Jerusalem. May his love constrain us to live to his glory, Henry prays, for he shall come to be glorified in his saints. — Matthew Henry (Concise)
