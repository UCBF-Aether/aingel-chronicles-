Study Notes — Joel

Chapter 1

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The word of the Lord came to Joel the son of Pethuel, and Henry dismisses the fancy that this Joel was Samuel's son, noting he lived long after and spoke of a sore judgment brought upon Judah for its sins. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: The old men and all inhabitants are asked whether such a thing had been in their days or their fathers', and Henry stresses the judgment was without precedent in memory or record, as those who outdo their predecessors in sin may expect sorer judgments. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: They are told to tell their children, and their children their children, so that a judgment without precedent in the past would not be forgotten in the ages to come. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: What the palmerworm left the locust ate, and what the locust left the cankerworm and caterpillar ate, and Henry reads the succession as armies of insects coming to eat the land's fruits, an image that also applies to destruction by a foreign enemy. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Drunkards are told to awake and weep because the new wine is cut off from their mouths, and Henry notes that God, Lord of hosts, can humble a proud people by the weakest and most contemptible creatures. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: A nation strong and without number has come up, with teeth like a lion's, and the insect army is thus described in the language of invasion. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: The invader has laid the vine waste and barked the fig tree, making it clean bare and casting it away, so that the branches are white, stripped trees standing as the visible ruin. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: The people must lament like a virgin girded with sackcloth for the husband of her youth, and Henry explains the image as grief for a lover lost between espousal and marriage, or a young husband suddenly taken, the sharpest private sorrow picturing a public loss. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: The meat and drink offerings are cut off from the house of the Lord, and the priests mourn, since the locusts have destroyed both the people's food and the material of their thanksgiving offerings. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: The field is wasted, the land mourns, the corn is wasted, the new wine dried up, and the oil languishes, and Henry notes the people had not been thankful for bread, wine, and oil, and are now justly brought to lament their loss. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Husbandmen are ashamed and vinedressers howl for wheat and barley, because the harvest has perished, and Henry warns that those who labour only for the meat that perishes will sooner or later be ashamed of their labour. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Vine, fig, pomegranate, palm, apple, and all the trees of the field are withered, because joy is withered away from the sons of men, so that the ruin of the orchards is also the ruin of the people's gladness. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Priests and ministers of the altar must gird themselves, lament, and lie all night in sackcloth, since the offerings are withholden from God's house, and Henry brings in clergymen alongside countrymen as sharers in the lamentation. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: A fast is to be sanctified, a solemn assembly called, and elders and inhabitants gathered into the house of the Lord to cry to him, turning lamentation into a directed appeal to God. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Alas for the day, for the day of the Lord is at hand, a destruction from the Almighty, and the locust plague is thus named as a foretaste of the great day itself. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: The meat is cut off before their eyes, with joy and gladness from the house of God, so that even worship loses its festal character when the gifts that sustained it are gone. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: The seed is rotten under the clods, the garners are desolate, and the barns broken down because the corn is withered, carrying the devastation from the standing crop into the storehouse and the seed for next year. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: The beasts groan, the herds are perplexed for want of pasture, and the flocks are made desolate, so that the judgment reaches the dumb creatures that share man's dependence on the field. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Joel himself cries to the Lord, for fire has devoured the pastures of the wilderness and flame has burned all the trees, the prophet turning from describing ruin to pleading against it. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Even the beasts of the field cry to God, for the rivers are dried up and fire has devoured the pastures, and the whole creation's cry becomes the prophet's closing argument for mercy. — Matthew Henry
