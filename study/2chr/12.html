Study Notes — 2 Chronicles

Chapter 12

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: And it came to pass, when Rehoboam had established the kingdom and strengthened himself, he forsook the law of the Lord, and all Israel with him. Henry notes his happy three years walking in David and Solomon's way expired, and he grew remiss; as long as his throne tottered he kept duty to make God his friend, but once it stood firm he thought he had no more occasion for religion, for the prosperity of fools destroys them. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: And it came to pass, in the fifth year of King Rehoboam, Shishak king of Egypt came up against Jerusalem, because they had transgressed against the Lord. Henry notes God quickly brought troubles to awaken them before hearts hardened: in the fourth year they began to corrupt themselves, and in the fifth Egypt came, by a hand they had little reason to suspect after friendly correspondence in the last reign. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: With twelve hundred chariots and threescore thousand horsemen, and people without number out of Egypt, Lubims, Sukkiims and Ethiopians. Henry notes Shishak came with a vast army, so violent that all Judah's fenced cities fell immediately, plainly showing it was from the Lord because they had transgressed. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: And he took the fenced cities which pertained to Judah, and came to Jerusalem. Henry notes the cities Rehoboam had lately fortified and garrisoned, on which he relied much for safety, fell without resistance, showing the calamity was from the Lord. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Then came Shemaiah the prophet to Rehoboam and the princes of Judah gathered at Jerusalem because of Shishak, and said, Thus saith the Lord, Ye have forsaken me, and therefore have I also left you in the hand of Shishak. Henry notes God by the word explained the rod: the reason Shishak prevailed was not impolitic management but forsaking God, for God never leaves any till they first leave him. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Whereupon the princes of Israel and the king humbled themselves, and they said, The Lord is righteous. Henry notes the rebukes of word and rod joined, king and princes penitently acknowledged sin and patiently accepted punishment, saying none but themselves were to blame and God is clear when he judges; even kings must either bend or break before God. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: And when the Lord saw that they humbled themselves, the word of the Lord came to Shemaiah, saying, They have humbled themselves, therefore I will not destroy them, but I will grant them some deliverance, and my wrath shall not be poured out upon Jerusalem by Shishak's hand. Henry notes God in mercy prevented destruction on the brink: the destroying angel at Jerusalem was forbidden, for those who acknowledge God righteous in afflicting them shall find him gracious. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: Nevertheless they shall be his servants, that they may know my service, and the service of the kingdoms of the countries. Henry notes God left them to smart in liberty: they who thought God's law too hard a yoke would try neighbouring princes' rule awhile, and learn that God's service, compared with others, is reasonable and easy, while disobedience's punishment is far harder. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: So Shishak king of Egypt came up against Jerusalem, and took away the treasures of the house of the Lord and of the king's house; he took all, and carried away also the shields of gold Solomon had made. Henry notes Egypt plundered temple and exchequer, treasuries Solomon left very full; David and Solomon, walking with God, filled them by war and merchandise, but Rehoboam, forsaking God's law, emptied them. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Instead of which King Rehoboam made shields of brass, and committed them to the chief of the guard, that kept the entrance of the king's house. Henry notes the golden shields were taken and brazen ones substituted, the visible mark of diminished glory after forsaking God. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: And when the king entered into the house of the Lord, the guard came and fetched them, and brought them again into the guard chamber. Henry's account of the substituted brazen shields applies: the guard fetched them for the king's entry and returned them to the guard chamber, ceremony now guarded by brass instead of gold. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: And when he humbled himself, the wrath of the Lord turned from him, that he would not destroy him altogether, and also in Judah things went well. Henry notes deliverance was partial, matching partial reformation, yet in Judah things went well and looked better: good things remained, good ministers, people and families made better by calamity, and affairs altered for the better on repentance. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: So King Rehoboam strengthened himself in Jerusalem, and reigned, for Rehoboam was forty-one when he began to reign, and reigned seventeen years in Jerusalem, the city the Lord chose out of all tribes to put his name there, and his mother's name was Naamah an Ammonitess. Henry's general character of Rehoboam applies: his reign in the chosen city is summed up as melancholy, a Judges-like account of evil, judgment and partial mercy. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: And he did evil, because he prepared not his heart to seek the Lord. Henry's whole chapter verdict applies: Rehoboam did evil in forsaking the law when established, growing remiss in worship, and preparing not his heart to seek God. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Now the acts of Rehoboam, first and last, are they not written in the book of Shemaiah the prophet, and of Iddo the seer concerning genealogies? And there were wars between Rehoboam and Jeroboam continually. Henry notes the chapter gives a fuller account than Kings, recorded by Shemaiah and Iddo, with continual war between the divided kingdoms. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: And Rehoboam slept with his fathers, and was buried in the city of David, and Abijah his son reigned in his stead. Henry's conclusion of Rehoboam's reign applies: the melancholy account closes with his burial in David's city and Abijah's succession. — Matthew Henry
