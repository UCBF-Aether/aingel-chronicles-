Study Notes — 2 Chronicles

Chapter 4

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Moreover he made a bronze altar twenty cubits long, twenty broad and ten high. Henry notes it was far larger than Moses' five-cubit altar, because Israel had grown numerous and rich, and returns should bear proportion to receivings; its height let worshippers see the sacrifice and be moved to sorrow for sin. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: He made a molten sea, ten cubits brim to brim, round in compass, five cubits high, with a line of thirty cubits compassing it. Henry notes it stood at the entrance to the priests' court like a font at a church door, signifying the fountain opened for washing from sin, and JFB notes the knops of Kings are here given as oxen. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Under it was the similitude of oxen, ten in a cubit, compassing the sea, in two rows cast with it when it was cast. JFB notes the basin and its supporting oxen were cast in one and the same mould, all of one piece. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: It stood upon twelve oxen, three looking north, west, south and east, with the sea set above and their hinder parts inward. JFB's treatment of the sea's supports covers this arrangement: the one-piece casting carried the great basin on the twelve oxen, facing every quarter. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Its thickness was a handbreadth, its brim like a cup with flowers of lilies, and it received and held three thousand baths. Henry notes the sea signified both gospel privilege, cleansing by Christ's blood, and gospel duty, cleansing ourselves by repentance; JFB reconciles the three thousand here with two thousand in Kings as the basin filled with all its fountain accompaniments. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: He made ten lavers, five on the right and five on the left, to wash the burnt offerings in, while the sea was for the priests to wash in. Henry notes that as priests must be washed, so must sacrifices: we must put away vain thoughts and corrupt aims that pollute our performances themselves. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: He made ten candlesticks of gold according to their form, five on the right and five on the left in the temple. Henry notes the written word is a lamp shining in a dark place, and the increase from one candlestick to ten signified light growing, with further books of Scripture to come; JFB adds it was a standing emblem that growing light was needed against growing darkness. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: He made ten tables, five on each side, and a hundred basins of gold. Henry notes that as the house was enlarged, so was its housekeeping, and God's table is well furnished, with bread enough for the whole family. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Furthermore he made the court of the priests and the great court, with doors overlaid with brass. Henry notes the doors were overlaid both for strength and beauty, and that they might not rot with exposure to the weather. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: He set the sea on the right side of the east end, over against the south. Henry's placement of the sea at the entrance to the priests' court explains the position: it stood ready for the priests to wash as they entered to minister. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Huram made the pots, shovels and basins, and finished the work he was to make for Solomon for the house of God. JFB refers this completion to the parallel account in Kings, marking Huram's brass work as the finished furnishing of the house. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: The work included the two pillars, the pommels, the chapiters on the pillars, and the two wreaths covering the pommels. Henry's chapter overview places these brass vessels among the court furniture, while the pillars themselves, Jachin and Boaz, were treated in chapter 3 as establishment and strength. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: There were four hundred pomegranates on the two wreaths, two rows on each wreath, covering the pommels. Henry's overview groups these with the pillar ornaments, the rich brass work of the open court, in view of all the people. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: He made also bases, and lavers upon the bases. Henry notes the ten lavers stood between porch and altar for washing the offerings, and these bases carried them in their service. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: One sea, and twelve oxen under it, completed the great washing vessel. Henry's sea typology applies again: the one great basin for the priests' cleansing stood on the twelve oxen, a single provision for all who ministered. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: The pots, shovels, fleshhooks and all their instruments Huram made for Solomon of bright brass. Henry groups these with the vessels of the altar in the open court, the brass furniture by which the sacrifices were handled in the people's view. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: In the plain of Jordan the king cast them, in the clay ground between Succoth and Zeredathah. Henry's account of the brass furniture's abundance fits this casting place: the great vessels were cast where clay ground served the foundry, in the Jordan plain. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Thus Solomon made all these vessels in great abundance, for the weight of the brass could not be found out. Henry's point on the enlarged altar applies to the whole furnishing: Israel, greatly enlarged in borders and wealth, enlarged God's house accordingly, and returns bore proportion to receivings. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Solomon made all the vessels for the house of God, the golden altar also, and the tables whereon the showbread was set. Henry notes that within the house all was gold, for the nearer we come to God the purer we must be, and the golden altar of incense points to Christ ever living to make intercession. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Moreover the candlesticks with their lamps, that they should burn after the manner before the oracle, of pure gold. Henry's chapter overview names these as appurtenances of the golden furnishings, and his candlestick typology applies: lamps burning before the oracle are the word shining in a dark place. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: The flowers, lamps and tongs he made of gold, and that perfect gold. Henry's overview again covers these appurtenances of the candlesticks and altar, all of pure gold within the house, where all must be pure. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: The snuffers, basins, spoons and censers were of pure gold, and the entry, the inner doors for the most holy place, and the doors of the temple, were of gold. Henry notes all within was gold, and the golden doors marked the passage into God's nearer presence, where purity is required. — Matthew Henry
