Study Notes — 2 Chronicles

Chapter 28

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Ahaz was twenty years old when he began to reign, and he reigned sixteen years in Jerusalem, but he did not that which was right in the sight of the Lord, like David his father. Henry notes never had a man greater opportunity of doing well, finding the kingdom rich and strong and religion established, yet he was wretchedly corrupted, for parents cannot give grace to children, and all his good education and example were lost upon him. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: For he walked in the ways of the kings of Israel, and made also molten images for Baalim. Henry notes he walked in the way of revolted Israelites and devoted Canaanites, making molten images contrary to the second commandment, and for Baalim contrary to the first. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Moreover he burnt incense in the valley of the son of Hinnom, and burnt his children in the fire, after the abominations of the heathen whom the Lord had cast out before Israel's children. Henry notes to complete his wickedness, divested of natural affection as well as religion and devoted to the great enemy, he burnt his children to Moloch, not thinking it enough to cause them to pass through the fire. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: He sacrificed also and burnt incense in the high places, and on the hills, and under every green tree. Henry notes he forsook the Lord's temple and sacrificed on hills, as if nearer heaven, and under every green tree, as if signifying heaven's protection by shade and dropping. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Wherefore the Lord his God delivered him into the hand of Syria's king, and they smote him, and carried away a great multitude captives to Damascus. And he was also delivered into Israel's king, who smote him with a great slaughter. Henry notes when he forsook God and at vast expense put himself under false gods, God delivered him to enemies: Syrians beat him and carried many captive, and Israel's king, though an idolater too, was made a scourge to him, for when a good king sheltered Judah, now a bad one left all defence departed. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: For Pekah son of Remaliah slew in Judah an hundred and twenty thousand in one day, which were all valiant men, because they had forsaken the Lord God of their fathers. Henry notes never was such bloody work among Judah since they were a kingdom, and by Israelites too: 120,000 valiant men slain in one day, which could not have been cut off if not dispirited by consciousness of guilt and God's righteous hand, for they had forsaken God and he had forsaken them. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: And Zichri, a mighty man of Ephraim, slew Maaseiah the king's son, and Azrikam governor of the house, and Elkanah that was next to the king. Henry notes among the first rank slain were the king's son, sacrificed justly to divine vengeance since Ahaz had sacrificed sons to Moloch, and his friend the prime minister, so that the king himself had a narrow escape. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: And Israel's children carried away captive of their brethren two hundred thousand, women, sons, and daughters, and took also much spoil, and brought the spoil to Samaria. Henry notes when the field army was routed, cities, towns and villages were stripped, inhabitants taken for slaves and wealth for prey, a great captivity of women and children. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: But a prophet of the Lord was there, whose name was Oded, and he went out before the host that came to Samaria, and said unto them, Behold, because the Lord God of your fathers was wroth with Judah, he hath delivered them into your hand, and ye have slain them in a rage that reacheth up unto heaven. Henry notes God sent a prophet, not to applaud valour or congratulate victory, but to tell faults and warn of judgments: the victory came not because God favoured Israel or they merited it, but because he was wroth with Judah and made them the rod of his indignation, and their slaughter was from a bad principle of enmity, with barbarous fury reaching to heaven. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: And now ye purpose to keep under Judah and Jerusalem's children for bondmen and bondwomen unto you, but are there not with you, even with you, sins against the Lord your God? Henry notes Israel was reproved for imperious treatment of prisoners, purposing to keep free-born brethren as slaves, and reminded of their own sins, for though made instruments of correcting Judah, they were not innocent themselves, and it ill becomes sinners to be cruel or proud. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Now hear me therefore, and deliver the captives again, which ye have taken captive of your brethren, for the fierce wrath of the Lord is upon you. Henry notes the prophet commanded them to release prisoners and send them home carefully, for having sinned, God's fierce wrath was upon them, and no other way of escaping it than by showing mercy. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Then certain heads of Ephraim's children, Azariah son of Johanan, Berechiah son of Meshillemoth, Jehizkiah son of Shallum, and Amasa son of Hadlai, stood up against them that came from the war. Henry notes the princes stood up against those flushed with victory, discovering obedient regard to God's word by his prophet and tender compassion toward brethren, wrought by God's tender mercy, who made them pitied by their captors. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: And said unto them, Ye shall not bring in the captives hither, for whereas we have offended against the Lord already, ye intend to add more to our sins and to our trespass, for our trespass is great, and there is fierce wrath against Israel. Henry notes the princes told them plainly they should not bring captives into Samaria, for they had sin enough already and would have nothing added to their trespass. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: So the armed men left the captives and the spoil before the princes and all the congregation. Henry notes the armed men, though they might by force have maintained title to what they got by the sword, acquiesced and left captives and spoil to the princes' disposal, showing more truly heroic bravery in yielding to reason and religion against interest than in taking them. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: And the men expressed by name rose up, and took the captives, and with the spoil clothed all naked among them, and arrayed them, and shod them, and gave them to eat and drink, and anointed them, and carried all the feeble upon asses, and brought them to Jericho, the city of palm trees, to their brethren, then they returned to Samaria. Henry notes the princes very generously sent home the poor captives well accommodated, clothed, shod, fed, anointed and carried to Jericho, for those who hope to find mercy with God must learn tenderness toward those at their mercy, though strangely these princes had not grace to root idolatry out of their kingdom, which soon after was its ruin. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: At that time did king Ahaz send unto the kings of Assyria to help him. Henry notes Ahaz added to national distress by courting strange kings: finding his kingdom weakened and naked, and unable to put confidence in God, he was at vast expense to get an interest in Assyria's king. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: For again the Edomites had come and smitten Judah, and carried away captives. Henry notes in particular the Edomites, to be revenged for Amaziah's cruel treatment of them, smote Judah and carried off many captives. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: The Philistines also had invaded the cities of the low country, and of the south of Judah, and had taken Bethshemesh, and Ajalon, and Gederoth, and Shocho with its villages, and Timnah with its villages, Gimzo also and its villages, and they dwelt there. Henry notes the Philistines also insulted Judah, taking and keeping several cities and villages near them, revenged for Uzziah's incursions upon them. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: For the Lord brought Judah low because of Ahaz king of Israel, for he made Judah naked, and transgressed sore against the Lord. Henry notes the Lord brought Judah low: lately very high in wealth and power, God made them as despicable as they had been formidable, for those who will not humble themselves under God's word will justly be humbled by his judgments, and Ahaz made Judah naked, debased and exposed to contempt and assault, for sin strips men. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: And Tilgathpilneser king of Assyria came unto him, and distressed him, but strengthened him not. Henry notes what did Ahaz get by Assyria's king? He came but distressed him and strengthened him not, his forces quartered on the country impoverishing it, growing insolent and imperious, like a broken reed which not only fails but pierces the hand. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: For Ahaz took away a portion out of the house of the Lord, and out of the king's house, and of the princes, and gave it unto the king of Assyria, but he helped him not. Henry notes he pillaged God's house, the king's house and squeezed the princes to hire foreign forces, yet Assyria helped him not, for wicked men themselves have no real affection for those who revolt to them, and a degenerate branch is looked upon as an abominable branch. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: And in the time of his distress did he trespass yet more against the Lord: this is that king Ahaz. Henry notes in distress, instead of repenting, he trespassed yet more, more mad than ever upon idols, and a brand of infamy is set upon him: this is that king Ahaz, the scandal of David's house and curse and plague of his generation, for those made worse by afflictions, instead of better, are wicked and vile indeed. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: For he sacrificed unto the gods of Damascus, which smote him, and he said, Because the gods of Syria's kings help them, therefore will I sacrifice to them, that they may help me. But they were the ruin of him, and of all Israel. Henry notes he sacrificed to Damascus' gods, not because he loved them, for he thought they smote him, but because he feared them, thinking they helped his enemies and would help him if brought into his interest, but foolishly, for it was his own God who smote him and strengthened Syria, and the gods of Syria befriended him no more than Assyria's kings, being the ruin of him and all Israel. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: And Ahaz gathered together the vessels of the house of God, and cut in pieces the vessels of the house of God, and shut up the doors of the house of the Lord, and he made him altars in every corner of Jerusalem. Henry notes he abused God's house: he cut its vessels in pieces that priests might not perform temple service, and at length shut its doors that people might not attend, worse than the worst kings before him, and confronted God's altar with altars in every corner of Jerusalem, like heaps in the furrows of the fields. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: And in every several city of Judah he made high places to burn incense unto other gods, and provoked to anger the Lord God of his fathers. Henry notes in Judah's cities, by power or purse or both, he erected high places to burn incense to whatever idols pleased, as if on purpose to provoke the God of his fathers. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: Now the rest of his acts and of all his ways, first and last, behold, they are written in the book of the kings of Judah and Israel. Henry notes the chapter concludes with the conclusion of Ahaz's reign, his acts referred to the book of the kings of Judah and Israel. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: And Ahaz slept with his fathers, and they buried him in the city, even in Jerusalem, but they brought him not into the sepulchres of the kings of Israel, and Hezekiah his son reigned in his stead. Henry notes for aught that appears he died impenitent and therefore inglorious, not buried in the kings' sepulchres, justly thought unworthy to be laid among them who had used kingly power for the church's destruction and not its protection or edification. — Matthew Henry
