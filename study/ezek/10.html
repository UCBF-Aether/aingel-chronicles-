Study Notes — Ezekiel

Chapter 10

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Above the cherubim Ezekiel sees, as it were, a sapphire stone like a throne, opening the vision of the city's burning. Henry places the whole first section under that heading: the fire to be taken from between the cherubim signifies God's wrath to be executed upon Jerusalem. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

2 COMMENTARY: The man clothed with linen is told to go between the wheels, under the cherub, fill his hand with coals of fire from between the cherubim, and scatter them over the city. Henry notes the fire of divine wrath that kindles judgment on a people is just and holy. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

3 COMMENTARY: The cherubim stand on the right side of the house as the man goes in, and the cloud fills the inner court. Henry's section point stands behind the scene: the cloud of God's presence fills the house even as the fire of his wrath is being fetched for Jerusalem. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

4 COMMENTARY: The glory of the Lord goes up from the cherub and stands over the threshold, while the house is filled with the cloud and the court with the brightness of his glory. Henry reads the movement as judgment prepared: the glory pauses at the threshold as the orders for the city's burning go out. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

5 COMMENTARY: The sound of the cherubim's wings is heard even to the outer court, as the voice of Almighty God when he speaks. Henry's first section gives the sound its meaning: it heralds the just and holy fire of divine wrath about to fall on Jerusalem. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

6 COMMENTARY: When the man clothed with linen is commanded to take fire from between the wheels, from between the cherubim, he goes in and stands beside the wheels. Henry notes the fire taken there signifies wrath to be executed, and in the great day the earth and all its works will be burnt up. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

7 COMMENTARY: One cherub stretches his hand to the fire between the cherubim, takes some, and puts it into the hands of the man clothed with linen, who takes it and goes out. Henry's point is that the fire is no accident: it is God's wrath, deliberately fetched and deliberately carried out. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

8 COMMENTARY: In the cherubim appears the form of a man's hand under their wings, the directing hand behind the vision. Henry's second section sees here the working of divine providence: the Spirit of life directs all creatures, in heaven and on earth, to serve the divine purpose. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

9 COMMENTARY: Four wheels stand by the cherubim, one wheel by one cherub, in the appearance of beryl. Henry reads the wheels as providence at work in the government of the lower world and its affairs, moving as God directs. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

10 COMMENTARY: The four wheels have one likeness, as if a wheel were in the midst of a wheel. Henry's providence point applies: however intricate their motions appear, all creatures and events are directed by one Spirit to one divine purpose. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

11 COMMENTARY: The wheels go upon their four sides and turn not as they go, following wherever the head looks. Henry notes that when God is leaving a people in displeasure, angels above and all events below further his departure, each moving exactly as directed. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

12 COMMENTARY: The whole body, backs, hands, wings, and wheels are full of eyes round about. Henry's providence reading gives the eyes their sense: nothing in the lower world escapes the directing Spirit of God, who sees and orders all. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

13 COMMENTARY: The wheels are cried to in Ezekiel's hearing, 'O wheel,' addressed as instruments in the vision. Henry sees providence itself summoned: the wheels are not blind fortune but servants of the divine purpose in the affairs of the world. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

14 COMMENTARY: Each cherub has four faces: a cherub, a man, a lion, and an eagle. Henry's section treats the whole living creature as part of providence's government, every face turned to the work the Spirit of life directs. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

15 COMMENTARY: The cherubim are lifted up, and Ezekiel recognises them as the living creature he saw by the river Chebar. Henry's departure theme begins here: the same creatures that bore God's glory at Chebar now rise to carry it away from the temple. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

16 COMMENTARY: When the cherubim go, the wheels go by them, and when the cherubim lift their wings to mount up, the wheels lift up too. Henry states the concurrence plainly: angels above and events below further God's departure together, because the Spirit of the living creature is in them. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

17 COMMENTARY: When the cherubim stand, the wheels stand, and when they are lifted up, the wheels lift up themselves also, for the spirit of the living creature is in them. Henry's point is that the Spirit of God directs all creatures alike, so none moves apart from the divine purpose. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

18 COMMENTARY: The glory of the Lord departs from the threshold of the house and stands over the cherubim. Henry warns that God removes by degrees from a provoking people, and this staged departure is the visible sign of it. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

19 COMMENTARY: The cherubim lift their wings and mount up from the earth in Ezekiel's sight, the wheels beside them, and they stand at the door of the east gate, with the glory of the God of Israel over them. Henry notes that when God is leaving a people, angels and events concur to further his going, yet he would return to a repenting, praying people. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

20 COMMENTARY: This is the living creature Ezekiel saw under the God of Israel by the river Chebar, and he knows now that they are the cherubim. Henry's providence section closes the identification: the same directed creatures govern the departure as governed the first vision. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

21 COMMENTARY: Each cherub has four faces and four wings, with the likeness of a man's hands under their wings. Henry reads the whole array as providence equipped for its work, every part directed by the Spirit of life. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

22 COMMENTARY: The likeness of their faces is the same as those seen by the river Chebar, and they go every one straight forward. Henry's closing warning applies: let this warn sinners to seek the Lord while he may be found, and cause us all to walk humbly and watchfully with our God. — Matthew Henry (Concise)
