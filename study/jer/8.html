Study Notes — Jeremiah

Chapter 8

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: At that time the bones of Judah's kings, princes, priests, prophets, and inhabitants shall be brought out of their graves. Henry notes that though no real hurt can be done to a dead body, disgrace to the remains of wicked persons may alarm those yet alive, reminding us that divine justice and punishments extend beyond the grave. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

2 COMMENTARY: The bones shall be spread before the sun, moon, and all the host of heaven, whom they loved, served, walked after, sought, and worshipped. Henry's point is the exposure: the very gods they served will look down on the disgrace of their worshippers. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

3 COMMENTARY: Death shall be chosen rather than life by all the residue of this evil family remaining in the places where God has driven them. Henry urges the lesson: whatever befalls us here, let us humble ourselves before God and seek his mercy before death becomes preferable to life. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

4 COMMENTARY: Shall they fall and not arise? Shall he turn away and not return? Henry begins the account of the ruin with reason: sin is backsliding, going back from the way that leads to life toward destruction, yet the fallen refuse the rising that reason itself demands. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

5 COMMENTARY: Why has Jerusalem slidden back by a perpetual backsliding, holding fast deceit and refusing to return? Henry notes the people would not act in the affairs of their souls with common prudence: they hold deceit fast rather than let it go. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

6 COMMENTARY: God hearkened and heard, but they spoke not aright: no man repented of his wickedness, saying, 'What have I done?' Every one ran his course like a horse rushing into battle. Henry observes they would not attend to conscience: true repentance begins in serious inquiry as to what we have done, from conviction that we have done amiss, and they never took that first step. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

7 COMMENTARY: The stork in heaven knows her appointed times, and the turtle, crane, and swallow observe the time of their coming, but God's people do not know the judgment of the Lord. Henry notes they would not attend to the ways of providence nor understand God's voice in them, and know not how to improve the seasons of grace; unless taught by the Spirit, the instinct of brutes is a surer guide than their supposed wisdom. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

8 COMMENTARY: How do ye say, 'We are wise, and the law of the Lord is with us'? Lo, certainly in vain made he it; the pen of the scribes is in vain. Henry notes they would not attend to the written word: many enjoy abundance of the means of grace, Bibles and ministers, but have them in vain, and will soon be ashamed of their devices. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

9 COMMENTARY: The wise men are ashamed, dismayed, and taken, for they have rejected the word of the Lord, and what wisdom is in them? Henry identifies the pretenders to wisdom as the priests and false prophets, who flattered people in sin and so flattered them into destruction. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

10 COMMENTARY: God will give their wives to others and their fields to inheritors, for from the least to the greatest every one is given to covetousness, and from prophet to priest every one deals falsely. Henry's judgment fits the cause: a people given to covetousness and false dealing lose wives, fields, and all they grasped. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

11 COMMENTARY: They have healed the hurt of God's people slightly, saying, 'Peace, peace,' when there is no peace. Henry warns that selfish teachers may promise peace when there is no peace, silencing fears and complaints with 'All is well,' and thus men encourage each other in committing evil. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

12 COMMENTARY: Were they ashamed when they committed abomination? Nay, they were not at all ashamed, neither could they blush, therefore they shall fall among the fallen and be cast down at God's visitation. Henry notes that in the day of visitation such flattered sinners will have no refuge to flee unto. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

13 COMMENTARY: God will surely consume them: no grapes on the vine, no figs on the fig tree, the leaf withered, and what he gave them passed away from them. Henry's ruin is total consumption: comforts, fruit, and gifts all pass away from a people who would not attend to reason, conscience, providence, or word. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

14 COMMENTARY: Why do we sit still? Assemble and enter the defenced cities, and be silent there, for the Lord has doomed us to perish and given us water of gall to drink, because we have sinned against him. Henry notes that at length they begin to see the hand of God lifted up: the defenced cities are not refuge but the place to perish. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

15 COMMENTARY: We looked for peace, but no good came, and for a time of health, but behold trouble. Henry observes that when God appears against us, everything that is against us appears formidable: peace hoped for becomes trouble found. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

16 COMMENTARY: The snorting of his horses was heard from Dan, and the whole land trembled at the neighing of his strong ones, for they come to devour the land, the city, and those dwelling in it. Henry's alarm is the invasion itself, formidable once God's hand is seen in it. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

17 COMMENTARY: Behold, God will send serpents and cockatrices among them, which will not be charmed, and they shall bite. Henry's point is that no charming will turn this judgment: the serpents cannot be charmed, as the flatterers' 'peace' could not charm God. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

18 COMMENTARY: When Jeremiah would comfort himself against sorrow, his heart is faint within him. Henry's lamentation begins here: the prophet who warned faithfully faints under the sorrow he foresaw. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

19 COMMENTARY: Behold the cry of the daughter of God's people from a far country: 'Is not the Lord in Zion? Is not her King in her?' And God answers, 'Why have they provoked me with carved images and strange vanities?' Henry notes salvation is found only in the Lord, and the cry from afar meets its cause: idols provoked the King who seemed absent. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

20 COMMENTARY: The harvest is past, the summer is ended, and we are not saved. Henry warns that the present moment should be seized: seasons of grace unimproved end in this cry, when harvest and summer are gone and salvation has not come. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

21 COMMENTARY: For the hurt of the daughter of God's people Jeremiah is hurt; he is black, and astonishment has taken hold on him. Henry's prophet shares the wound: hurt for hurt, black as a mourner, astonished at the misery. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

22 COMMENTARY: Is there no balm in Gilead? Is there no physician there? Why then is not the health of God's people recovered? Henry answers yes: God is able to help and heal, the blood of Christ is balm in Gilead and his Spirit the all-sufficient Physician, so the people may be healed but will not; thus men die unpardoned and unchanged, for they will not come to Christ to be saved. — Matthew Henry (Concise)
