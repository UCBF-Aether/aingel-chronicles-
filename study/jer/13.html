Study Notes — Jeremiah

Chapter 13

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Thus says the Lord unto me: Go and get thee a linen girdle, and put it upon thy loins, and put it not in water. Henry notes it was usual with the prophets to teach by signs: the linen girdle, worn unwashen, is the sign Israel must read. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

2 COMMENTARY: So I got a girdle according to the word of the Lord, and put it on my loins. Henry's prophet obeys the sign literally: the girdle is got and worn according to the word, so that the sign itself is God's teaching. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

3 COMMENTARY: And the word of the Lord came unto me the second time, saying. Henry's sign unfolds in two words: the girdle first worn, then hidden, that the second word may explain the first. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

4 COMMENTARY: Take the girdle that thou hast got, which is upon thy loins, and arise, go to Euphrates, and hide it there in a hole of the rock. Henry's sign is burial: the girdle that cleaved to the loins is hidden in a hole of rock by Euphrates, as Israel would be buried in foreign earth. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

5 COMMENTARY: So I went, and hid it by Euphrates, as the Lord commanded me. Henry notes they had by their idolatries and sins buried themselves in foreign earth, mingled among the nations: the prophet's hiding mirrors the people's self-burial. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

6 COMMENTARY: And it came to pass after many days, that the Lord said unto me, Arise, go to Euphrates, and take the girdle from thence, which I commanded thee to hide there. Henry's many days are the exile's length: the girdle left hidden until God commands its recovery, that its state may be seen. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

7 COMMENTARY: Then I went to Euphrates, and digged, and took the girdle from the place where I had hid it: and, behold, the girdle was marred, it was profitable for nothing. Henry notes they were so corrupted that they were good for nothing: the recovered girdle, marred and profitable for nothing, is Israel after mingling among the nations. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

8 COMMENTARY: Then the word of the Lord came unto me, saying. Henry's explanation follows the sign: the word that commanded the girdle now interprets it, for we have the explanation in verses 9-11. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

9 COMMENTARY: Thus says the Lord: After this manner will I mar the pride of Judah, and the great pride of Jerusalem. Henry notes that if we are proud of learning, power, and outward privileges, it is just with God to wither them: the marred girdle is the marred pride of Judah and Jerusalem. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

10 COMMENTARY: This evil people, which refuse to hear my words, which walk in the imagination of their heart, and walk after other gods, to serve them, and to worship them, shall even be as this girdle, which is good for nothing. Henry notes the minds of men should be awakened to a sense of their guilt and danger: refusing the word, walking after imagination and other gods, leaves a people good for nothing, like the girdle. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

11 COMMENTARY: For as the girdle cleaves to the loins of a man, so have I caused to cleave unto me the whole house of Israel and the whole house of Judah, says the Lord; that they might be unto me for a people, and for a name, and for a praise, and for a glory: but they would not hear. Henry notes the people of Israel had been to God as this girdle: he caused them to cleave to him by the law he gave them, the prophets he sent among them, and the favours he showed them, but they would not hear. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

12 COMMENTARY: Therefore thou shalt speak unto them this word: Thus says the Lord God of Israel, Every bottle shall be filled with wine: and they shall say unto thee, Do we not certainly know that every bottle shall be filled with wine? Henry's second sign is the bottle: as the bottle was fitted to hold the wine, so the sins of the people made them vessels of wrath, fitted for the judgements of God. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

13 COMMENTARY: Then shalt thou say unto them, Thus says the Lord: Behold, I will fill all the inhabitants of this land, even the kings that sit upon David's throne, and the priests, and the prophets, and all the inhabitants of Jerusalem, with drunkenness. Henry notes all ranks should suffer misery: kings, priests, prophets, and all Jerusalem shall be filled, not with wine only, but with the judgements for which their sins fitted them. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

14 COMMENTARY: And I will dash them one against another, even the fathers and the sons together, says the Lord: I will not pity, nor spare, nor have mercy, but destroy them. Henry warns they should be filled till they caused each other's destruction: fathers dashed against sons, without pity, spare, or mercy, is the vessel of wrath broken from within. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

15 COMMENTARY: Hear ye, and give ear; be not proud: for the Lord has spoken. Henry exhorts them to give glory to God by confessing their sins, humbling themselves in repentance, and returning to his service: hearing and giving ear, without pride, is the first step of that return. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

16 COMMENTARY: Give glory to the Lord your God, before he cause darkness, and before your feet stumble upon the dark mountains, and, while ye look for light, he turn it into the shadow of death, and make it gross darkness. Henry's exhortation is urgent: give glory to God before darkness comes, lest feet stumble on dark mountains and looked-for light turn to the shadow of death. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

17 COMMENTARY: But if ye will not hear it, my soul shall weep in secret places for your pride; and mine eye shall weep sore, and run down with tears, because the Lord's flock is carried away captive. Henry notes all misery, witnessed or foreseen, will affect a feeling mind, but the pious heart must mourn most over the afflictions of the Lord's flock: otherwise they would be carried into other countries in all the darkness of idolatry and wickedness, and the prophet weeps in secret for it. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

18 COMMENTARY: Say unto the king and to the queen, Humble yourselves, sit down: for your principalities shall come down, even the crown of your glory. Henry's message is sent to king Jehoiakim and his queen: their sorrows would be great indeed, and the crown of glory must come down before humility is learned. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

19 COMMENTARY: The cities of the south shall be shut up, and none shall open them: Judah shall be carried away captive all of it, it shall be wholly carried away captive. Henry's captivity is total: shut cities, none to open, Judah wholly carried away, is the awful message to Jerusalem and its king. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

20 COMMENTARY: Lift up your eyes, and behold them that come from the north: where is the flock that was given thee, thy beautiful flock? Henry asks where the beautiful flock has gone: lifted eyes see the northern invaders, and the flock given to Jerusalem is no longer hers. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

21 COMMENTARY: What wilt thou say when he shall punish thee? For thou hast taught them to be captains, and as chief over thee: shall not sorrows take thee, as a woman in travail? Henry's punishment is self-taught: those taught to be captains and chiefs over Jerusalem now punish her, and sorrows take her as travail takes a woman. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

22 COMMENTARY: And if thou say in thine heart, Wherefore come these things upon me? For the greatness of thine iniquity are thy skirts discovered, and thy heels made bare. Henry answers the question: do they ask, Wherefore come these things upon us? Let them know, it is for their obstinacy in sin, and for the greatness of their iniquity their skirts are discovered and heels made bare. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

23 COMMENTARY: Can the Ethiopian change his skin, or the leopard his spots? Then may ye also do good, that are accustomed to do evil. Henry notes we cannot alter the natural colour of the skin, and so it is morally impossible to reclaim and reform these people by their own power: sin is the blackness of the soul, its discolouring, and we were shapen in it, so that we cannot get clear of it by any power of our own. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

24 COMMENTARY: Therefore will I scatter them as the stubble that passeth away by the wind of the wilderness. Henry's scattering is the lot of the unreformed: stubble before the wilderness wind, because obstinacy in sin leaves nothing to hold them. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

25 COMMENTARY: This is thy lot, the portion of thy measures from me, says the Lord; because thou hast forgotten me, and trusted in falsehood. Henry's lot is measured by forgetfulness: Jerusalem forgot God and trusted in falsehood, and the portion measured to her is scattering and shame. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

26 COMMENTARY: Therefore will I discover thy skirts upon thy face, that thy shame may appear. Henry notes that if any poor slave of sin feels that he could as soon change his nature as master his headstrong lusts, let him not despair; for things impossible to men are possible with God, and the shame discovered is the first sight of the nature that needs his changing. — Matthew Henry (Concise)

27 COMMENTARY: I have seen thine adulteries, and thy neighings, the lewdness of thy whoredom, and thine abominations on the hills in the fields. Woe unto thee, O Jerusalem! Wilt thou not be made clean? When shall it once be? Henry closes with the Lord's question to Jerusalem: whether she is determined not to be made clean. Almighty grace is able to change the Ethiopian's skin, and neither natural depravity nor strong habits of sin form an obstacle to the working of God, the new-creating Spirit; let us then seek help from him who is mighty to save. — Matthew Henry (Concise)
