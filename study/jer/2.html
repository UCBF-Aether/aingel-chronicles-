Study Notes — Jeremiah

Chapter 2

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The word of the Lord comes to Jeremiah with a message to cry in Jerusalem's ears. Henry introduces the chapter as likely Jeremiah's first sermon after his ordination, a lively and pathetic reproof meant to show God's people their transgressions and bring them to repentance before ruin comes. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: God remembers the kindness of Israel's youth and the love of their espousals, when they followed him in a wilderness land not sown. Henry notes that those who begin well but do not persevere are justly upbraided with their hopeful beginnings, and God's spiritual Israel must own their obligations for safe conduct through this world's dangerous wilderness. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Israel was holiness to the Lord and the firstfruits of his increase, so that all who devoured him would offend and evil would come upon them. Henry's point is the aggravation: professions once so devoted now aggravate the crime of desertion. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: All the house of Jacob and all the families of Israel are summoned to hear the word of the Lord. Henry frames the whole chapter as reproof and conviction, its charge high and its arguments close, so that the people might repent and prevent the coming ruin. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: God asks what iniquity their fathers found in him, that they went far from him, walked after vanity, and became vain. Henry stresses there could be no excuse: those who desert religion commonly oppose it more than those who never knew it. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: They never asked where the Lord was who brought them up out of Egypt and led them through deserts, pits, drought, and the shadow of death, a land no man passed through or dwelt in. Henry notes the obligation they forgot: safe conduct through a wilderness so dangerous to the soul. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: God brought them into a plentiful country to eat its fruit and goodness, but when they entered they defiled his land and made his heritage an abomination. Henry's charge is ingratitude: kindness so great makes the defilement inexcusable. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: The priests did not ask where the Lord was, those who handled the law did not know him, the pastors transgressed, and the prophets prophesied by Baal and walked after things that do not profit. Henry's section shows the failure reaching every office: priest, teacher, pastor, and prophet alike. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: God will yet plead with them, and with their children's children he will plead. Henry notes that before God punishes sinners he pleads with them to bring them to repentance, urging what we should plead with ourselves. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: They are sent to pass over to the isles of Chittim and to Kedar, to consider diligently whether any such thing has happened. Henry's point is the search for a precedent: God pleads by asking the nations to witness an unheard-of exchange. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Has a nation changed their gods, which are yet no gods? But God's people have changed their glory for what does not profit. Henry calls this unparalleled fickleness and folly: even idolatrous nations kept their gods, while Israel traded the true God for nothing. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Heaven itself is called to be astonished, horribly afraid, and very desolate at this. Henry notes the horror is proportioned to the sin: be afraid to think of the wrath and curse that will be the portion of those who throw themselves out of God's grace and favour. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: God's people have committed two evils: forsaking him, the fountain of living waters, and hewing out broken cisterns that can hold no water. Henry compares grace in Christ to fountain water, cooling, refreshing, cleansing, and fruitful, living water that quickens the dead and issues in eternal life; the cisterns are the world and men's inventions, followed and depended on in vain. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Is Israel a servant, or a homeborn slave, that he should be spoiled? Henry answers no: they are the seed of Abraham, and the soul of man is likewise no slave by nature, but has sold its own liberty and enslaved itself to divers lusts and passions. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: The young lions roared upon him and made his land waste, his cities burned without inhabitant. Henry identifies the lions as the Assyrian princes who prevailed against Israel once it departed from the Lord. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: The children of Noph and Tahapanes have broken the crown of his head. Henry notes the people of Egypt destroyed Israel's glory and strength, a calamity Israel brought on itself by departing from the Lord. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Hast thou not procured this unto thyself, in forsaking the Lord thy God when he led thee by the way? Henry's application is repentance: repent of the sin, so that correction may not become ruin. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: What has Israel to do in the way of Egypt, drinking the waters of Sihor, or in the way of Assyria, drinking the waters of the river? Henry turns the question on the reader: what has a Christian to do in the ways of forbidden pleasure, vain mirth, covetousness, or ambition? — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Their own wickedness shall correct them and their backslidings reprove them, until they know and see how evil and bitter it is to forsake the Lord and to have no fear of him. Henry urges the use of the correction: let it reprove, not ruin. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: God broke their yoke and burst their bands long ago, and they said they would not transgress, yet upon every high hill and under every green tree they wander, playing the harlot. Henry notes that despite all their advantages, Israel became like the wild vine bearing poisonous fruit, breaking covenant and degenerating from good beginnings. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: God planted a noble vine, wholly a right seed, yet it turned into the degenerate plant of a strange vine. Henry observes how men fall under unbridled desires and sinful lusts as much as brute beasts fall under instinct. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: Though they wash with nitre and take much soap, their iniquity is marked before God. Henry's charge is that their wickedness is too plain to be concealed and too bad to be excused: no washing covers what God has marked. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: How can they say they are not polluted and have not gone after Baalim? Let them see their way in the valley and know what they have done, like a swift dromedary traversing her ways. Henry points to the evidence itself: the valley ways expose the denial. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Like a wild ass used to the wilderness, snuffing up the wind at her pleasure, whom none can turn away in her occasion, Israel pursues idolatry indefatigably. Henry notes they persist wilfully and obstinately, irreclaimable in their idolatries, and weary themselves only in pursuits bringing distress and misery. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: They are told to withhold their foot from being unshod and their throat from thirst, but they answer there is no hope, for they have loved strangers and after them they will go. Henry warns neither to despair of God's mercy, sufficient to pardon, nor of his grace, able to subdue corruptions however strong, yet Israel chooses hopeless pursuit instead. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: As the thief is ashamed when found, so the house of Israel is ashamed, with their kings, princes, priests, and prophets. Henry notes they shamed themselves by idolatry, and would shortly be made ashamed of it when their idols proved unable to help them. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: They say to a stock, 'Thou art my father,' and to a stone, 'Thou hast brought me forth,' having turned their back to God and not their face, yet in trouble they will say, 'Arise, and save us.' Henry calls this a downright disowning of their obligations to the Father who made them. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: Where are the gods they made for themselves? Let them arise, if they can save in the time of trouble, for according to the number of Judah's cities are her gods. Henry's point is the coming shame: idols multiplied by the city will fail by the city in the day of trouble. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Why will they plead with God? They have all transgressed against him. Henry notes the nation, unwrought upon by God's judgments, sought instead to justify themselves, but the plea collapses against universal transgression. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: In vain God smote their children, for they received no correction, and their own sword devoured their prophets like a destroying lion. Henry observes they had not been convinced or reformed by the rebukes of providence they had been under. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: O generation, see the word of the Lord: has he been a wilderness to Israel, or a land of darkness, that his people say, 'We are lords, we will come no more unto thee'? Henry answers that the world is a wilderness and land of darkness only to those who make it their home and portion, while those who dwell in God have pleasant places; the presumption lies in calling freedom from God lordship. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: Can a maid forget her ornaments, or a bride her attire? Yet God's people have forgotten him days without number. Henry notes how many days of our lives pass without suitable remembrance of him, the Jews having long thrown off serious thoughts of God. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: Why does she trim her way to seek love, and so teach the wicked ones her ways? Henry observes men employ all their ingenuity in sin, trimming their path to win lovers and then teaching even the wicked a worse way. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: In her skirts is found the blood of the souls of the poor innocents, not found by secret search but upon all these. Henry charges that with their idolatries they mixed the most unnatural murders, shedding innocent blood openly. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: Yet she says, 'Because I am innocent, surely his anger shall turn from me,' so God will plead with her because she says she has not sinned. Henry calls this the language of presumptuous sinners, who cannot find excuse for sin yet insist on innocence. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: Why does she gad about so much to change her way? She shall be ashamed of Egypt as she was ashamed of Assyria. Henry notes the Lord was displeased with their confidences and would not prosper them therein; they may shift from one sin to another, but none ever hardened himself against God and prospered. — Matthew Henry

37 COMMENTARY: She shall go forth from Egypt with her hands upon her head, for the Lord has rejected her confidences, and she shall not prosper in them. Henry closes that men employ all their ingenuity but cannot find happiness in the way of sin, or an excuse for it. — Matthew Henry
