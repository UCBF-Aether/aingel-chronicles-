Study Notes — Jeremiah

Chapter 46

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Henry notes this verse is the title of the part of the book concerning the neighbouring nations: it is the word of the LORD against the Gentiles, for God is King and Judge of nations who will call to account those who know him not, and Henry contrasts the Old Testament word against the Gentiles with the New Testament word for them, that those afar off are made nigh. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

2 COMMENTARY: Henry begins with Egypt because they were of old Israel's oppressors and of late their deceivers when Judah put confidence in them, and notes Pharaoh-necho's overthrow at Carchemish in the fourth year of Jehoiakim was so complete that Babylon recovered all that pertained to Egypt from the river of Egypt to the Euphrates, weakened Pharaoh so he came no more out of his land, and avenged Josiah, whom Necho had slain four years before. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

3 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the irony of human preparation: the prophet calls Egypt to order buckler and shield and draw near to battle, and Henry reflects on what care, trouble, and vast expense men spend to kill one another, as if they did not die fast enough of themselves. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

4 COMMENTARY: Henry notes Egypt was famous for horses, so the call to harness them and mount the cavalry well is the utmost of military preparation, yet Henry adds the moral that those going to war should not only order the buckler and harness the horses, but repent of their sins and pray for God's presence. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

5 COMMENTARY: Henry contrasts the mighty preparations with the shameful retreat: when the Chaldean army faces them, even their mighty ones are dismayed and turned back, fleeing by consent in confusion with neither time nor heart to look back, because fear is round about them. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

6 COMMENTARY: Henry stresses they gain the shame of flight without its benefit: the swift shall not flee away and the mighty shall not escape, but shall stumble and fall toward the north, toward their enemy's country, in such confusion that instead of making homeward they made forward, proving the race is not to the swift nor the battle to the strong. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

7 COMMENTARY: Henry compares Egypt's marching out to the rising of the Nile: Egypt rises up like a flood, scorning to keep within its own banks and threatening to overflow all neighbouring lands, a formidable army brought into the field. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

8 COMMENTARY: Henry notes Egypt's boast, I will go up and cover the earth, I will destroy the city and its inhabitants, and observes that they knew their own thoughts and God knew them, but they knew not the thoughts of the LORD, for he gathers them as sheaves into the floor. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

9 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the prophet challenges Egypt to bring all its confederate troops, the Ethiopians descended from the same stock and their neighbours and allies, and the Libyans and Lydians seated in Africa west of Egypt, yet all their art and interest shall be in vain, for God will fight against them and against him there is no wisdom nor counsel. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

10 COMMENTARY: Henry explains that the Egyptians meant a day for themselves, but God says it shall be his day, the day of the LORD God of hosts in which he will be exalted in their overthrow, a day of vengeance for Josiah's death and a day of sacrifice to divine justice to which multitudes of Egypt's sinners shall fall as victims. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

11 COMMENTARY: Henry pictures the daughter of Egypt, a damsel living in great pomp and state, sorely wounded by this defeat, and notes that though she seek balm in Gilead and use all the medicines her wise men can prescribe, it shall be in vain, for she shall never again bring such a powerful army into the field. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

12 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the shame needs no conqueror's triumph to spread it: the nations have heard of Egypt's shame, and the shrieks and outcries of the conquered fill the country, as in flight one mighty man stumbled upon another and dashed against another, so that both together became an easy prey to the pursuers. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

13 COMMENTARY: Henry marks this as a second, later prediction, fulfilled by the same hand, Nebuchadnezzar, many years after, at least twenty, and probably about the same time as the prediction of the same event in chapter 43, showing how Babylon should come and smite the land of Egypt and bring war into their own bosoms which they had carried into his borders. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

14 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the alarm must be proclaimed in all parts of Egypt, particularly Migdol, Noph, and Tahpanhes, precisely where the Jewish refugees had planted themselves in contempt of God's command, so they may hear what a sorry shelter Egypt is likely to be to them, since the sword is already devouring round about. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

15 COMMENTARY: Henry explains the valiant men are swept away as with a sweeping rain: none can stand their ground because the LORD drives them from their posts, driving them by his terrors and by enabling the Chaldeans to drive them, since those whom the wrath of God chases cannot fix. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

16 COMMENTARY: Henry notes that when their day comes to fall, the enemy need not throw them down, for they shall fall one upon another, every man a stumbling-block to his fellow, and in consternation the mercenary troops make homeward, saying, Arise, let us go again to our own people from the oppressing sword, since little confidence can be put in troops that fight purely for pay with no interest in those they fight for. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

17 COMMENTARY: Henry explains the cry against Pharaoh: he had probably promised to come within a set time with a gallant army to support his frontier troops, but failed them and left them abandoned to the invaders, so they desert crying that Pharaoh king of Egypt is but a noise, a great talker who brings nothing to pass and has passed the time appointed, and Henry notes those who make most noise are frequently but a noise. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

18 COMMENTARY: Henry stresses the certainty sworn by the King whose name is the LORD of hosts: as Tabor overtops the mountains and Carmel overlooks the sea, so shall the king of Babylon overpower all the force of Egypt, such command and sway shall he bear, before whom the mightiest kings on earth are but as grasshoppers. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

19 COMMENTARY: Henry draws the practical lesson to prepare for trouble: the daughter dwelling securely and delicately in Egypt must furnish herself to go into captivity, exchanging rich clothes that would tempt the enemy for plain warm clothes and fine shoes for strong ones, and inuring herself to hardship, for Noph shall be waste and desolate without an inhabitant, so general shall the slaughter and captivity be. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

20 COMMENTARY: Henry pictures Egypt as a very fair heifer, fat and shining and not accustomed to the yoke, wanton as a well-fed heifer, with a possible allusion to Apis, the calf the Egyptians worshipped from whom Israel learned the golden calf, yet destruction comes out of the north, as butchers come to kill and cut up this fair heifer. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

21 COMMENTARY: Henry compares Egypt's hired men to fatted bullocks, lusty, able-bodied, and high-spirited men likely for action, yet they are turned back and fled away together because the day of their calamity had come, and Henry adds that some read the comparison as luxury: they had wantoned in pleasures and were unfit for hardships, and therefore could not stand. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

22 COMMENTARY: Henry explains the serpent's voice as Egypt's voice grown low and submissive: no longer lowing like a fair heifer making a great noise, but hissing out of holes like serpents, venting grief in silent murmurs and begging for life with entreaties, while the Chaldeans march with an army and come against her with axes as hewers of wood. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

23 COMMENTARY: Henry notes Egypt shall be felled as a forest by hewers of wood: though populous like a forest whose trees cannot be searched or numbered and rich in hidden treasures, the Chaldeans, more than the grasshoppers and innumerable, shall make a great spoil, for the LORD of hosts has numberless hosts at his command. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

24 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the daughter of Egypt shall be confounded and delivered into the hand of the people of the north, her tune changed and her pride brought down, as the Egyptians are tamed rather than merely beaten. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

25 COMMENTARY: Henry stresses that no multitude or rank escapes: the multitude of No, called populous No, shall be punished, for though hand join in hand they shall not escape and God will be too many for them, and their kings and petty princes shall fall, and their gods too, their tutelar idols proving no protection, with Pharaoh brought down and all those that trust in him, particularly the Jews who sojourned in his country trusting in him rather than in God. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

26 COMMENTARY: Henry notes all shall be delivered into the hand not only of Nebuchadnezzar but of his servants, according to the curse on Ham's posterity, of which the Egyptians were, that they should be servants of servants, into the hand of those who seek their lives, yet Henry also notes the intimation that in process of time Egypt shall recover: afterwards it shall be inhabited and peopled again, whereas this destruction almost dispeopled it, and Ezekiel foretells this at the end of forty years, a reminder that nations are emptied and increased again, so prospering nations should not be secure nor those in thraldom despair. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

27 COMMENTARY: Henry applies the comfort to the captives in Babylon, or more generally to all the people of God in difficult times when God's judgments are abroad among the nations, repeating chapter 30: God would not have his people timorous, but Jacob shall be found out and gathered though far off, redeemed though held fast in captivity, and shall return to rest and ease with none making him afraid. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

28 COMMENTARY: Henry stresses the distinguishing mercy: wherever Jacob is, God is with him, a very present help, and while a full end shall be made of the nations that oppressed God's Israel, as Egypt and Babylon, mercy is kept in store for Israel, which shall be corrected but not cast off, the correction in measure in degree and continuance, and in the gospel church, God's spiritual Israel, this promise has its full accomplishment, since though God correct it he will never make a full end of it. — Matthew Henry (Complete)
