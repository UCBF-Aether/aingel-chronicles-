Study Notes — Jeremiah

Chapter 5

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Run to and fro through Jerusalem's streets and broad places to see if there is a man who executes judgment and seeks truth, and God will pardon the city. Henry notes Jerusalem had become like the old world where all flesh corrupted their way, yet God is so ready to forgive that one righteous man in a thousand would have spared it, as ten would have spared Sodom. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Though they say, 'The Lord liveth,' surely they swear falsely. Henry explains the professors are insincere: they honour God with their lips while their hearts are far from him, and though they swear by God only, they call him to witness a lie, affronting him as the God of truth. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Are not God's eyes upon the truth? He has stricken them, but they have not grieved; consumed them, but they have refused correction, making their faces harder than rock and refusing to return. Henry notes they set themselves to outface the divine sentence, unable to blush for shame or pale for fear, pressing forward like a horse into battle. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Jeremiah excuses the poor at first: surely these are poor and foolish, for they know not the way of the Lord nor the judgment of their God. Henry notes prevailing ignorance is the lamentable cause of abounding impiety, yet the devil's poor are willingly ignorant, so ignorance will not be their excuse. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Jeremiah will go to the great men, who have known the way of the Lord, but these have altogether broken the yoke and burst the bonds. Henry observes the rich are insolent and wilful: knowing their Master's will, they resolve to have their own, thinking themselves too goodly to be controlled even by the sovereign Lord. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Therefore a lion out of the forest shall slay them, a wolf of the evenings shall spoil them, and a leopard shall watch over their cities, tearing everyone who goes out, because their transgressions are many and backslidings increased. Henry pictures the enemy as lion, strong and irresistible, wolf, hungry and ravenous at evening, and leopard, swift and careful not to miss its prey, leaving the cities starved within or stabbed without. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: How shall God pardon thee for this? Thy children have forsaken him and sworn by gods that are no gods, and when fed to the full they committed adultery and assembled by troops in harlots' houses. Henry calls spiritual whoredom the first unpardonable crime: forsaking the God to whom they were born and dedicated, to covenant with those who are no gods. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: They were as fed horses in the morning, every one neighing after his neighbour's wife. Henry notes that having forsaken God for idols, they were given up to vile affections, and unbridled lust made them like brute beasts, with fulness of bread feeding Sodom's fire. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Shall God not visit for these things, and shall not his soul be avenged on such a nation as this? Henry appeals to equity: a jealous God of infinite purity cannot connive at such idolatry and uncleanness, for his soul, not only his word, takes vengeance, and nations as such are punished in this life. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Go up upon her walls and destroy, but make not a full end, taking away her battlements, for they are not the Lord's. Henry notes walls of stone before a divine commission are but mud walls: battlements not erected in God's fear, and trusted in more than God, are paper walls God will not patronise. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: The house of Israel and the house of Judah have dealt very treacherously against God. Henry observes that though at variance with each other, both agreed in treachery: forsaking God's worship, violating their covenants, revolting from him, and playing the hypocrite with him. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: They have belied the Lord, saying, 'It is not he; neither shall evil come upon us, neither shall we see sword nor famine.' Henry traces ruin to vain hopes of impunity: they belied God himself, as if he did not see or require, repeating Satan's artifice, 'Ye shall not surely die.' — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: The prophets shall become wind, and the word is not in them; thus shall it be done unto them. Henry notes impenitent sinners will not own anything to be God's word that makes against them: they jested that prophecy was mere wind and melancholy fancy, and threatened the prophets with the very famine and sword they foretold. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Because ye speak this word of contempt, God will make his words in Jeremiah's mouth fire, and this people wood, and it shall devour them. Henry notes God owns the words men denied, and the word will be too hard for those who contend with it: those who will not bow before it shall break. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Lo, God will bring a nation upon them from far, a mighty nation, an ancient nation, whose language they know not nor understand. Henry observes God has all nations at his command and sometimes makes heathen nations a scourge to a hypocritical Israel; this nation comes from afar, mighty, haughty in antiquity, and speaking an unknown tongue that makes treating for peace difficult. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: Their quiver is as an open sepulchre, and they are all mighty men. Henry notes the invaders are well armed: arrows flying so thick, hitting so sure, and wounding so deep that they breathe nothing but death, wielded by able-bodied, effective soldiers. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: They shall eat up the harvest and bread that sons and daughters should eat, flocks, herds, vines, and fig trees, and impoverish the fenced cities wherein Judah trusted, with the sword. Henry calls it a grievous vexation to see enemy camps victualled with one's stores while one's children perish for want, and just that God should impoverish what men make their confidence. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Nevertheless in those days God will not make a full end with them. Henry marks the tender compassion: the enemy must destroy and lay waste, but with a 'nevertheless' setting bounds, 'Hitherto it shall come, and no further,' because God has mercy in store. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: When they ask why the Lord does all these things to them, they shall be answered: as ye have forsaken me and served strange gods in your land, so shall ye serve strangers in a land not yours. Henry notes unhumbled hearts charge God with injustice, but God will be justified when he speaks: read their sin in their punishment. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Declare this in the house of Jacob and publish it in Judah. Henry sends the prophet on a new errand: to persuade them to fear God, which would be an effectual principle of reformation, as the want of that fear had been at the bottom of their apostasy. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Hear, O foolish people without understanding, who have eyes and see not, ears and hear not. Henry notes their understandings were darkened: like the idols they worshipped, they had faculties but did not employ them, sitting in darkness in a land of light and living in sin in a holy land. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: Fear ye not me, and will ye not tremble at my presence, who placed the sand for the bound of the sea by a perpetual decree that it cannot pass? Henry argues from the sea kept within compass by a wall of sand: waves toss and roar yet cannot prevail, so why do revolting hearts refuse the check the sea obeys? — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: But this people has a revolting and a rebellious heart; they are revolted and gone. Henry observes the corrupt bias of the will bribes and besots the understanding, and none are so blind as those who will not see: a revolting heart produces a revolting life, withdrawing from allegiance and then taking up arms against God. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Neither say they in their heart, 'Let us now fear the Lord our God, that giveth rain, both the former and the latter, in his season, reserving the appointed weeks of harvest.' Henry notes the root of apostasy is refusing this good thought: it is God's work to put his fear in hearts, but our work to stir ourselves to fear him by fastening on proper considerations. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: Your iniquities have turned away these things, and your sins have withheld good things from you. Henry notes sin stops the current of God's favour: the former and latter rains in due season were withheld, harvests disappointed, and the heavens became as brass and the earth as iron. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: For among God's people are found wicked men who lay wait as one setting snares, setting a trap and catching men. Henry calls them spiteful and malicious, caught in the act like hunters delighting in mischief, contriving harm against good people, reprovers, rivals, and owners of coveted estates. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: As a cage is full of birds, so are their houses full of deceit, therefore they have become great and waxen rich. Henry notes their whole family business runs on deceit: wealth obtained by fraud and arts of defrauding, cheating whoever deals with them because they make no conscience of what they say or do. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: They are waxen fat, they shine, they overpass the deeds of the wicked, judging not the cause of the fatherless, yet prospering, and not judging the right of the needy. Henry observes deceit under colour of law does more mischief than open force, and their prosperity hardens them: sleek, admired, and escaping expected troubles, they take no cognizance of distressed cases except to protect wrongdoers. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Shall God not visit for these things, and shall not his soul be avenged on such a nation as this? Henry repeats the reckoning with certainty: vengeance must come if the sinner repents not, and conscience itself testifies that such abominations deserve punishment. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: A wonderful and horrible thing is committed in the land. Henry calls the degeneracy of so privileged a people wonderful to view with amazement, and horrible to detest: to frighten ourselves from sin, let us call it a horrible thing. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: The prophets prophesy falsely, the priests bear rule by their means, and God's people love to have it so; and what will ye do in the end thereof? Henry notes the combination: false prophets counterfeit a heavenly commission while priests use them as tools of ambition and tyranny, and the people love a loose rein from rulers who will not restrain lusts and teachers who will not reprove. The end of such a way is greater sin and utter ruin. — Matthew Henry
