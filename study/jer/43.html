Study Notes — Jeremiah

Chapter 43

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Henry notes Jeremiah made them hear all he had to say, though he saw them uneasy at it, since it was what the Lord their God had sent him to speak, and they shall have it all. The case had been made so very plain that one would have thought no more words were needed; what follows shows it quite otherwise. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

2 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the cause of their disobedience was pride: only by pride comes contention both with God and man, and these proud men could not bear the contradiction of their sentiments and the control of their designs, no, not by the divine wisdom and will itself. The proud, unhumbled heart of man, he says, is one of the most daring enemies God has on this side of hell. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

3 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the colour for their disobedience was denying the message to be God's word, while their slander against Baruch was a likely absurdity: had Jeremiah and Baruch been so well affected to the Chaldeans, they would have gone away at first with Nebuzaradan to Babylon rather than stay with this despised, ungrateful remnant. Men deny the Scriptures to be God's word, he says, because they are resolved not to conform to Scripture rules, making obstinate infidelity the sorry subterfuge of wilful disobedience. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

4 COMMENTARY: Henry notes they determined to go to Egypt notwithstanding, resolving with one consent not to dwell in the land of Judah as God had ordered them. Their fancy for Egypt would not be restrained by anything God himself said to the contrary, as with the builders of Babel: now nothing will be restrained from them which they have imagined to do. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

5 COMMENTARY: Henry notes they took along with them all the remnant of Judah returned from all nations, not leaving to their liberty those who had come out of sincere affection to dwell in Judah. It is the pride of great men, he says, to force those under their power to follow them, though ever so much against their duty and interest. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

6 COMMENTARY: Henry notes they compelled even Jeremiah the prophet and Baruch his scribe to go along, carrying them as prisoners, partly to punish them by forcing them against their consciences, and partly to put reputation upon their own way. Though the prophets were under force, the proud men would make the world believe they went voluntarily, so that none could blame the people for acting contrary to God's word if the prophets themselves had acted so. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

7 COMMENTARY: Henry notes they came to Tahpanhes, a famous city of Egypt, the same as Hanes, now the metropolis where Pharaoh's house stood. No place could serve these proud men to settle in but the royal city near the court, he says, so little mindful were they of Joseph's wisdom in settling his brethren in Goshen; had they the spirit of Israelites, they would rather dwell in the wilderness of Judah than in the most pompous, populous cities of Egypt. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

8 COMMENTARY: Henry notes that even in Tahpanhes, among idolatrous Egyptians and treacherous Israelites, the word of the Lord came to Jeremiah. God can find his people with the visits of his grace wherever they are, and when his ministers are bound, yet the word of the Lord is not bound; the spirit of prophecy was not confined to the land of Israel, and going into Egypt by constraint did not withdraw God's wonted favour from him. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

9 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the sign: great stones, such as are used for foundations, were to be laid in the clay of the brick-kiln at the entry of Pharaoh's house, in the sight of the men of Judah rather than the Egyptians. The foundation of Egypt's desolation was laid in that clay, in a land famous for brick-kilns, recalling the slavery of Israel forced to make bricks; since Jeremiah could not prevent their going, he might bring them to repent of it. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

10 COMMENTARY: Henry notes Nebuchadnezzar, the very king employed in destroying Jerusalem, shall come in person against Egypt and set his throne in the very place where these stones were laid, spreading his royal pavilion over them. This minute circumstance is foretold so that, when accomplished, they might be confirmed in the extent and certainty of divine prescience; God calls Nebuchadnezzar his servant because he executes God's will and is the tool providence makes use of. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

11 COMMENTARY: Henry notes Nebuchadnezzar shall smite Egypt and have all at his mercy: whom he will he shall slay, whether by pestilence, by the sword in cold blood or hot, and whom he will he shall save alive and carry into captivity. By going into Egypt, the Jews brought the Chaldeans thither, ill repaying those who entertained them and exposing to Babylon those who had promised to protect Israel from him. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

12 COMMENTARY: Henry notes God will kindle a fire in the houses of Egypt's gods, with a fire of God's own kindling: Nebuchadnezzar shall burn some temples and carry others captive, and shall array himself with the rich spoils of Egypt as a shepherd puts on his garment, slipping on the heavy booty with ease and going forth in peace, so effectually reduced shall the land be. Babylon lay far from Egypt, yet God can make far-fetched judgments strike home. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

13 COMMENTARY: Henry notes Beth-shemesh, the house of the sun, named for its sun temple where worshippers gathered, shall have its standing images broken in pieces and their rich materials carried away, and the houses of Egypt's gods burned with fire. The king of Babylon was himself a great idolater, yet he is employed to destroy Egypt's idols: thus God sometimes makes one wicked man, or wicked nation, a scourge and plague to another. — Matthew Henry (Complete)
