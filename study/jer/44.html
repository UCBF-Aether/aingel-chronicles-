Study Notes — Jeremiah

Chapter 44

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the Jews in Egypt were dispersed into Migdol, Noph, and other places, and Jeremiah was sent on an errand from God to them, delivering this message either when most of them were together in Pathros or by going about from place to place preaching to this purport. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

2 COMMENTARY: Henry observes that the fugitives in Egypt seem to have forgotten the desolations of Judah and Jerusalem, though the captives by the rivers of Babylon were daily mindful of them, and so needed to be put in mind of what they had seen with their own eyes. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

3 COMMENTARY: Henry traces the desolation to idolatry in particular: the people gave to counterfeit deities, creatures of their own fancy and work of their own hands, the honour due to the true God only, forsaking a God known among them for gods they knew not, upstart deities whose original was obscure. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

4 COMMENTARY: Henry stresses the frequent and fair warnings given by the prophets, sent with great care saying, Oh, do not this abominable thing that I hate, and draws the lesson that sin should be spoken of with dread and detestation because it is what God hates. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

5 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the aggravation: they hearkened not nor inclined their ear, but still persisted in their idolatries, and the outcome showed what came of it, since God's anger was poured out upon them as at this day. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

6 COMMENTARY: Henry insists that whoever were the instruments of the destruction, they were but instruments: it was God's fury and anger that kindled the fire which made Jerusalem and the cities of Judah waste and desolate. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

7 COMMENTARY: Henry reads this as self-injury: in sinning they commit a great evil against their own souls, wronging and deceiving them with what is false, and cutting off from themselves man and woman, child and suckling, so that Judah would be left without heirs. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

8 COMMENTARY: Henry explains that God forbade the going into Egypt because he knew it would be a snare, and contrasts the two exiles: those God sent to Chaldea were by grace weaned from idolatry, while those who went against God's mind into Egypt became more wedded than ever to their idolatries, since thrusting oneself without cause or call into temptation leaves one justly to oneself. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

9 COMMENTARY: Henry notes they filled up the measure of their fathers' iniquity, adding to it rather than being humbled by it, and were reminded of the sins and punishments of the kings of Judah, who great as they were did not escape judgment for idolatry, and of their wives who had seduced them to it, with Lightfoot's suggestion that Solomon's Egyptian wives are tacitly reflected on. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

10 COMMENTARY: Henry laments that they are not humbled even unto this day by all the humbling providences they have been under, and draws the rule that those who walk not in the law of God thereby show they are destitute of the fear of God. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

11 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the reversal of resolve: they set their faces to go into Egypt against God, and now God is resolute against them, setting his face to cut off all Judah, since those who think not only to affront but to confront God Almighty will find themselves outfaced. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

12 COMMENTARY: Henry stresses the sentence admits no exception of rank or order: they shall all be consumed, from the least to the greatest, high and low, rich and poor, by the very judgments they thought to escape by flying into Egypt. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

13 COMMENTARY: Henry notes they shall be consumed by the same judgments used for Jerusalem, sword, famine, and pestilence, not wasted by natural deaths as Israel in the wilderness, but by these sore judgments, which they thought Egypt would put out of their reach. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

14 COMMENTARY: Henry observes the irony of their desires: in Judah they desired Egypt, and in Egypt they desired to return to Judah, lifting up their soul to it, yet because they would not dwell there when God commanded it, they shall not dwell there when they desire it, and only a very few shall escape to return. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

15 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the defiance was general, not one man's obstinacy: the men who knew their wives had burned incense, and the women who stood by and had joined in the idolatrous usages, were touched in a sore place by reproof and kicked against the pricks, and consciousness of guilt made them impatient of it. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

16 COMMENTARY: Henry calls this scarcely matched for downright daring contradiction to God himself: they own Jeremiah spoke in the name of the LORD, yet tell him flatly, we will not hearken unto thee, which Henry reads as the genuine language of the rebellious heart. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

17 COMMENTARY: Henry identifies the queen of heaven with the moon, though some understand the sun, much worshipped in Egypt and at Jerusalem, or all the host of heaven, and notes the rebels plead antiquity, authority, unity, universality, visibility, the Jerusalem mother-church, and prosperity, yet the law of God, not the practice of men, must rule and judge. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

18 COMMENTARY: Henry exposes their perverse reading of providence: they claimed judgments came because they had left off burning incense to the queen of heaven, whereas the true reason was that they still retained idols in their hearts, so afflictions meant to separate them from sin instead confirmed them in it. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

19 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the women's plea that they did not act without their husbands, since the men kindled the fire while the women kneaded the dough for cakes and poured the drink offerings, but calls it frivolous: it is sad when nearest relations harden each other in sin instead of quickening each other to good, and husbands' approval avails nothing against God's mind. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

20 COMMENTARY: Henry remarks that daring sinners may speak many bold words, but God will have the last word, and Jeremiah immediately replied to correct their wilful mistake about the cause of their calamities. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

21 COMMENTARY: Henry explains that the incense burned in Judah and Jerusalem went long unpunished because God was long-suffering, and during the day of his patience it was perhaps well with them, but at length their old guilt, unrepented, came into God's mind and was brought to account. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

22 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the Lord could no longer bear the evil of their doings and their abominations, so their land became a desolation, an astonishment, and a curse without inhabitant, not for any late reformation but for old transgressions. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

23 COMMENTARY: Henry draws the general lesson that whatever evil comes upon us, it is because we have sinned against the LORD, having not obeyed his voice nor walked in his law, statutes, and testimonies, and we should therefore stand in awe and sin not, since rightly understanding the cause of troubles should go far toward curing our sins. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

24 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the privilege and duty in this address: Judah dwelling in Egypt still has God speaking to them even there, and they are to observe what he says, with Jeremiah speaking to the women particularly from the LORD of hosts. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

25 COMMENTARY: Henry warns that no vow can make lawful what God has made sin: the people claim they will surely perform their vows to the queen of heaven, as if swearing to do it justified doing it, and God answers by repeating their own words back to them before declaring his own vow. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

26 COMMENTARY: Henry explains the oath by God's great name: what little remained of religion among them should be lost, so that his name would no more be named by any man of Judah in Egypt in their solemn oath, the LORD liveth, and they would forget their religion and learn to swear by the life of Pharaoh, or else God would resent any mention of his name from them as an affront. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

27 COMMENTARY: Henry reads the watching over them for evil as God letting slip no opportunity to bring judgment upon them until there be an end of them, and notes that to those God finds impenitent sinners he will be found an implacable Judge. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

28 COMMENTARY: Henry frames the contest as whose word shall stand, God's or theirs: they said they would recover themselves by returning to the queen of heaven, God said they would ruin themselves, and the event would show which was right, while only a small number escaping the sword would return, next to none beside the great numbers returning from Chaldea. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

29 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the sign is given so they may know God's words shall surely stand against them for evil: the threatened consumption in Egypt will be accomplished in its season, and the sign itself will confirm the whole sentence. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

30 COMMENTARY: Henry reports the two identifications of Pharaoh-hophra's enemies, his own rebellious subjects under Amasis according to Herodotus, or Nebuchadnezzar invading his kingdom according to Josephus, and notes the Jews depended on this potent and politic prince for protection, yet creature-comforts and confidences we promise ourselves most from may fail as soon as those we promise least from, and the sacred history's silence afterward implies these Jews in Egypt were lost there, for no word of God shall fall to the ground. — Matthew Henry (Complete)
