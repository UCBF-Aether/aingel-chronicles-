Study Notes — Jeremiah

Chapter 4

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: If Israel will return, let her return to God, putting away her abominations out of his sight, and then she shall not remove. Henry notes these verses belong with the last chapter: sin must be put away out of the heart, else it is not put away out of God's sight, for the heart is open before him. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Israel shall swear, 'The Lord liveth,' in truth, in judgment, and in righteousness, and the nations shall bless themselves in him and glory in him. Henry's condition is inward truth: an oath in truth and righteousness follows a heart truly returned, not a mouth only. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Break up your fallow ground, and sow not among thorns, says the Lord to Judah and Jerusalem. Henry compares the unhumbled heart to ground untilled: it may be improved, it is our ground let out to us, but it lies fallow, overgrown with thorns and weeds, the natural product of the corrupt heart. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Circumcise yourselves to the Lord and take away the foreskins of your heart, lest his fury come forth like unquenchable fire because of the evil of your doings. Henry urges the prayer behind the command: entreat the Lord to create a clean heart and renew a right spirit, for except a man be born again he cannot enter the kingdom of heaven. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Declare in Judah and publish in Jerusalem: blow the trumpet, gather together, and assemble into the defenced cities. Henry notes the fierce conqueror of the neighbouring nations was to make Judah desolate, and the trumpet call is the approach of that enemy described. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Set up the standard toward Zion, retire and stay not, for evil comes from the north and great destruction. Henry's section warns that the northern conqueror's approach is now the sentence against a people lesser calamities did not rouse. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: The lion has come up from his thicket, the destroyer of the Gentiles is on his way to make the land desolate and the cities waste without inhabitant. Henry notes the prophet was afflicted to see the people lulled into security by false prophets while this destroyer marched. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: For this, gird with sackcloth, lament and howl, for the fierce anger of the Lord is not turned back. Henry observes some attention was paid in Jerusalem to outward reformation, but sackcloth without washed hearts is not enough: hearts must be washed in true repentance and faith from the love and pollution of sin. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: At that day the heart of the king shall perish, and the heart of the princes, the priests shall be astonished and the prophets wonder. Henry's point is the collapse of false security: when misery approaches, every order that trusted in outward profession fails together. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Jeremiah cries, 'Ah, Lord God! Surely thou hast greatly deceived this people and Jerusalem, saying, Ye shall have peace, whereas the sword reaches unto the soul.' Henry explains the complaint as the prophet's affliction at false prophets lulling the people into security with promises of peace. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: At that time it shall be said to this people and Jerusalem that a dry wind of the high places blows toward the daughter of God's people, not to fan nor to cleanse. Henry notes the wind is judgment, not winnowing: it comes to destroy, not to purify. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Even a full wind from those places shall come, and God will now give sentence against them. Henry states the principle: when lesser calamities do not rouse sinners and reform nations, sentence will be given against them. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Behold, he shall come up as clouds, his chariots as a whirlwind, his horses swifter than eagles, and woe to us, for we are spoiled. Henry's section describes the enemy's approach in exactly these terms: swift, overwhelming, and irresistible once sentence is given. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: O Jerusalem, wash thine heart from wickedness, that thou mayest be saved; how long shall vain thoughts lodge within thee? Henry returns to the chapter's demand: outward reformation is not enough; the heart itself must be washed in true repentance and faith. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: A voice declares from Dan and publishes affliction from Mount Ephraim. Henry notes the Lord's voice declares misery approaching from the north, first heard at the border and moving toward Jerusalem. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: Make mention to the nations and publish against Jerusalem that watchers come from a far country and give out their voice against the cities of Judah. Henry's section has the nations themselves summoned as witnesses to Judah's coming desolation. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: As keepers of a field, the watchers are against her round about, because she has been rebellious against God. Henry notes the siege encirclement matches the rebellion: the city that rebelled is watched on every side. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Thy way and thy doings have procured these things unto thee; this is thy wickedness, because it is bitter, because it reaches unto thine heart. Henry closes the section plainly: when misery overtakes wicked professors, it will be seen that the fruit of wickedness is bitter and the end fatal. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: My bowels, my bowels! I am pained at my very heart, my heart makes a noise in me, I cannot hold my peace, because my soul has heard the trumpet, the alarm of war. Henry notes the prophet had no pleasure in delivering messages of wrath: the alarm pains him even as he sounds it. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Destruction upon destruction is cried, for the whole land is spoiled, suddenly tents spoiled and curtains in a moment. Henry sees in vision the whole land in confusion, everything out of order compared with what it was. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: How long shall I see the standard and hear the sound of the trumpet? Henry's vision is unrelenting: standard and trumpet recur because the land's confusion is total, not partial. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: My people is foolish, they have not known me, sottish children without understanding, wise to do evil but with no knowledge to do good. Henry calls this wretched: to be like foolish children in the concerns of their souls, whatever else they may know. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: I beheld the earth, and lo, it was without form and void, and the heavens had no light. Henry reads the vision as creation undone: the land in confusion mirrors a world returned to formlessness. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: I beheld the mountains, and lo, they trembled, and all the hills moved lightly. Henry notes everything is out of order: even the mountains, the land's fixed frame, shake at God's presence. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: I beheld, and lo, there was no man, and all the birds of the heavens were fled. Henry's vision empties the land of its life: man gone, birds fled, the country unpeopled. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: I beheld, and lo, the fruitful place was a wilderness, and all its cities were broken down at the presence of the Lord and by his fierce anger. Henry stresses the agent: it is God's presence and fierce anger that break the cities down. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: The whole land shall be desolate, yet God will not make a full end. Henry draws the limit that saves the vision from despair: the ruin of the Jewish nation would not be final, and every end of our comforts is not a full end. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: For this the earth shall mourn and the heavens above be black, because God has spoken it, purposed it, and will not repent or turn back from it. Henry notes that though the Lord may correct his people very severely, yet he will not cast them off; the sentence stands, but so does the remnant. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: The whole city shall flee for the noise of horsemen and bowmen, going into thickets and climbing upon rocks, every city forsaken with not a man dwelling therein. Henry warns that no outward privileges, profession, or contrivances will prevent destruction once it is purposed. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: When thou art spoiled, what wilt thou do? Though thou clothest thyself with crimson, deckest thyself with gold, and rentest thy face with painting, in vain shalt thou make thyself fair, for thy lovers will despise thee and seek thy life. Henry notes ornaments and false colouring are of no avail: the lovers Judah courted will turn executioners. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: I have heard a voice as of a woman in travail, anguish as of her first child, the voice of the daughter of Zion bewailing herself and spreading her hands: 'Woe is me now, for my soul is wearied because of murderers.' Henry closes that as sin will find out the sinner, so sorrow will sooner or later find out the secure. — Matthew Henry
