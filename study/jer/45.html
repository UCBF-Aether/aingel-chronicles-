Study Notes — Jeremiah

Chapter 45

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Henry explains the placement: though this prophecy concerns Baruch only and stands after the destruction of Jerusalem and the dispersion, it was delivered long before, in the fourth year of Jehoiakim, and belongs with the story of Baruch writing and reading Jeremiah's roll in chapter 36, but was thrown to the latter end of the book as a private person's matter, as Philemon stands after Paul's other epistles. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

2 COMMENTARY: Henry stresses the comfort in the address itself: though Baruch was only Jeremiah's scribe, God despises not any of his servants but graciously concerns himself for the meanest and weakest, for Baruch the scribe as well as for Jeremiah the prophet. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

3 COMMENTARY: Henry describes Baruch as a young man well affected to God, willing to serve God and his prophet, who hoped reading the roll publicly would gain him reputation and preferment, but instead found himself driven into a corner in danger of prison or worse, crying that the LORD had added grief to his sorrow after the grief of writing prophecies of his country's ruin, and Henry calls this his infirmity, noting that young beginners in religion are apt to faint at first difficulties and that even dear saints may disquiet themselves with melancholy apprehensions beyond cause. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

4 COMMENTARY: Henry reads the reproof as searching the wound to its root: Baruch's complaints came from raised expectations too high in this world, and God shows him the ship was sinking, since what God had built as a house for himself he was breaking down and what he had planted as a vineyard he was plucking up, even this whole land, so it was folly to desire abundance of wealth and honour at such a time. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

5 COMMENTARY: Henry calls it absurd to paint one's own cabin while the ship sinks, and unbecoming to seek great things for oneself when the public is in danger, yet gives the encouragement that though Baruch should not be great he should be safe: his life would be given him for a prey in all places whither he goes, escaped often very narrowly and saved as by fire, and Henry notes that preservation of life at such times is a signal favour, since life is more than meat and prolongs the opportunity to glorify God. — Matthew Henry (Complete)
