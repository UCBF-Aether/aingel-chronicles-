Study Notes — Jeremiah

Chapter 40

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Henry observes the heading seems misapplied, since this chapter holds no prophecy: the story only introduces the message of chapter 42 and shows its occasion, and Jeremiah, being himself concerned in the story, was the better able to give an account of it. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

2 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the captain solemnly owns Jeremiah a true prophet, glorying that he had been God's instrument to fulfil what Jeremiah had been God's messenger to foretell. The heathen prince plainly sees what Israel's princes would never acknowledge, though evident as if written with a sunbeam: their sin is the cause of their miseries. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

3 COMMENTARY: Henry stresses that the people had often been told from the pulpit by their prophets that sin brought this ruin, and would not regard it; now they are told it from the bench by the conqueror, whom they dare not contradict. Sooner or later, he says, men shall be made sensible that their sin is the cause of all their miseries. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

4 COMMENTARY: Henry notes Nebuzaradan loosed Jeremiah from his chains a second time and invited him to Babylon not as a captive but as a friend and companion, promising to set his eye upon him, that is, to show him respect and see him safe and well provided for. A great change for a man lately tossed from prison to prison, now free to walk at liberty. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

5 COMMENTARY: Henry notes that while Jeremiah stood considering what to do, neither inclined to Babylon nor determined elsewhere, Nebuzaradan turned the scale by advising him to go to Gedaliah, governor under Babylon. The counsel did not bind him, Henry adds: it is friendly to give advice, but unfriendly to prescribe and be angry if advice is not taken. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

6 COMMENTARY: Henry says Jeremiah not only accepted the captain's kindness but took his advice and dwelt with Gedaliah at Mizpah among the people left in the land. Whether his prudence can be commended is doubtful, since the event did not prove to his comfort, but his pious affection to Israel may be commended: he would rather dwell with the poor in the holy land than with princes in an unholy one. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

7 COMMENTARY: Henry notes a bright sky opening upon the remnant: Gedaliah, one of themselves rather than a prince of Babylon, was made governor, so the people might be sure he would seek their peace. He was the son of Ahikam, who took Jeremiah's part in chapter 26, a man of great wisdom and mild temper under whom the few left might have been very happy. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

8 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the great resort to Gedaliah from all parts: the captains who had escaped the Chaldeans by force came and quietly submitted to him for their own safety and common preservation, coming with their men and so strengthening one another. Babylon, Henry adds, was not jealous of their growing numbers but rather pleased with them. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

9 COMMENTARY: Henry describes Gedaliah's oath as the model of the new government settled by an original contract: the people must own that the property of their lands was in the Chaldeans, yet fear not the sin, the reproach, or the consequences of serving Babylon, since it was what God had brought them to. If they lived peaceably, Henry says, peaceably they should live. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

10 COMMENTARY: Henry notes Gedaliah undertook to dwell at Mizpah to serve the Chaldeans in the people's name, receiving orders and paying tribute when the Chaldeans came, so that all dealings between them and Babylon would pass through his hand. The people, meanwhile, were to gather wine, summer fruits, and oil for their own use, like those who live in a land of peace and hope to eat the labour of their hands. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

11 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the dispersed Jews in Moab, Ammon, and Edom were induced by love of their own land to return as soon as they heard Gedaliah was in authority there. Canaan itself would be an unsafe, unpleasant country with no government, he says, and it was a great reviving for the dispersed to come together again under a governor of their own nation: in wrath God remembered mercy. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

12 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the returnees gathered wine and summer fruits very much, the spontaneous products of a fertile soil, their corn harvest having passed before Jerusalem was taken. While Gedaliah cared for public safety he left them to enjoy the public plenty and demanded no tribute, seeking not his own profit but the profit of many. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

13 COMMENTARY: Henry marks this as a dark cloud gathering over the infant state, threatening a dreadful storm: how soon the hopeful prospect is blasted, for when God begins in judgment he will make an end. The captains' coming to Mizpah carries the warning that pride, ambition, or revenge still bearing rule will form new projects ending in ruin. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

14 COMMENTARY: Henry notes Baalis king of the Ammonites had a particular spite at Gedaliah and contrived to take him off, employing Ishmael as the instrument of his malice. Johanan, having got scent of the plot, takes it for granted Gedaliah must already know it, the proofs being so plain, yet Gedaliah believed them not, being a man of sincerity himself. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

15 COMMENTARY: Henry notes Johanan gave Gedaliah private intelligence and proffered his service to prevent the murder by secretly slaying Ishmael, arguing that otherwise the gathered Jews would scatter and the remnant perish. In this, Henry says, Johanan showed more courage and zeal than sense of justice: if it were lawful to kill for prevention, who then could be safe, since malice always suspects the worst? — Matthew Henry (Complete)

16 COMMENTARY: Henry notes Gedaliah would by no means give credit to the information of Ishmael's treachery, answering that Johanan spoke falsely of Ishmael. Herein he discovered more good humour than discretion, Henry says, more of the innocence of the dove than the wisdom of the serpent, and many have been ruined by being over-confident of the fidelity of those about them. — Matthew Henry (Complete)
