Study Notes — Jeremiah

Chapter 49

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Henry brings God's action against Ammon for illegally encroaching on Gad's territory after the Assyrian deportation left it almost unguarded. He notes the Ammonites even murdered unborn heirs to bar future claims (Amos 1:13), yet might among men, however often it prevails against right, is controlled by the Almighty who judges aright. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

2 COMMENTARY: Henry reads the alarm of war in Rabbah, the strong capital, and the burning of her daughter cities as the judgment for that violence. He adds the reversal at the verse's end, that Israel shall be heir to those who were his heirs, as the equity of divine providence recompensing the injured out of the unjust gains of the injurious. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

3 COMMENTARY: Henry pictures the flight and sackcloth lament as the people lose all they had and know not where to betake themselves. The king, his priests and his princes going into captivity completes the evacuation of the land, from the least to the greatest. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

4 COMMENTARY: Henry calls Ammon a backsliding daughter, since all idolaters turn away from the worship of the true God that came first, and charges her with glorying in her flowing valley and treasures, saying, Who shall come unto me? Those who turn from God, he notes, have little reason to take complacency or confidence in any worldly enjoyment. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

5 COMMENTARY: Henry notes that God, who has all armies at his command, brings fear upon Ammon from all around, so that each man is driven straight out and none gathers up the wanderer. God has many ways, he observes, to terrify those who have been a terror to his people. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

6 COMMENTARY: Henry closes the Ammonite oracle with a prospect of mercy hereafter, as before to Moab: the captivity of Ammon shall be brought again. In human affairs, he remarks, the wheel goes round. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

7 COMMENTARY: Henry observes that Edom, once famous for wisdom with statesmen thought to excel in politics, shall take such wrong measures that onlookers ask in wonder whether wisdom is gone from Teman. When God designs a people's ruin, he notes, whom he will destroy he infatuates. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

8 COMMENTARY: Henry reads the call to flee, turn back and dwell deep as the onset of the calamity of Esau, long deserved for old sins and now at hand as the time of God's visitation. The Edomites must hide in close caverns from the sword that is coming. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

9 COMMENTARY: Henry contrasts ordinary spoilers with Edom's destroyers: grape-gatherers leave some gleanings, and even thieves know when they have enough and destroy no further. Those who destroy Edom, he says, shall never be satiated. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

10 COMMENTARY: Henry says Esau shall be made quite bare, his most hidden treasures discovered and rifled, so that he cannot hide himself and his seed is spoiled. His brethren the Moabites and his neighbours the Philistines are spoiled as well, and so disabled from giving him succour or shelter. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

11 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the desolation leaves no relation, friend, or parish officer to care for the widows and fatherless left behind. He also records the Chaldee reading, which takes these words as God's promise to his own people, distinguishing Israel from Edom: whatever becomes of Edom's orphans, God will preserve Israel's and let her widows rest on his word. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

12 COMMENTARY: Henry argues from the lesser to the greater: even those whose doom was not to drink of the cup, including Israel itself, have been made to drink the bitter cup, so Edom shall surely drink it and not go unpunished. When judgment begins at God's house, he notes, it will reach the strangers. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

13 COMMENTARY: Henry stresses that God has sworn by himself that Bozrah shall become a desolation, a reproach, a waste and a curse, with all its cities perpetual wastes. The decree has gone forth and cannot be resisted. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

14 COMMENTARY: Henry reads the rumour from the LORD and the ambassador sent to the nations as God's own summons into this service: the nations are mustered and told to gather and come against Edom. The matter is settled, for this is the counsel God has taken against her. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

15 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the reversal of Edom's standing: a people that made a mighty figure shall be made small among the heathen and despised among men. Those who despised God's people, he observes, shall themselves be despised. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

16 COMMENTARY: Henry says Edom's terribleness deceived her: feared by all her neighbours and secure in mountain passes she thought unassailable, she grew proud and conceited of her strength. High as she builds her nest, God will bring her down, for there is no wisdom and no might against the LORD. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

17 COMMENTARY: Henry says Edom shall be made such a desolation that everyone going by shall be astonished, and even made a prodigy and a terror. Her fall becomes a spectacle to the nations. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

18 COMMENTARY: Henry compares Edom's ruin to the overthrow of Sodom and Gomorrah, so frightful a place that no man shall abide there or dwell in it. The land is left uninhabitable, not merely conquered. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

19 COMMENTARY: Henry identifies the lion coming up from the swelling of Jordan as Nebuchadnezzar, roaring to devour all in his way and coming suddenly upon the strong habitation. God's questions, who is like me and who can stand before me, show that when God has work to do he finds the fittest instruments, and none can engage against it. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

20 COMMENTARY: Henry treats this verse as the settled counsel of the LORD against Edom and Teman: the decree has gone forth and there is no resisting it. Even the least of the flock, the meanest servant in Nebuchadnezzar's retinue, shall draw them out for slaughter, for God can bring about the greatest works by instruments least likely. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

21 COMMENTARY: Henry says the noise of Edom's fall shall move the earth and put all nations into concern, with the outcry heard as far as the Red Sea, where ships take in lading and carry the news to the remotest shore. The fall of those who affected to make a noise with pomp and power makes so much the greater noise. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

22 COMMENTARY: Henry pictures Nebuchadnezzar coming not only like a lion but like an eagle, the king of birds, flying swiftly and strongly to clap his wings upon Bozrah. At once the hearts of Edom's mighty men fail them, like a woman in her pangs, seeing an enemy it is vain to struggle with. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

23 COMMENTARY: Henry says Syria's judgment begins with terrible fright: Hamath and Arpad hear evil tidings of Babylon's approach, are confounded, and lie like the troubled sea that cannot be quiet. See how easily God can dispirit nations most celebrated for valour, he remarks. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

24 COMMENTARY: Henry says Damascus, a city that thought she could face the most formidable enemy, now waxes feeble, turns to flee, and owns it is as vain to contend with her fate as for a woman in labour to contend with her pains. Fear seizes her outright, and anguish takes her. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

25 COMMENTARY: Henry explains the city of praise as a city commended by strangers, and the city of joy as one where there was an affluence of all the delights of the sons of men. Those who place their happiness in carnal joys deceive themselves, he notes, for God can soon make a city of praise a reproach and a city of joy a terror to itself. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

26 COMMENTARY: Henry says Damascus's fall ends in slaughter: the young men who should defend the city shall fall by the sword in her streets, and all the men of war, expert and engaged in their country's service, shall be cut off in that day. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

27 COMMENTARY: Henry says the fire kindled in the wall of Damascus by the besiegers shall devour all before it, the palaces of Ben-hadad particularly. Those palaces are marked because so much mischief was formerly hatched there against God's Israel, for which it is now thus visited. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

28 COMMENTARY: Henry says this oracle foretells the desolation Nebuchadnezzar shall make among Kedar, descended from Ishmael's son, and the petty kingdoms of Hazor joined to them. Babylon acts to gratify its own covetousness and ambition, he notes, but God orders it for correcting an unthankful people and warning a careless world. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

29 COMMENTARY: Henry observes that the Chaldeans shall take the Kedarenes' tents, flocks, curtains, vessels and camels, crying, Fear is on every side. The enemy need not strike a stroke, he says: terror alone shall shout them out of their tents. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

30 COMMENTARY: Henry remarks it is strange that so great a prince should stoop to such small game, yet all is fish that comes to the ambitious covetous man's net. The command to flee far off and dwell deep answers Nebuchadnezzar's counsel and purpose, and shows that not having given offence is no defence against such a man. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

31 COMMENTARY: Henry describes Kedar's people as shepherds dwelling in tents with neither gates nor bars, no fortified cities, no soldiers and no merchants, living within themselves. They are called a wealthy nation, he notes, because they had sufficiency for life's occasions and were content with it, and those who live innocently may live securely though they have neither gates nor bars. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

32 COMMENTARY: Henry says the Kedarenes' camels shall be booty and their cattle a spoil, and they themselves scattered into all winds from the utmost corners, with calamity brought from all sides. No marvel there are fears on every side, he remarks, when there are foes on every side. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

33 COMMENTARY: Henry says Hazor shall lie uninhabited forever, a dwelling for dragons, because it lies remote and out of all high roads, with neither cities nor lands inviting to strangers. Busy men's places are striven for when they fall, he notes, but no man cared to abide where these easy, quiet men had lived meanly. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

34 COMMENTARY: Henry dates this prophecy to the beginning of Zedekiah's reign and thinks the other prophecies against the Gentiles going before were probably at the same time. The Elamites, he says, were the Persians, descended from Elam the son of Shem, or at least that part of Persia nearest the Jews called Elymais. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

35 COMMENTARY: Henry says the Elamites were famous archers, and breaking the bow of Elam ruins their artillery, so that the chief of their might is gone. God often orders it, he notes, so that what we most trust to first fails us, and what was the chief of our might proves the least of our help. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

36 COMMENTARY: Henry pictures the four winds bringing enemies from all parts of the world, carrying some Elamites captive and driving others to flee in every direction, so that no nation lacks Elam's outcasts. We know not from what point the wind of trouble may blow, he remarks, but if God encompasses us with his favour we are safe whichever way the storm comes. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

37 COMMENTARY: Henry says Elam shall be dismayed before her enemies, and the sword shall be sent after her till she is consumed, for God's fierce anger has all evil in it. God can make his judgments follow those who think by flight to escape them, he notes, for evil pursues sinners. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

38 COMMENTARY: Henry reads setting God's throne in Elam as the throne of Nebuchadnezzar or of Cyrus, who began his conquests with Elymais, or else the throne on which God sits for judgment. Either way, the king and princes of Elam are destroyed, for kings are accountable to God, who is above the highest of them. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

39 COMMENTARY: Henry closes with the limit on Elam's destruction: it shall not be perpetual, for in the latter days God will bring again the captivity of Elam. As with Ammon and Moab, judgment gives way at last to a promised return. — Matthew Henry (Complete)
