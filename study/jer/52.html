Study Notes — Jeremiah

Chapter 52

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Henry notes Zedekiah had arrived at years of discretion, being twenty-one when he came to the throne, and reigned eleven years in Jerusalem. The narrative begins no higher than his reign, he says, probably drawn up by some of those carried away with him, as a reproach for flattering themselves that they should not go into captivity after their brethren. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

2 COMMENTARY: Henry says Zedekiah was none of the worst kings, since we never read of his idolatries, yet his character is that he did evil in the eyes of the LORD, for he did not do the good he should have done. Doing evil, he implies, includes the failure to do the good required. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

3 COMMENTARY: Henry reads this as God's just displeasure carried to casting Judah and Jerusalem out from his favourable, gracious presence, expelling them from the land and city and temple that bore tokens of his presence in bounty and covenant love. Yet none are cast out from God's gracious presence, he notes, but those who by sin have first thrown themselves out of it, a fruit of sin to be deprecated above all else. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

4 COMMENTARY: Henry says the Chaldeans sat down before Jerusalem in the ninth year of Zedekiah, in the tenth month, and blocked it up. In captivity, he notes, the Jews kept a fast in the tenth month in remembrance of this step towards their ruin, as they kept fasts in the fourth, fifth and seventh months for the city's breaking, the temple's burning and Gedaliah's murder. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

5 COMMENTARY: Henry pictures the eighteen months' siege as a sad time beyond imagining, with provisions cut off and the enemy's attacks ever renewed, while the inhabitants obstinately resolved to hold out to the last extremity. The city was besieged unto the eleventh year of Zedekiah, with nothing remaining but a fearful looking for of judgment. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

6 COMMENTARY: Henry says what disabled Jerusalem to hold out, yet could not prevail with her to capitulate, was the famine: there was no bread for the people of the land, so soldiers could not make good their posts and were rendered wholly unserviceable. Walls, he observes, will not hold out long without men, any more than men without walls, nor will both together stand without God and his protection. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

7 COMMENTARY: Henry says that once the soldiers were unserviceable for famine, no wonder the city was broken up, with the men of war fleeing by night by the gate between the two walls, by the king's garden, while the Chaldeans were round about. Walls without men, he remarks, are no defence. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

8 COMMENTARY: Henry calls Zedekiah's flight inglorious and vain: overtaken in the plains of Jericho, his guards dispersed and all his army scattered from him. There is no escaping the judgments of God, he notes, for they come upon the sinner and overtake him, let him flee where he will. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

9 COMMENTARY: Henry says the king was carried up to the king of Babylon at Riblah, where judgment was given upon him as a rebel. One cannot think without vexation, he remarks, that a king of Judah, of the house of David, should be arraigned as a criminal at the bar of a heathen king, but he humbled not himself before Jeremiah the prophet, and therefore God thus humbled him. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

10 COMMENTARY: Henry says that pursuant to the conqueror's sentence, Zedekiah's sons were slain before his eyes, and all the princes of Judah at Riblah. The sentence was immediately put in execution upon the rebel king and his house. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

11 COMMENTARY: Henry says Zedekiah's eyes were then put out, and he was bound in chains, carried in triumph to Babylon, and kept in prison till the day of his death, wearing out the remainder of his life in darkness and misery. Jeremiah had often told him what it would come to, he adds, but he would not take warning when he might have prevented it. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

12 COMMENTARY: Henry says that a month after the city was taken, Nebuzaradan, captain of the guard, called in the margin the chief of the slaughter-men or executioners, came into Jerusalem in the nineteenth year of Nebuchadnezzar. Soldiers are but slaughter-men, he observes, and God employs them as executioners of his sentence against a sinful people, though Nebuzaradan himself had no eye to God but served Babylon's king and his own designs. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

13 COMMENTARY: Henry says Nebuzaradan burnt the house of the LORD, that holy and beautiful house where their fathers praised him, and the king's house, probably Solomon's palace, and all the houses of Jerusalem, that is, all the houses of the great men. If any escaped, he adds, it was only some sorry cottages for the poor of the land. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

14 COMMENTARY: Henry says the Chaldean army broke down all the walls of Jerusalem round about, to be revenged upon them for standing in the way of the army so long. Thus a defenced city was made a ruin, he notes, and when the walls were demolished the pillars of the temple were pulled down too, in token that God, the strength and stay of civil and ecclesiastical government, had departed. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

15 COMMENTARY: Henry says Nebuzaradan carried away many into captivity: certain of the poor of the city, the residue that remained after sword and famine, and the deserters, such as God thought fit, for he had already determined some for pestilence, some for the sword, some for famine and some for captivity. The poor of the country, by contrast, he left behind. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

16 COMMENTARY: Henry notes that Nebuzaradan left certain of the poor of the land for vinedressers and husbandmen, while the poor of the city were carried away. Only sorry cottages for the poor remained when the great men's houses were burnt. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

17 COMMENTARY: Henry says nothing is more particularly related than the carrying off of the temple's appurtenances: the pillars of brass, the bases and the brazen sea were broken and their brass carried to Babylon. When the walls were demolished the pillars fell too, he explains, in token that God had departed, for the pillars were not for support but for ornament and significancy, named Jachin, He will establish, and Boaz, In him is strength, so their breaking signified God would no longer establish his house nor be its strength. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

18 COMMENTARY: Henry says all the vessels that belonged to the brazen altar, the caldrons, shovels, snuffers, bowls, spoons and all the brass vessels of ministry, were taken away. The iniquity of Jerusalem, he observes, like that of Eli's house, was not to be purged by sacrifice or offering. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

19 COMMENTARY: Henry says that what was of great value, the vessels of silver and gold, had been carried away before, yet some of that sort remained and were now carried away, gold in gold and silver in silver. When the gold was gone the brass soon went after it, he notes, because the people repented not, according to Jeremiah's prediction. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

20 COMMENTARY: Henry says the brass of the two pillars, the one sea and the twelve brazen bulls under the bases, which Solomon had made, was without weight. So it was in the making, he notes, when the weight of the brass was not found out, and so it was in the destroying, for those who made great spoil of them did not stand to weigh them, as purchasers do, since whatever they weighed, it was all their own. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

21 COMMENTARY: Henry says the pillars are here very particularly described from 1 Kings 7:15, that their extraordinary beauty and stateliness may affect us the more with the demolishing of them. The eighteen cubits of height, the fillet of twelve cubits and the four fingers of thickness belong to that deliberate, affecting description. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

22 COMMENTARY: Henry continues the particular description: a chapiter of brass five cubits high, with network and pomegranates round about, all of brass, the second pillar likewise. These pillars were for ornament and significancy, not support, he stresses, and their breaking signified that God would no longer establish his house nor be the strength of it. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

23 COMMENTARY: Henry's point in the minute reckoning, ninety-six pomegranates on a side and a hundred round about upon the network, is the same deliberate description from 1 Kings 7:15: the extraordinary beauty and stateliness of the work should affect us the more with its demolishing. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

24 COMMENTARY: Henry puts Seraiah the chief priest first among those taken, noting that his sacred character could not exempt him from this stroke, since he himself had profaned it by sin. This Seraiah the priest, he adds, was perhaps not like Seraiah the quiet prince of chapter 51:59, but unquiet and turbulent, making himself obnoxious to Babylon's king. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

25 COMMENTARY: Henry counts among those taken an eunuch in charge of the men of war, seven men near the king's person, the principal scribe of the host, and sixty men of the people of the land. The account agrees with 2 Kings 25 except that there five, here seven, were near the king, which Lightfoot reconciles by noting that seven were taken but two, Jeremiah himself and Ebed-melech, were discharged, leaving five put to death. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

26 COMMENTARY: Henry says Nebuzaradan brought them to the king of Babylon at Riblah, as prisoners taken from temple, city, court and country. The leaders of the people had caused them to err, he observes, and now they are in a particular manner made monuments of divine justice. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

27 COMMENTARY: Henry calls this a very melancholy account of slaughter in cold blood at Riblah, seventy-two in number, of all ranks, probably those most forward to excite and promote the rebellion against Babylon's king. With their deaths, he says, Judah was carried away captive out of his own land, and the land spued them out as it had spued out the Canaanites before them. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

28 COMMENTARY: Henry notes this captivity in the seventh year of Nebuchadnezzar, the same as the eighth year of 2 Kings 24:12, was the captivity of Jeconiah, numbering 3,023 Jews. The sums here are very small compared with the 18,000 of 2 Kings 24:14, 16, he observes, and Lightfoot conjectures that, these accounts being joined to the Riblah slaughter, all here said to be carried away were put to death as rebels. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

29 COMMENTARY: Henry identifies the captivity of the eighteenth year of Nebuchadnezzar, the same as the nineteenth year of verse 12, with 832 persons carried from Jerusalem. This too belongs to the small sums he discusses, small against what one would expect when all the residue of the people were carried away. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

30 COMMENTARY: Henry notes a third captivity not mentioned before, in the twenty-third year of Nebuchadnezzar, when Nebuzaradan carried away 745 Jews, four years after Jerusalem's destruction, probably in revenge for Gedaliah's murder, taking aiders and abetters of Ishmael. If 4,600 is the sum total, he marvels how strangely reduced the people were, and how they came to be numerous again, for as in Egypt, so in Babylon, the LORD made them fruitful in affliction, and the more oppressed the more they multiplied. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

31 COMMENTARY: Henry says Evil-merodach, in the first year of his reign, lifted up the head of Jehoiachin and brought him forth out of prison in the thirty-seventh year of his captivity. New lords make new laws, he observes: Nebuchadnezzar's son, well-affected to the prisoner, could procure him no favour from his father, but once the old man was dead, his son countenanced Jehoiachin. The two days' difference from 2 Kings 25:27, he adds, is slight: the orders were likely given on the twenty-fifth, but Jehoiachin was not presented till the twenty-seventh. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

32 COMMENTARY: Henry says Evil-merodach spoke kindly to Jehoiachin and set his throne above the thrones of the kings with him in Babylon. Jehoiachin, who fell from a throne into a prison, is advanced again to a throne of state, though not of power, he notes, for such chequer-work is this world, with prosperity and adversity set one against the other. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

33 COMMENTARY: Henry says Jehoiachin's prison garments were changed, and he ate bread continually before the king all the days of his life. As before the robes were changed into prison-garments, so now they were converted into robes again, he observes, and though the night of affliction was very long, thirty-seven years since he was eighteen, we must not despair that the day may dawn at last. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

34 COMMENTARY: Henry closes with the continual diet given Jehoiachin every day until his death, and draws the general lesson: God can make his people find favour in the eyes of their oppressors and unaccountably turn their hearts to pity them. Those under oppression, he says, will find it is not in vain to hope and quietly wait for the salvation of the LORD, for no word of God shall fall to the ground. — Matthew Henry (Complete)
