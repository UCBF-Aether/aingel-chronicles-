Study Notes — Jeremiah

Chapter 41

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Henry notes Ishmael, being of the seed royal, was jealous of Gedaliah's growing greatness and enraged that he should merit and accept a commission under the king of Babylon. His ten men, princes of the king too, were guided by the same peevish resentments, and came again to Mizpah to eat bread with the governor they meant to kill. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

2 COMMENTARY: Henry stresses that those who ate bread with Gedaliah lifted up the heel against him: they picked no quarrel but watched an opportunity when they had him alone and assassinated him. Neither Babylon's commission nor Gedaliah's goodness and blessing to his country could secure him against such perfidious cruelty. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

3 COMMENTARY: Henry notes Ishmael likewise put to the sword all found in arms there, both Jews and Chaldeans employed under Gedaliah or in any capacity to revenge his death. As if not enough Israelite blood had been shed by the Chaldeans, their own princes here mingled it with the blood of the Chaldeans. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

4 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the massacre was carried on and concealed so artfully that on the second day after Gedaliah was slain no man knew it, the vine-dressers and husbandmen being busy in the fields. Yet murder will out, as Johanan's later pursuit shows; concealment only delays the reckoning. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

5 COMMENTARY: Henry notes these eighty men came from Shechem, Samaria, and Shiloh, places once famous but now reduced, belonging to the ten tribes yet retaining an affection for the worship of the God of Israel. They went toward the house of the Lord to pay their respects to its ashes and see its ruins, favouring its dust, in the habit of mourners with clothes rent and heads shaven, carrying offerings and incense in case any altar or priest remained. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

6 COMMENTARY: Henry notes Ishmael went out to meet the pilgrims with crocodile tears, pretending to bewail Jerusalem's desolations as much as they, and courted them into the town to try how they stood affected to Gedaliah. His invitation, Come to Gedaliah, pretended he would have them come and live with him, when really he intended they should come and die with him. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

7 COMMENTARY: Henry notes that when Ishmael had the pilgrims in the midst of the town he fell upon them and slew them, casting them into the pit, and no doubt took their offerings and converted them to his own use. He who would not stick at such a murder, Henry says, would not stick at sacrilege. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

8 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the ten who escaped worked not on their captors' compassion but on their covetousness, offering hidden treasures of wheat, barley, oil, and honey in exchange for their lives. Sometimes, he observes, the ransom of a man's life is his riches; but those who think thus to bribe death when it comes with commission will find death inexorable and themselves wretchedly deceived. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

9 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the pit was the one Asa king of Judah had dug when he built or fortified Mizpah as a frontier garrison for fear of Baasha king of Israel. Those who dig pits with a good intention, he warns, know not what bad use they may be put to; Ishmael slew so many he threw them all promiscuously into one pit rather than afford each a grave. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

10 COMMENTARY: Henry notes Ishmael carried off prisoners the king's daughters and the poor of the land committed to Gedaliah's charge, leading them toward the Ammonites, probably intending to make a present of them as trophies to the king who set him on. This melancholy story warns us never to be secure in this world: the end of one trouble we fancy final may prove the beginning of a greater. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

11 COMMENTARY: Henry says it would have been well if Johanan, when he warned Gedaliah, had stayed with him, for he and his captains might have been a life-guard to Gedaliah and a terror to Ishmael, preventing the mischief without bloodshed. Instead they were out upon some expedition and out of the way when they should have been on the best service; at length, hearing of all the evil Ishmael had done, they resolved to try an after-game. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

12 COMMENTARY: Henry notes Johanan gathered what forces he could and went to fight Ishmael, pursuing and overtaking him by the great waters of Gibeon. On Johanan's appearing with a force something like a match for him, Ishmael's heart failed him and his guilty conscience flew in his face: the most cruel, Henry observes, are often the most cowardly. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

13 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the poor captives were glad when they saw Johanan and his captains, looking upon them as their deliverers. Their gladness marks the turn of the whole episode: the prey Ishmael counted secure begins to slip from him the moment a rescuer appears. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

14 COMMENTARY: Henry notes the captives immediately found a way to wheel about and come over to Johanan, Ishmael not offering to detain them once he saw Johanan. Those who would be helped must help themselves, he says: they stayed not till their conquerors were beaten, but took the first opportunity to escape as soon as their friends appeared. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

15 COMMENTARY: Henry notes Ishmael quitted his prey to save his life and escaped with eight men, two of his ten assassins having deserted him or been killed in the engagement. He made his way to the Ammonites as a perfect renegade, abandoning all relation to the commonwealth of Israel though of the seed royal, and we hear no more of him. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

16 COMMENTARY: Henry notes Johanan now took the remnant he had recovered from Ishmael at Mizpah, mighty men of war and women and children and eunuchs, whom he had brought again from Gibeon, and so became their commander-in-chief. He prevailed only to rescue the captives, not to take revenge on the murderers, yet the prisoners were recovered and he now held command. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

17 COMMENTARY: Henry calls Johanan's resolution to enter Egypt very rash: instead of sitting down quietly and governing peaceably as Gedaliah did, he led the people toward Egypt, using his honestly won command little better than Ishmael would have used his. They encamped for a time in the habitation of Chimham by Bethlehem, David's city, steering their course toward Egypt from personal affection to that country or an ancient national confidence in the Egyptians. — Matthew Henry (Complete)

18 COMMENTARY: Henry judges the stated reason for going to Egypt very frivolous: he cannot think they really feared the Chaldeans would revenge Gedaliah's murder upon those who had appeared so vigorously against the murderers. They used this fear as a sham to cover their corrupt inclination to return into Egypt, and those who excuse themselves in sin with pretended fears will justly lose their comfort in real fears. — Matthew Henry (Complete)
