Study Notes — Revelation

Chapter 9

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The fifth angel sounded, and John saw a star fall from heaven to the earth, given the key of the bottomless pit. Henry reads the star as an eminent churchman turned antichrist, who, having ceased to be Christ's minister and lost the church's keys, becomes the devil's turnkey by Christ's permission, to loose hell's powers against the churches. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: He opened the bottomless pit, and smoke arose as from a great furnace, darkening the sun and the air. Henry notes the devil carries on his designs by blinding men's eyes, extinguishing light and knowledge, and promoting ignorance and error, first deceiving men and then destroying them. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Out of the smoke came locusts upon the earth, given power as the scorpions of the earth have power. Henry calls them the devil's emissaries headed by antichrist, the rout of antichristian orders promoting superstition, idolatry, error, and cruelty, like a plague of Egypt. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: They were commanded not to hurt the grass, any green thing, or any tree, but only those men who do not have the seal of God in their foreheads. Henry notes God's electing, distinguishing grace preserves his people from total and final apostasy, while the unsealed are left to the infection. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: They were given power not to kill men but to torment them five months, with torment like a scorpion's sting. Henry explains the hurt was spiritual, not military: not a persecution by fire and sword but a secret poison in souls, robbing them first of purity and then of peace, limited to a short season. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: In those days men shall seek death and shall not find it, desiring to die, while death flees from them. Henry notes the sharpness of the poison: those feeling its malignity in their consciences would grow weary of life, for a wounded spirit who can bear? — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: The locusts' shapes were like horses prepared for battle, with crowns like gold on their heads and faces as the faces of men. Henry reads the crowns as counterfeit authority with a show of assured victory, and the men's faces as a show of wisdom and sagacity masking the spirit of devils. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: They had hair as the hair of women, and their teeth were as the teeth of lions. Henry notes the allurements of seeming beauty and gaudy worship to ensnare minds, joined to real cruelty beneath the tenderness. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: They had breastplates as of iron, and the sound of their wings was as the sound of chariots of many horses running to battle. Henry notes the defence of earthly powers and the mighty noise of their movement from country to country, like an army on the march. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: They had tails like scorpions, with stings in their tails, and their power was to hurt men five months. Henry notes that though they first soothed and flattered with a fair appearance, the cup of their abominations would at length bite like a serpent and sting like an adder. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: They had a king over them, the angel of the bottomless pit, whose name in Hebrew is Abaddon, and in Greek Apollyon. Henry describes him as a fallen angel, prince and governor of the infernal regions, whose name means destroyer, his business and delight being the destruction of souls. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: One woe is past, and behold, two more woes come hereafter. Henry marks the end of one woe and the beginning of another, in the chain of judgments. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: The sixth angel sounded, and John heard a voice from the four horns of the golden altar before God. Henry notes the church's enemies are restrained until God gives the word to loose them, and that the voice from the altar frames the judgment that follows. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: The voice said to the sixth angel with the trumpet, "Loose the four angels which are bound in the great river Euphrates." Henry notes these instruments of anger lay far from Europe, at the Euphrates, where the Turkish power had its rise, so no danger was apprehended from them. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: The four angels were loosed, prepared for an hour, a day, a month, and a year, to slay the third part of men. Henry notes the time is fixed for beginning and end, and the execution limited to a third part, since God makes man's wrath praise him and restrains the remainder. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: The number of the army of horsemen was two hundred thousand thousand, and John heard their number. Henry notes the vastness of the armies of the Mahomedan empire, with infantry left to guess, showing the Lord of hosts has vast armies at his command. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: John saw horses with breastplates of fire, jacinth, and brimstone, heads as the heads of lions, and out of their mouths issued fire, smoke, and brimstone. Henry notes their fierce, martial equipage, clad in bright and costly armour with all ensigns of courage, zeal, and resolution. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: By these three plagues was the third part of men killed: by the fire, smoke, and brimstone issuing from the horses' mouths. Henry takes this as artillery, great guns first used by the Turks at Constantinople, terrible and destructive, going only as far as their commission suffered. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Their power was in their mouths and in their tails, for their tails were like serpents with heads, and with them they hurt. Henry parallels the two visions: as antichrist had spiritual scorpions poisoning minds, so the Turks had stings to hurt and kill the bodies of those who had murdered so many souls. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: The rest of the men not killed by these plagues yet repented not of the works of their hands, worshipping devils and idols of gold, silver, brass, stone, and wood, which cannot see, hear, or walk. Henry lists their idolatry first among the flagrant crimes: they would not cast away images that could do them no good. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Neither did they repent of their murders, sorceries, fornication, or thefts. Henry concludes that impenitence under divine judgments is itself the ruin of sinners: though God revealed his wrath against these crimes, they persisted, obstinate and judicially hardened, until destroyed. — Matthew Henry
