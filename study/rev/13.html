Study Notes — Revelation

Chapter 13

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: John stood on the sand of the sea and saw a beast rise out of the sea, having seven heads and ten horns, with ten crowns on his horns, and on his heads the name of blasphemy. Henry notes the monster's form, like Daniel's four beasts combined, proclaims direct enmity to God's glory by promoting idolatry. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: The beast was like a leopard, with feet as a bear's and a mouth as a lion's, and the dragon gave him his power, and his seat, and great authority. Henry notes the beast combines the fierceness, strength, and swiftness of Daniel's beasts, set up and supported by the devil to do his work. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: One of the beast's heads was as it were wounded to death, and his deadly wound was healed, and all the world wondered after the beast. Henry suggests the wound was the abolishing of pagan idolatry, healed by the introducing of popish idolatry, the same in substance in a new dress. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: They worshipped the dragon which gave power to the beast, and they worshipped the beast, saying, "Who is like unto the beast? who is able to make war with him?" Henry notes the world's darkness and madness: they admired the beast's power, policy, and success, and thought no power could withstand him. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: There was given to him a mouth speaking great things and blasphemies, and power was given to him to continue forty and two months. Henry notes his reign is limited in time, suited to the other prophetical characters of antichrist's reign. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: He opened his mouth in blasphemy against God, to blaspheme his name, and his tabernacle, and them that dwell in heaven. Henry notes the malice levels at God in images of the invisible, at the tabernacle, Christ's human nature dishonoured by transubstantiation, and at glorified saints wronged by being prayed to in place of pagan demons. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: It was given to him to make war with the saints and to overcome them, and power was given him over all kindreds, tongues, and nations. Henry notes the saints on earth are more exposed to his cruelty, and he is sometimes permitted to triumph over and trample upon them, though never to conquer their souls. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: All who dwell on the earth shall worship him, whose names are not written in the book of life of the Lamb slain from the foundation of the world. Henry notes the beast is limited as to persons: only those not written in the Lamb's book are entirely subjected, while Christ's chosen remnant, redeemed and sealed, can never be made to forsake their Saviour. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: If any man have an ear, let him hear. Henry reads this as a demand of attention to the discovery of the church's great sufferings, with assurance that God will turn his hand against her enemies once his refining work on Zion is accomplished. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: He that leads into captivity shall go into captivity; he that kills with the sword must be killed with the sword. Henry notes this is proper exercise for the patience and faith of the saints: patience under the prospect of such sufferings, and faith in the prospect of so glorious a deliverance. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: John saw another beast coming up out of the earth, having two horns like a lamb, but speaking as a dragon. Henry notes the second beast acts under the disguise of religion and charity: the pope shows a lamb's horns, pretending to be Christ's vicar, but his false doctrines and cruel decrees betray him as belonging to the dragon. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: He exercises all the power of the first beast before him, and causes the earth and its dwellers to worship the first beast, whose deadly wound was healed. Henry notes the second beast promotes the same design in substance: drawing men from worshipping the true God to worshipping those who by nature are no gods. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: He does great wonders, so that he makes fire come down from heaven on the earth in the sight of men. Henry notes the lying wonders, pretended miracles by which the unwary are deluded, and observes the papal kingdom has long been supported by pretended miracles. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: He deceives those dwelling on the earth by the miracles he had power to do, saying they should make an image to the beast which had the wound by a sword and did live. Henry notes the deceit aims at worshipping the former beast in a new image or shape, as God sometimes permits enemies, like Egypt's magicians, to do things that seem wonderful. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: He had power to give life to the image of the beast, that the image should both speak, and cause as many as would not worship the image to be killed. Henry counts excommunications and anathemas among the second beast's methods: pretending to cut men off from Christ, they deliver them to the secular power to be put to death. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: He causes all, both small and great, rich and poor, free and bond, to receive a mark in their right hand or in their foreheads. Henry notes the mark, the name, and the number may all signify the same thing: open profession of subjection to the papacy in the forehead, and using all one's interest and power to promote papal authority in the right hand. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: No man might buy or sell, save he that had the mark, or the name of the beast, or the number of his name. Henry notes disfranchisement: allowing none natural, civil, or municipal rights who will not worship the beast, citing pope Martin V's bull prohibiting heretics from dwelling, bargaining, trading, or bearing civil office. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Here is wisdom. Let him that has understanding count the number of the beast: for it is the number of a man, and his number is six hundred threescore and six. Henry confesses uncertainty whether the number is of errors and heresies or of years from rise to fall, but holds this certainty: God has numbered his enemies' days, and they shall be finished, while his kingdom endures for ever. — Matthew Henry
