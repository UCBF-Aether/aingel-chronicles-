Study Notes — Revelation

Chapter 8

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: When the Lamb opened the seventh seal, there was silence in heaven about the space of half an hour. Henry reads this either as the silence of peace, with no complaints rising to the Lord of hosts, or as a silence of expectation, as heaven and earth stood still to see what God was doing, after the pattern of "Be silent, O all flesh, before the Lord." — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: John saw the seven angels who stood before God, and to them were given seven trumpets. Henry notes the angels are the wise and willing instruments of providence, furnished with materials and instructions from God, and that as the church's angels sound the gospel trumpet, heaven's angels sound the trumpet of providence. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Another angel, having a golden censer, came and stood at the altar, and was given much incense to offer with the prayers of all saints on the golden altar before the throne. Henry identifies this angel as the Lord Jesus, high priest of the church, whose fulness of merit is the incense that makes the saints' prayers acceptable. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: The smoke of the incense, with the prayers of the saints, ascended up before God out of the angel's hand. Henry notes all saints are a praying people, times of danger and expectation should be praying times, and no prayer thus recommended was ever denied audience or acceptance. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: The angel took the censer, filled it with fire of the altar, and cast it into the earth, and there were voices, thunderings, lightnings, and an earthquake. Henry notes these commotions were God's answers to the saints' prayers and tokens of his anger, showing he would avenge himself and his people on their enemies. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: The seven angels who had the seven trumpets prepared themselves to sound. Henry notes all things were now prepared: the seals had introduced a new chain of providence, one part linked to another in one wise, well-connected design in God's hand. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: The first angel sounded, and there followed hail and fire mingled with blood, cast upon the earth, and the third part of trees was burnt up, and all green grass was burnt up. Henry notes the terrible storm, whether of heresies or of Gothic war, and its limit: even the severest calamities have bounds set by God. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: The second angel sounded, and as it were a great mountain burning with fire was cast into the sea, and the third part of the sea became blood. Henry notes the mountain as Rome, five times sacked by Goths and Vandals, with a third part of the people destroyed, yet limited to a third, for in judgment God remembers mercy. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: The third part of the creatures in the sea died, and the third part of the ships were destroyed. Henry ties this to the maritime and merchandising cities of the empire, on which the storm fell heavily, still within the limit of a third part. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: The third angel sounded, and a great star fell from heaven, burning as a lamp, and fell upon the third part of the rivers and the fountains of waters. Henry notes the falling star as either a political governor or an ecclesiastical light like Pelagius, corrupted and corrupting the churches. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: The star's name is Wormwood, and the third part of the waters became wormwood, and many men died of the waters because they were made bitter. Henry explains the bitterness: either civil laws, the springs of liberty and safety, were poisoned by arbitrary power, or gospel doctrines, the springs of spiritual life, were embittered by dangerous errors, so men found ruin where they sought refreshment. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: The fourth angel sounded, and the third part of the sun, moon, and stars was smitten, so a third part was darkened, and the day shone not for a third part of it, and the night likewise. Henry reads the darkened luminaries as guides and governors of church or state, and draws the practical lesson that corruptions of doctrine and worship are themselves great judgments and tokens of others. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: John beheld an angel flying through the midst of heaven, saying with a loud voice, "Woe, woe, woe, to the inhabiters of the earth by reason of the other voices of the trumpet of the three angels, which are yet to sound!" Henry notes the three woes show how much the coming calamities would exceed those past, warning that if lesser judgments do not take effect, greater must be expected. — Matthew Henry
