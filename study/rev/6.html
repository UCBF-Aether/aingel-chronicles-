Study Notes — Revelation

Chapter 6

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The Lamb opened the first seal, and one of the living creatures called with a voice like thunder, "Come and see." Henry notes Christ now enters on opening and accomplishing God's purposes, with a minister of the church summoning John to observe what appears. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: John saw a white horse, whose rider had a bow, received a crown, and went forth conquering and to conquer. Henry notes the white horse is the pure gospel riding swiftly, the bow's sharp arrows are convictions reaching at a distance, the crown is the earnest of victory, and Christ keeps conquering in every age, in his people's sanctification and over the world. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: When the Lamb opened the second seal, the second living creature said, "Come and see." Henry groups the next three seals as desolating judgments avenging the covenant on those who refuse or abuse the gospel. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Another horse, red, went out, and its rider was given power to take peace from the earth, so that men should kill one another, with a great sword given to him. Henry notes war takes away peace, one of earth's greatest blessings, and sets men who should love and help one another upon killing one another. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: When the third seal was opened, the third living creature said, "Come and see," and John saw a black horse, whose rider had a pair of balances in his hand. Henry reads the black horse as famine, with men eating bread by weight, as threatened in Leviticus. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: A voice in the midst of the living creatures said, "A measure of wheat for a penny, and three measures of barley for a penny; and see thou hurt not the oil and the wine." Henry explains the measure was a single quart and the penny a large sum, so this was famine, falling hardest on the poor, while the rich's dainties were spared, though dainties cannot replace the staff of life. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: When the fourth seal was opened, the fourth living creature said, "Come and see." Henry continues the chain of judgments: war draws famine after it, and famine draws pestilence, since God is never at a loss for ways to punish a wicked people. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: John saw a pale horse, whose rider's name was Death, and Hell followed with him, given power over a fourth part of the earth to kill with sword, hunger, death, and the beasts of the earth. Henry calls Death the king of terrors, pestilence reigning over a nation, with hell, eternal misery, following impenitent death, and notes the beasts prey on the remnant left in a wasted land. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: At the fifth seal John saw under the altar the souls of those slain for the word of God and for the testimony they held. Henry notes persecutors can only kill the body, while the souls live near Christ in heaven, received not by their own death but by the Lamb's sacrifice, for the noblest cause: faith in God's word and confession of it. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: They cried with a loud voice, "How long, O Lord, holy and true, dost thou not judge and avenge our blood on them that dwell on the earth?" Henry notes even perfected spirits retain a proper resentment of wrong, yet commit vengeance to God, desiring judgment for God's honour and the terror of others rather than avenging themselves. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: White robes were given to each of them, and they were told to rest yet for a little season, until their fellowservants and brethren, who should be killed as they were, should be fulfilled. Henry notes the robes are victory and honour, an abundant recompense, and that when the appointed number of martyrs is complete, God will take just revenge on persecutors and give the troubled rest. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: At the sixth seal there was a great earthquake, the sun became black as sackcloth of hair, and the moon became as blood. Henry refers this to the revolutions at Constantine's fall of paganism, or, with great probability, to Jerusalem's destruction as an emblem of the general judgment, with foundations shaken and rulers fallen. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: The stars of heaven fell to the earth, as a fig tree casts her untimely figs when shaken by a mighty wind. Henry reads the falling stars as men of note and influence in lower spheres, in a general desolation. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: The heaven departed as a scroll when it is rolled together, and every mountain and island was moved out of its place. Henry takes this as the perishing of the ecclesiastical state and a judgment astonishing all nations, high and seemingly secure alike. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: The kings of the earth, great men, rich men, chief captains, mighty men, bondmen, and freemen hid themselves in the dens and rocks of the mountains. Henry notes no authority, grandeur, riches, valour, or strength could support men then, and even slaves with nothing to lose were amazed. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: They said to the mountains and rocks, "Fall on us, and hide us from the face of him that sitteth on the throne, and from the wrath of the Lamb." Henry notes their terror made them wish for non-being, and that the wrath of the Lamb is exceedingly dreadful, since if the Redeemer who appeases God's wrath is himself the enemy, no friend remains to plead. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: For the great day of his wrath is come, and who shall be able to stand? Henry concludes that as men have their day of opportunity and seasons of grace, so God has his day of righteous wrath, when the most stout-hearted sinners cannot stand before him. — Matthew Henry
