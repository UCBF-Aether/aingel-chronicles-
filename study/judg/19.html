Study Notes — Judges

Chapter 19

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: In those days, when there was no king in Israel, a Levite sojourning on the side of Mount Ephraim took a concubine out of Bethlehemjudah, and JFB notes this episode belongs to the same early period of anarchy and disorder, and that a concubine wife, though a secondary relationship, possessed the true essence of marriage and was lawful. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: His concubine went away from him to her father's house at Bethlehemjudah and was there four whole months, and JFB notes other versions, with Josephus, represent the flight as disgust at frequent brawls, rather than the cause assigned in the translation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Her husband arose and went after her to speak friendly to her, in Hebrew to speak to her heart, and bring her again, and JFB notes the father rejoiced to meet him, hoping for complete reconciliation, and the Levite yielded to hospitable importunities and prolonged his stay for days. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Her father retained him, and they ate and drank and lodged three days, the reconciliation sealed by days of hospitality. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: On the fourth day they arose early, and the damsel's father said to his son-in-law, Comfort thine heart with a morsel of bread, and afterward go your way, hospitality again delaying departure. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: They sat down, ate and drank together, and the father said, Be content and tarry all night, so that the morning's departure became another night's stay. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: When the man rose to depart, his father-in-law urged him again to tarry, and he lodged there again that night, the pattern of detention repeating. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: On the fifth day he arose early to depart, but the father said, Comfort thine heart, and tarry until afternoon, and JFB notes he tarried with reluctance until the decline of day, since Eastern breakfast, taken late, was purposely protracted to argue for a further stay. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: The man rose to depart, but the father said the day drew toward evening and tarry all night, and JFB notes the pitching time of day was when travellers usually halted, so it was too late to begin a journey, though duty perhaps obliged the Levite to delay no further. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: The man would not tarry that night, but departed with his concubine, servant, and two asses, and came over against Jebus, which is Jerusalem, and JFB notes the Levite resisted his servant's advice to enter Jebus, still in old inhabitants' hands, preferring to press on to Gibeah, occupied by Israelites, though the event showed it would have been better to trust aliens than his own countrymen. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: When they were by Jebus the day was far spent, and the servant said, Let us turn into this city of the Jebusites and lodge in it, proposing the nearer shelter. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: His master answered they would not turn aside to a city of strangers not of Israel, but pass over to Gibeah, choosing Israelite lodging over alien safety. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: He said they would lodge in Gibeah or Ramah, Gibeah five miles northeast of Jerusalem and Ramah four or five miles north, as JFB locates them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: They passed on, and the sun went down upon them when they were by Gibeah, which belongs to Benjamin, so that night found them at the very place the Levite had chosen. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: They turned aside to lodge in Gibeah, and when he went in he sat down in a street of the city, and no man took them into his house, and JFB notes Palestine towns then had no inns, and travellers without introduction spread bedding in the streets, while in Gibeah even the usual urgent invitation failed, owing to the people's bad character. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: An old man came from his work in the field at evening, himself of Mount Ephraim and sojourning in Gibeah, and JFB notes his hospitality was perhaps quickened by learning the stranger's occupation and his return to duties at Shiloh. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: The old man saw the wayfaring man in the street and asked whence he came and whither he went, breaking the city's silence with a question. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: The Levite answered that they were passing from Bethlehemjudah toward Mount Ephraim, whence he was, and had been to Bethlehemjudah, and were going to the house of the Lord, but no man received them into his house. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: He added that they had straw and provender for their asses and bread and wine for himself, the maid, and the young man, and lacked nothing, and JFB notes Eastern travellers always carried provisions, since even khans afforded only rest and shelter, and used chopped straw with barley or beans for provender. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: The old man said, Peace be with thee, let all thy wants lie upon me, only lodge not in the street, and JFB notes his earnest dissuasive arose from acquaintance with the infamous practices of the place. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: He brought them into his house, gave provender to the asses, washed their feet, and they ate and drank, so that the Levite's own provisions were kept for emergencies while the old man's hospitality supplied the night. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: As they were making their hearts merry, certain sons of Belial beset the house and beat at the door, demanding the man be brought out that they might know him, and JFB notes the narrative discloses a morality that would seem incredible if not on sacred testimony, and that both men ought to have protected the women at the expense of their lives, though the guilt is not fastened on Gibeah's general population. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: The old man went out and pleaded, Nay, my brethren, do not so wickedly, seeing this man is come into mine house, do not this folly, appealing to hospitality against the demand. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: He offered his virgin daughter and the man's concubine, saying, humble them and do with them what seemeth good, but unto this man do not so vile a thing, a proposal JFB's note on the whole outrage leaves standing as part of the horror, not as a justification. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: But the men would not hearken, so the Levite took his concubine and brought her out to them, and they knew her and abused her all night until morning, and let her go at daybreak. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: Then the woman came in the dawning of the day and fell down at the door of the man's house where her lord was, till it was light, and died there at the threshold. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Her lord rose in the morning, opened the doors, and went out to go his way, and behold the woman was fallen at the door with her hands upon the threshold. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: He said, Up, and let us be going, but none answered, so he took her up upon the ass and rose to his place, carrying the dead woman home in silence. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: When he came to his house he took a knife, laid hold on his concubine, and divided her into twelve pieces with her bones, and sent her into all the coasts of Israel, and JFB notes the want of regular government warranted this extraordinary step, and no method could more certainly rouse universal horror and indignation. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: He sent the pieces throughout Israel, and all who saw said there had been no such deed since Israel came up from Egypt, bidding all Israel consider, take advice, and speak, so that the summons itself became the nation's call to judgment. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
