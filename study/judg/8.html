Study Notes — Judges

Chapter 8

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The men of Ephraim complained that Gideon had not called them to fight Midian, and JFB notes their wounded pride and old jealous rivalry with Gideon's tribe, though the complaint was groundless since Gideon had acted by divine direction. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Gideon answered mildly, asking what he had done in comparison with Ephraim, and JFB calls this a truly modest answer breathing the spirit of a great and good man, which threw oil on troubled waters by ascribing to his brethren more merit than belonged to them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: He reminded them that God had delivered Oreb and Zeeb into their hands, and then their anger abated, so that the pacification JFB describes in the preceding verse was completed by crediting Ephraim with the princes' capture. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: Gideon came to Jordan and passed over, faint yet pursuing, and JFB stresses he was much exhausted but eager to continue the pursuit until the victory was consummated. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: He asked Succoth, a place of tents in Gad's territory, for loaves for his faint pursuing people, and JFB notes that, being engaged in Israel's common cause, he had a right to expect support from his countrymen everywhere. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: The princes of Succoth refused, asking whether Zebah's and Zalmunna's hands were already in his, and JFB calls this insolent and time-serving, since they dreaded the vengeance of the neighbouring Midianite chiefs. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Gideon threatened to tear their flesh with thorns of the wilderness and briers, a cruel torture JFB describes as thorns pressed on naked bodies by sledges or heavy implements dragged over them. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: He went up to Penuel, a neighbouring city in Gad near the Jabbok named by Jacob, and spoke to them likewise, receiving the same refusal. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Gideon promised that when he returned in peace he would break down Penuel's tower, postponing merited vengeance until his return, and JFB notes his confident anticipation of triumph and the proud boast the threat probably answered. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Zebah and Zalmunna were in Karkor with about fifteen thousand men, all that remained of the eastern host, and JFB notes the wreck of the Midianite army had halted there on Gad's eastern confines. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Gideon went up by the way of tent-dwellers on the east and came upon them unexpectedly while they rested secure among their nomadic tribes, tracking them across Gilead to the northeast of the Jabbok. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: When the two kings fled, Gideon pursued and took them, and JFB calls this a third conflict in which the conquest of the Midianite horde was completed by surprise. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Gideon returned from the battle by the heights of Heres, the sun-hills, which the translation renders as returning before the sun was up, taking a nearer route back toward Succoth. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: He caught a young man of Succoth who wrote down the names of the city's seventy-seven princes and elders, the very men from whom he had received such inhospitable treatment. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Gideon showed the men of Succoth their captured kings and taunted them with the upbraiding they had given him, introducing the punishment JFB describes in the following verse. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: He took the elders of the city and with thorns and briers taught the men of Succoth, and JFB notes that refusing refreshment to soldiers was a public crime as well as inhumanity, punished horribly with the region's great thorn bushes. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Gideon beat down the tower of Penuel and slew the men of the city, executing the threat he had made on his way out and completing the vengeance postponed in verse 9. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: He asked Zebah and Zalmunna what manner of men they had slain at Tabor, and JFB notes this was one of countless atrocities of their seven lawless years, raised only now when their fate was to be determined, while their victims resembled children of a king in beauty and majesty. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Gideon answered that they were his brethren, the sons of his mother, and JFB explains that son of my mother implies a closeness and warmth never awakened by the looser term brother. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: He told Jether his firstborn to rise and slay them, but the youth feared because he was still young, and JFB notes Gideon intended to honour his son by the task, since the executioner was chosen of rank proportioned to the condemned, and Gideon then did the deed himself. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: The kings themselves told Gideon to rise and fall upon them, for as the man is so is his strength, and Gideon arose, slew them, and took the ornaments from their camels' necks, finishing what his son's fear had left to him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: The men of Israel asked Gideon and his house to rule over them because he had delivered them, and Gideon refused, saying the Lord shall rule over you, and JFB praises his refusal to sacrifice the theocracy to personal and family ambition. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: Gideon repeated that neither he nor his son would rule, but the Lord shall rule over them, and JFB notes he would willingly act as judge, but the Lord alone was King of Israel. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Gideon asked only for the earrings of the prey, and JFB notes the contribution of a single earring each from soldiers laden with Arabian gold and pearl, estimated at a great quantity of gold, given liberally. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: The men answered willingly, spread a garment, and cast in every man the earrings of his prey, so that the request was met with the same liberality JFB notes in the offering itself. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: The gold earrings weighed seventeen hundred shekels, besides the crescent ornaments, collars, and purple raiment of the Midianite kings, and JFB explains the ornaments as gold plates on camels' necks and royal purple housings. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Gideon made an ephod of the gold and set it up in Ophrah, and Israel went whoring after it, and JFB argues Gideon intended it only as a civil magistrate's robe, but it became by perversion a snare to him and his house. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: Thus Midian was subdued before Israel and the country was quiet forty years in Gideon's days, and JFB calls this invasion as alarming as the Huns' irruption into Europe, a deliverance remembered for centuries. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

No note for verse(s) 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
