Study Notes — Judges

Chapter 5

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Deborah and Barak sang on the day of victory, and Henry notes that no time should be lost in returning thanks, since praises are most acceptable when they flow from a full heart. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: The song begins with praise for the avenging of Israel and for a people who willingly offered themselves, so that both God's vengeance and human willingness are honoured together. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Kings and princes are summoned to hear, for Deborah will sing to the Lord God of Israel, making the victory a public testimony rather than a private boast. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: The song recalls the Lord marching from Seir and Edom, when the earth trembled and the heavens dropped water, so that the recent battle is set within God's ancient, awesome marches. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: The mountains melted before the Lord, the God of Israel, and Henry insists that whatever Deborah, Barak, or the army had done, the Lord must have all the praise, since will, power, and success were all from him. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: In the days of Shamgar and Jael the highways were unoccupied and travellers took byways, a picture of how thoroughly Jabin's tyranny had broken ordinary life and travel. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: The villages ceased until Deborah arose, a mother in Israel, and Henry notes she was a mother by diligently promoting the salvation of souls, not merely by holding office. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: Israel chose new gods with new names, and war came to the gates, while among forty thousand there was neither shield nor spear, showing that idolatry had disarmed the nation it promised to enrich. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Deborah's heart turns to the governors who offered themselves willingly, and she calls on all who shared the deliverance to bless the Lord for it. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Riders on white asses, judges, and travellers on the way are all called to speak of the victory, since public peace, especially for villagers, is itself a gift to be praised. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: By the places of drawing water they rehearse the Lord's righteous acts toward his villages, and the people go down to the gates, so that the song becomes the nation's memory kept alive at its wells. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Deborah calls on her own soul to awake and on Barak to lead captivity captive, and Henry notes that one who would set others' hearts on fire must burn himself, and that praising God is work to awake to. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: A remnant of the people gained dominion over the nobles, and the Lord gained dominion for Deborah over the mighty, reversing the hierarchy that oppression had imposed. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Ephraim, Benjamin, Machir, and Zebulun are named among those who came down to the battle, each tribe honoured by name for taking its part. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: The princes of Issachar stood with Deborah and Barak, but Reuben's divisions brought great searchings of heart, honour and hesitation set side by side in the same roll. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: Deborah asks why Reuben abode among the sheepfolds to hear the bleatings of the flocks, since the love of ease and of worldly business kept many from duty when difficulty and danger appeared. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Gilead stayed beyond Jordan, Dan remained in ships, and Asher continued on the seashore, and Henry groups them with Reuben as those kept away by fear of trouble and undue affection for their own advantage. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Zebulun and Naphtali, by contrast, jeoparded their lives on the high places of the field, and are praised precisely for risking what the others guarded. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: The kings of Canaan fought at Taanach by the waters of Megiddo but took no gain of money, so that the enemy's hired warfare profited nothing against the Lord. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: The stars in their courses fought against Sisera, and Henry reads this as the whole creation at war with those to whom God is an enemy. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: The river Kishon swept the enemy away, that ancient river, and Henry explains that a usually shallow stream, swollen by great rain, drowned those who tried to cross it. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: The horsehoofs were broken by the pransings of the mighty, a vivid close-up of chariot horses floundering in the very flood that undid their army. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: The angel's curse falls on Meroz, because its people came not to the help of the Lord, and Henry warns that neutrality in the contest between the Lord and his enemies falls under the curse against workers of iniquity. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Jael is blessed above women in the tent, and Henry notes that those in a low and narrow sphere who serve God with the powers given them shall not lose their reward. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: Sisera asked water and she gave milk in a lordly dish, hospitality turned, in the song's telling, into the prelude of judgment. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: She put her hand to the nail and her right hand to the workman's hammer, and smote Sisera through the head, the song lingering on the deed that ended the oppressor. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: At her feet he bowed, fell, and lay dead, the conqueror brought low in the tent of a woman he had thought a refuge. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: Sisera's mother looked through the lattice and cried over his delayed chariot, expecting triumph and dreading delay in the same breath. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Her wise ladies answered, and she answered herself, rehearsing the spoil she expected, and Henry calls this a picture of an ungodly and sensual heart doting on vain glory. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: The imagined spoil is counted out: a damsel or two for each man, dyed needlework for Sisera, embroidered spoil for his neck, the very finery that reveals what his house loved. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: The song ends by praying that all the Lord's enemies perish as Sisera did, while those who love him shine as the sun in its strength, and then the land had rest forty years. — Matthew Henry
