Study Notes — Judges

Chapter 1

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: After Joshua's death the tribes did not presume on their own counsel but inquired of the Lord who should lead the renewed war, showing that direction must be sought from God when a former leader is gone. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: God appointed Judah to go first, and Henry notes the pattern that the tribe first in dignity must be first in duty, with success promised yet still requiring Judah's own action. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Judah, the strongest tribe, begged help from Simeon, the least, and Henry draws the lesson that the strong should desire the help of the weak and that mutual aid in a common cause may hope for God's help to both. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: The Lord delivered the Canaanites and Perizzites into Judah's hand at Bezek, where ten thousand were slain, showing that the promised success followed obedience in going up. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Adoni-bezek, the local king, was found at Bezek and fought there, so that the campaign struck at the enemy's leadership as well as his army. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Adoni-bezek fled, was caught, and had his thumbs and great toes cut off, a severe but deliberate act that disabled a warlord who had lived by conquest. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Adoni-bezek himself confessed the justice of it, having done the same to seventy kings, and Henry notes how God in providence often makes the punishment answer the sin. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: Judah then fought against Jerusalem, took it, struck it with the sword and set it on fire, pressing the conquest from the field into the enemy's strong city. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: Judah went on against the Canaanites of the mountains, the south, and the lowland, carrying the war into every kind of country rather than settling for one victory. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: At Hebron Judah slew Sheshai, Ahiman, and Talmai, striking down the famed Anakim whose size had once terrified Israel. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: From Hebron Judah advanced against Debir, formerly Kirjath-sepher, showing the campaign moving steadily from one fortified city to the next. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Caleb offered his daughter Achsah to whoever took Kirjath-sepher, using honour and family reward to stir courage for a hard city. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Othniel, Caleb's younger kinsman, took the city and married Achsah, and Henry's account presents him as a man already rising toward the judgeship he would later hold. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Achsah urged Othniel to ask for a field, and herself alighted from her ass to press her father, showing a household that sought provision boldly rather than passively. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Caleb gave her the upper and lower springs as well as the land, so that the gift was made fruitful with water, the very thing that makes a southern holding liveable. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: The Kenites, a quiet and contented people who molested none, were allowed to settle where they pleased among Judah, and Henry notes that the meek are left unmolested and inherit the earth. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Judah and Simeon together smote Zephath and utterly destroyed it, renaming it Hormah, a joint victory that repaid Simeon's help in kind. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: Judah also took Gaza, Askelon, and Ekron with their coasts, extending the conquest along the Philistine plain. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: The Lord was with Judah in the mountains, yet they could not drive out the valley dwellers because of their iron chariots, and Henry blames not the chariots but fear prevailing against faith. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Hebron was given to Caleb, who drove out the three sons of Anak, fulfilling the old promise to the faithful spy. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Benjamin failed to drive the Jebusites from Jerusalem, so that enemy and Israelite dwelt together there, the first of many half-conquests that stored up later trouble. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: The house of Joseph went up against Bethel with the Lord with them, a venture that began well where other tribes had already failed. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: Joseph's spies watched a man coming out of Bethel, using careful reconnaissance rather than a blind assault. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: The spies promised the man mercy if he showed them the entrance, trading life for the intelligence that would open the city. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: The man showed the entrance, the city was smitten, and he and his family were spared, so that mercy and judgment fell in the same hour. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: The spared man went to the Hittites and built Luz, carrying the old city's name into a new place, a small picture of how incomplete victories scatter rather than end the enemy. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: Manasseh failed to take Beth-shean and its towns, and the Canaanites continued to dwell in the land, so that a strong tribe left its own inheritance half-won. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: When Israel grew strong they put the Canaanites to tribute instead of driving them out, and Henry names covetousness alongside sloth and cowardice: they kept the enemy for profit. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Ephraim likewise failed to drive out Gezer's Canaanites, who dwelt among them, repeating the same compromise in Joseph's other half-tribe. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: Zebulun failed to drive out Kitron and Nahalol, settling instead for tribute from the people they were commanded to dispossess. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: Asher failed worst of all, leaving Acco, Sidon, and several other cities untaken, and Henry says Asher truckled to the Canaanites more than any tribe. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: Asher therefore dwelt among the Canaanites instead of ruling over them, the guest becoming the tolerated neighbour of the people he should have removed. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: Naphtali failed at Beth-shemesh and Beth-anath, and again the Canaanites became tributaries rather than exiles, a half-measure Henry traces to unbelief. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: The Amorites forced Dan into the mountains and would not let them come down to the valley, so that Dan was straitened and kept out of the full possession of its lot. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: The Amorites held their ground in several towns until the house of Joseph prevailed and put them to tribute, a late and partial recovery of what should have been a complete conquest. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: The Amorite border ran from the ascent of Akrabbim upward, marking on the map how much ground Israel had left unconquered through carelessness of duty and interest. — Matthew Henry
