Study Notes — Judges

Chapter 11

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Jephthah the Gileadite was a mighty man of valour, son of a harlot, or concubine or foreigner in an inferior marriage, and JFB notes that whatever dishonour attached to his birth, his high and energetic character made him early a person of note, his father belonging to Manasseh. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Gilead's wife's sons thrust Jephthah out, saying he should not inherit, since as son of a secondary wife he had no share while legitimate children lived, and JFB notes the brothers' rude violence suggests secret ill-will. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Jephthah fled to the land of Tob north of Gilead, where vain, idle and desperate men gathered to him and went out with him, leading a freebooting life against Ammonites and neighbours, like David when driven from Saul's court. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: In process of time the Ammonites made war against Israel, and JFB notes the historian here resumes the thread from chapter 10, the Ammonites having invaded so that active hostilities were inevitable. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: The elders of Gilead went to fetch Jephthah from Tob, since all eyes were directed to him as the only man with the qualities needed to preserve the country, and a deputation was sent from the camp at Mizpeh. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: They asked him to come and be their captain to fight Ammon, the very office his exile had kept from him now offered by necessity. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: Jephthah answered, Did ye not hate me and expel me, and why come ye now in distress, giving them at first a haughty and cold reception, and JFB notes he was now in circumstances to make his own terms, binding them to invest him with unlimited authority. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: The elders replied that they therefore turned again to him, that he might go with them, fight Ammon, and be head over all Gilead, conceding the terms his question had forced. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Jephthah asked whether, if he returned and the Lord delivered Ammon, he would indeed be their head, making the bargain explicit before he risked his life. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: The elders swore, The Lord be witness between us that we will do according to thy words, and JFB notes the solemn oath was afterwards ratified in a general assembly at Mizpeh, where Jephthah uttered all his words before the Lord, his inauguration as judge solemnised by prayer. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: Jephthah went with the elders, and the people made him head and captain, and he uttered all his words before the Lord in Mizpeh, completing the covenant the deputation had sworn in Tob. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Jephthah sent messengers to Ammon's king asking what quarrel he had with Israel that he came to fight their land, and JFB calls this first judicial act a credit to his prudence, moderation, justice, and humanity, since the bravest officers are averse to war and he sought to show hostilities were forced upon him. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Ammon's king claimed Israel had taken his land from Arnon to Jabbok and Jordan when they came from Egypt, and JFB summarises Jephthah's unanswerable reply in three arguments: the land was not Ammon's when Israel took it from the Amorites, three hundred years of possession gave prescriptive right, and Israel held it by the Lord's grant as Ammon held its own by Chemosh's. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: Jephthah sent messengers again, opening his full answer, and the embassy itself shows a leader who would argue before he fought. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: Thus saith Jephthah, Israel took not the land of Moab nor the land of Ammon, denying the very premise of the Ammonite claim. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: When Israel came from Egypt they walked through the wilderness to the Red Sea and came to Kadesh, a journey of dependence, not of conquest against Ammon. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Israel sent to Edom's and Moab's kings asking passage, and both refused, so Israel abode in Kadesh, showing they had sought permission rather than seizing territory. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: They then went through the wilderness, compassed Edom and Moab, came by the east side, pitched on the other side of Arnon, and came not within Moab's border, a route that deliberately avoided Ammonite and Moabite land. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: Israel sent to Sihon king of the Amorites at Heshbon, asking only to pass through his land to their own place, a request for transit, not for territory. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: Sihon trusted Israel not, gathered his people, pitched at Jahaz, and fought Israel, so that the war with the Amorites was begun by Sihon's refusal, not Israel's aggression. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

21 COMMENTARY: The Lord delivered Sihon and all his people into Israel's hand, and Israel possessed all the Amorites' land, the conquest JFB cites as the first of Jephthah's three arguments. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: Israel possessed all the Amorites' coasts from Arnon to Jabbok and from the wilderness to Jordan, defining exactly the land Ammon now claimed. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

23 COMMENTARY: The Lord God of Israel dispossessed the Amorites before his people, Jephthah argued, and shall Ammon now possess it, pressing the divine grant as the ground of Israel's title. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

24 COMMENTARY: Wilt thou not possess what Chemosh thy god gives thee, Jephthah asked, so likewise we will possess what the Lord our God has dispossessed before us, turning Ammon's own principle of divine grant against its claim. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

25 COMMENTARY: He asked whether Ammon's king was any better than Balak son of Zippor, who never strove or fought against Israel over this land, so that even Moab's king had not pressed the claim Ammon now revived. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: While Israel dwelt in Heshbon, Aroer, and their towns for three hundred years, Ammon never recovered the land, and JFB cites this lapse of undisputed possession as Jephthah's second, prescriptive argument. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

27 COMMENTARY: Jephthah closed by declaring he had not sinned against Ammon, Ammon did him wrong by warring, and the Lord the Judge would judge that day between Israel and Ammon, the solemn appeal to God JFB notes as the conclusion of this admirable diplomatic statement. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

28 COMMENTARY: But Ammon's king hearkened not to Jephthah's words, and JFB notes the remonstrances were disregarded, war became inevitable, and preparations were made for determined resistance. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

29 COMMENTARY: The Spirit of the Lord came upon Jephthah, and he passed over Gilead and Manasseh to levy troops, then returned to Mizpeh and marched against Ammon, and JFB notes the calm wisdom and energy displayed were evidence he was qualified by higher resources than his own. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

30 COMMENTARY: Jephthah vowed that if the Lord delivered Ammon, whatsoever came out of his house to meet him would be the Lord's, and he would offer it as a burnt offering, a vow JFB sets in the ancient custom of generals promising a costly oblation on the eve of battle, though difficult to bring within the law's legitimate range. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

31 COMMENTARY: The vow pointed not to an animal, which might be an unclean dog, but to a person, and JFB notes Jephthah, bred beyond Jordan and near heathen sacrifice, may have imagined a human immolation acceptable, while the alternative reading offers dedication to sanctuary service instead of burnt offering. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

32 COMMENTARY: Jephthah passed over to Ammon, fought, and the Lord delivered them into his hand, meeting them at Aroer on the Arnon, and JFB notes the decisive victory and pursuit over about sixty miles to Abel, the plain of vineyards. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

33 COMMENTARY: He smote them from Aroer to Minnith, twenty cities, and to Abel, with a very great slaughter, and Ammon was subdued before Israel, completing the victory the vow had preceded. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

34 COMMENTARY: Jephthah came to Mizpeh to his house, and his daughter came out to meet him with timbrels and dances, leading the female band that hailed returning victors, and JFB notes the vow was full in his mind and had not been communicated, or precautions would have been taken, while her grief indicates her life was to be forfeited. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

No note for verse(s) 35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
