Study Notes — Judges

Chapter 16

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Samson went to Gaza, saw a harlot, and went in unto her, and Henry calls this a profanation of his honour as Israelite and Nazarite, noting his sin began in his eye, with which he should have made a covenant, and lust conceived brought forth sin. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: The Gazites were told Samson had come, compassed him in, and laid wait all night in the gate, saying they would kill him in the morning, and Henry notes the gates were shut and guards set in quiet, so that Samson might suspect no danger, as sensual sleepers are surrounded by spiritual enemies. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Samson lay till midnight, arose, and took the city gate's doors, posts, and bar upon his shoulders to a hill before Hebron, and Henry reads this as disdain for their attempt to secure him, a proof of God-given strength, and a type of Christ carrying away the gates of the grave. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Afterwards he loved a woman in the valley of Sorek named Delilah, and Henry notes that though more than once brought into mischief by love of women, he would not take warning, but was again taken in the same snare, and this third time paid for all. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: The Philistine lords offered Delilah eleven hundred pieces of silver each if she would entice Samson and learn where his strength lay and how he might be bound to be afflicted, and Henry notes they bid high, over a thousand pounds sterling in all, and that the love of money is the root of this horrid wickedness. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Delilah asked Samson to tell her where his great strength lay and wherewith he might be bound to afflict him, pretending, Henry notes, only to satisfy curiosity, as though it were impossible he should be bound except by her charms. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Samson answered that if bound with seven green withs never dried he would be weak as another man, the first of his put-offs to keep his counsel. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: The lords brought seven green withs, and Delilah bound him with them, trying the first false secret in earnest. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: With men lying in wait in the chamber, Delilah cried, The Philistines be upon thee, Samson, and he broke the withs as a thread of tow broken when it touches fire, so his strength was not known. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Delilah complained he had mocked her and told lies, and pressed him again to tell wherewith he might be bound, continuing the importunity Henry calls restless and unreasonable. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: Samson answered that if bound fast with new ropes never occupied he would be weak as another man, his second evasion. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Delilah took new ropes and bound him, again crying that the Philistines were upon him, with liers in wait in the chamber, and he broke them from his arms like a thread. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Delilah again said he had mocked her and told lies, and demanded wherewith he might be bound, and Samson answered that if she wove the seven locks of his head with the web he would be weakened, an answer Henry notes came nearer the matter than any before. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: She fastened his locks with the pin, cried that the Philistines were upon him, and he awoke and went away with the pin of the beam and the web, showing by the very strength of his hair that the secret lay there. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Delilah then teased him, asking how he could say he loved her when his heart was not with her, since he had mocked her three times and not told his strength, and Henry notes the expostulation rests on a true principle, that those have our love who have our hearts, while her use of it was falsehood and flattery. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: She pressed him daily with her words and urged him until his soul was vexed unto death, and Henry asks why he did not leave her, answering that he was captivated and intoxicated by lust falsely called love. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: He told her all his heart: no razor had come upon his head, for he was a Nazarite from his mother's womb, and if shaven his strength would go and he would be like any other man, and Henry explains the hair was the badge of consecration and pledge of strength, since his strength was by promise, not by nature. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: When Delilah saw he had told her all his heart, she sent for the Philistine lords, who came up with the money in their hands, and Henry notes her care to secure the wages of unrighteousness before delivering him, as one of the bravest men alive was sold like a sheep for slaughter. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: She made him sleep upon her knees and had a man shave off the seven locks of his head, and she began to afflict him, and his strength went from him, and Henry notes Satan ruins men by rocking them asleep and flattering them into safety, then robbing them of strength and honour. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: She cried, The Philistines be upon thee, Samson, and he awoke saying he would go out as at other times and shake himself, but he wist not that the Lord was departed from him, and Henry warns that many have lost God's favourable presence and are not aware of it, imputing their languishing to anything but the right cause. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: The Philistines took him, put out his eyes, brought him down to Gaza, bound him with brass fetters, and made him grind in the prison, and Henry notes his eyes were the inlets of his sin, and now his punishment began there, while Gaza, where he had shown strength, now saw him in weakness and jest. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: However, the hair of his head began to grow again after he was shaven, and Henry reads this as the return of the sign of his Nazariteship and a token that God accepted him again as a Nazarite upon his repentance, without the restoration ceremonies he had no opportunity to perform. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: The Philistine lords gathered to offer a great sacrifice to Dagon and rejoice, saying their god had delivered Samson into their hand, and Henry calls Dagon a dunghill-deity fit only to patronise the villainy by which Samson had actually been taken. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: When the people saw Samson they praised their god, saying he had delivered their enemy, the destroyer of their country who slew many of them, and Henry notes they ascribed to Dagon what Delilah's paid treachery had done. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: When their hearts were merry they called for Samson to make them sport, and they set him between the pillars, and Henry notes that having sacrificed, eaten, and drunk, they rose up to play, and Samson must be the fool in the play, laughed at as, being blind, he stumbled. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: Samson asked the lad who held his hand to let him feel the pillars on which the house stood, that he might lean upon them, feeling for the supports of the theatre in which he was exhibited. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: The house was full of men and women, all the Philistine lords were there, and about three thousand on the roof beheld while Samson made sport, so that the whole Philistine leadership was gathered in one place. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: Samson called on the Lord to remember him and strengthen him only this once, that he might be avenged of the Philistines for his two eyes, a prayer Henry sets within his repentance, since God was reconciled to him as the growing hair had already shown. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: He took hold of the two middle pillars on which the house stood, one with his right hand and the other with his left, the blind champion laying hold of the building's strength. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: Samson said, Let me die with the Philistines, and bowed himself with all his might, and the house fell upon the lords and all the people in it, so that the dead he slew at his death were more than those he slew in his life, and Henry calls this his setting beam, a type of Christ conquering by death. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: His brethren and all his father's house came down, took him, brought him up, and buried him between Zorah and Eshtaol in Manoah's buryingplace, and he had judged Israel twenty years, his evening, Henry notes, not commending his day, yet his death lessening, though not rolling away, its reproach. — Matthew Henry
