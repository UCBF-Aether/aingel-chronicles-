Study Notes — Judges

Chapter 17

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: There was a man of Mount Ephraim named Micah, and JFB notes this and the following narratives form an appendix to Judges, from a period of great disorder and corruption, connected with chapter 1, in which Micah founded a miniature Shiloh of his own, sincere in intending to honour God yet blended with ignorance and delusion. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Micah told his mother the eleven hundred shekels taken from her, about which she had cursed, were with him, for he had taken them, and she blessed him, so that theft, curse, and blessing meet in a single household exchange. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: When he restored the silver, his mother said she had wholly dedicated it to the Lord for her son to make a graven image and a molten image, and JFB explains the graven image as carved wood or stone plated with silver, and the molten as solid metal cast in a mould, the two hundred shekels given to the founder sufficing for small figures with their pedestals. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: He restored the silver to his mother, who gave two hundred shekels to the founder, who made a graven and a molten image, which were placed in Micah's house, so that the restored theft became the material of idolatry. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Micah had a house of gods, made an ephod and teraphim, and consecrated one of his sons as priest, and JFB notes the house was a domestic chapel, the teraphim household tutelary gods, and the assumption of priesthood outside Aaron's family a direct violation of divine law. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: In those days there was no king in Israel, and every man did that which was right in his own eyes, and JFB notes that for want of settled government no one called Micah to account, and no punishment followed any crime. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: There was a young man out of Bethlehem-judah, of the family of Judah, who was a Levite and sojourned there, and JFB notes Bethlehem-judah is distinguished from Bethlehem in Zebulun, and the Levite belonged to Judah by his mother's side, which accounts for his being there rather than in a Levitical city. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: The man departed from Bethlehem-judah to sojourn where he could find a place, and came to Micah's house in Mount Ephraim, and JFB notes his wandering, despite the provision secured for Levites, showed a roving disposition, and Micah, learning what he was, engaged his permanent services. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Micah asked the Levite whence he came, and he answered he was a Levite of Bethlehem-judah going to sojourn where he could find a place, the wandering priest meeting the private chapel-keeper. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: Micah said, Dwell with me, and be unto me a father and a priest, offering ten shekels of silver yearly, a suit of apparel, and victuals, and JFB notes father means spiritual father to conduct the establishment's services, and the apparel included vestments for priestly functions. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: The Levite was content to dwell with Micah, and the young man became to him as one of his sons, replacing the son Micah had first consecrated. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Micah consecrated the Levite, filling his hand, and the young man became his priest and was in Micah's house, and JFB notes this consecration was no less unlawful for Micah to perform than for the Levite to receive. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: Then Micah said, Now I know the Lord will do me good, seeing I have a Levite as priest, and JFB notes the Levite's installation satisfied his conscience that orderly ministrations would bring prosperity, an expression evincing the united influence of ignorance and superstition. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
