Study Notes — Judges

Chapter 6

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Israel again did evil, and the Lord delivered them to Midian seven years, and Henry states the standing rule of the book: let all that sin expect to suffer. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: Midian's hand prevailed so that Israel made dens in the mountains, caves, and strongholds, and Henry reads this hiding as the effect of a guilty conscience, since sin dispirits men. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: Whenever Israel had sown, Midian, Amalek, and the children of the east came up against them, timing their raids to steal the harvest itself. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: The invaders encamped against Israel and destroyed the increase of the earth, leaving no sustenance for man or beast, so that what Israel had prepared for Baal was justly taken away. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: They came with their cattle and tents like grasshoppers for multitude, with innumerable camels, and entered to destroy the land, a human locust plague. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Israel was greatly impoverished, and cried to the Lord, and Henry notes that affliction again made them cry to the God they had scarcely spoken to in prosperity. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: When they cried, God sent not at once a deliverer but a prophet to teach them, and Henry calls faithful ministers a token that God has mercy in store for a land. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: The prophet rehearsed the exodus: the Lord brought Israel up from Egypt and out of the house of bondage, grounding the rebuke in remembered grace. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: God had delivered them from Egyptians and all oppressors, driven out their enemies, and given them the land, so that their present misery could not be blamed on God's failure. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: The prophet closed with the command they had broken, to fear no gods of the Amorites, and Henry notes that real repentance chiefly laments sin as disobedience to God. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: The angel of the Lord sat under the oak at Ophrah while Gideon threshed wheat by the winepress to hide it from Midian, a brave and active man in obscurity, stirred up to something great. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: The angel greeted Gideon as a mighty man of valour with the Lord with him, and Henry quotes Bishop Hall: while God calls Gideon valiant, he makes him so. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Gideon answered with the people's complaint, asking where the Lord's miracles were and why Israel was forsaken and delivered to Midian, since his weak faith could not reconcile God's presence with Israel's distress. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: The Lord answered by commissioning him directly, to go in his might and save Israel, telling him that the sending itself was enough warrant to act. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: Gideon pleaded his family's poverty in Manasseh and his own least place in his father's house, and Henry notes that God delights to advance the humble. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: The Lord promised, I will be with thee, and thou shalt smite Midian as one man, so that the assurance rested on God's presence, not on Gideon's standing. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: Gideon asked for a sign that it was truly God who talked with him, desiring his weak faith to be confirmed before he acted. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: He asked the visitor not to depart until he brought his present, holding the heavenly guest with the hospitality of one who suspects he entertains more than a man. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Gideon prepared a kid and unleavened cakes and brought them to the oak, an offering of food that would be transformed into something far greater. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: The angel told him to lay the flesh and cakes on the rock and pour out the broth, arranging the gift as a sacrifice rather than a meal. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: The angel touched the offering with his staff, fire rose from the rock and consumed it, and the angel departed, showing he was not a man needing meat but the Son of God to be served by sacrifice. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: Seeing the angel depart, Gideon feared he would die for having seen the angel face to face, since a message from heaven is a terror to sinful man who scarcely dares expect good tidings. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: The Lord spoke peace to him, fear not, thou shalt not die, and God's own word steadied the courage that had just failed. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: Gideon built an altar there and called it Jehovah-shalom, the Lord is peace, a monument in Ophrah to the word that had calmed him. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: That night God ordered Gideon to take his father's bullock, throw down Baal's altar, cut down the grove, and Henry stresses the order: he must make peace with God before making war on Midian, and not merely cease worshipping but throw the altar down. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: He was to build an altar to the Lord on the rock in orderly fashion and offer the second bullock with the grove's wood, replacing idolatry with true sacrifice on the very site of the offence. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: Gideon took ten servants and did it by night, fearing his father's household and the men of the city, yet doing it nonetheless, and Henry bids us do our duty and trust God with our safety. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: In the morning the city found Baal's altar cast down, the grove cut, and the bullock offered on the new altar, so that the secret act of the night confronted the whole town at dawn. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: The men asked who had done this, and learned it was Gideon son of Joash, the reformer now named and exposed to the city's anger. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: The men demanded that Joash bring out his son to die for breaking Baal's altar, turning the town's loyalty to its idol into a demand for blood. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: Joash answered by challenging Baal's defenders, asking whether they would plead for Baal and declaring that whoever pleaded for him should die by morning, since a god who cannot avenge himself needs no human advocate. — Matthew Henry

32 COMMENTARY: From that day Gideon was called Jerubbaal, let Baal plead against him, because he had thrown down Baal's altar, and the nickname itself became a standing challenge to the idol. — Matthew Henry

33 COMMENTARY: Midian, Amalek, and the east gathered and pitched in Jezreel, so that the reformed town immediately faced the foreign army its reformation had been preparing it to meet. — Matthew Henry

34 COMMENTARY: The Spirit of the Lord clothed Gideon, he blew a trumpet, and Abiezer gathered to him, the timid thresher now visibly animated by the Spirit for public action. — Matthew Henry

35 COMMENTARY: Messengers went to Manasseh, Asher, Zebulun, and Naphtali, and they came up to meet Gideon, widening the muster beyond his own clan. — Matthew Henry

36 COMMENTARY: Gideon asked God to confirm that Israel would be saved by his hand as he had said, returning to signs even after the altar and the trumpet. — Matthew Henry

37 COMMENTARY: He laid a fleece on the threshing floor, asking that dew fall only on the fleece while the ground stayed dry, a sign that God could distinguish a small Israel from the vast floor of Midian. — Matthew Henry

38 COMMENTARY: In the morning it was so, and Gideon wrung a bowl full of water from the fleece, receiving the first sign in full measure. — Matthew Henry

39 COMMENTARY: Gideon asked God not to be angry if he proved once more, this time with the fleece dry and the ground wet, desiring the dew of grace both for himself in particular and for all Israel. — Matthew Henry

40 COMMENTARY: God did so that night, reversing the sign exactly, and Henry notes that the means of grace still come in different measures, as in one congregation one soul is the moistened fleece and another the dry ground. — Matthew Henry
