Study Notes — Judges

Chapter 3

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The nations the Lord left were left to prove Israel, and JFB explains the double design: to discipline Israel morally through their inroads and to teach the young, who had never known war, the use of weapons. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: The new generations were to learn war, since Israel was not to be idle and slothful, and Henry notes that trials both detect the wickedness of sinners' hearts and strengthen believers' graces in daily conflict. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: The nations left included five Philistine lords, all the Canaanites, the Sidonians, and the Hivites of Mount Lebanon, a deliberate remnant left on every border of the land. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: They remained to prove whether Israel would hearken to the Lord's commandments, so that the test was obedience, not merely courage in battle. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: Israel dwelt among the Canaanites, Hittites, Amorites, Perizzites, Hivites, and Jebusites, and JFB observes that the two classes gradually came into habits of contact until Israel relaxed its principles. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: They intermarried with them and served their gods, so that the friendship of the world proved more fatal than its enmity, murdering souls where war could only kill the body. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: Israel did evil, forgot the Lord their God, and served Baalim and the groves, exchanging the memory of deliverance for the worship of the land's idols. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: The Lord's anger was hot, and he sold them into the hand of Chushan-rishathaim of Mesopotamia for eight years, a distant oppressor for a people who had looked to distant gods. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: When Israel cried to the Lord he raised up Othniel, and Henry notes that affliction makes those cry to God who before would scarcely speak to him. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: The Spirit of the Lord came upon Othniel, giving wisdom, courage, and power, and he first judged Israel, reproving and reforming them, before he went to war, since sin at home must be conquered first. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: The land had rest forty years until Othniel died, a long quiet that showed what reformation followed by deliverance could give, and how much depended on one faithful judge. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Israel again did evil, and God strengthened Eglon of Moab against them, for though the Moabites did worse, God punishes his own people's sins in this world, weakening Israel and strengthening its enemy. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Eglon gathered Ammon and Amalek and took the city of palm trees, so that Israel's old enemies combined against a people weakened by their own sin. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Israel served Eglon eighteen years, a longer servitude than before, since lesser troubles had not done their work and God sent a greater. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: When Israel cried again, the Lord raised up Ehud, a Benjamite, a man left-handed, an unlikely deliverer chosen precisely so that the power would appear to be God's. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: Ehud made himself a double-edged dagger a cubit long and girded it under his raiment on his right thigh, preparing in secret the instrument of Moab's judgment. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: He brought the present to Eglon, who was a very fat man, and the tribute itself became the occasion for gaining access to the king. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: After presenting the present Ehud sent away the bearers, clearing the chamber so that what followed would be between the judge and the oppressor alone. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: Ehud returned from the quarries by Gilgal with a secret errand, and Henry notes his bold address: what message from God but vengeance can a proud rebel expect, though ministers now bear a message of mercy to those who accept it. — Matthew Henry

20 COMMENTARY: Eglon rose from his seat in the summer parlour to hear the message, and his deference to a word from God became the moment of his judgment. — Matthew Henry

21 COMMENTARY: Ehud put forth his left hand, took the dagger, and thrust it into Eglon's belly, executing as a minister of divine justice the sentence on an enemy of God and Israel. — Matthew Henry

22 COMMENTARY: The haft went in after the blade and the fat closed over it, so that Ehud could not draw the dagger out, and the wound itself bore witness to the completeness of the stroke. — Matthew Henry

23 COMMENTARY: Ehud went out through the porch, shut the parlour doors and locked them, concealing the deed long enough to make his escape. — Matthew Henry

24 COMMENTARY: The servants, finding the doors locked, supposed the king was covering his feet in his chamber, and their respectful delay gave Ehud the time he needed. — Matthew Henry

25 COMMENTARY: Ashamed at the long wait, they took a key and opened the doors to find their lord fallen dead, and the court that had trusted in its king discovered its own helplessness. — Matthew Henry

26 COMMENTARY: Ehud escaped past the quarries to Seirath while the servants lingered, so that God's deliverer slipped away as quietly as he had come. — Matthew Henry

27 COMMENTARY: In the hill country of Ephraim Ehud blew a trumpet and led Israel down from the hills, turning a single assassination into a national rising. — Matthew Henry

28 COMMENTARY: He ordered the taking of the fords of Jordan, so that no Moabite could escape back across the river, and the victory was made complete by holding the crossings. — Matthew Henry

29 COMMENTARY: Israel slew about ten thousand Moabites, all lusty men of valour, and not a man escaped, showing that the deliverance was thorough, not partial. — Matthew Henry

30 COMMENTARY: Moab was subdued that day under Israel, and the land had rest eighty years, a rest Henry sets against the saints' everlasting rest in the heavenly Canaan. — Matthew Henry

31 COMMENTARY: After Ehud came Shamgar, who with an ox-goad slew six hundred Philistines and also delivered Israel, and Henry notes that God makes serviceable those whose birth and employment are mean, since it is no matter what the weapon is if God directs the arm. — Matthew Henry
