Study Notes — Obadiah

Chapter 1

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The book opens as a vision, that is, a prophecy revealed to Obadiah rather than imagined by him, and its subject is Edom, the neighbouring people descended from Esau who were both brothers and implacable enemies of Israel. JFB explains the 'we' as Obadiah and his people, the rumour as a report resting on the Lord's own authority and therefore certain, and the ambassador as a messenger sent to stir the nations — first Assyria, then Chaldea — against Edom, a summons Jeremiah later quotes. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown; John Gill

2 COMMENTARY: Edom's reduction to insignificance is spoken of as already accomplished, because God's sentence makes it as sure as if done. JFB notes Edom then stretched from Dedan of Arabia to Bozrah in the north, and Jeremiah's echo of this verse confirms the reading: a people once considerable is made small and despised among the nations. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Edom's pride deceived it, and its rock clefts and high dwelling fed that deception: a natural fortress felt like safety itself. Henry's warning is that God easily lays low those who magnify and exalt themselves, and carnal security only ripens men for ruin and makes the ruin worse when it comes. — Matthew Henry; Matthew Henry (Concise)

4 COMMENTARY: No height of self-exaltation escapes this sentence — not the eagle's flight, not a nest set among the stars. Henry groups these verses under pride humbled: the higher Edom climbs in its own heart, the more directly the Lord says, 'thence will I bring thee down.' — Matthew Henry; Matthew Henry (Concise)

5 COMMENTARY: Henry places these verses under Edom's wealth plundered, and the comparison makes the completeness plain: ordinary thieves steal until they have enough, and grapegatherers leave some grapes behind. Edom's stripping leaves nothing, for what thieves and gleaners spare, judgment takes. — Matthew Henry; Matthew Henry (Concise)

6 COMMENTARY: The cry of astonishment marks a total exposure: the things of Esau are searched out and even his hidden stores are sought up. Henry's heading for the passage is plunder, and his concise summary agrees — Edom shall be spoiled and brought down, with nothing kept back from the search. — Matthew Henry; Matthew Henry (Concise)

7 COMMENTARY: Every support Edom trusted fails at once: its confederates escort it only as far as the border and abandon it, its friends at peace deceive and prevail against it, and those who ate its bread lay a wound under it. Henry's moral is exact — those who make flesh their trust arm it against themselves, and trusting men with whom one joins oneself may prove a wound and dishonour. — Matthew Henry; Matthew Henry (Concise)

8 COMMENTARY: The wisdom Edom boasted in — Teman was famed for it — is destroyed out of it, and understanding is taken from the mount of Esau. Henry reads this as just denial: God withholds the understanding to keep out of danger from those who would not use their understanding to keep out of sin. — Matthew Henry; Matthew Henry (Concise)

9 COMMENTARY: Even Edom's mighty men, the warriors it stayed itself on, are dismayed, so that every one of the mount of Esau is cut off by slaughter. Henry's summary covers it: all the enemies of God's church shall be disappointed in the very things they stay themselves on, and Edom's strength proves no stay at all. — Matthew Henry; Matthew Henry (Concise)

10 COMMENTARY: The charge is named plainly: violence against brother Jacob, and the sentence matches it — shame shall cover Edom and it shall be cut off forever. Henry stresses that all violence is sin, but violence against God's people is far worse, and Edom's barbarous conduct toward Judah and Jerusalem is charged upon it here. — Matthew Henry; Matthew Henry (Concise)

11 COMMENTARY: Edom did not need to strike the blow to share the guilt: it stood on the other side while strangers carried away Jacob's forces, foreigners entered Jerusalem's gates, and lots were cast upon the city. Henry's rule applies — he who joins in with evildoers and abets their deeds shall be reckoned, and reckoned with, as one of them, and idle spectators of a neighbour's troubles have a great deal to answer for. — Matthew Henry; Matthew Henry (Concise)

12 COMMENTARY: JFB reads 'looked on' as a malignant pleasure and a brutal stare at a brother's day of calamity, when he became a stranger banished as an alien from his own land. Edom should not have rejoiced over Judah's destruction nor spoken proudly — literally, made great the mouth — in the day of distress, for God sends heavy calamities on those who rejoice in their enemies' calamities, and Gill likewise dates the prophecy after Jerusalem's destruction by Nebuchadnezzar. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown; John Gill

13 COMMENTARY: The indictment grows from looking to entering: Edom went into the gate of God's people in their calamity, gazed on their affliction, and laid hands on their substance. Henry's note is that by meddling with the spoil Edom became a partaker of the crime, an accessory after the fact, and those who think to enrich themselves by the ruins of God's people only impoverish themselves. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Worse still, Edom stood in the crossway where roads met to cut off fugitives making their escape, and delivered up those who remained in the day of distress. Henry's account is blunt: Edom not only robbed its brethren but murdered them, intercepting those the Chaldean sword had spared and handing them back to destruction. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: The day of the Lord is near upon all the heathen, not Edom alone, and the measure returns exactly: as thou hast done, it shall be done unto thee, and thy reward shall return upon thine own head. Henry adds that though judgment begins at the house of God, it shall not end there, and the cup that went round to Israel now goes round to its oppressors. — Matthew Henry; Matthew Henry (Concise)

16 COMMENTARY: As Edom and the nations drank in triumph upon God's holy mountain, so shall all the heathen drink continually of the same bitter cup, swallowing it down until they are as though they had never been. Henry's close of the passage holds both ends together: the troubles of the righteous will soon end, but those of the wicked will be eternal. — Matthew Henry; Matthew Henry (Concise)

17 COMMENTARY: The turn of the book is deliverance on Mount Zion, with holiness, where the house of Jacob shall possess its own possessions again. Henry's outline marks this as the second part of the prophecy: after the threatenings against Edom come gracious promises to Israel of restoration, reform, and victory over the Edomites. — Matthew Henry; Matthew Henry (Concise); John Gill

18 COMMENTARY: The restored houses become the instruments of Edom's end: Jacob a fire, Joseph a flame, and Esau stubble kindled and devoured until no survivor remains of the house of Esau. Henry reads this as Israel victorious over the Edomites and master of their land, and Gill agrees that the Edomites' wasted land passes to those they wronged — 'for the Lord hath spoken it' makes it certain. — Matthew Henry; John Gill

19 COMMENTARY: Gill maps the restored borders in detail: those of the south seize Idumea lying next to them, those of the plain take Philistia — Azotus, Gaza, Ashkelon, Gath, and Ekron — and Israel repossesses the fields of Ephraim and Samaria, while Benjamin is enlarged to take in Gilead beyond Jordan. Some of this, Gill notes, was fulfilled in Maccabean times, but the full settlement looks to the latter day. — John Gill

20 COMMENTARY: The returning captives possess the Canaanite land as far as Zarephath, called Sarepta of Sidon, where Elijah once dwelt, and the captivity of Jerusalem in Sepharad possesses the cities of the south. Gill reads this as a greatly enlarged border in the latter day, or else as the enlargement of the church of Christ when the gospel spread through those parts. — John Gill

21 COMMENTARY: Saviours — deliverers raised up by God — come up on Mount Zion to judge the mount of Esau, and the book ends where it must: the kingdom shall be the Lord's. Henry's outline closes the prophecy here with the setting up of the kingdom of the Messiah by the bringing in of the great salvation, and Gill calls this the glorious prophecy of the kingdom of the Messiah with which the book concludes. — Matthew Henry; John Gill
