Study Notes — 2 Corinthians

Chapter 3

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: Do we begin again to commend ourselves? or need we, as some others, epistles of commendation to you, or letters of commendation from you? Henry notes Paul asks the question his critics ask, and the answer is no: he needs no letters, because Corinth itself is his letter. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Ye are our epistle written in our hearts, known and read of all men. JFB notes the Corinthians are a letter Paul carries in his heart, yet also read by all men, since their changed lives are public evidence. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

3 COMMENTARY: Forasmuch as ye are manifestly declared to be the epistle of Christ ministered by us, written not with ink, but with the Spirit of the living God; not in tables of stone, but in fleshy tables of the heart. Henry notes the contrast: Moses' law on stone, Christ's epistle by the Spirit on hearts, and Paul is only the minister who delivered it. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

4 COMMENTARY: And such trust have we through Christ to God-ward. JFB notes the trust is through Christ and toward God: Paul's confidence about his ministry rests on neither himself nor Corinth. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

5 COMMENTARY: Not that we are sufficient of ourselves to think any thing as of ourselves; but our sufficiency is of God. Henry notes this answers chapter 2's question: no man is sufficient, but God makes ministers sufficient, and the sufficiency is his gift. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

6 COMMENTARY: Who also has made us able ministers of the new testament; not of the letter, but of the spirit: for the letter kills, but the spirit gives life. JFB notes the new covenant's ministry is of the Spirit: the letter alone, the law without the Spirit, condemns and kills, while the Spirit gives life. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

7 COMMENTARY: But if the ministration of death, written and engraven in stones, was glorious, so that the children of Israel could not steadfastly behold the face of Moses for the glory of his countenance; which glory was to be done away. Henry notes even the killing ministry had glory, shining on Moses' face, though it was a passing glory. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

8 COMMENTARY: How shall not the ministration of the spirit be rather glorious? JFB notes the argument from the lesser to the greater: if death's ministry shone, life's ministry must shine far more. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: For if the ministration of condemnation be glory, much more does the ministration of righteousness exceed in glory. Henry notes the two ministries are named by their effects: condemnation and righteousness, and righteousness exceeds as life exceeds death. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

10 COMMENTARY: For even that which was made glorious had no glory in this respect, by reason of the glory that excels. JFB notes the old glory is eclipsed, not denied: beside the gospel's glory it counts as no glory, as a candle beside the sun. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: For if that which is done away was glorious, much more that which remains is glorious. Henry notes permanence is the final contrast: what is abolished was glorious, but what remains is glorious for ever. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

12 COMMENTARY: Seeing then that we have such hope, we use great plainness of speech. JFB notes hope produces plainness: ministers of a remaining glory need no veil and no hiding. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

13 COMMENTARY: And not as Moses, which put a vail over his face, that the children of Israel could not steadfastly look to the end of that which is abolished. Henry notes Moses veiled a fading glory so Israel could not see its end; the gospel ministry unveils what lasts. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

14 COMMENTARY: But their minds were blinded: for until this day remains the same vail untaken away in the reading of the old testament; which vail is done away in Christ. JFB notes the veil has moved from Moses' face to the readers' minds: it lifts only in Christ, when the old testament is read through him. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

15 COMMENTARY: But even unto this day, when Moses is read, the vail is upon their heart. Henry notes the veil is now on the heart, not the page: the words are read, but their meaning is hidden until the heart turns. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: Nevertheless when it shall turn to the Lord, the vail shall be taken away. JFB notes the remedy is a turning, not a new argument: when the heart turns to the Lord, the veil that hid the old covenant falls. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: Now the Lord is that Spirit: and where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is liberty. Henry notes liberty is the Spirit's mark: where he rules, there is freedom from the letter's bondage and from the veil's blindness. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: But we all, with open face beholding as in a glass the glory of the Lord, are changed into the same image from glory to glory, even as by the Spirit of the LORD. JFB notes the beholding itself transforms: open-faced, in a mirror, believers are changed into Christ's image, glory by glory, by the Spirit's work. — Matthew Henry; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
