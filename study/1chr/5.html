Study Notes — 1 Chronicles

Chapter 5

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: The sons of Reuben the firstborn are introduced with a parenthesis explaining why his genealogy is not first: he defiled his father's bed and forfeited the birthright. JFB explains the birthright meant dominion and a double portion, both transferred to Joseph, whose two sons became two tribes. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

2 COMMENTARY: Judah prevailed above his brethren, and of him came the chief ruler, but the birthright was Joseph's. JFB explains the genealogy is not reckoned after the birthright but by the superior honour given to Judah, from whom David and the Messiah sprang. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

9 COMMENTARY: Eastward he inhabited unto the entering of the wilderness from the Euphrates, because their cattle multiplied in Gilead. JFB describes Reuben's quiet pastoral history east of Jordan: cattle in Gilead, wars with the Bedouin sons of Hagar, and pasture rights over a great mountain range. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

11 COMMENTARY: The children of Gad dwelt over against them in Bashan unto Salcah. JFB notes Gad's genealogy is given with Reuben's because Gad, Reuben, and half Manasseh formed a separate trans-Jordan colony. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

16 COMMENTARY: They dwelt in Gilead in Bashan, in her towns, and in all the suburbs of Sharon, upon their borders. JFB notes Sharon here is an east-Jordan Sharon, a term for any place of extraordinary beauty and productiveness, and Gad occupied the intermediate land between Gilead and Bashan. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

17 COMMENTARY: All these were reckoned by genealogies in the days of Jotham king of Judah and Jeroboam king of Israel. JFB notes Jotham's long, peaceful reign was favourable for taking a census, and the Jeroboam named is the second of that name. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

18 COMMENTARY: The sons of Reuben, the Gadites, and half Manasseh were 44,760 valiant men able to bear buckler and sword, shoot with bow, and skilful in war. JFB's comment on this muster turns to their enemies, the Hagarites, originally synonymous with Ishmaelites and later a particular Arab tribe. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

19 COMMENTARY: They made war with the Hagarites, with Jetur, Nephish, and Nodab. JFB notes Jetur's descendants were the Itureans, skilled archers of Auranitis, invaded by the confederate trans-Jordan tribes in reprisal for marauding raids, with divine providence favouring the Hebrew army. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

20 COMMENTARY: They were helped against them, and the Hagarites were delivered into their hand, for they cried to God in the battle and he was entreated of them because they put their trust in him. JFB presents this as a just war in which providence remarkably favoured the Hebrew army. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

22 COMMENTARY: There fell many slain, because the war was of God, and they dwelt in their enemies' stead until the captivity. JFB's account of the Hagarite war ends with the trans-Jordan tribes dispossessing the inhabitants and colonising the district themselves. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

26 COMMENTARY: The God of Israel stirred up the spirit of Pul king of Assyria, and of Tilgath-pilneser, who carried away the Reubenites, Gadites, and half Manasseh to Halah, Habor, Hara, and the river Gozan. JFB identifies Pul with the Phalluka of the Ninevite monuments and calls this removal the first captivity. — Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

No note for verse(s) 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 12, 13, 14, 15, 21, 23, 24, 25 — the public-domain sources consulted give no distinct explanation for these verses beyond the list itself, so nothing was invented for them.
