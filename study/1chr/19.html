Study Notes — 1 Chronicles

Chapter 19

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

1 COMMENTARY: After Nahash king of Ammon dies, his son reigns in his stead, setting up the chapter's story of David's war with the Ammonites and their Syrian allies. Henry notes the chapter repeats the history already told in 2 Samuel 10, now in Chronicles' own telling. — Matthew Henry

2 COMMENTARY: David resolves to show kindness to Hanun, because Hanun's father showed kindness to him, and sends messengers to comfort him. Henry observes that good people should be neighbourly and grateful, returning kindness received from parents to their children when the parents are gone. — Matthew Henry

3 COMMENTARY: The Ammonite princes tell Hanun that David's comforters have really come to search, overthrow, and spy out the land. Henry applies the ancient proverb that wickedness proceeds from the wicked: those base and designing ill themselves are apt to suspect ill of others without cause. — Matthew Henry

4 COMMENTARY: Hanun takes David's servants, shaves them, cuts off their garments in the middle, and sends them away. Henry notes Hanun hearkened to the groundless suggestion and, against the law of nations, treated David's ambassadors villainously. — Matthew Henry

5 COMMENTARY: When David hears how his men were served, he sends to meet them, for they are greatly ashamed, and bids them tarry at Jericho until their beards grow. Henry draws the lesson that masters ought to protect their servants with tenderness when they suffer loss or damage in their service. — Matthew Henry

6 COMMENTARY: Seeing they have made themselves odious to David, Hanun and the Ammonites send a thousand talents of silver to hire chariots and horsemen from Mesopotamia, Syrian Maacah, and Zobah. Henry observes how hearts marked for ruin harden to their destruction: wisdom would have sought peace and offered satisfaction, but instead they prepared for war and brought on desolations David never intended. — Matthew Henry

7 COMMENTARY: They hire thirty-two thousand chariots, with the king of Maacah and his people, who encamp before Medeba, while the Ammonites gather from their cities to battle. Henry notes the Ammonites did their utmost to make the best of their position, bringing as good a force into the field as policy could dispose. — Matthew Henry

8 COMMENTARY: When David hears of it, he sends Joab and all the host of the mighty men. Henry's second section frames the campaign: against this hired array stands Israel's army under a commander whose courage difficulties will heighten rather than break. — Matthew Henry

9 COMMENTARY: The Ammonites put themselves in battle array before the gate of the city, while the kings who came to help are by themselves in the field. Henry notes the Ammonites disposed their force with as much policy as possible, yet, having a bad cause and acting in defence of wrong, it would not do. — Matthew Henry

10 COMMENTARY: Joab sees the battle set against him before and behind, and chooses the choice men of Israel to array against the Syrians. Henry observes that the courage of brave men is heightened by difficulties: instead of meditating retreat, Joab doubles his resolution and, though he cannot double his army, divides it with great presence of mind. — Matthew Henry

11 COMMENTARY: The rest of the people Joab delivers to his brother Abishai, who arrays them against the Ammonites. Henry notes Joab not only speaks but acts like a gallant man when surrounded, dividing his forces so each front is met. — Matthew Henry

12 COMMENTARY: Joab covenants mutual help with Abishai: if the Syrians are too strong, Abishai will help him, and if the Ammonites are too strong, Joab will help Abishai. Henry highlights this engagement for mutual assistance as the mark of a commander acting with presence of mind. — Matthew Henry

13 COMMENTARY: Joab charges them to be of good courage and behave valiantly for their people and the cities of their God, leaving the issue to the Lord to do what is good in his sight. Henry notes Joab excites himself and the officers to act vigorously in their posts, with an eye to God's glory and their country's good, not their own honour, and then leaves the issue to God. — Matthew Henry

14 COMMENTARY: Joab and his men draw near to the Syrians in battle, and the Syrians flee before him. Henry's lesson is that the greatest art and strength are vain against justice and equity: right will prevail and triumph at last. — Matthew Henry

15 COMMENTARY: When the Ammonites see the Syrians fled, they likewise flee before Abishai and enter the city, and Joab comes to Jerusalem. Henry notes the Ammonites, for all their policy and force, are put to the worst because they defend a wrong. — Matthew Henry

16 COMMENTARY: When the Syrians see they are put to the worse, they send messengers and draw forth the Syrians beyond the river, with Shophach captain of Hadarezer's host. Henry observes how little purpose there is in rallying again without God on one's side: beaten mercenaries call in the Syrians from beyond Euphrates only to retrieve their honour. — Matthew Henry

17 COMMENTARY: David hears, gathers all Israel, passes over Jordan, and sets the battle in array against them. Henry's account of the second campaign turns on the same point: the reinforced Syrians face not only Joab now, but David and all Israel. — Matthew Henry

18 COMMENTARY: The Syrians flee before Israel, and David slays seven thousand men who fought in chariots and forty thousand footmen, and kills Shophach. Henry notes the rally comes to nothing: they still flee before Israel, losing seven thousand men, reckoned as the men of seven hundred chariots. — Matthew Henry

19 COMMENTARY: When Hadarezer's servants see they are put to the worse, they make peace with David and become his servants, and the Syrians will help Ammon no more. Henry closes that those who meddled with strife not their own do well to learn wit at length and meddle no further, agreeing quickly while they are in the way. — Matthew Henry
