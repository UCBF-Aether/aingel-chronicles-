Study Notes — Baruch

Chapter 6

Everything below is COMMENTARY on the chapter — it explains the verses; it is not Scripture, and it never changes the Scripture text. Notes are condensed from public-domain commentators, and each note names its source.

Read the chapter itself: Standard · Modern

2 COMMENTARY: Haydock's authorities read the 'seven generations' of the captivity as seventy years, with 'seven' often standing for a general or round number. Calmet adds that eighteen of those seventy years had already elapsed when the letter was written. — George Leo Haydock

3 COMMENTARY: The gods borne upon shoulders reflect a very ancient custom, which Haydock traces to the avarice of the priests, who begged on such occasions. The spectacle itself is meant to expose, not honour, the idols. — George Leo Haydock

4 COMMENTARY: Haydock glosses the warning 'fear' simply: worship not such things. The fear the Babylonians' idols inspire is not to be mistaken for reverence owed to a god. — George Leo Haydock

6 COMMENTARY: The angel promised to be with the captives is Michael, the conductor of Israel in the desert, cited from Daniel 10:13 and Exodus 33:2. Worthington adds that the same protector guarded them also in Babylon. — George Leo Haydock

8 COMMENTARY: The gold crowns made for the idols' heads are likened to dressing a maiden that loves to go gay, and Calmet remarks that nothing could be more despicable. The finery advertises vanity, not divinity. — George Leo Haydock

10 COMMENTARY: The priests give the idols' gold and silver even to harlots under the same roof, while adorning the idols like men with garments. Haydock contrasts this with the Lord, who rejected the harlot's hire: the idols suffer themselves to be despoiled or adorned at pleasure. — George Leo Haydock

12 COMMENTARY: The idols' faces must be wiped because of the dust of the temple, on account of the many votaries crowding in. Haydock cites Arnobius ridiculing such gods, and Worthington adds that these absurdities show how foolish it is to serve idols or take any image to be a god. — George Leo Haydock

13 COMMENTARY: The idol holding a sceptre as though a judge of the country merely carries a badge of power: the sceptre and spear were such badges, as the sword was for Mars and the club for Hercules. Calmet notes how little is actually known of the idols of Babylon behind the show. — George Leo Haydock

16 COMMENTARY: Haydock's note on the vessel explains the comparison: the word covers armour or any utensil, and as a broken vessel becomes useless, so are gods set up in a temple. A thing that cannot serve its use cannot be a god. — George Leo Haydock

18 COMMENTARY: The candles lit for the idols are literally lamps, and Haydock notes some Egyptian temples were famous for their numbers. The Lord prescribed lamps in his own worship, but no one ever imagined he needed them to see, as idolaters supposed their idols did. — George Leo Haydock

19 COMMENTARY: The creeping things said to gnaw the idols' hearts are explained plainly: the wood is worm-eaten. The gods cannot even feel what eats them. — George Leo Haydock

24 COMMENTARY: Things with no breath in them are bought for a most high price, and Worthington's gloss is that buyers foolishly believe the images have divine power. The price measures the buyer's folly, not the idol's worth. — George Leo Haydock

26 COMMENTARY: The gifts set before fallen idols as before the dead recall the Chaldean supposition that Bel could eat, cited from Daniel 14, and the custom of placing food on tombs, which Augustine abolished in Africa. Offerings that feed no one expose the idol. — George Leo Haydock

27 COMMENTARY: The Greek for the priests' taking reads 'salt': they lay up part of the sacrifices in salt and give none to the poor, for fear their imposture would be detected, though they allow even the unclean to eat. The charity the law required is exactly what the idol cult withholds. — George Leo Haydock

29 COMMENTARY: Calmet's remark on the women setting meat before the gods is blunt: they make the gods. A deity that must be fed by its worshippers is the worshippers' own work. — George Leo Haydock

30 COMMENTARY: The priests sit with clothes rent and heads and beards shaven, as in mourning, contrary to the custom of Israel in Leviticus 21:10. Haydock adds that the Jewish priests wore mitres or caps, not this mourning disarray. — George Leo Haydock

31 COMMENTARY: The roaring and crying before the gods, as at a feast when one is dead, recalls the worship of Adonis, which Calmet says is here ridiculed. At Byblos people bewailed his death and proclaimed him alive the next day, cutting off their hair, with the hire consecrated to Venus. — George Leo Haydock

34 COMMENTARY: The Greek for 'requite' reads 'brass': the idols cannot give riches nor requite evil, much less gold. A god that cannot return even base metal for a vow is no god at all. — George Leo Haydock

40 COMMENTARY: Even the Chaldeans themselves dishonour their idols, and Calmet observes that the priests despise them most, exposing them to contempt by pretending they work miracles. Haydock quotes Varro and Augustine: setting up representations of the gods deprived cities of reverential awe and increased the error. — George Leo Haydock

41 COMMENTARY: The Greek reads that the dumb man's presenters, reflecting on the idol's senselessness, cannot still abandon it, for the gods themselves have no sense. Calmet's gloss is that worshippers are so stupid they will not quit such impotent idols. — George Leo Haydock

42 COMMENTARY: The women with cords about them recall the Babylonian rite in honour of Venus, or Mylitta, in which women sat crowned in the temple until some stranger selected them and broke their cords, as Herodotus describes. Aristophanes called harlots 'corded bodies,' and Haydock cites the passage against the rite. — George Leo Haydock

44 COMMENTARY: The idols are made by carpenters and goldsmiths, and can be nothing else than the workmen will have them to be, as Isaiah 44:14 shows. Haydock quotes Horace's Priapus explaining his origin as a useless piece of fig-tree, which the workman, unsure whether to make a bench or a god, chose to make a god. — George Leo Haydock

51 COMMENTARY: The Greek has simply, 'Who then knows not that they are no gods?' The verse states the letter's whole argument as a question no reader can honestly answer otherwise. — George Leo Haydock

53 COMMENTARY: The idols can neither judge a cause nor redress a wrong, and Calmet says they have no greater influence than jackdaws between heaven and earth. Creatures of the air outrank them in power. — George Leo Haydock

58 COMMENTARY: The Septuagint repeats the comparison after 'satisfied' and adds 'or a pillar of wood in palaces, than' such false gods. A king who shows his power, a profitable vessel, or a door keeping things safe is better than an idol. — George Leo Haydock

59 COMMENTARY: The sun, moon, and stars are obedient to God, appointed to mark out the seasons according to Genesis 1:14, and Calmet notes that if any creature were worthy of adoration, they would be. The Babylonians adored them as well as statues, which is why their proper destination is specified here. — George Leo Haydock

69 COMMENTARY: Like a scarecrow in a garden of cucumbers, the idols keep nothing, and Haydock notes Priapus answered this same glorious purpose in the workmen's idol of verse 44. Calmet adds that once birds grow accustomed to such things, they mind them not. — George Leo Haydock

71 COMMENTARY: The purple and scarlet rotting upon the idols mark them: the Greek speaks of marble-coloured wood, and Grotius would read a pearl, which decays through age. The finery itself rots, and the idols are at last consumed and left a reproach. — George Leo Haydock

72 COMMENTARY: Better is the just man who has no idols, for he shall be far from reproach, provided, Haydock adds, he also avoids the idols of the mind and of the heart. The letter ends where it began: freedom from idols, outward and inward. — George Leo Haydock
