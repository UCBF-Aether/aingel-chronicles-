Sabbath Teaching · Week 1

The Seventh Day

Sabbath, October 24, 2026 · Readings: Genesis 1–31

Why does the first account of time end with a day on which nothing is made? This week you read Genesis 1–31, and its crown is not a creature but a day. Read Genesis 2:1–3 slowly, and count. The words “the seventh day” sound three times. The work of God is twice declared complete — “Thus the heavens and the earth were finished, and all the host of them.” On the seventh day God “ended his work which he had made”. Twice we are told he rested, and once that he blessed the day: “And God blessed the seventh day, and sanctified it: because that in it he had rested from all his work which God created and made.” Notice what is missing: every other day of the week closes with “the evening and the morning”; the seventh day never does. And notice what is added: everything God made was called good, the whole of it very good — but only this day is called holy. The text is teaching before it explains. The seventh day is not the week's leftover. It is its crown, and its only holy day.

So what is this rest? Not recovery. The Hebrew word under “rested” means to cease — to stop, deliberately, at a work that is done. God was not tired; his strength does not run down. He stopped because nothing was lacking: “God saw every thing that he had made, and, behold, it was very good.” Rest, in the Bible's first teaching on it, belongs to a finished work received, not to wages earned by exhaustion. It is received, not earned. You do not qualify for the seventh day by wearing yourself out in the six; the day was blessed before you did anything at all.

C. H. Spurgeon, preaching on the rest of God, taught that God's rest at the end of creation is the pattern of all true rest for men, that the Sabbath stands as a perpetual memorial of that rest, and that God intended his people to be partakers of it — not spectators of a rest God once enjoyed, but guests at his own table. And because the day is sanctified, Spurgeon presses the point most of us miss: “to make a true Sabbath, there must be a sanctifying of the day–it must be a holy day if it is to be a restful day!” A merely empty day is not a Sabbath, and an idle day is not yet a holy one. The rest God gives lives in a day set apart to him.

The rest of this week's reading shows you the world without that rest, so you will know what is being given back. In Eden, the man and woman reach for what was not given, and work turns to toil: “In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread”. Cain, who murdered his brother, is sentenced to restlessness itself — “a fugitive and a vagabond shalt thou be in the earth.” Lamech names his son Noah as a prayer against the curse: “This same shall comfort us concerning our work and toil of our hands”. At Babel, men build to secure themselves — “let us make us a name” — and are scattered. Against all of it stands Abraham, who receives a promise he cannot manufacture and learns the seventh day's lesson in advance: “And he believed in the LORD; and he counted it to him for righteousness.”

Tomorrow, then, keep the day the way it was given. First, cease: lay down your ordinary work, and the striving that hides inside it, and let one unfinished thing stay unfinished until the new week. Your stopping is a confession — the world is held by God's finished work, not by your grip. Second, sanctify: give the day to worship, to the word read slowly, to mercy, and to the people at your table; make it holy, and it will become restful. Third, receive: eat, walk, laugh, and sleep as gifts, not wages. The seventh day was blessed before the fall, before any law, before there was an Israel. It is creation's gift, and it is still given every week to everyone willing to stop and take it.

Cease, because God finished; keep it holy, because God blessed it — receive the seventh day as the gift it was made to be.
