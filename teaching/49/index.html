Sabbath Teaching · Week 49

The Sinodos — the Church's Rulebook

Sabbath, September 25, 2027 · Readings: Sirate Tsion (Sinodos 1) 7–10; Tizaz (Sinodos 2) 11–19; Gitsew (Sinodos 3) 20–28; Abtilis (Sinodos 4) 29–37; Gessew 1–57; 1 Book of the Covenant 1–10

What happens to a movement when its founders die? Someone must decide, in writing, how the community actually lives — who may lead, who is cared for, how disputes end, how the table is kept. This week you hold this canon's answer entire: the Sinodos, thirty-seven chapters in its four traditional parts, read straight through beside its companion the Gessew. A word of honesty belongs at the front of this teaching, and the sources record below states it plainly: historic preaching on these books is scarce to the point of absence in the traditions this bank usually draws on. There is no Spurgeon on the Sinodos. So this teaching grounds itself where the week's own discipline requires — in the texts themselves, read closely — and says so, rather than borrowing a sermon from neighbouring ground and pretending it belongs here.

Look at the architecture first, because the order is a doctrine. Sirate Tsion, the Order of Zion, chapters 1–10, opens the collection — you began it last week, and its first chapter does not begin with officers at all. It begins with Peter speaking commandments for every believer: "Thou shalt not kill a soul; and thou shalt not commit fornication; and thou shalt not corrupt a young child." Before this book ordains anyone, it moralises everyone. Church order, in the Sinodos's own arrangement, is downstream of ordinary holiness. Tizaz, the Commandments, chapters 11–19, follows; Gitsew, chapters 20–28, is the third part; Abtilis, chapters 29–37, stands last and closes the book. Across all four parts the concerns stay stubbornly concrete: leaders chosen and held to account, prayer and fasting ordered, the sick visited, disputes settled inside the household rather than paraded before the world, and the poor and the widows carried as named responsibilities, not good intentions.

The Gessew sharpens the same vision into single sentences. All fifty-seven chapters of it this week — the fifty-six apostolic canons and a closing epilogue counted as canon 57 — and its very first ruling sets the tone of shared responsibility: "A bishop shall be ordained by two or by three bishops". No lone hands, no self-appointment. Authority in this tradition is something the church does together, in the open, with witnesses. Where the Sinodos ranges, the Gessew rules briefly — offerings, ordination, discipline, correction — the working grammar of a community that expects to still exist in a hundred years.

Two facts of transmission belong in the teaching itself, because they are the week's honesty. The Sinodos was translated anew for this edition, directly from the Ge'ez of G. Horner's 1904 printed edition, The Statutes of the Apostles; Horner's own English served only as a check. The Gessew was translated anew from the Ge'ez of W. Fell's Canones apostolorum Aethiopice of 1871. And a naming thread ties the pair together: the Gessew is traditionally the Sinodos's third part, under the spelling Gitsew — the same name you met over chapters 20–28. One thing it is not, and let this be said without blur: the Gessew is not the "Book of Adam and Eve." That label belongs elsewhere and is never to be attached to it.

Doctrine, plainly: the church's common life is not scaffolding around the faith; ordered love — leaders accountable, poor fed, disputes reconciled, worship kept — is the faith taking institutional shape, and a community that will not order itself will not outlive its founders. Practice this week at household scale: pick one piece of your own common life that currently runs on goodwill alone — a shared meal, a visit to someone sick, a standing act of help for someone poorer — and give it the Sinodos treatment. Name who is responsible, fix when it happens, and write it down. Order is how love survives a busy week.

A church that loves its story must also order its life — begin with one named duty, kept.
