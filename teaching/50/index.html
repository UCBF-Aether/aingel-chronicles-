Sabbath Teaching · Week 50

The Books of the Covenant

Sabbath, October 2, 2027 · Readings: 1 Book of the Covenant 11–47; 2 Book of the Covenant 1–27; Qalementos (Ethiopic Clement) 1–65

What would the risen Lord say to his church about an ordinary Tuesday? That is the claim these books make about themselves, and this week you finish them: the two Books of the Covenant, forty-seven chapters and twenty-seven, standing complete at last. As with last week, the honest preface belongs inside the teaching: historic English preaching on the Testament of Our Lord is effectively absent from the sermon tradition this bank draws on, so — as the sources record states — this teaching is grounded in the books themselves, read closely, and in their documented history, which turns out to be a sermon in its own right about telling the truth.

Hear how the second book begins its practical work. Newcomers are not simply waved into the assembly: in Cooper and Maclean's English, those who first come to hear the Word are to "let them be examined as to all the cause" of their coming, with witnesses who know them and can speak for their life. Slow doors, warm welcome: the community is glad of the newcomer and serious about the faith at the same time. That balance runs through both books. The first book, completed in this week's opening stretch, presents the risen Lord ordering his church with the end of the age in view — the character required of bishop, presbyter, and deacon; prayer and the Eucharist kept in order; widows, virgins, and the poor honoured and provided for, by rule and not by chance. The second book widens the same care to the whole community's daily ordering: its gatherings, its discipline, its work, the responsibilities of ordinary believers alongside their leaders.

Watch the direction of travel, because it is the books' theology in motion. The teaching begins at the altar and does not stop until it has reached the household, the purse, and the treatment of the least regarded person in the congregation. A church, these pages insist, is known by how it arranges its common life when no miracle is happening. That is not a lesser kind of spirituality. In these books it is the whole point of having been taught by the risen Lord at all.

Now the receipts, because this edition's rule is to show them. The English printed here is J. Cooper and A. J. Maclean's The Testament of Our Lord, published in 1902 and translated from the Syriac — received as a public-domain text, with small repairs made against the printed edition. These books have been available in English since 1902. No new discovery is claimed for them in this edition, and none should be claimed anywhere. They stand in this canon for a plainer and better reason: the Ethiopian Church kept them as Scripture's companions when most other traditions kept neither. Meeting them here is not about novelty; it is about meeting them in their Ethiopian place, in their Ethiopian order, in the company they actually kept for centuries.

Beside them this week stands the Qalementos, the Ethiopic Clement, sixty-five of its sixty-seven chapters — Clement speaking as Peter's helper, gathering teaching on ministry, worship, conduct, and discipline, translated anew for this edition directly from the Ge'ez. Its last two chapters finish next week. Doctrine, plainly: Christ's lordship is not fully confessed by a church that orders its worship carefully and its common life carelessly; the risen Lord of these books claims both. Practice this week in his pattern: examine one ordinary arrangement of your household or congregation — who is welcomed, who is examined kindly, who is quietly provided for — and repair the weakest joint in it, concretely, before next Sabbath.

No discovery is claimed here — only a home restored, a people provided for, and a keeping honoured.
