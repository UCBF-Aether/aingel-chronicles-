Sabbath Teaching · Week 50

The Books of the Covenant

Sabbath, October 2, 2027 · Readings: 1 Book of the Covenant 11–47; 2 Book of the Covenant 1–27; Qalementos (Ethiopic Clement) 1–65

What would the risen Lord say to his church about its ordinary days? That is the claim these books make about themselves, and this week you finish them: the two Books of the Covenant, forty-seven chapters and twenty-seven, standing complete in your hands at last.

Together they form the work known in Latin as the Testamentum Domini, the Testament of Our Lord. The first book, which you began last week and completed in this week's opening stretch, presents the risen Lord ordering his church against the end of the age: who may serve as bishop, presbyter, and deacon, and what character they must show; how prayer and the Eucharist are to be kept; how widows, virgins, and the poor are to be honoured and provided for. The second book turns the same care toward the community's daily ordering — its gatherings, its discipline, its work, and the responsibilities of ordinary believers as well as leaders. Watch for that widening. The teaching begins at the altar and does not stop until it has reached the household, the purse, and the treatment of the least regarded person in the congregation. A church, these books insist, is known by how it arranges its common life when no one is watching a miracle.

Now the honest word about their English, because this edition's rule is to show its receipts. The text printed here is J. Cooper and A. J. Maclean's translation, The Testament of Our Lord, published in 1902 and made from the Syriac — received as a public-domain text, with small repairs against the printed edition. These books have been available in English since 1902. No new discovery is claimed for them, and none should be. They stand in this canon for a simpler reason: the Ethiopian Church kept them as Scripture's companions when most other traditions kept neither. Reading them here is not about novelty. It is about meeting them in their Ethiopian place, in order, in the company they actually kept for centuries.

Beside them this week opens a larger Ethiopian voice: the Qalementos, the Ethiopic Clement, in sixty-five of its sixty-seven chapters. Clement speaks as the helper of Peter, chief of the apostles, gathering teaching on ministry, worship, conduct, and discipline. For this edition it was translated anew, directly from the Ge'ez — one of the books that survives in English only in rare, scattered, or long-unavailable forms, carried over fresh so it could stand complete beside its companions. Its closing two chapters finish next week, on the year's last stretch of road.

So receive the Covenant books plainly: old friends in English, newcomers only in their address. The shelf they live on is this canon's gift — and it is gift enough.

No discovery is claimed here — only a home restored, and a church's keeping honoured.
