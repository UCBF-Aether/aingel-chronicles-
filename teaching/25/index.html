Sabbath Teaching · Week 25

The Man Who Argued with God

Sabbath, April 10, 2027 · Readings: 3 Meqabyan 18–21; Job 1–42; Psalms 1–8

Did you notice what you just did? You read the whole of Job in a single week — forty-two chapters, the whole argument, from the ash heap to the whirlwind — after finishing 3 Meqabyan. Hold the two together for a moment: Meqabyan's confessors suffer for refusing idolatry, and everyone knows why. Job suffers, and nobody in the book — not Job, not his friends, not you the reader, until the prologue you alone are shown — is allowed a why. That difference is the book.

Watch the friends' logic, because the book spends thirty chapters dismantling it. Their speeches share one tidy system: suffering is punishment, therefore Job must confess the sin that caused it. It is neat, it is orthodox-sounding, and God himself finally calls it wrong — the friends, he says, have not spoken rightly, as Job has. Job's courage is the refusal of the tidy system. He will not confess what he has not done. He will not call cruelty wisdom because it arrives in religious language. He argues — with his friends, with his circumstances, and finally demands a hearing with God himself. It is not polite faith. Job's speeches are the longest act of honest prayer in the Bible, and the book honours them by letting them stand, unanswered, for most of its length.

Then God answers — and notice, preachers have always noticed, how. “Then the LORD answered Job out of the whirlwind, and said, Who is this that darkeneth counsel by words without knowledge? Gird up now thy loins like a man; for I will demand of thee, and answer thou me. Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth? declare, if thou hast understanding.” Job demanded a trial; God gives him a tour — foundations, morning stars, sea, snow, wild goats, Leviathan — a world ordered, glorious, and wildly beyond his case. Spurgeon, preaching Job's confession in “Job Among the Ashes,” put the result exactly: God displayed only the skirts of his ways, yet Job laid his hand on his mouth — because what Job received was better than an explanation. Hear Job say it: “I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee. Wherefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes.” The movement of the whole book is in those two verbs: heard to seeth. Job's questions were not answered. Job met the Answerer.

And then the restoration — and read its condition carefully, because it is easy to miss: “the LORD turned the captivity of Job, when he prayed for his friends.” Not when his friends apologised. Not when the losses were explained. When the wounded man prayed for the very men who had misjudged him for thirty chapters, his own captivity turned. The friends who came with a system leave carried by the intercession of the man their system condemned.

The week closes with a quiet doorway: the Psalter opens. Psalms 1–8 set the prayer book's two ways before you — the man who delights in the law, “like a tree planted by the rivers of water, that bringeth forth his fruit in his season.” After Job's argument, Israel's prayers begin, in words God himself gives. That ordering is worth savouring: the people who argue honestly with God are then taught, by God, how to pray to him.

The doctrine: suffering is not a reliable ledger of sin; God vindicates honest argument over tidy falsehood, and his presence — not an explanation — is his final answer. The practice: do Job's two acts this week. First, bring one unanswered thing to God honestly, in speech as plain as Job's, without dressing it up. Second, pray — by name, out loud — for one person who has misjudged you. Job's captivity turned at exactly that point. Do not skip to the restoration. Pray for your friends, and let God do the turning.

Argue honestly, then pray for your friends — hearing becomes seeing when God himself draws near.
