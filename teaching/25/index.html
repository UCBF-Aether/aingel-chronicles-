Sabbath Teaching · Week 25

The Man Who Argued with God

Sabbath, April 10, 2027 · Readings: 3 Meqabyan 18–21; Job 1–42; Psalms 1–8

Did you notice what you just did? You read the whole of Job in a single week — all forty-two chapters, the whole argument, from the ash heap to the whirlwind — after finishing the last chapters of 3 Meqabyan. Few weeks in this year ask so much of a reader, and few repay it so well. Job is introduced as blameless and upright, and then, in two chapters of catastrophe, he loses his wealth, his children, and his health. What follows is not a story so much as a long, magnificent argument: Job on the ash heap, scraping his sores, and his friends arriving with explanations.

The friends' speeches are the week's long middle, and they share one tidy logic: suffering must be punishment, so Job must confess the sin that caused it. Job answers with the book's real courage. He will not confess what he has not done, he will not call his comforters' cruelty wisdom, and he will not let go of either his innocence or his God. He argues — with his friends, with his circumstances, and finally, in speech after speech, he demands a hearing with God himself. It is not polite faith. It is honest faith, and the book honours it by letting it fill most of forty-two chapters without rebuke.

Then God answers — out of the whirlwind, in chapters 38–41 — and the answer is, famously, not an explanation. “Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth? declare, if thou hast understanding” (Job 38:4). Instead of reasons, Job is given a tour: the foundations of the earth, the morning stars singing together, the wild creatures in their freedom, a world ordered and glorious far beyond his case. It is enough — not because his questions were answered, but because he has met the Answerer. And the restoration, when it comes in chapter 42, comes alongside Job praying for the very friends who misjudged him: “the LORD turned the captivity of Job, when he prayed for his friends” (42:10).

And then, in the same week, a door opens quietly at the close: the Psalter. Psalms 1–8 set the prayer book's two ways before you — the tree planted by the rivers of water, and the way of the ungodly that perishes. After Job's argument, Israel's prayers begin. That is no accident of ordering to savour: after arguing with God, God's people learn to pray to him, in words he himself has given.

Argue honestly if you must — then pray for your friends, and take the words God gives.
