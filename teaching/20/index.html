Sabbath Teaching · Week 20

The Return Begins

Sabbath, March 6, 2027 · Readings: 1 Enoch 85–108; Ezra 1–10; Nehemiah 1–3

What does a beginning sound like after long ruin? This week it sounds like two noises at once, and the text refuses to separate them. But start where Ezra starts: with the decree you already heard at the close of Chronicles, now opening a book instead of ending one. Cyrus stirs, the vessels of God's house are counted out of a pagan treasury, the families are named name by name, and a people goes up. The return begins as paperwork and obedience — altar first, then feast, then foundation.

Now open Ezra 3 and read the foundation scene like a preacher, watching the verbs. The builders lay the foundation. The priests take their places “in their apparel with trumpets,” the Levites with cymbals — dressed for a finished temple while standing on bare footings. They sing the old refrain, “because he is good, for his mercy endureth for ever toward Israel.” And the response splits in two: “many of the priests and Levites and chief of the fathers, who were ancient men, that had seen the first house, when the foundation of this house was laid before their eyes, wept with a loud voice; and many shouted aloud for joy: So that the people could not discern the noise of the shout of joy from the noise of the weeping of the people.”

Preachers have always stopped at that verse, and rightly. The classic exposition of Ezra 3 (the Bible Hub sermon outline drawn from the old Expositor's tradition) notices what the scene's order teaches: the people spent months laying foundations lower than stone — unity, reverence, obedience, self-sacrifice — before the first visible stone was laid, and the joy that followed was “the joy of the morning” after a seventy-year night, made sweeter, not spoiled, by the sorrow mixed into it. That is close reading, and it is true to the text. The weeping old men are not faithless. They are the only ones who know exactly what was lost. The shouting young ones are not naive. They are the only ones who never saw it, and can simply rejoice that anything stands at all. Ezra puts both sounds in one verse and calls the mixture praise. God does not ask the returning church to agree on one emotion before he accepts the song.

Beside this beginning stands an ending. Enoch closes this week — the dream-visions of chapters 85–90 retelling all history in beasts and shepherds, and the seer's final blessing left for those who come after him. The old seer's book shuts looking forward. The exiles' book opens looking home. Read in one week, they rhyme: God's people live by a word about what comes next.

And then Nehemiah hears a report in a foreign palace: “the wall of Jerusalem also is broken down, and the gates thereof are burned with fire.” His response is the week's third sound — neither shout nor public weeping, but this: “I sat down and wept, and mourned certain days, and fasted, and prayed.” Before the night ride around the walls, before “let us rise up and build,” there is a cupbearer sitting down in the sorrow. Foundations, in this week's books, are always laid by people who first let the ruin reach them.

The doctrine: a true beginning is God's work before it is ours — decree, altar, foundation — and it can hold joy and grief in the same sound without either cancelling the other. The practice: start small and start dressed. Take one broken thing you have been waiting to feel ready to rebuild — a prayer habit, a relationship, a duty — and lay only its foundation this week: one altar-act (a first prayer, a first apology, a first hour given), done properly, in order, with thanksgiving spoken aloud. Do not despise the footing because you remember the former house. Shout and weep if you must. Lay the stone.

A foundation laid is still a beginning — shout and weep together, and lay the stone.
