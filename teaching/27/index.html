Sabbath Teaching · Week 27

The Prayer at the End of the Psalms

Sabbath, April 24, 2027 · Readings: Psalms 84–151; Proverbs 1–9

Why does this prayer book refuse to end at the border most Bibles draw? You began the week in Psalm 84, longing for God's house — "How amiable are thy tabernacles, O LORD of hosts!" — and "My soul longeth, yea, even fainteth for the courts of the LORD: my heart and my flesh crieth out for the living God." You ended at Psalm 150, where everything is praise: "Let every thing that hath breath praise the LORD. Praise ye the LORD." Spurgeon, closing his Treasury of David on that psalm, called it the last summit of the mountain-chain of Psalms, a rapture that stays not to argue or explain but cries, "Praise him." And yet in this canon, after the summit, there are two more stops — and both are lowly.

The first is Psalm 151, David's own song, printed here from Charles Thomson's 1808 English Septuagint. Read its shape closely. It opens with smallness, not greatness: "I was little among my brethren, and the youngest of my father's family. I fed my father's flocks." The hands come next: "My hands had made an organ; and my fingers had tuned a psaltery." Then the choosing, and notice who does every verb that matters: "He sent his messenger and took me from my father's flocks and anointed me with anointing oil." His brothers are named only to be passed by: "My brothers were comely and great; but the Lord did not delight in them." Even the victory is told so that God keeps the credit: "I went out to meet the Philistine, and he cursed me by his idols." "But I drew his own sword and cut off his head, and took away reproach from the children of Israel." David's last psalm is a memoir of being little and being taken. That is the doctrine: God delights not in the comely and great, but in the shepherd he chooses.

Then comes the Prayer of Manasseh, the Psalter's appendix, fifteen verses from the King James Apocrypha, handed down under the name of the king remembered elsewhere as an idolater who humbled himself. Its centre is a counted sin and an uncountable mercy. "for I have sinned above the number of the sands of the sea. My transgressions, O Lord, are multiplied: my transgressions are multiplied, and I am not worthy to behold and see the height of heaven for the multitude of mine iniquities." Against that arithmetic stands one sentence about God: "but thy merciful promise is unmeasurable and unsearchable;" And its posture is the one line to carry home: "Now therefore I bow the knee of mine heart, beseeching thee of grace." Not the knee of the body only — the knee of the heart. A king bows inside, where no one can perform it for him.

So the Psalter ends wider here on purpose: a little shepherd taken, and a guilty king heard. No one is too small to be chosen, and no one who bows is too guilty to be received. That is not a feeling; it is what these two texts, placed back to back, actually say.

Then Proverbs opens, and the voice changes from singing to calling. "Wisdom crieth without; she uttereth her voice in the streets:" She does not wait in a library. Her foundation is stated in one line: "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge: but fools despise wisdom and instruction." And her worth is stated just as plainly: "For wisdom is better than rubies; and all the things that may be desired are not to be compared to it."

Live it this week in three small obediences. First, pray Manasseh's posture once a day — name your sin without excusing it, and bow the knee of your heart, not just your head. Second, when you feel little and passed over, remember that littleness is Psalm 151's opening line, not its disqualification. Third, listen for Wisdom in ordinary places — the street, the kitchen, the workplace — because that is where she says she cries. The songs taught you how to praise and how to repent; now let Wisdom teach you how to walk.

Bow the knee of your heart — the little are taken, the guilty are heard, and Wisdom is calling in the streets.
