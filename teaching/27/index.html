Sabbath Teaching · Week 27

The Prayer at the End of the Psalms

Sabbath, April 24, 2027 · Readings: Psalms 84–151; Proverbs 1–9

Did you notice, as you finished the Psalter this week, that it does not end where most Bibles end it? Psalm 150 still closes with its great call — "Let every thing that hath breath praise the LORD" — but in this canon the songbook keeps going for one more psalm, and then, quietly, for one more prayer. Watch for what that wider ending is teaching you about who gets to pray, and how a prayer book can close.

Psalm 151 is David's own song of his youth. It is not in the King James Version, so here it is printed from Charles Thomson's 1808 English Septuagint. It is short — seven verses with its superscription — and it tells the Goliath story in David's own voice, looking back: "I was little among my brethren, and the youngest of my father's family. I fed my father's flocks." The hands that made an organ and tuned a psaltery, he says, were taken from the flocks and anointed. His brothers were comely and great, but the Lord did not delight in them. He went out to meet the Philistine, and he took away reproach from the children of Israel. Read it slowly. This is the shepherd remembering, in old age's book, the morning everything changed — and giving the credit entirely away from himself.

Then, after Psalm 151, stands the Prayer of Manasseh, printed here as the Psalter's appendix — a companion to the Psalms rather than a Psalm itself. It comes from the King James Apocrypha, and it is only fifteen verses long. Tradition hands it down under the name of Manasseh, king of Judah, remembered elsewhere as an idolater who later humbled himself and prayed. The prayer gives that turning its words. There is confession without excuse: sins multiplied above the number of the sands of the sea. There is wonder at God's patience: repentance appointed, mercy unmeasurable. And at its centre there is a posture worth carrying into your own prayers: "Now therefore I bow the knee of mine heart, beseeching thee of grace." A king, on the inside, on his knees.

So the prayer book ends twice over with humility — a shepherd who was little, and a king who was guilty, both heard. And then this week Proverbs opens, and Wisdom herself takes to the streets, calling aloud in the chief place of concourse, refusing to whisper. The fear of the LORD, she says, is the beginning of knowledge. The songs have taught you how to pray; now Wisdom will teach you how to live what you prayed.

The littlest shepherd and the guiltiest king were both heard — bow the knee of your heart, and pray.
