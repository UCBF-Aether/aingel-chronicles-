Sabbath Teaching · Week 22

The Books of the Hidden Years

Sabbath, March 20, 2027 · Readings: Tobit 1–14; Judith 1–16; Esther 1–17; 1 Meqabyan 1–11

What does faithfulness look like when God's people have no king, no army, and no temple they can reach? This week the shelf widens suddenly — story after story from the hidden years under foreign rule — and each book answers with a different face. Tobit gives us the road. Judith gives us courage. Esther gives us the queen. And a fourth voice, Ethiopia's own, opens at the week's edge.

Tobit first. An old exile in Nineveh, blinded, who kept burying the abandoned dead when burying them was dangerous, sends his son Tobias on a journey he cannot make himself. God sends a travelling companion: Raphael, the angel unawares, walking chapters 5–12 beside the young man as guide and protector. The book's genius is its ordinariness — a road, a fish, a marriage, a homecoming, sight restored. Heaven's help does not arrive with trumpets. It arrives as a hired guide who knows the road. And when Raphael finally names himself, his farewell teaching is as plain as doctrine gets: “Prayer is good with fasting and alms and righteousness.” Not prayer alone as a theory — prayer with fasting, prayer with money given away, prayer with righteousness done. Catholic preaching on this passage has long made the same observation from the other side: Raphael says he presented Tobit's prayers before the Glory — and what he names, when he lists what God noticed, is the buried dead and the dinner left uneaten to bury them. In Tobit, God keeps a record of mercies done in secret.

Judith answers differently. Bethulia is besieged, its water failing, and its leaders make a bargain that sounds pious and is not: they will give God five days, and surrender if he has not acted by the deadline. Judith — a widow who has spent years fasting and praying — summons them and rebukes the bargain to its face: “who are ye that have tempted God this day, and stand instead of God among the children of men?” Setting God a deadline, she argues, is not faith but its opposite: you do not summon the Almighty like a servant late with water. Then she prays — chapter 9, at length, on her face, in ashes — before she acts at all. The order is the teaching. Prayer is not her preparation for courage as a warm-up; it is the source of it. Only then does she go out to the enemy camp, trusting God, and the siege breaks.

Esther stands third, and here in her fuller, seventeen-chapter form: the familiar ten chapters first, and after them, as chapters 11–17, the Greek additions this canon keeps — Mordecai's dream framing the whole story, the royal letters in full, and the prayers of Mordecai and Esther restored to the open. John Donne, preaching on Esther's fast, noticed the detail the additions underline: Esther did not only ask others to fast for her — she and her maids fasted likewise. The queen put her own body where she asked her people to put theirs. In the fuller Esther, the hidden hand of God gets a visible companion: a praying people, and a queen on her face before she is on her feet before the king.

And then 1 Meqabyan opens — Ethiopia's book of confessors under an idol-demanding king, whose witness the next two Sabbaths will take up. Keep the threads where this week leaves them: an old man, a traveller, a widow, a queen, and confessors — all keeping faith inside somebody else's empire, and heaven, in every story, keeping faith with them.

The doctrine: under foreign rule and hidden years, God's instruments are not armies but prayer joined to mercy, courage that refuses to bargain with God, and fasting that begins with the one who calls it. The practice this week, one from each book: do one mercy in secret that interrupts your own table, as Tobit left his dinner; refuse one deadline you have set God — surrender the timetable in prayer, as Judith demanded; and if you ask anyone to pray or fast for you this week, go first yourself, as Esther did.

Pray, give, and act bravely — heaven keeps faith with those who keep faith in the hidden years.
