Sabbath Teaching · Week 2

The Long Road of Joseph

Sabbath, October 31, 2026 · Readings: Genesis 32–50; Exodus 1–10

What do you do when the road between a promise and its fulfilment runs through a pit and a prison? This week began with Jacob alone at night, wrestling until morning, and hearing a new name spoken over his limp: “Thy name shall be called no more Jacob, but Israel: for as a prince hast thou power with God and with men, and hast prevailed.” It ends with Jacob’s family grown great in Egypt, and a shadow already falling across them: “Now there arose up a new king over Egypt, which knew not Joseph.” Between those two verses lies the long road of Joseph, and the text wants you to walk it slowly, because its whole teaching hides in its length.

Count what the road costs, and notice what the narrator refuses to say. Joseph is seventeen when envy throws him into a pit and sells him. He serves in Potiphar’s house and is falsely accused. He interprets dreams in prison and is forgotten by the man he helped — two full years of forgetting, the text says plainly. At no point in those chapters does anyone explain to Joseph what God is doing. There is no voice from heaven in the pit, no angel in the prison. What there is, repeated like a heartbeat in Genesis 39, is one quiet sentence: “And the LORD was with Joseph.” In the house: “And the LORD was with Joseph, and he was a prosperous man.” In the prison: “But the LORD was with Joseph, and shewed him mercy, and gave him favour in the sight of the keeper of the prison.” Presence, not explanation, is what God gives on the long road.

That is why Joseph can say, when he finally stands revealed before his brothers, what no bitter man could say. First comes the unveiling itself. Spurgeon, preaching on Genesis 45 in “Jesus and His Brethren,” lingers over the moment Joseph clears the room and weeps aloud, and observes: “It is the way of love to desire to make itself known.” Joseph does not toy with his brothers forever. Love ends the suspense: “I am Joseph your brother, whom ye sold into Egypt.” Then, in Genesis 45, the interpretation: “Now therefore be not grieved, nor angry with yourselves, that ye sold me hither: for God did send me before you to preserve life.” And at the very end of Genesis, after Jacob’s death, when the brothers fear the reckoning has only been postponed, Joseph gives the sentence the whole road was travelling toward: “Fear not: for am I in the place of God?” “But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive.”

Read that sentence word by word, because it will not let you soften either half of it. “ye thought evil” — Joseph names the deed truly; forgiveness here is not amnesia, and it is not pretending the pit was a nice place. “but God meant it unto good” — the same events carry two meanings, and God’s meaning outlasts theirs. And forgiveness does not stop at words. Joseph feeds the family that sold him: “Now therefore fear ye not: I will nourish you, and your little ones. And he comforted them, and spake kindly unto them.” Doctrine stated plainly: God is sovereign over evil without being its author, and the forgiven-by-God man becomes a nourisher of the very people who wronged him. Providence is not a theory in this chapter. It is bread, given kindly, to brothers who came to buy it in fear.

Then Exodus opens, and the nourished family becomes an enslaved one. Do not miss the honesty of that sequence. The good God meant did not exempt Israel from Pharaoh’s brick pits; it preserved a people through whom the story could go on. Already a child is hidden, drawn from the water, and called at a burning bush — but Moses himself will spend forty years learning the long road before he leads anyone down it.

So practise Joseph’s sentence this week before you can see its ending. First, name evil plainly where you have suffered it; Joseph did, and truth-telling is part of forgiveness, not its enemy. Second, refuse God’s place: vengeance is the place of God, and Joseph would not sit in it. Third, do one nourishing act toward someone who has not earned it from you — a kindness spoken, a need quietly met. You may not yet see the good God means. Joseph could not see it from the pit either. What he had there, and what you have, is the sentence that held in both places: the Lord was with him.

Name the evil plainly, refuse God’s place, and nourish anyway — the Lord is with you on the long road.
