Sabbath Teaching · Week 23

Not the Maccabees

Sabbath, March 27, 2027 · Readings: 1 Meqabyan 12–36; 2 Meqabyan 1–9

Is this the book of the Maccabees? Nearly every first-time reader asks it, and this Sabbath we answer it plainly, because the confusion hides a treasure. No. Meqabyan is not Maccabees. The two sound alike in English and share almost nothing else — different kings, different martyrs, different stories. The books of Maccabees tell of Judas and the revolt against Antiochus. This canon — the Ethiopian canon — has no 1–2 Maccabees at all. It has the three Meqabyan instead: 1 Meqabyan in thirty-six chapters, 2 and 3 Meqabyan in twenty-one chapters each, preserved in Ge'ez by the Ethiopian Orthodox Church and translated anew, directly from the Ge'ez, for this edition. Scholarly writing on these books is still scarce and recent — they were long among the least-read canonical books in the world — and that scarcity is itself part of the story: you are reading a witness one church carried for centuries while the rest of the Christian world let it go.

So what have you actually been reading this week? You finished 1 Meqabyan and opened the second book, and by now its shape should be clear. The narrative spine is thin on purpose. Kings rise who demand idolatry — who require God's people to bow where they cannot bow — and the books keep returning to the same response, stated early as a charge to the faithful: “Remember, my brethren, and forget not what the ancients told you.” Memory is Meqabyan's first weapon. Its heroes are not conquerors but confessors: believers who answer power with trust, strengthen one another, and refuse. And around their witness the book keeps turning its great doctrinal promise over and over, like a stone in the hand: “And He shall be unto them a helper in their tribulation — unto them that love His name; and they shall be kept, and He will forgive them all their sins.” A helper in tribulation — notice, not always a rescuer out of it. The promise is presence and keeping, not exemption.

Hear also how the book ends, because endings reveal what a book believes the story is about. 1 Meqabyan does not close with a battle won but with the resurrection: “the righteous that have done good shall reign with Him for ever and ever.” The deliverances in these chapters — “And thus God delivered the children of Israel by the hand of Mebqeyus and his brethren” — are real, and the book celebrates them. But they are not the final deliverance. The confessors can afford to lose an earthly verdict because they do not believe the earthly verdict is the last one.

Why, then, are the Maccabees absent from your year? Simply because this canon kept other books. Every canon is a history of keeping, and Ethiopia's keeping ran wider and different — the Maccabean era itself is not lost to you; Josippon will tell that history when the plan reaches him later on. For now, let Meqabyan be itself: not a substitute for a missing book, but a treasure with its own name.

The doctrine: God helps his confessors in tribulation, keeps them, forgives them — and the resurrection, not the battlefield, is where their vindication is finally read out. The practice this week is Meqabyan's own first weapon: remember, out loud. Tell one story of a believer who held a confession at cost — from these chapters, from church history, from your own family — to someone who needs the strength of it. Confessors are made by other confessors' stories. And where you face pressure to bow in some small, reasonable-looking way, answer as these books teach: together, out loud, and without matching the persecutor's methods.

Meqabyan is its own witness — remember the ancients, and answer power with trust.
