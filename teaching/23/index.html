Sabbath Teaching · Week 23

Not the Maccabees

Sabbath, March 27, 2027 · Readings: 1 Meqabyan 12–36; 2 Meqabyan 1–9

Is this the book of the Maccabees? It is the question nearly every first-time reader asks, and the answer matters, so this Sabbath let us say it plainly: no. The Meqabyan books are not the Maccabees. They are entirely different works that happen to sound alike in English, and this canon — the Ethiopian canon — has the three Meqabyan and no 1–2 Maccabees at all. What you have been reading is a witness the Ethiopian Orthodox Church alone preserved in its Bible, and for this edition the three books — 1 Meqabyan in thirty-six chapters, 2 and 3 Meqabyan in twenty-one chapters each — were translated anew, directly from the Ge'ez, because no usable English for them was waiting on a shelf. You are reading them, in other words, about as directly as an English reader can.

So what have you actually been reading? This week you finished 1 Meqabyan and opened the second book, and the shape of the witness is now clear. Here are rulers who demand idolatry — who require God's people to bow where they cannot bow — and here are families and fellow believers who refuse. The heroes of Meqabyan are not conquerors. They are confessors: men and women who answer power with simple trust in God, suffer rather than compromise, and strengthen one another to stand. Around their witness the books keep turning the same truths over: proud and violent power does not escape God's judgement; repentance is never wasted; there is hope beyond death; and false teaching — the smooth voice that would make compromise sound reasonable — is a danger as real as any sword.

That is why the Ethiopian church kept these books as a school of endurance. They teach ordinary believers how to hold a confession when holding it costs: not by matching the persecutor's violence, but by refusing, together, out loud, and to the end if need be.

Why, then, are the Maccabees absent? Simply because this canon kept other books — every canon is a history of keeping, and Ethiopia's keeping ran wider and different. The Maccabean era itself is not lost to your year of reading: it is history that Josippon will tell when the plan reaches him, later on. For now, let Meqabyan be itself — not a substitute for a missing book, but a treasure with its own name, its own voice, and its own martyrs, newly Englished from the Ge'ez for readers meeting them, very often, for the first time.

Do not mistake the name — Meqabyan is its own witness, and its confessors answer power with trust.
