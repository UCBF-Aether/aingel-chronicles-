Sabbath Teaching · Week 8

From Jericho's Walls to Shechem's Stones

Sabbath, December 12, 2026 · Readings: Joshua 6–24; Judges 1–7

What does a conquered wall teach, and what does a farewell stone require? This week ran almost the whole book of Joshua, from Jericho’s gate to Joshua’s grave, and into the first cycle of Judges. Begin at Jericho, and notice what Israel’s first battle in the land does not contain: no siege engines, no assault, no strategy anyone would write in a manual. Priests carry the ark, trumpets sound, the people march in silence for six days, and on the seventh day they march seven times and shout. “So the people shouted when the priests blew with the trumpets: and it came to pass, when the people heard the sound of the trumpet, and the people shouted with a great shout, that the wall fell down flat, so that the people went up into the city, every man straight before him, and they took the city.” The first victory of the conquest is deliberately unusable as a technique. You cannot repeat Jericho at the next city — and Israel does not; at Ai, immediately after, they are beaten, because there is hidden sin in the camp. The lesson set at the land’s very gate is that the gift is God’s, won by obedience to his word, and that nearness to him matters more than momentum.

The long middle of the book refuses triumphalism. There is a covenant made too hastily with Gibeon, long campaigns in south and north, land divided by lot, cities of refuge appointed for the manslayer, towns given to the Levites. Joshua grows old with the work unfinished — the book says so honestly. Possessing a promised land, it turns out, is slower and more ordinary than taking Jericho: boundaries walked, inheritances settled, justice provided for in advance for the day someone kills unintentionally. Faithfulness has paperwork. God is in that too.

Then, at Shechem, the old leader gathers the tribes for the last time, and the farewell sermon follows the Bible’s own pattern: first the story, then the choice. Joshua retells it all — Abraham’s father serving other gods beyond the flood, Egypt, the sea, the wilderness, the land with cities Israel did not build and vineyards Israel did not plant. Gratitude is given its full length before obligation is given one verse. Then the choice, stated with its alternatives honestly named: “And if it seem evil unto you to serve the LORD, choose you this day whom ye will serve; whether the gods which your fathers served that were on the other side of the flood, or the gods of the Amorites, in whose land ye dwell: but as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD.” Spurgeon, preaching this verse in “Decision — Illustrated by the Case of Joshua,” saw that Joshua was confronting a people outwardly serving the Lord while privately keeping other gods, and that the old man’s refusal of half-heartedness was the sermon: as the record of his preaching puts it, “He demanded a decision and rightly so.” Joshua even argues with the people’s quick yes — you cannot serve the Lord, he warns; he is a holy God — because a vow made lightly at Shechem would be broken cheaply at home. When they insist, he makes them witnesses against themselves and sets up a great stone under an oak: “this stone shall be a witness unto us.” Stones, in this book, are memory made heavy enough not to drift.

Then Judges opens, and you can watch the choice decay within a generation. The conquest stalls tribe by tribe; a people arises that knew not the Lord; the cycle begins — forsaking, oppression, crying, deliverance — and this week ends with Gideon: a fearful man threshing wheat in hiding, greeted by heaven as a “mighty man of valour,” and sent against Midian with an army deliberately cut down to three hundred, “lest Israel vaunt themselves against me, saying, Mine own hand hath saved me.” Jericho’s lesson, repeated in a minor key: the victory will be God’s, so that the credit cannot be stolen.

Doctrine stated plainly: a gift received is not yet a faith kept. Every generation must choose for itself, deliberately, with the story told first and the cost counted honestly — and what a generation chooses carelessly, the next inherits as a cycle.

So choose again this week, concretely. Tell the story first: recount to your household, briefly, what God has done — then make one decision of allegiance you have been leaving vague, and make it visible: a practice begun, an idol quietly kept at home removed, a commitment spoken aloud to a witness who can remind you of it. Set up your stone — some fixed thing that will outlast the mood in which you chose.

Tell the story, count the cost, and choose this day — then set up something that remembers for you.
