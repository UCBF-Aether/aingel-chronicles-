Sabbath Teaching · Week 36

The Watchtower and the Twelve

Sabbath, June 26, 2027 · Readings: Amos 6–9; Micah 1–7; Joel 1–3; Obadiah 1; Jonah 1–4; Nahum 1–3; Habakkuk 1–3; Zephaniah 1–3; Haggai 1–2; Zechariah 1–14; Malachi 1–4

What do you do when God's answer troubles you more than your question did? Habakkuk knows. This week you read the whole remaining company of the Twelve — Amos finished, Micah, Joel, Obadiah's single chapter, Jonah, Nahum, Habakkuk, Zephaniah, Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi — a whole choir of shorter prophets in seven days. Let one of them teach you how to stand inside the whole chorus: Habakkuk, on his watchtower.

His little book is a conversation, not a sermon. He looks at violence and wrongdoing in his own land and asks God plainly how long it will stand. God's answer — that a proud empire will be raised up as an instrument, and then judged itself — troubles the prophet even more, and he does the bravest thing in his book: he asks again, and then he stations himself. "I will stand upon my watch, and set me upon the tower, and will watch to see what he will say unto me." The answer he receives holds a sentence the whole Bible after him leans on: "the just shall live by his faith." And his book closes not with explanations but with a song, chapter 3, where he chooses his joy in advance of his circumstances: although the fig tree shall not blossom, neither shall fruit be in the vines, the fields yield no meat and the stalls stand empty — "yet I will rejoice in the LORD, I will joy in the God of my salvation." That is watchtower faith: questions asked honestly, answers waited for standing up, joy kept when the fields fail.

The week's other voices sing in the same key. Jonah runs from his commission, is carried back by a great fish, preaches one short, reluctant sentence — and Nineveh, the feared enemy city, repents, while the prophet sulks outside its walls and God has the last word on compassion. Micah, a country prophet addressing two capitals at once, gathers the whole law into the sentence you may know best in all the Twelve: "He hath shewed thee, O man, what is good; and what doth the LORD require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?" Around them the rest call out: Joel's Spirit poured on all flesh, Zephaniah's God who ends his book singing over his people, Zechariah's humble king riding on a donkey, Haggai's tired builders told to consider their ways.

And then Malachi, the last page. The Old Covenant shelf closes with arguments answered, a book of remembrance written for those who feared the LORD, and a promise of a messenger and of Elijah sent before the great day. The prophets do not end with an ending. They end looking forward — and so, now, do you.

Stand on your watchtower: ask honestly, wait standing, and rejoice before the fields recover.
