Sabbath Teaching · Week 36

The Watchtower and the Twelve

Sabbath, June 26, 2027 · Readings: Amos 6–9; Micah 1–7; Joel 1–3; Obadiah 1; Jonah 1–4; Nahum 1–3; Habakkuk 1–3; Zephaniah 1–3; Haggai 1–2; Zechariah 1–14; Malachi 1–4

What do you do when God's answer troubles you more than your question did? That is not a hypothetical this week; it is Habakkuk's book, three chapters long, and it is the most honest prayer manual among the Twelve you have just finished — Amos completed, Micah, Joel, Obadiah's single chapter, Jonah, Nahum, Habakkuk, Zephaniah, Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi, a whole choir in seven days. Let Habakkuk teach you how to stand inside the chorus.

His book is a conversation, not a sermon. He opens by crying out at violence in his own land and asking how long God will look on. God's answer — a proud empire raised up as an instrument, and judged in its turn — troubles him more, not less. Many believers, at that point, either stop praying or start pretending. Habakkuk does neither. He asks again, and then he stations himself: "I will stand upon my watch, and set me upon the tower, and will watch to see what he will say unto me, and what I shall answer when I am reproved." Picture it. He does not storm off the wall; he climbs it. He expects to be answered, and he even expects to be reproved — "what I shall answer when I am reproved" — and he puts himself where an answer can find him. The answer, when it comes, contains the sentence the whole later Bible leans on: "Behold, his soul which is lifted up is not upright in him: but the just shall live by his faith."

And then chapter 3, which is not an explanation but a song, sung in advance of any change in circumstances: "Although the fig tree shall not blossom, neither shall fruit be in the vines; the labour of the olive shall fail, and the fields shall yield no meat; the flock shall be cut off from the fold, and there shall be no herd in the stalls:" "Yet I will rejoice in the LORD, I will joy in the God of my salvation." "The LORD God is my strength, and he will make my feet like hinds’ feet, and he will make me to walk upon mine high places. To the chief singer on my stringed instruments." Count the losses: blossom, fruit, labour, yield, flock, herd — a total agricultural collapse, itemised. The rejoicing is not denial; it is a decision made standing on the tower, with empty stalls in full view. Spurgeon, in his Morning and Evening devotion for October 19, called Habakkuk's song a divine song — a song in the night that no man makes of himself, but which God himself gives. That is exactly the text's claim: this joy is received, not manufactured, and it is received by a man who stayed on the watch.

The week's other voices sing in the same key. Micah gathers the whole requirement into one sentence: "He hath shewed thee, O man, what is good; and what doth the LORD require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?" Jonah preaches one reluctant sentence and a feared enemy city repents — "And God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way; and God repented of the evil, that he had said that he would do unto them; and he did it not." Zechariah shows the king arriving lowly, riding upon an ass. And Malachi closes the shelf with remembrance and a promise: "Then they that feared the LORD spake often one to another: and the LORD hearkened, and heard it, and a book of remembrance was written before him for them that feared the LORD, and that thought upon his name." "Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the coming of the great and dreadful day of the LORD:"

State the doctrine plainly. The just live by faith — not by sight of blossom, fruit, or herd. Questioning God honestly is not unbelief; abandoning the watch is. Live it this week: take one troubling question to God in prayer and stay on the tower for the answer — return to it daily instead of dropping it. Practise Micah's triad in one concrete act each: do one just thing, show one mercy, walk humbly in one conversation where you would rather win. And sing Habakkuk's third chapter aloud once, over whatever empty stall you are looking at, before the fields recover.

Stand on the tower, ask honestly, wait standing — and rejoice before the fields recover.
