Sabbath Teaching · Week 3

The Sea and the Song

Sabbath, November 7, 2026 · Readings: Exodus 11–37

Why does Israel sing before it does anything else in freedom? This week's reading, Exodus 11–37, moves from a dark night in Egypt to a mountain wrapped in cloud. It begins with the last plague and the first Passover: a lamb killed, blood on the doorposts, a meal eaten with loins girded and staff in hand, and a people walking out while Egypt buries its firstborn. Every year after, that night will be remembered at a table. Deliverance in this book is never just an idea. It is eaten, told, and passed to children who ask what it means.

Then comes the sea. Israel, trapped between Pharaoh's army and the water, cries out and complains in the same breath — and Moses answers, “stand still, and see the salvation of the LORD.” The sea opens, Israel crosses on dry ground, and the army that pursued them is thrown into the water. Only then, on the far shore, does the singing begin: “Then sang Moses and the children of Israel this song unto the LORD… I will sing unto the LORD, for he hath triumphed gloriously: the horse and his rider hath he thrown into the sea.” Miriam takes a timbrel, and the women answer her.

Notice the order. The song comes after the sea, not before it. Israel does not sing its way into courage and then get delivered. God delivers, and the delivered people discover they have a song. That is why this song will be sung, in one form or another, for the rest of the Bible. It is the sound a rescued people makes when it finally believes the rescue is real.

From the sea, the road leads to Sinai. In chapter 20 God speaks the Ten Words — no other gods, no graven image, the Sabbath kept holy, father and mother honoured, and life, marriage, property, and truth guarded between neighbours. The same God who broke Pharaoh's power now gives his freed people a shape for freedom, so they do not become Egypt to one another. Around those words this week also gives manna in the wilderness, water from the rock, and the first careful plans for a tabernacle.

Watch, then, for how closely rescue and command belong together in Exodus. The Passover says whose you are. The sea says what God has done. The song says how it feels to believe it. And Sinai says how a saved people now lives — not to earn the deliverance behind them, but because it already happened.

Sing on the far shore first, and then learn, gladly, how free people live.
