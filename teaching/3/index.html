Sabbath Teaching · Week 3

The Sea and the Song

Sabbath, November 7, 2026 · Readings: Exodus 11–37

Why does a delivered people sing before it does anything else — and why does the song come after the sea, never before it? This week ran from a dark night in Egypt to a mountain wrapped in cloud. It began with the last plague and the first Passover: a lamb killed at twilight, blood struck on the doorposts, and a meal eaten standing, dressed for a road: “And thus shall ye eat it; with your loins girded, your shoes on your feet, and your staff in your hand; and ye shall eat it in haste: it is the LORD’s passover.” Deliverance in Exodus is never an abstraction. It is eaten, remembered at a table, and explained to children who ask what it means.

Then the sea. Israel is trapped — Pharaoh’s army behind, water ahead — and the people cry out and complain in the same breath, certain they have been brought out only to die. Moses’ answer is the week’s first great sentence, and it is mostly a command to stop: “Fear ye not, stand still, and see the salvation of the LORD, which he will shew to you to day: for the Egyptians whom ye have seen to day, ye shall see them again no more for ever.” Then the second: “The LORD shall fight for you, and ye shall hold your peace.” Notice what Israel contributes to its own rescue at the sea: nothing but standing still and then walking through on dry ground. The fighting is God’s. The walking is theirs. That order — God’s act, then the people’s obedient feet — is the grammar of the whole book.

Only on the far shore does the singing begin, and its timing is its theology: “Then sang Moses and the children of Israel this song unto the LORD, and spake, saying, I will sing unto the LORD, for he hath triumphed gloriously: the horse and his rider hath he thrown into the sea.” Spurgeon, preaching this text in “Jubilate,” opens with the observation this teaching borrows with gratitude: this is, as he puts it, “the very first of those sacred songs preserved in Scripture” — and it is first in merit, he argues, as well as in time. Israel does not sing to summon courage so that God might deliver them. God delivers, and the delivered discover they have a song. Praise, in Exodus, is not the price of rescue. It is the sound rescue makes when it is finally believed.

Hear how the song itself preaches. Its centre is not Israel’s bravery — Israel had shown none at the shore — but a confession in three tenses: “The LORD is my strength and song, and he is become my salvation: he is my God, and I will prepare him an habitation; my father’s God, and I will exalt him.” My strength — present. He is become my salvation — accomplished. My father’s God — inherited. Miriam takes a timbrel, the women answer with the refrain, and the song becomes the people’s memory carried in their mouths: teachable, repeatable, singable by children not yet born. A people that can sing its deliverance can survive a wilderness; the song goes where monuments cannot.

From the sea the road climbs to Sinai, and chapter 20 gives the delivered people the Ten Words — beginning not with a command but with who is speaking, and fenced round with gifts like this one: “Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy.” The same God who broke Pharaoh now shapes freedom, so that Israel does not become Egypt to itself: no other gods, no image, no theft, no false witness, parents honoured, neighbours protected in life, marriage, property, and truth. Rescue and command belong together, in that order. The Passover says whose you are. The sea says what God has done. The song says how it feels to believe it. Sinai says how a saved people now lives — not to earn the deliverance behind them, but because it already happened.

Practise the order this week. First, stand still once a day: before you strategise, rehearse aloud one deliverance God has already accomplished for you, and thank him for it specifically. Second, sing after deliverance, not instead of obedience — let praise be your first response to mercy received, today, not when life improves. Third, take one command from the Ten Words and obey it in a concrete neighbour-ward way this week: a truth told at cost, a parent honoured in deed, a Sabbath actually kept. Free people are not formless people. They are people whose shape is gratitude.

Stand still and see what God has done; sing on the far shore; then walk shaped by the gift.
