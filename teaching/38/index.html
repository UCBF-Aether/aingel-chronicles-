Sabbath Teaching · Week 38

How a Book Is Born

Sabbath, July 10, 2027 · Readings: Sirach 50–51; Josippon 1–33

How does a book written more than a thousand years ago actually reach your hands? Not by magic, and not by a single leap. It travels the way a flame travels — from witness to witness, copyist to copyist, each hand either guarding it or endangering it — until one day it arrives as paper and ink in yours. This week's readings let you watch that journey twice: once in a wisdom book closing in worship, and once in a history book whose own chain of custody this edition can name, link by link.

First, close Sirach with gratitude. Its final chapters end the school in praise. Chapter 50 lifts up Simon the high priest: "Simon the high priest, the son of Onias, who in his life repaired the house again, and in his days fortified the temple:" — a man remembered for repairing and fortifying, shown moving through the liturgy in glory, and the chapter turns the memory into a present blessing: "Now therefore bless ye the God of all, which only doeth wondrous things every where, which exalteth our days from the womb, and dealeth with us according to his mercy." Chapter 51 is the teacher's own hymn — "I will thank thee, O Lord and King, and praise thee, O God my Saviour: I do give praise unto thy name:" — and his school door left open at the end: "Draw near unto me, ye unlearned, and dwell in the house of learning." A wisdom book that ends in thanksgiving and an open door has taught its last lesson by example: learning that does not end in praise has stopped one step short.

Then Josippon opens, and the story of the Jewish people begins again from Adam. Josippon is a history in 104 chapters — biblical history retold alongside accounts drawn from older historians — which the Ethiopian church took into its own canon in Ge'ez dress, as the Zena Ayhud, with a Christian redaction. You have read its first thirty-three chapters this week: creation, the patriarchs, the Exodus generation, and the long march of Israel's story beginning to unfold in a historian's voice rather than a prophet's.

Now the honest part, and it is the real teaching of this week. The Josippon in this edition was read for this edition from photographs of a single Ge'ez codex: British Library EAP704/2/3, a manuscript copied in 1595–1605, carrying the church's Christian redaction of the work. That dating and that shelfmark are not decoration; they are the book's birth certificate in this edition. Folio by folio, the photographs were transcribed; chapter by chapter, the transcription was verified; the translation was made from that verified text, and then audited independently — a second reader comparing the English against the Ge'ez and reporting every divergence. That chain — witness, photograph, transcription, verification, translation, audit — is how any ancient book is honestly born into a new language. Every link in it is human, which is precisely why every link in it is checked. Where the chain in other books runs through printed editions, this edition says so just as plainly — received public-domain English where it is received, fresh translation where it is fresh, and no claim stretched past its record.

Why tell you this in a Sabbath teaching? Because trust should never be asked to travel blind, and gratitude should know what it is thanking God for. When you read Josippon this month, you are not reading "a text" in the abstract. You are reading one named manuscript, copied by a named tradition's scribes four centuries ago, photographed, and carried across into English word by word. Somewhere in that chain are hands you will never know, copying in a scriptorium, keeping the flame alight through years when no one alive could have imagined you reading it on a Sabbath in your own language.

A note on grounding, in the same spirit: there is no historic sermon tradition on Josippon to draw on — preachers have not preached this book — so this teaching's sources are the week's two texts themselves and the edition's documented record of its witness. That is stated here rather than dressed up.

Live it this week. Thank God, by name where you can, for the chain behind one book you own — an author, a copyist tradition, a translator, a printer — instead of receiving it as if it fell from the sky. And become a link yourself: copy one passage by hand, slowly, and give it to someone. You will feel, in your own wrist, what four centuries of copyists did for you — and you will understand why every link is checked, and why every link is thanked.

Thank God for the chain of witnesses — one named codex, guarded hand to hand, ending in praise and an open door.
