Sabbath Teaching · Week 38

How a Book Is Born

Sabbath, July 10, 2027 · Readings: Sirach 50–51; Josippon 1–33

How does a book written more than a thousand years ago actually reach your hands? Not by magic, and not by a single leap. It travels the way a flame travels — from witness to witness, copyist to copyist, each hand either guarding it or endangering it — until one day it arrives as paper and ink in yours. This week's new book, Josippon, lets you watch that journey closely, because this edition can tell you exactly which hands carried it last.

First, close Sirach with gratitude. Its final chapters, 50 and 51, end the school in worship: Simon the high priest, son of Onias, who in his lifetime repaired the house and fortified the temple, moving through the liturgy in glory — and then the teacher's own hymn of thanksgiving, and his invitation to the unlearned to draw near and lodge in his house of learning. A wisdom book that ends in praise has taught its last lesson by example.

Then Josippon opens, and the story of the Jewish people begins again from Adam. Josippon is a history in 104 chapters — biblical history retold alongside accounts drawn from older historians — which the Ethiopian church took into its own canon in Ge'ez dress, as the Zena Ayhud, with a Christian redaction. You have read its first thirty-three chapters this week: creation, the patriarchs, the Exodus generation, and the long march of Israel's story beginning to unfold in a historian's voice rather than a prophet's.

Now the honest part, and it is the real teaching of this week. The Josippon in this edition was read for this edition from photographs of a single Ge'ez codex: British Library EAP704/2/3, a manuscript copied in 1595–1605, carrying the church's Christian redaction of the work. Folio by folio, the photographs were transcribed; chapter by chapter, the transcription was verified; the translation was made from that verified text, and then audited independently, a second reader comparing the English against the Ge'ez and reporting every divergence. That chain — witness, photograph, transcription, verification, translation, audit — is how any ancient book is honestly born into a new language. Every link in it is human, which is why every link in it is checked.

Why tell you this in a Sabbath teaching? Because trust should never be asked to travel blind. When you read Josippon this month, you are not reading "a text" in the abstract. You are reading one named manuscript, copied by a named tradition's scribes four centuries ago, photographed, and carried across into English word by word. Next week you will learn what the translators did at the places where that manuscript grows hard to read. For today, give thanks for the chain — and for every copyist in it who chose to guard the flame.

Thank God for the chain of witnesses — and read as one who knows the flame was guarded hand to hand.
