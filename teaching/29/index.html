Sabbath Teaching · Week 29

Vanity of Vanities, Song of Songs

Sabbath, May 8, 2027 · Readings: Ecclesiastes 2–12; Song of Songs 1–8; Isaiah 1–28

Can a sigh and a song both tell the truth? This week the Preacher finished his long experiment and the Song opened its garden, and the canon sets them side by side without apologising for either. Hear the Preacher first, on his own terms, because his book is not unbelief — it is an audit. He tried wisdom, pleasure, building, and wealth, and reported what each returned. His final chapter is where the audit closes, and it deserves close reading, not a shrug.

Chapter 12 opens with an imperative, not a mood: "Remember now thy Creator in the days of thy youth, while the evil days come not, nor the years draw nigh, when thou shalt say, I have no pleasure in them;" Spurgeon's verse exposition on this chapter lingers over the poem that follows — the keepers of the house trembling, the grinders ceasing because they are few, the windows darkened — reading it, plainly, as the body in old age: hands, legs, teeth, eyes. The Preacher is not being obscure; he is being exact. Youth is the time to remember, because the house will not always stand straight. Then the end of a life, in one line: "Then shall the dust return to the earth as it was: and the spirit shall return unto God who gave it." And then the book's true last word, which is not vanity at all: "Let us hear the conclusion of the whole matter: Fear God, and keep his commandments: for this is the whole duty of man." Vanity was the clearing of the ground. Reverence is the building. Any reading that stops at the vapour has stopped one chapter too early.

Then turn the page, and the Song sings. It is a love poem — lovers praising each other across gardens and streets — and the canon is not embarrassed by it. Generations of readers have also heard in it a picture of love between God and his people; the book itself celebrates desire and delight as good gifts, and its climax answers the Preacher directly. "Set me as a seal upon thine heart, as a seal upon thine arm: for love is strong as death; jealousy is cruel as the grave: the coals thereof are coals of fire, which hath a most vehement flame." Then: "Many waters cannot quench love, neither can the floods drown it: if a man would give all the substance of his house for love, it would utterly be contemned." Spurgeon preached a whole sermon, The Shulamite's Choice Prayer, on those verses, and another on love as unpurchasable: love, he argued, cannot be bought at any price — offer all the substance of a house for it, and the offer itself is contemned. The Preacher says everything under the sun passes. The Song agrees — and names the one thing that does not pass in the passing: love, which cannot be quenched and cannot be bought.

Then, before the week closed, Isaiah opened with an invitation and a call. The invitation: "Come now, and let us reason together, saith the LORD: though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow; though they be red like crimson, they shall be as wool." The call, in the temple, to a young prophet undone by glory: "Also I heard the voice of the Lord, saying, Whom shall I send, and who will go for us? Then said I, Here am I; send me."

State it plainly, then. What is made under the sun will not last, and pretending otherwise is vanity. What God gives — reverence, love, cleansing, a sending — lasts. Live it this week: remember your Creator now, not later, with one concrete act of reverence today rather than a plan for someday. Tell one person, in words, that you love them, without trying to purchase the moment with a gift. And when the scarlet passage is read to you, believe it as an offer made to you personally: reason together, and be made white as snow.

Remember your Creator now, love what cannot be bought or quenched — and answer when he calls.
