Sabbath Teaching · Week 29

Vanity of Vanities, Song of Songs

Sabbath, May 8, 2027 · Readings: Ecclesiastes 2–12; Song of Songs 1–8; Isaiah 1–28

Can two books that seem to say opposite things both be telling the truth? This week you finished the Preacher, who looked at everything under the sun and called it vapour — and then you opened the Song of Songs, where everything under the sun, gardens and vineyards and spring rain, blazes with delight. One book sighs; the other sings. The canon keeps them side by side on purpose, and together they tell the whole truth about longing.

Hear the Preacher out first, because he has earned his hearing. He tried wisdom, pleasure, building, and wealth, and he reports honestly that none of it lasts and none of it finally satisfies. Generations pass, the sun rises and sets, the rivers run to a sea that is never full. But do not mistake him for a man without faith. His book is a long clearing of the ground, and at its end, after the famous poem of times and seasons, he lands where he always meant to land: "Remember now thy Creator in the days of thy youth." Remember him early, he says — before the evil days come, before the keepers of the house tremble and the silver cord is loosed, and the dust returns to the earth as it was, and the spirit returns to God who gave it. Vanity was never his last word. Reverence is.

Then turn to the Song, and let it be what it is: a love poem, lovers calling to each other across gardens and city streets, praising each other's beauty without embarrassment. The canon makes room for joy as well as instruction — love poetry is Scripture too. Generations of readers have also heard in it a picture of God's love for his people, and you may hear that echo as you read; the book itself simply celebrates desire, longing, and delight as good gifts. Its great claim stands near the end, and it answers the Preacher directly: "Many waters cannot quench love, neither can the floods drown it." The Preacher says everything passes. The Song says one thing does not pass in the passing. Both are true, and your heart needs both truths.

And then, before the week closed, thunder: Isaiah opened, reasoning with a rebellious people — though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow — and a young prophet in the temple, undone by glory, heard a voice ask who would go, and answered, "Here am I; send me." Longing, answered at last by a sending God. Next week, comfort.

Let the Preacher clear away what cannot last, let the Song teach you what can — and remember your Creator now.
