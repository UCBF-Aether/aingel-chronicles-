Sabbath Teaching · Week 7

The Torch Passes

Sabbath, December 5, 2026 · Readings: Deuteronomy 9–34; Joshua 1–5

How does a people go on when the leader who carried it for forty years is gone? This week ended Deuteronomy on a mountaintop and opened Joshua in a camp holding its breath. First, the farewell preparations — notice how deliberate they are. Moses does not cling to office, and he does not leave a vacuum. In Deuteronomy 31 he commissions Joshua publicly: “Be strong and of a good courage: for thou must go with this people unto the land which the LORD hath sworn unto their fathers to give them; and thou shalt cause them to inherit it.” Then he grounds the courage in something sturdier than Joshua’s ability: “And the LORD, he it is that doth go before thee; he will be with thee, he will not fail thee, neither forsake thee: fear not, neither be dismayed.” In chapter 32 he teaches Israel a song — a witness to live in their mouths when his voice is gone. He blesses the tribes one by one. Then he climbs Nebo, sees the land stretched out to the sea, and dies within sight of it: “So Moses the servant of the LORD died there in the land of Moab, according to the word of the LORD.” The torch is not dropped in a panic. It is passed, on purpose, while the passer still lives.

Then God speaks to Joshua, beginning with the fact everyone is avoiding: Moses is dead — therefore arise. Alexander Maclaren, opening his exposition of Joshua 1, puts the principle plainly: “No man is indispensable. God’s work goes on uninterrupted.” The instruments are changed; the Master-hand is the same. The mission was never Moses’ private possession, and it does not die in Moses’ grave.

Now listen to the charge itself, and count its repetitions, because repetition is the sermon’s emphasis. God says to Joshua: “I will not fail thee, nor forsake thee.” “Only be thou strong and very courageous, that thou mayest observe to do according to all the law, which Moses my servant commanded thee: turn not from it to the right hand or to the left, that thou mayest prosper whithersoever thou goest.” “Have not I commanded thee? Be strong and of a good courage; be not afraid, neither be thou dismayed: for the LORD thy God is with thee whithersoever thou goest.” Three times the courage; twice the presence; and between them, the condition that surprises modern ears: courage is tied not to temperament but to a book. “This book of the law shall not depart out of thy mouth; but thou shalt meditate therein day and night, that thou mayest observe to do according to all that is written therein.” Spurgeon, preaching this verse in “Joshua’s Obedience,” draws out what the text assumes: Joshua was highly favoured in promises, yet he was not to treat the promise as permission to sit still — the promise was a spur to diligent obedience, not a substitute for it. Strength, in Joshua 1, grows out of staying close to the words Moses left behind and then doing them, in order, without swerving.

The people answer in the same words at the chapter’s end, urging their new leader to be strong and of good courage. Then the book slows down: the Jordan crossed on dry ground, twelve stones lifted from the riverbed as a memorial, a new generation circumcised at Gilgal, the first Passover eaten in the land. The next morning the wilderness itself retires: “And the manna ceased on the morrow after they had eaten of the old corn of the land; neither had the children of Israel manna any more; but they did eat of the fruit of the land of Canaan that year.” Forty years of daily bread end the morning after the land’s own food is tasted. God times the ending of one provision to the beginning of another, and not a day of hunger falls between them.

Doctrine stated plainly: leadership in God’s people is stewardship, not ownership. The faithful leader prepares a successor, blesses him publicly, and lets go; the faithful successor receives the charge, keeps the book in his mouth, and goes over Jordan. Neither man is the point. The presence is the point: as I was with Moses, so I will be with thee.

Practise the passing and the receiving this week. If you carry any responsibility — in a family, a church, a workplace — do one deliberate act of handing-on: teach someone younger the thing you usually just do yourself, and bless them for it aloud. And if a charge has come to you, take it up the Joshua way: meditate in one passage daily this week, morning and night, and obey its plainest command before the week ends. The same presence goes with you.

No man is indispensable, and no charge is orphaned — be strong, keep the book close, and go over.
