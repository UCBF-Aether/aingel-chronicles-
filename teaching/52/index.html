Sabbath Teaching · Week 52

The Year's Last Sabbath

Sabbath, October 16, 2027 · Readings: Didascalia 17–36

Can you believe where you are standing? A year ago the reading began with light spoken over waters and a God who rested on the seventh day. This Sabbath — the year's last — you keep that same rest in the middle chapters of the Didascalia, near the end of the widest canon in Christendom. As with the other church-order weeks, the record below says plainly that historic preaching on this ground is scarce; Augustine's great closing meditation on the Sabbath, reached at the end of this teaching, is the one historic voice that belongs here by right, because this week is finally about rest itself.

Read chapters 17–36 as the pattern of last week, carried further into the household. The section opens with widows — chapter 17 is headed OF WIDOWS — and its teaching is characteristically unsentimental in both directions: a widow is to do the works that befit the name, and the church is to honour and sustain the women it has enrolled. Chapter 20 exhorts widows and unmarried women to receive what is bestowed "with thanksgiving" — provision on one side, gratitude on the other, dignity on both. The section runs on through prayer, discipline, and the ordering of gifts, and it reaches, at chapter 36, a thanksgiving over the chrism that can stand as the whole year's tone in miniature: "We give thanks to Thee, Creator of all things, for the savour of this chrism, and for this oil of immortality, which Thou hast revealed to us by Jesus Christ Thy Son." Creation, gratitude, Christ: the first page of Genesis and the last weeks of the Didascalia are, it turns out, praying the same prayer.

There is a fitness in ending the plan's Sabbaths here rather than on a battlefield or a throne. The canon that began with God making a world ends its teaching year with his church at table, at prayer, receiving one another in good order — the story come home to ordinary faithfulness, kept by people whose names mostly appear in no chronicle. If the year's reading has a final doctrine, that is it: the God who spoke light, delivered slaves, sang through psalmists, became flesh, and poured out his Spirit intends, at the end of it all, a household.

And now the bank's kept rule, one last time: the Didascalia runs to forty-three chapters, and its final seven — chapters 37–43 — are not in this week's reading. They are tomorrow's page, the last page of the 365-day plan. They will not be taught today. A teaching gathers the week you have finished; it does not run ahead of the reader, not even by seven chapters, not even at the finish line. Consider this a hand raised toward a door, not a step through it. Tomorrow you finish the book, and the year, yourself — which is exactly as it should be. No teacher should stand between a reader and a last page earned chapter by chapter.

Augustine ended his Confessions looking at this same seventh day, and his close reading is the one this year has been making since October: the seventh day, alone among the days, has no evening — because God sanctified it to an everlasting continuance, a Sabbath of eternal life in which we too, after works that are good because he gave them, shall rest in him. Weekly rest, in Augustine's handling and in Genesis's, is not the year's reward snatched at the end. It is a foretaste given every seven days, all along the way. So gather the year — creation and covenant, exile and return, Enoch and the Meqabyan, Daniel in fourteen chapters, the Gospels, the roads, the letters, the Seer, the rulebooks — eighty-one books, gaps and all, and nothing invented for you along the way. Next Sabbath the wheel turns to Teaching #1 and Genesis again: "And God blessed the seventh day, and sanctified it: because that in it he had rested from all his work which God created and made." Rest now. The first word and the last word are the same word.

Rest now — and next Sabbath, we begin again at the beginning: in the beginning, God.
