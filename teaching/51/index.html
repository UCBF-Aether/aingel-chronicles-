Sabbath Teaching · Week 51

The Didascalia — Teaching of the Apostles

Sabbath, October 9, 2027 · Readings: Qalementos (Ethiopic Clement) 66–67; Didascalia 1–16

What kind of ruler is a bishop meant to be? The Didascalia answers in its second verse, before it legislates anything, and the answer is an image, not a title: "The bishop is in the likeness of the shepherd of the sheep, and the presbyter in the likeness of a teacher, and the deacon as a minister". Shepherd, teacher, minister — in that order, at the head of a church-order book. A document that could have opened with authority opens with resemblance: before the bishop may command anything, he must look like someone. This week's grounding, again stated honestly in the record below, is the text itself; historic preaching on the Ethiopic Didascalia is not part of the sermon tradition this bank can draw on, and the teaching does not pretend otherwise. What the text offers, read slowly, is more than enough.

The book presents itself as the apostles speaking together — "We, the Twelve Apostles," its first verse begins, gathered with Paul and James — handing down teaching for the ordering of the church. Its first sixteen chapters then take up the bishop's charge in the pattern that image predicts. He is to teach, because his people cannot live on what they have not heard. He is to judge fairly when accuser and accused stand before him, hearing both, because a shepherd who only listens to the loudest sheep will lose the quiet ones. Above all, in passage after passage, he is to receive the repentant back rather than drive them away. Rules in this book repeatedly begin with a prohibition and end with an instruction to restore. The Didascalia is strict about sin and stubborn about the sinner's way home, in the same breath — and it treats a church that manages only the first half of that sentence as a church that has not yet learned its own teaching.

The widows, orphans, and strangers are not an appendix to that charge; they are its test. Care for them is ordered out of the church's own store, which means the bishop's shepherding is measured at other people's tables, not only at his own desk. Prayer has its order here, reconciliation has its discipline, and mercy — this is the book's distinctive note — has its structure. Mercy with structure survives a leader's bad week. Mercy as a mood does not.

This week the Qalementos also closes, and its ending is worth hearing at full value: "And know thou, my son, that all which I have commanded thee — with the commandment of God have I commanded thee." Clement's long voice — sixty-seven chapters, finished in this week's first two — lays itself down beside the Didascalia's opening like one teacher handing a class to another. The English you read for the Didascalia is J. M. Harden's The Ethiopic Didascalia of 1920, received as a public-domain text and carefully repaired where its scan had tangled Harden's footnotes into the text; every restored word is Harden's.

One paragraph, deliberately kept from the old bank, on the wax and the gold. Ge'ez tradition calls a text's surface sense the wax and its hidden spiritual sense the gold beneath. This edition translates the wax — the words as they stand. The gold belongs to the church's commentary tradition, kept honourably in its own place, never blended into the text. Doctrine, plainly: authority in Christ's church is shepherding patterned on the Shepherd — teaching, fair judgment, structured mercy, restoration as the goal of discipline. Practice: this week, restore one person. Someone in your household, church, or past has been quietly driven to the edge by a failure everyone remembers. Do the second half of the Didascalia's sentence: go to them, name the way back, and walk the first step of it with them.

A physician of souls, slow to condemn and ready to restore — that is the teacher's pattern, and now it is yours to practise.
