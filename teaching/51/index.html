Sabbath Teaching · Week 51

The Didascalia — Teaching of the Apostles

Sabbath, October 9, 2027 · Readings: Qalementos (Ethiopic Clement) 66–67; Didascalia 1–16

What kind of ruler is a bishop meant to be? This week the Didascalia answers — and its answer surprises readers who expect a rulebook to be all severity. The bishop in these chapters is a shepherd and a physician of souls: slow to condemn, quick to restore, and forbidden to enjoy the fall of anyone he is charged to heal.

The Didascalia presents itself as the teaching of the apostles together, handed down for the ordering of the church. Its first sixteen chapters take up the bishop's charge in detail: how he is to teach, how he is to judge fairly when accused and accuser stand before him, how the repentant are to be received back rather than driven away, and how widows, orphans, and strangers are to be cared for out of the church's own store. Prayer has its order here, reconciliation has its discipline, and mercy has — this is the point — its structure. Watch for how often a rule that begins with a prohibition ends with an instruction to restore. The book is strict about sin and stubborn about the sinner's way home, in the same breath. A church that only does the first half of that sentence, the Didascalia suggests, has not yet learned its own teaching.

The English you are reading is J. M. Harden's The Ethiopic Didascalia, published in 1920 and received as a public-domain text. It arrived with a wound worth knowing about: the scan from which it was assembled had tangled Harden's footnotes and page furniture into the text itself, so that notes stood where verses should be. The repair was done against his printed pages in phases, restoring the damaged body text — and every restored word is Harden's. Nothing was supplied from anywhere else. When you read a clean page here, you are reading a page that was untangled, not rewritten.

This week the Qalementos also closes, its final two chapters bringing Clement's long voice to rest beside the Didascalia's opening. Two church books shaking hands at the door is a fitting picture for the year's last full week of new reading.

One paragraph, before we close, on the wax and the gold. Ge'ez tradition names a text's surface sense the wax, and its hidden, spiritual sense beneath the gold. This edition translates the wax — the words as they stand, as faithfully as they can be rendered. The gold belongs to the church's own commentary tradition, kept as a separate layer beside the text, never blended into it. That restraint is not coldness. It is the same honesty that left Josippon's gaps blank: what the book says, we give you; what teachers have drawn from it lives honourably in its own place, under its own name.

A physician of souls, slow to condemn and ready to restore — that is the teacher's pattern.
