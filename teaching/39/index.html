Sabbath Teaching · Week 39

Reading the Marks

Sabbath, July 17, 2027 · Readings: Josippon 34–71

Why would translators mark their own uncertainty — thousands of times — and then hide the marks from you? That double decision stands behind every page of Josippon's long middle, chapters 34 to 71: empires rising and falling, the temple's fortunes turning. This Sabbath, consider the hands that carried the text to you — because the marks are not a confession of failure. They are what honesty looks like at the scale of a million words.

Here is the system, exactly, in two kinds, because there are two kinds of uncertainty and honesty requires distinguishing them. In the workshop files — the working texts from which this Bible was set — a reading marked [?] is uncertain: the word is there on the page, but faded, damaged, or written in a hand that leaves honest room for doubt. A reading marked (?) is probable: the best reading the evidence supports, held just a little less firmly than a sure one. Across the whole corpus the counts are kept and published, and they are worth saying aloud, slowly: 23,327 readings marked [?], and 9,556 marked (?). Together, 32,883 places where someone stopped, looked hard, and refused to pretend. Those numbers are not an embarrassment to be buried in a preface. They are a measurement — the size, exactly counted, of the translators' refusal to lie to you smoothly.

Now the distinction this teaching exists to make, and it matters more than any other sentence here: hard to read is not missing. In every one of those 32,883 places, the text exists. The word stands in the manuscript; only its reading is in question. A mark is a raised hand, not a hole. The translators could have done what would have been easier and looked better: quietly choose a reading everywhere, print a smooth page, and let you believe every word was sure. That smooth page would have been a small, well-printed lie, repeated thousands of times — and you would never have known which words you could lean your weight on. Instead they marked. Marking is slower. Marking invites questions. Marking is what people do when they expect to be checked and intend to survive the checking.

So why do the books you actually read show none of this? Because the reader's editions strip the marks entirely, so the word reads clean, as a Bible should when you sit down with it on a Sabbath. A page crowded with brackets would stand between you and the text at the very moment you came to hear, not to audit. But the marks were not destroyed. They live in the workshop record, consultable by anyone who wants to see where a reading is sure, where probable, and where uncertain. Cleaning your page and keeping the record are the same honesty seen from two sides: we do not clutter your reading, and we do not hide our work. The translator's note printed in the books says the whole thing in one sentence, and it is the sentence this whole quarter of work stands under: "We did our best with every word."

There is a doctrine under the method, and it is worth stating plainly. Truth is not served by confidence you have not earned. In Scripture's own terms, a faithful witness does not inflate his testimony; he reports what he saw, distinguishes it from what he infers, and lets the hearer weigh it. That is all the marks are: testimony given under discipline, at scale, for years. And there is a fittingness in learning this over Josippon, of all books — a history this honest about nations, their rises and their ruins, carried to you by translators this honest about words.

A note on grounding: no historic sermon preaches this week's ground — the marks are this edition's own practice, not a preacher's text — so the sources below are the edition's documented record itself. That, too, is the week's lesson applied to itself: claim only what your record supports.

Live it this week in your own dealings. Practise the two marks in speech: this week, distinguish out loud between what you know, what you think probable, and what you are unsure of — with your family, at work, in prayer. Refuse one smooth lie of false confidence, however small, and replace it with an honest mark. And when you read Josippon's next chapters, read clean and grateful, knowing the doubts were neither hidden from the record nor scattered across your page. Next week the history ends — and you will meet the one kind of mark this week did not describe: the places where the parchment itself is gone.

Read clean, knowing the doubts were marked, counted, and kept — we did our best with every word.
