Sabbath Teaching · Week 39

Reading the Marks

Sabbath, July 17, 2027 · Readings: Josippon 34–71

Why would translators mark their own uncertainty — thousands of times — and then hide the marks from you? That strange, double decision stands behind every page of this book, and this week, deep in Josippon's long middle, is the time to understand it. Because the marks are not a confession of failure. They are what honesty looks like when it is practised at the scale of a million words.

Here is the system, exactly. In the workshop files — the working texts from which this Bible was set — every reading the translators could not hold with full confidence is marked, in two kinds, because there are two kinds of uncertainty. A reading marked [?] is uncertain: the word is there on the page, but faded, damaged, or written in a hand that leaves honest room for doubt. A reading marked (?) is probable: the best reading the evidence supports, held just a little less firmly. Across the whole corpus the counts are kept and published: 23,327 readings marked [?], and 9,556 marked (?). Together that is 32,883 places where someone stopped, looked hard, and refused to pretend.

Now the distinction this teaching exists to make, and it matters: hard to read is not missing. In every one of those 32,883 places, the text exists. The word stands in the manuscript; only its reading is in question. A mark is a raised hand, not a hole. The translators marked rather than quietly choosing a reading and letting you believe it was sure — because a smooth page that hides its doubts would be a small, well-printed lie, repeated thousands of times.

So why do the books you actually read show none of this? Because the reader's editions strip the marks entirely, so the word reads clean, as a Bible should when you sit down with it on a Sabbath. A page crowded with brackets would stand between you and the text. But the marks were not destroyed. They live in the workshop record, consultable by anyone who wants to see where a reading is sure, where probable, and where uncertain. Cleaning your page and keeping the record are the same honesty seen from two sides: we do not clutter your reading, and we do not hide our work. The translator's note printed in the books says the whole thing in one sentence: "We did our best with every word."

Meanwhile, Josippon's long middle has been marching on beneath this reflection — empires rising and falling, the temple's fortunes turning, the history keeping its own steady pace from chapter 34 to chapter 71. That is fitting. A history this honest about nations deserves translators this honest about words. Next week the history ends — and you will meet the one kind of mark this week did not describe: the places where the parchment itself is gone.

Read clean, knowing the doubts were marked, counted, and kept — we did our best with every word.
