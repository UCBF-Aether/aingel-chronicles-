Sabbath Teaching · Week 44

Finished, and Begun

Sabbath, August 21, 2027 · Readings: John 7–21; Acts 1–10

How can one word be both an ending and a starting gun? John places it at the cross, in the exact centre of this week's reading: "When Jesus therefore had received the vinegar, he said, It is finished: and he bowed his head, and gave up the ghost." Note John's order. Jesus does not collapse and then expire; having said the word, he bows his head and gives up his spirit — a deliberate act, performed by a man in command of the moment. Spurgeon, preaching "It Is Finished!" on this verse in 1861, pressed that same detail from the other side: he asked his hearers to observe the clearness of the Saviour's mind in his last agonies — how, with dying eyes, he read every type and prophecy as fulfilled in his own body and summed the whole chain in one sentence. Finished, in Spurgeon's handling, is not the cry of a victim whose strength ran out. It is a conqueror's audit, spoken aloud.

What exactly is finished? Not God's work in the world — Acts is sitting right there in the same week's reading to prove that. Finished is the work the Father gave the Son to do in the flesh: the offering made, the Scriptures fulfilled, the debt paid in full — the single Greek word behind "It is finished" was used on receipts. Thomas, a week later, shows you what that finished work purchases for doubters: standing before the risen Lord, he makes the highest confession in any Gospel, "My LORD and my God," and he is not rebuked for it. He is answered — blessed are they that have not seen, and yet have believed. That blessing reaches this page. It was spoken about you.

Then turn the page and feel the hinge swing. Acts 1–2 gives you the same Lord, ascended, and then the sound Luke describes with unusual precision: "And suddenly there came a sound from heaven as of a rushing mighty wind, and it filled all the house where they were sitting." Spurgeon, preaching "Pentecost" on Acts 2:1–4, drew the doctrine out plainly: the apostles had sat for three years under perfect teaching and were still told to wait, because even experienced, faithful people without the Spirit are, in his phrase, weak as water — the church's power was never going to be its own ability. The wind and fire are not the church showing off. They are the church being given what it did not have.

So the week's two halves interpret each other. "Finished" means the foundation is complete and cannot be added to. "Begun" means the building now rises on it, by the Spirit, through ordinary witnesses. Stephen's death in chapter 7 and Philip's chariot-side sermon in chapter 8 show the building's first courses: a martyr praying for his killers, and a man of Ethiopia — a treasurer who "had come to Jerusalem for to worship," reading Isaiah in his chariot — who hears that "Philip opened his mouth, and began at the same scripture, and preached unto him Jesus," believes, is baptized, and goes on his way rejoicing toward home. In this canon of all canons, mark that road well: the gospel's first recorded journey south is toward Ethiopia, carried by a traveller with a scroll and a question.

Practise the hinge this week. First, rest in the finished half: name one debt of guilt you keep trying to pay again, and leave it at the cross in prayer, paid. Second, work in the begun half: like Philip, begin at the same Scripture a questioner is already reading. Do not wait to feel powerful. The apostles did not begin when they felt ready; they began when the Spirit came, and obedience was their part of the miracle.

What Jesus finished cannot be added to — and what his Spirit has begun has not finished with you yet.
