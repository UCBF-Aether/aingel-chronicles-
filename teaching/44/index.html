Sabbath Teaching · Week 44

Finished, and Begun

Sabbath, August 21, 2027 · Readings: John 7–21; Acts 1–10

How can an ending be a beginning? This week John's Gospel closes at a cross and a garden tomb, and Acts opens with wind and fire — and the two books sit side by side in one week's reading like a door swinging on its hinge.

John's second half deepens everything the first half promised. Jesus speaks at feasts in Jerusalem, calls himself the light of the world and the good shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep, and raises Lazarus, dead four days, from the tomb — a sign so public that the plot against him hardens into decision. Then comes the upper room: feet washed, a new commandment given, long words of comfort, and the great prayer of chapter 17 for those who will believe through the disciples' word. That includes you, reading a year of canon to reach this room. Sit with that a while.

At the cross, John hears one word above the suffering: "When Jesus therefore had received the vinegar, he said, It is finished: and he bowed his head, and gave up the ghost." Finished — not failed. The work given him is complete, and the resurrection chapters prove it: Mary Magdalene at the tomb before dawn, the doors locked and Jesus standing in the midst, Thomas moving from doubt to the Gospel's highest confession — my Lord and my God — and breakfast on the shore of the sea, where a threefold question restores Peter: lovest thou me? John's purpose is plain at the end: these things are written that you might believe, and believing, have life.

Then Acts begins in the same city, with the same risen Lord, and everything starts moving. The promise of the Spirit is given, Jesus ascends, and at Pentecost the church is born in public — wind, fire, and Peter preaching to a crowd from every nation. Three thousand are baptized. A lame man rises at the temple gate. The community shares its goods, faces its first threats, and grows. "Finished" turns out to mean the foundation is laid; "begun" is what gets built on it.

Two moments in the week's last chapters deserve your lingering. Stephen, the first martyr, sees heaven opened as he dies, and prays for his killers — the church's witness sealed with forgiveness. And on the desert road to Gaza, Philip meets a man of Ethiopia, a treasurer who "had come to Jerusalem for to worship," reading Isaiah in his chariot and asking for a guide. Philip explains the Scripture, the man believes and is baptized, and goes on his way rejoicing. Ethiopia enters the story early, on the road home — in this canon, of all canons, that should make you smile: the good news carried toward Ethiopia in the hands of a traveller with a scroll, asking questions.

What Jesus finished, his Spirit has begun in us — the story is still being written.
