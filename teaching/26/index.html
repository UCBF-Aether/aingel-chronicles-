Sabbath Teaching · Week 26

The Middle of the Prayer Book

Sabbath, April 17, 2027 · Readings: Psalms 9–83

How do you pray when the prayer is long — when you are not at the beginning of trouble, or at its end, but in the middle of it? This week you prayed the long middle of the Psalter, Psalms 9–83, and that is exactly the country these psalms know best. Triumphant openings are behind us; the great concluding praises are still weeks ahead. Here, in the middle, Israel's prayer book becomes a school of honest prayer — and three psalms from the week's reading can teach the whole curriculum.

First, Psalm 22: prayer as a cry. “My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? why art thou so far from helping me, and from the words of my roaring?” (Psalm 22:1). The psalm does not tidy this up. The sufferer is surrounded, mocked, poured out like water — and he prays anyway, remembering God's past faithfulness to the fathers even while he cannot feel it himself. And the psalm does not stay in the cry: it travels, by its end, to vows and praise and the ends of the earth turning to the Lord. Watch the shape. Lament, in the Psalter, is not the opposite of faith. It is faith refusing to be silent, holding on by the only thread it has — address. It still says My God.

Second, Psalm 51: prayer as confession. This is David's psalm, the tradition tells us, after Nathan came to him — and it asks for nothing less than re-creation: “Create in me a clean heart, O God; and renew a right spirit within me” (51:10). No excuses, no bargaining, no blaming of circumstances: a broken and a contrite heart, the psalm says at its close, God will not despise. In the middle of the prayer book stands the prayer every reader eventually needs most.

Third, Psalm 73: prayer as a near-stumble, steadied. The psalmist confesses that his feet had almost gone — he envied the foolish, seeing the prosperity of the wicked, who seem to have no troubles at all. What saves him is not winning the argument but entering the sanctuary: “until I went into the sanctuary of God; then understood I their end” (73:17). Worship re-orders his sight, and the psalm lands in one of the Psalter's tenderest confessions: “My flesh and my heart faileth: but God is the strength of my heart, and my portion for ever” (73:26).

Cry, confession, and a steadied heart — keep praying through the middle. The Psalms do not ask you to feel better before you pray. They give you words until you do.

Stay in the middle and keep praying — God is the strength of your heart, and your portion for ever.
