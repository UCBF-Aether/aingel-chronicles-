Sabbath Teaching · Week 26

The Middle of the Prayer Book

Sabbath, April 17, 2027 · Readings: Psalms 9–83

How do you pray in the middle — not at trouble's beginning, when adrenaline carries you, nor at its end, when you can see the shape of rescue, but in the long middle, when nothing has changed yet? This week you prayed the long middle of the Psalter, Psalms 9–83, and that is exactly the country these psalms know best. Three of them can teach the whole curriculum: a cry, a confession, and a steadied heart.

First, Psalm 22 — prayer as a cry that refuses to let go. It opens with the most desolate sentence in the book: “My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? why art thou so far from helping me, and from the words of my roaring?” Read the grammar before anything else. The sufferer does not say God has forsaken him, full stop. He says my God — twice — and asks why. The address itself is faith: he is still talking to the one he cannot feel. Then watch his method in the next breath: “But thou art holy, O thou that inhabitest the praises of Israel. Our fathers trusted in thee: they trusted, and thou didst deliver them.” He argues with his own despair out of memory — God's past faithfulness, recited as evidence in a present darkness. Spurgeon preached on this verse in “Cries from the Cross” and called it beholding the Saviour in the depths of his agonies; in his Treasury of David he divides the psalm exactly where your own reading divides it — a most pitiful cry for help, then a foretaste of deliverance — because the psalm itself travels: by its end the ends of the earth are turning to the Lord, and the sufferer is vowing praise in the congregation. Lament, in the Psalter, is not the opposite of faith. It is faith holding on by the thread of address. It still says my God.

Second, Psalm 51 — prayer as confession, and the middle of the prayer book's hardest lesson: what to ask for when you are the problem. David does not ask for better circumstances, lighter consequences, or help managing his image. He asks for creation: “Create in me a clean heart, O God; and renew a right spirit within me.” Spurgeon's exposition lingers on that verb, and his note deserves quoting exactly: “It is a creation: the very word is used which is employed concerning the creation in the first chapter of Genesis.” David knows a clean heart is not a repair job he can supervise. What is ruined must be made, and only the Maker makes. And the psalm's last word on what God actually accepts: “a broken and a contrite heart, O God, thou wilt not despise.” No excuses, no bargaining. In the long middle of the Psalter stands the prayer every reader eventually needs most.

Third, Psalm 73 — prayer as a near-stumble, steadied. Asaph confesses it in the open: “But as for me, my feet were almost gone; my steps had well nigh slipped. For I was envious at the foolish, when I saw the prosperity of the wicked.” His honesty matches Ezra's questions from weeks ago — the wicked prosper, the pure-hearted are plagued, and cleansing his heart looks vain. What saves him is not winning the argument alone at his desk. It is a place and a practice: “Until I went into the sanctuary of God; then understood I their end.” Worship re-ordered his sight — in the sanctuary he saw the wicked's prosperity as a slope, not a summit — and the psalm lands in the Psalter's tenderest confession: “My flesh and my heart faileth: but God is the strength of my heart, and my portion for ever.” Portion — not a prize for good behaviour, but an inheritance that does not fail when flesh and heart do.

The doctrine: honest prayer is God's own school for the middle of trouble — lament that keeps addressing God, confession that asks for creation rather than cosmetics, and worship that re-orders sight when envy distorts it. The practice, one exercise from each psalm this week: pray Psalm 22's method — name your trouble plainly, then recite three specific past deliverances as evidence before you ask for anything. Confess once this week in Psalm 51's manner — no clause beginning with “but.” And when envy at someone's ease rises, do not argue with it alone: go to the sanctuary — the gathered worship of God's people — and let the end be understood there.

Stay in the middle and keep praying — God is the strength of your heart, and your portion for ever.
