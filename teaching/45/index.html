Sabbath Teaching · Week 45

Paul's Roads, Paul's Letter

Sabbath, August 28, 2027 · Readings: Acts 11–28; Romans 1–12

What carries a message across an empire — roads, or words on a page? This week refuses to choose. Acts closes with Paul travelling courtroom, storm, and shipwreck into Rome itself, and Romans opens beside it: the one great letter Paul wrote to a church he had not yet visited. Read them as two views of the same man. Acts shows you the gospel moving. Romans shows you the gospel argued, stone by stone, until it can bear the weight of the hardest questions a sinner can ask.

Follow Luke's rhythm first, because the repetition is the teaching. Antioch, where the disciples are first called Christians. A council in Jerusalem settling what Gentile believers must bear. Then city after city in the same pattern — synagogue, preaching, some believe, trouble rises, the road again. Paul is not wandering; he is planting. And Luke ends his book deliberately untied: Paul in his own hired house in Rome, preaching the kingdom to all who come, no man forbidding him. Acts does not conclude. It hands the story to its readers.

Then Romans 1:16 explains why that man could not stop travelling: "For I am not ashamed of the gospel of Christ: for it is the power of God unto salvation to every one that believeth; to the Jew first, and also to the Greek." Notice — Paul does not say the gospel contains power, or points to power. It is the power of God, aimed at a person: "to every one that believeth." Chapters 1–3 then do the uncomfortable work of making that "every one" include everyone: Jew and Greek alike stand guilty, so that the mercy announced will be mercy for all and boasting for none.

Abraham is Paul's star witness, and Paul quotes Genesis to call him: "Abraham believed God, and it was counted unto him for righteousness." Justification by faith is not a new slogan Paul invented on the road; on his own showing, it is the oldest pattern in Israel's story, read carefully. And by chapter 8 the argument has become assurance. Spurgeon, preaching "In Christ No Condemnation" on Romans 8:1 in 1886, made a close reader's point worth stealing: the chapter division between Romans 7 and 8 is a clumsy axe-cut in an unbroken argument — the believer is in a state of conflict, Spurgeon said, but not in a state of condemnation, and both are true at the same time. The text itself carries that reading: "There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus, who walk not after the flesh, but after the Spirit" follows Paul's own cry of inner war without a pause. Then the chapter closes with the catalogue nothing can break — "neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor powers, nor things present, nor things to come… shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord."

Chapters 9–11 guard the whole argument from pride. Paul grieves over Israel inside his own theology, insists God's promises have not failed, and warns the grafted branches not to boast over the root that bears them. Doctrine, plainly: a sinner is put right with God by faith in Christ, apart from works of the law — and the person so justified is secure, not because the conflict ended, but because the verdict did.

Practise both halves this week. Travel Paul's road in miniature: carry the gospel one deliberate step outside your usual circle — one conversation, one visit, one letter you have been postponing. And sit with Romans 8 once, whole, aloud, letting its first word and its last word do their work: no condemnation; no separation.

The gospel Paul carried on his feet, he also carried onto the page — and neither road has closed.
