Sabbath Teaching · Week 30

Comfort Ye My People

Sabbath, May 15, 2027 · Readings: Isaiah 29–66; Jeremiah 1

Where does Isaiah turn? If you read this week's long stretch attentively, you felt the weather change: the besieged, warned city of the earlier chapters gives way, at chapter 40, to a voice that opens with a command to comfort — and the command is doubled, as if once were not enough. "Comfort ye, comfort ye my people, saith your God." "Speak ye comfortably to Jerusalem, and cry unto her, that her warfare is accomplished, that her iniquity is pardoned: for she hath received of the LORD’s hand double for all her sins." Spurgeon preached on this verse in 1856, in a sermon called Comfort Proclaimed, and he stopped, as we should, on the two possessives: what a sweet title, he said — "My people!" — and what a cheering revelation — "Your God!" The comfort is not first a change of circumstances. It is a relationship restated by the stronger party: you are still mine; I am still yours.

How is that comfort carried to its destination? Isaiah answers with a figure who appears, withdraws, and appears again, each time clearer: the servant. In chapter 42 he is introduced with God's own delight in him: "Behold my servant, whom I uphold; mine elect, in whom my soul delighteth; I have put my spirit upon him: he shall bring forth judgment to the Gentiles." And his manner is as important as his mission: "A bruised reed shall he not break, and the smoking flax shall he not quench: he shall bring forth judgment unto truth." A bruised reed is a thing most hands would snap and discard; a smoking flax is a wick most would pinch out. This servant does neither. In chapter 49 his commission widens past Israel's borders in God's own words: "It is a light thing that thou shouldest be my servant to raise up the tribes of Jacob, and to restore the preserved of Israel: I will also give thee for a light to the Gentiles, that thou mayest be my salvation unto the end of the earth." In chapter 50 he has a student's ear and a sufferer's tongue: "The Lord GOD hath given me the tongue of the learned, that I should know how to speak a word in season to him that is weary: he wakeneth morning by morning, he wakeneth mine ear to hear as the learned."

And in chapter 53 the portrait completes itself at a cost the comfort of chapter 40 never hid: "But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed." Read the grammar. The wounds are his; the transgressions are ours; the peace is ours; the chastisement was upon him. Isaiah's doctrine is substitution stated in parallel lines, and it is the ground under every comfort in the book's second half. Comfort is not God overlooking. It is God providing.

The book ends wider still: "For, behold, I create new heavens and a new earth: and the former shall not be remembered, nor come into mind." And its promise to the weary is not escape but renewal: "But they that wait upon the LORD shall renew their strength; they shall mount up with wings as eagles; they shall run, and not be weary; and they shall walk, and not faint."

Then the week handed you to Jeremiah, a boy objecting that he is a child — "But the LORD said unto me, Say not, I am a child: for thou shalt go to all that I shall send thee, and whatsoever I command thee thou shalt speak." — and shown an almond rod: "Moreover the word of the LORD came unto me, saying, Jeremiah, what seest thou? And I said, I see a rod of an almond tree." "Then said the LORD unto me, Thou hast well seen: for I will hasten my word to perform it."

Live this week as a bruised reed, not a broken one: bring God the specific place where you are bruised, and receive comfort as pardon, not as mood. Then become the servant's manner toward someone else — speak one word in season to a weary person, gently, without snapping their reed. Wait on the LORD in one definite matter instead of forcing it. God writes second halves; let him write yours.

Receive comfort as pardon, handle the bruised gently — God writes second halves.
