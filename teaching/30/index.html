Sabbath Teaching · Week 30

Comfort Ye My People

Sabbath, May 15, 2027 · Readings: Isaiah 29–66; Jeremiah 1

When did Isaiah change his tune? If you read this week's long stretch with fresh ears, you felt the turn happen. Earlier chapters thundered; the people were besieged, proud, and warned. But deep in the book a door opens, and the voice on the other side of it is tender. Chapter 40 begins the second half of your week's reading like a hand on a shoulder: "Comfort ye, comfort ye my people, saith your God." Speak comfortably to Jerusalem. Her warfare is accomplished. From there to the book's end, comfort is not a pause in Isaiah's message. It is the message.

Watch for how the comfort is carried. Again and again Isaiah sets a figure before you — a servant. In chapter 42 he comes gently: God's elect, in whom his soul delighteth, who will not cry out or break a bruised reed. In chapter 49 he is called from the womb to bring Jacob back, and told it is too small a thing to restore Israel only; he will be a light to the Gentiles as well. In chapter 50 he has the tongue of the learned, to speak a word in season to the weary. And in chapters 52 and 53 the portrait deepens into its climax: a servant despised and rejected, a man of sorrows, who "was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed." Read those chapters slowly this Sabbath. The comfort of chapter 40 has a cost, and chapter 53 tells you who paid it.

And where does it all end? Not merely with a forgiven city, but with a remade world: "For, behold, I create new heavens and a new earth: and the former shall not be remembered, nor come into mind." Isaiah's last chapters look past return from exile to a creation healed all the way down — Jerusalem a joy, weeping heard no more, the wolf and the lamb feeding together. The book that began with a rebellious nation ends with God delighting in his people. If your own story feels stuck in its first half, Isaiah is your book: God writes second halves.

Then the week closed by opening another. Jeremiah chapter 1: a young man from a priest's family in Anathoth, told he was known before he was formed, and objecting that he is only a child. God shows him a rod of an almond tree — the early-blossoming branch, the watcher tree — and a seething pot tilted from the north. The comforter gives way to the weeping prophet. You will live with him for weeks now. Let him come.

God writes second halves — receive Isaiah's comfort, and let its cost humble and heal you.
