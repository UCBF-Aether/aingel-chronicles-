Sabbath Teaching · Week 43

The Word Became Flesh

Sabbath, August 14, 2027 · Readings: Luke 8–24; John 1–6

Where does this story actually begin? Not in Bethlehem. John refuses every smaller starting point: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." Read those three clauses as steps, because John built them as steps. The Word was — already existing when everything else began. The Word was with God — a person in fellowship, not a force or an idea. The Word was God — not a lesser being, not a first creature. Then verse 14 lands the whole weight of it on one sentence: "And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, (and we beheld his glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father,) full of grace and truth." The word for "dwelt" is a tent word — he pitched his tent among us. The glory Israel once saw on a tabernacle now has a face.

Chrysostom, opening his Homilies on John, asked the obvious question in front of his congregation: why do Matthew, Mark, and Luke begin with the birth, the Baptist, the manger — while John passes by all of it and discourses at once on the eternal generation? His answer, in substance, is pastoral: because the others had told the story of the flesh so fully, John must lift grovelling minds higher, lest hearers rest in the earthly story alone and miss who this child eternally is. Chrysostom also models how to hear such a text — little by little, he says, repeating the same short words until they are implanted, rather than swallowing the whole table at once. Take John 1 that way this week. Fourteen verses at a time is plenty.

John's first six chapters then show you the Word at work, and the pattern is always the same: a sign, then a long, searching conversation in which Jesus explains himself. Water becomes wine, and the disciples believe. Nicodemus comes by night and hears that a man must be born again. A Samaritan woman — an outsider in every way her neighbours counted — hears living water promised and becomes her town's first preacher. In chapter 6 the wilderness crowd is fed, and Jesus names the sign: "I am the bread of life: he that cometh to me shall never hunger; and he that believeth on me shall never thirst." Many who followed for the loaves turn back at that sentence. John wants you to notice: following Jesus for what he gives and believing who he is are two different things, and chapter 6 separates them in public.

This week Luke also closes, and his ending and John's beginning belong in one hand. On the Emmaus road the risen Jesus walks with two grieving men who do not know him, "And beginning at Moses and all the prophets, he expounded unto them in all the scriptures the things concerning himself." Their verdict afterward is the reader's verdict on this whole year: "Did not our heart burn within us, while he talked with us by the way, and while he opened to us the scriptures?" Luke says the whole Scripture was always pointing here. John says the One it pointed to was there before its first page. Both are true, and you have now read enough of the canon to feel both at once.

Doctrine, plainly: Jesus Christ is not a man who became God, nor a God who only seemed to be a man. He is the eternal Word made flesh — full God and full man in one person, "full of grace and truth." Practice, this week: read John 1:1–14 aloud each morning, slowly, the Chrysostom way — the same short words repeated until they settle. Then, once this week, do Emmaus in reverse: take a discouraged believer for a walk and open one Scripture to them the way Jesus opened the Scriptures on that road.

The Word who made all things has pitched his tent among us — come, hear him explain himself.
