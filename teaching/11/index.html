Sabbath Teaching · Week 11

You Are the Man

Sabbath, January 2, 2027 · Readings: 2 Samuel 10–24; 1 Kings 1–7

How do you tell a king he has sinned, when he holds your life in his hand? This week, Nathan the prophet found a way: he told David a story. A rich man with many flocks took a poor man's one little ewe lamb — the lamb that ate from his table and lay in his bosom — and served it to a traveller. David burned with anger. “The man that hath done this thing shall surely die.” And Nathan said to David, “Thou art the man.”

Hold that moment still. David had taken Bathsheba, Uriah's wife, and arranged Uriah's death in battle to cover it — and for months, it seems, the king felt nothing sharp enough to bring him to confession. A story did what the deed itself could not. Judging a stranger's sin honestly, David pronounced his own sentence, and when Nathan named it, the king did not do what Saul did. He did not excuse, explain, or reach for a sacrifice. He said, simply, “I have sinned against the LORD.”

The consequences still come, and 2 Samuel does not spare you them: the child dies, and sorrow enters David's house and stays. Absalom, his own son, steals the hearts of Israel, rises in revolt, drives his father weeping over the Mount of Olives, and dies hanging in a tree — while David's cry rings out, “O my son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom! would God I had died for thee.” A king can be forgiven and still reap a harvest in his own household. Grace, in this book, is real, and it is not cheap.

As David's reign ends, a new king asks for the one gift his father most needed and most lacked in that dark year: a hearing heart. Solomon, young and feeling his smallness, asks God in 1 Kings 3 not for riches or long life but for “an understanding heart to judge thy people, that I may discern between good and bad.” God gives it, and the wisdom of the judgment between the two mothers becomes famous in Israel.

Then, in chapters 5–7, the temple rises in Jerusalem — cedar and stone, gold and carved cherubims, seven years of building toward a house for the God who once travelled in a tent. Watch for what this week joins together: a king confronted, a king's son discerning, a house ascending. The God who said “Thou art the man” to David is the same God preparing to dwell, again, in the middle of his people.

Let the story find you before you are named — confess quickly, like David, when it does.
