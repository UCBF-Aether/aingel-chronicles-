Sabbath Teaching · Week 11

You Are the Man

Sabbath, January 2, 2027 · Readings: 2 Samuel 10–24; 1 Kings 1–7

How do you tell a king he has sinned, when he holds your life in his hand? Nathan found the way, and this week’s lead passage is a masterclass in how conscience works. First, set the scene. David has seen Bathsheba from the roof, taken her, and — when her husband Uriah would not cooperate with the cover-up — arranged his death at the battle’s front, using the enemy’s sword so the king’s hands stay clean. A son is born. The court moves on. And the chapter closes with the most quietly devastating sentence in Samuel: “But the thing that David had done displeased the LORD.” Months of royal silence follow. Then God sends Nathan, and Nathan does not begin with an accusation. He begins with a story.

Two men in one city: one rich, with many flocks; one poor, with a single ewe lamb that ate from his morsel, drank from his cup, and lay in his bosom — kept not as livestock but as family. A traveller comes; the rich man spares his own flock and takes the poor man’s lamb. David hears it as a case for judgment, and he burns: “And David’s anger was greatly kindled against the man; and he said to Nathan, As the LORD liveth, the man that hath done this thing shall surely die.” Then Nathan stops being a storyteller: “And Nathan said to David, Thou art the man.” Four words in the English of this edition, and the whole architecture of David’s self-deception collapses in one breath. Note the order, because it is the passage’s close teaching: David judges truly — instantly, righteously, severely — so long as the sin is someone else’s. The conscience that was asleep toward his own adultery and murder was wide awake toward a stranger’s theft. Nathan’s parable works precisely because it borrows David’s own unclouded judgment and turns it, like a mirror, at the moment the verdict leaves his lips. God then speaks through the prophet in the first person — I anointed thee, I delivered thee, I gave thee — gifts listed, ingratitude named, judgment pronounced: the sword shall never depart from David’s house.

David’s answer is five words, and they separate him forever from Saul: “I have sinned against the LORD.” No blaming the people, no request to be honoured before the elders, no sacrifice offered as a substitute. Spurgeon, preaching on the psalm this repentance produced, marks exactly this turning point: it needed the prophet’s word before David’s heart could see itself, but once Nathan spoke, concealment ended — “Pardoned sin makes an honest heart,” he writes, while sin unconfessed keeps a man twisting and hiding. Nathan answers confession with absolution and consequence in a single verse: “The LORD also hath put away thy sin; thou shalt not die.” The child dies. And the sword does not depart: Absalom steals Israel’s hearts, drives his father weeping over the Mount of Olives, and dies hanging in an oak — while the king’s cry reaches the whole army: “O my son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom! would God I had died for thee, O Absalom, my son, my son!” Forgiveness in this book is real and spoken by God himself — and it is not the erasing of consequences. A king can be pardoned and still reap, in his own house, a harvest he planted in another man’s.

As the reign closes, the new king asks for the very thing his father lacked in the dark year: a hearing heart. Solomon asks God not for riches or long life but: “Give therefore thy servant an understanding heart to judge thy people, that I may discern between good and bad: for who is able to judge this thy so great a people?” God gives it — and the temple too, rising across 1 Kings 5–7 toward next week’s dedication.

Doctrine stated plainly: God confronts sin through his word, often through a story that lets us judge ourselves before we know we are doing it; confession — plain, unexcused, five words long — is the door to pardon; and pardon, though full, does not promise to unpick the past. Grace is not cheap in 2 Samuel. It is free to the confessor and costly everywhere else.

So let the story find you this week before anyone has to name you. Read the parable once and ask, slowly: whose lamb have I taken — whose time, whose trust, whose reputation, whose spouse’s honour, whose wage? Where your conscience answers, confess in David’s grammar: I have sinned — to God first, and then, where it can be borne without fresh harm, to the person wronged. Do not bring God a sacrifice in place of the confession. He has told you which one he delights in.

Let Nathan’s story find you first — confess in five plain words, and receive the pardon that tells the truth.
