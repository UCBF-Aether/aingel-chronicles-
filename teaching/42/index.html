Sabbath Teaching · Week 42

The Child and the Star

Sabbath, August 7, 2027 · Readings: Mark 1–16; Luke 1–7

Why does heaven announce its greatest news to night-workers in a field? Open Luke 2 slowly and count the singers, because Luke has built this chapter as a choir. Mary sings first, in chapter 1, of a God who scatters the proud and lifts the lowly. Zechariah sings when his tongue is loosed. The angels sing over Bethlehem. Simeon sings in the temple. Anna gives thanks. Between the songs, notice the plain facts Luke refuses to dress up: "And she brought forth her firstborn son, and wrapped him in swaddling clothes, and laid him in a manger; because there was no room for them in the inn." No room — and into that no-room the angel's sentence falls: "Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace, good will toward men."

Watch who hears it first. Not the Sanhedrin. Shepherds — men whose testimony counted for little, working the night shift outside a small town. Luke's point is structural, and he will repeat it all Gospel long: the news goes first to the people who know they need it. Anna, who has served God with fastings and prayers night and day for decades, is honoured in the same chapter — Luke's eye for long, unnoticed faithfulness is already at work. Mary's own answer in chapter 1 is the doorway everything else walks through: "Behold the handmaid of the Lord; be it unto me according to thy word." Faith, in Luke, begins as a yes spoken by someone with no power to make the promise come true herself.

Then Simeon. Spurgeon, preaching "Nunc Dimittis" on Luke 2:29–30 in 1871, dwelt on the wonder that an old man could hold a baby and call him, in the same breath, salvation itself: "Lord, now lettest thou thy servant depart in peace, according to thy word:" — "For mine eyes have seen thy salvation," he sings. Spurgeon's theme was preparedness — grace had taught Simeon what flesh and blood could not, and the same grace loosened his grip on earth. Luke gives you the facts underneath that sermon: Simeon was "just and devout, waiting"; the Holy Ghost was upon him; he came "by the Spirit into the temple" on the right day. A lifetime of waiting met a promise kept. That is not luck. That is a man who arranged his old age around an expectation.

Read this beside Matthew 1–3, which you read two weeks ago — a legitimate look back, and Luke invites it. Matthew follows a star, kings, a flight into Egypt: the birth seen from the road of nations. Luke stays in the temple courts and the fields: the birth seen by Israel's waiting poor. Chrysostom's Christmas homily catches the same paradox in a single sweep — beholding, as he put it, the Godhead here on earth and man raised in heaven, Bethlehem resembling heaven for a night. Two Gospels, one child, no contradiction: the King of the Jews is also the Saviour lying where animals feed.

Beside Luke's slow opening, Mark runs. Sixteen chapters, no birth story at all, and one word striking like a drum: "immediately." "And immediately the spirit driveth him into the wilderness." Mark's Jesus is always on the move — healing, calling, casting out, and already, by chapter 3, being plotted against. Read Mark whole this week and let its speed do its work: this good news will not wait.

So practise Simeon's discipline this week. Pick one promise of God you have quietly stopped expecting, name it in prayer each morning, and go about your ordinary duties "waiting" the way he did — justly, devoutly, in the temple, in the way of obedience. And when a small answer comes, do what the shepherds did: go, see, and tell.

Heaven's greatest news was first sung to shepherds — go and see, as they did, and then go and tell.
