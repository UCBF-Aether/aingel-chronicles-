Sabbath Teaching · Week 28

The Souls of the Righteous

Sabbath, May 1, 2027 · Readings: Proverbs 10–31; Wisdom of Solomon 1–19; Ecclesiastes 1

What does this canon say about death — not in a whisper, but in a full sentence you can repeat at a grave? Proverbs has spent three weeks teaching you how to live: how to work, speak, lend, and answer. Then the Wisdom of Solomon turns the same face toward dying, and chapter 3 gives the wider canon's clearest word on it. Open there, and read slowly, because the chapter is built as a contrast between two ways of seeing the same death.

First, the sentence itself: "But the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God, and there shall no torment touch them." Notice what is claimed and what is not. The righteous are not said to have escaped dying. They are said to be held. A hand is personal; it is not a place, a theory, or a hope you manufacture. Then the contrast: "In the sight of the unwise they seemed to die: and their departure is taken for misery," "And their going from us to be utter destruction: but they are in peace." Three verbs of appearance — seemed, taken for, utter destruction — are set against one quiet fact: they are in peace. Wisdom does not argue with grief by denying the grave. It argues by distinguishing sight from truth. What death looks like to the unwise is not what death is to the held.

Then the chapter explains the road that led there: "For though they be punished in the sight of men, yet is their hope full of immortality." "And having been a little chastised, they shall be greatly rewarded: for God proved them, and found them worthy for himself." Suffering, in this text, is a proving, not a verdict. God proved them — the word is a tester's word — and found them worthy for himself. John Wesley, preaching on the last judgment in his sermon The Great Assize, laboured this same hope from the other side: the righteous, judged and acquitted, shine in the kingdom of their Father, and no description, he insists, can do that state justice. The traditions differ in dress; the doctrine is one: death does not have the last custody of the righteous. God does.

Now set that promise back into the week that carried it, because Proverbs will not let hope float free of conduct. "He becometh poor that dealeth with a slack hand: but the hand of the diligent maketh rich." "A soft answer turneth away wrath: but grievous words stir up anger." And the book closes with its great portrait: "Who can find a virtuous woman? for her price is far above rubies." — ending, "Give her of the fruit of her hands; and let her own works praise her in the gates." Proverbs' wisdom is a life you can point to: diligent hands, a governed tongue, works that praise a person when she is not in the room to praise herself.

Hold the two books together and you have the whole teaching, stated plainly. How you live the days matters eternally — diligence, honesty, and gentleness are not small things. And when the days end, the righteous are not lost, scattered, or tormented; they are in the hand of God, in peace, their hope full of immortality. At the week's edge the Preacher clears his throat — "Vanity of vanities, saith the Preacher, vanity of vanities; all is vanity." — and next week you will hear him out. He will test everything. Wisdom 3 is the anchor to hold while he does.

Practice it this week. Memorise Wisdom 3:1 until you can say it without the page; you will need it someday at short notice, for yourself or for someone you love. Then do one Proverbs act each day: one soft answer instead of a grievous word, one diligent task finished without being asked. Live the days as Proverbs teaches, and trust the end of them to the hand that holds the righteous.

Live diligently, answer softly — and trust the end of your days to the hand that holds the righteous.
