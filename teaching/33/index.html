Sabbath Teaching · Week 33

The Scribe Who Stayed

Sabbath, June 5, 2027 · Readings: Lamentations 5; Baruch 1–6; 4 Baruch 1–9; Ezekiel 1–18

Every prophet needs someone who stays. Jeremiah had Baruch — the scribe who wrote down the prophet's words, read them aloud when Jeremiah could not go, and stayed beside him through siege, prison, and flight. This week you read Baruch twice over, in two books that bear his name, and the doubling itself is worth pausing on. What does faithfulness look like when its whole work is to carry someone else's words, and then to carry a people's confession?

The first book, Baruch in six chapters, opens in Babylon. The exiles gather, and Baruch reads to them the book he has written — a confession that does not flinch. They have sinned; the curses have come justly; God is righteous and they are ashamed. Money is collected and sent to Jerusalem with the scroll, asking those at home to pray for the exiles and even for Nebuchadnezzar, under whose shadow they must live. Then the confession widens into one of the finest hymns in the wider canon, a praise of Wisdom in chapter 3: God has found out all the way of knowledge and given it to Jacob his servant — and "afterward did he shew himself upon earth, and conversed with men." Wisdom is not found in the land of merchants or the treasuries of kings. She is God's gift, given where God chooses. The book closes with chapter 6, which is the Letter of Jeremiah itself, standing as Baruch's sixth chapter: a long, dry-eyed warning against Babylon's idols — gods of wood and gold that must be dusted, that cannot save themselves from thieves, let alone save a worshipper. A god that needs carrying cannot carry you.

Then comes 4 Baruch, the things Baruch saw — nine chapters, translated anew, directly from the Ge'ez, for this edition, because no usable English existed for it. It belongs to the same Jeremiah circle: when the city falls, Jeremiah and Baruch commit the temple vessels to the earth, trusting the ground to keep what the people cannot; an eagle carries Baruch's letter across the exile to Jeremiah in Babylon; and Abimelech, sent out with a basket of figs, sleeps sixty-six years under a tree, like a man kept out of history itself, and wakes to find the figs still fresh, their milk still dripping. Part history, part legend, and wholly moving — a story about what survives when everything sacred seems lost, and about a scribe still at his post when the sleeper comes home.

Your week began with Lamentations' last prayer — chapter 5's plea, "Remember, O LORD, what is come upon us" — and ended with a new prophet's first vision: Ezekiel, among the captives by the river Chebar, watching the heavens open over exile itself. Baruch stayed through the ending. Ezekiel will show you the beginning hidden inside it.

Stay at your post, carry the words faithfully, and trust the ground to keep what you cannot.
