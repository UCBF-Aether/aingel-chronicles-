Sabbath Teaching · Week 6

Moses Tells It Again

Sabbath, November 28, 2026 · Readings: Numbers 20–36; Deuteronomy 1–8

Why tell a story again to people who have just lived through its consequences? This week you finished Numbers among funerals and opened Deuteronomy within sight of the land. In Numbers 20, Miriam dies. Then, at Meribah, a thirsty people quarrels, and Moses — weary, provoked — strikes the rock instead of speaking to it. Water flows; grace to the people is not withheld. But the sentence on the leaders stands: “Because ye believed me not, to sanctify me in the eyes of the children of Israel, therefore ye shall not bring this congregation into the land which I have given them.” Soon Aaron dies on Mount Hor and Israel mourns him thirty days. The Exodus leaders are stepping off the stage, and the man telling Deuteronomy knows he is next.

Before that, one more wilderness sign, the strangest mercy in the book. In Numbers 21 the people speak against God and Moses; fiery serpents come; many die. The people confess — “We have sinned” — and Moses prays, and God’s answer is not to remove the serpents but to lift up a likeness of them: “Make thee a fiery serpent, and set it upon a pole: and it shall come to pass, that every one that is bitten, when he looketh upon it, shall live.” “And Moses made a serpent of brass, and put it upon a pole, and it came to pass, that if a serpent had bitten any man, when he beheld the serpent of brass, he lived.” Spurgeon, preaching this passage in “The First Setting Up of the Bronze Serpent,” warns that “its original institution teaches us much which ought not to be overlooked” — beginning here: the sign was set up among Israel, God’s own people, for bitten believers who had sinned and confessed. The cure asks nothing but a look: a dying man cannot climb, pay, or perform; he can only turn his eyes toward the mercy God himself lifted up. That is the whole doctrine in one image: salvation by looking, for people who cannot save themselves.

Then Deuteronomy opens, and the form of the book is itself the teaching. Deuteronomy means a second telling: Moses stands before a generation that was small, or unborn, at the sea and at Sinai, and puts the past into their ears before he puts the future into their hands. He retells the judges, the spies, the refusal, the long years. And he tells it tenderly. Of the wilderness he says: “And in the wilderness, where thou hast seen how that the LORD thy God bare thee, as a man doth bear his son, in all the way that ye went, until ye came into this place.” A father carrying a child — that is Moses’ chosen summary of forty hard years. He rehearses the Ten Words again. He gives Israel its great confession: “Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD:” “And thou shalt love the LORD thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might.” And he tells them how a story survives a generation: “And thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children, and shalt talk of them when thou sittest in thine house, and when thou walkest by the way, and when thou liest down, and when thou risest up.” Spurgeon’s verse exposition on Deuteronomy 6 presses the same word — diligently — asking parents whether they have done this teaching at home, in ordinary talk, and not only in formal moments.

He also warns them about fullness. In Deuteronomy 8, God “humbled thee, and suffered thee to hunger, and fed thee with manna, which thou knewest not, neither did thy fathers know; that he might make thee know that man doth not live by bread only, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of the LORD doth man live.” The danger ahead is not starvation; it is satisfaction — full houses, full tables, hearts emptied of memory.

Doctrine stated plainly: a people lives by a story faithfully retold. Forgetting is not neutral in Deuteronomy; it is the first step of apostasy, and remembering is not nostalgia — it is obedience rehearsed aloud, at home, morning and night.

So retell one story this week, on purpose. Choose one thing God has done for your family — a provision, a rescue, a mercy in a hard year — and tell it at a table, to a child or a younger friend, in plain words, with the dates and the details, the way Moses told Israel theirs. And when failure bites — yours, confessed the way Israel confessed — do the wilderness thing: look, first, to the mercy God has lifted up, before you try any remedy of your own.

Tell it again at your own table — a people that remembers can cross over.
