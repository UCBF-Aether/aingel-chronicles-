Sabbath Teaching · Week 6

Moses Tells It Again

Sabbath, November 28, 2026 · Readings: Numbers 20–36; Deuteronomy 1–8

Why tell a story again to people who have just lived it? This week you finished Numbers and opened Deuteronomy — and Deuteronomy, at its heart, is Moses telling the whole story one more time. A new generation stands on the plains of Moab, within sight of a land their parents refused. Most of them were children, or not yet born, when the sea opened and Sinai shook. So Moses retells it: the appointing of judges, the spies and the refusal, the long years in the wilderness, the victories already won east of Jordan. The past is put into their ears before the future is put into their hands.

The last chapters of Numbers show why this retelling is so tender, and so grave. In chapter 20, Miriam dies. Then, at Meribah, a thirsty people quarrels, and Moses — weary, provoked — strikes the rock instead of speaking to it. Water comes, but Moses and Aaron are told they will not bring this congregation into the land. Soon after, Aaron dies on Mount Hor, and the people mourn him thirty days. The leaders of the Exodus are themselves coming to the edge of the story.

In chapter 21, when the people speak against God and against Moses yet again, fiery serpents come among them, and healing is given in a strange, lifted-up sign: a serpent of brass on a pole, so that every one that is bitten, when he looketh upon it, shall live. It is mercy in a form no one would have invented — life, by looking, for people who cannot save themselves.

By the time Deuteronomy opens, then, you know how much this new generation needs to hear it all again, and how much Moses loves them as he tells it. “The LORD thy God,” he reminds them, carried them “as a man doth bear his son.” He rehearses the Ten Words again, gives them the great confession — “Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD” — and warns them not, in the land's fullness, to forget the God who fed them in its emptiness.

Watch for the kindness in repetition. Moses tells it again not because the story failed the first time, but because stories are how a people remembers who it is. The generation that enters the land will live, or fail, by whether the old story has become its own.

Tell the story again to those coming after you — memory, faithfully repeated, is how a people crosses over.
