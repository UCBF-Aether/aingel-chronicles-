Sabbath Teaching · Week 4

God in the Middle of the Camp

Sabbath, November 14, 2026 · Readings: Exodus 38–40; Leviticus 1–23

What changes when God does not stay on the mountain, but moves into the middle of the camp? This week you finished Exodus and opened Leviticus. The last chapters of Exodus are full of careful, almost tedious work: boards and curtains, sockets and rings, gold measured out, garments sewn, everything made “as the LORD commanded Moses.” Then the work is raised up, anointed, and finished — and the book ends not with human craftsmanship, but with what the craftsmanship was for: “Then a cloud covered the tent of the congregation, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle.”

Sit with that ending a moment. The God who spoke from Sinai's fire now dwells in a tent among tents, so close that Moses himself cannot enter for the glory. The camp has a centre, and the centre is not Israel. It is the presence of God, travelling with them through a wilderness they have not yet crossed. Every journey after this will be taken from that tent, and every camp will be pitched around it.

Leviticus then answers the obvious next question: how do sinful, ordinary people live this close to a holy God without either presuming on him or running from him? Its answer fills this week's chapters — offerings and priests, clean and unclean, feasts and Sabbaths, care with blood and body and speech. Some of it will feel strange, and some of it severe. Read it slowly, as instructions for neighbours learning to share a camp with glory at its heart, not as a list to rush through.

And in the middle of Leviticus, in chapter 19, the holiness takes a shape anyone can recognise: “Thou shalt not avenge, nor bear any grudge against the children of thy people, but thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself: I am the LORD.” Holiness, in this book, is not only what happens at the altar. It is gleaning left for the poor, wages paid on time, no talebearing, no hatred hidden in the heart. The God in the middle of the camp cares how the people at the edges of the camp treat one another.

Watch for that joining of glory and neighbourliness as Leviticus goes on. The tabernacle is filled, and then the teaching spills outward into fields, tables, and tongues. The point of God dwelling among his people was never only that they could point to the cloud. It was that a holy presence would make a holy people — beginning, very practically, with the neighbour next to you.

Live this week as a camp with God at its centre — and love the neighbour at its edge.
