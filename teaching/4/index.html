Sabbath Teaching · Week 4

God in the Middle of the Camp

Sabbath, November 14, 2026 · Readings: Exodus 38–40; Leviticus 1–23

What changes when God stops speaking from the top of a mountain and moves into the middle of the camp? This week you finished Exodus amid work that looks almost tedious on the page: boards and curtains, sockets and rings, gold weighed, garments sewn, everything made as the Lord commanded Moses. Then the refrain that has run under all the work resolves into one plain line: “So Moses finished the work.” Only after the finishing comes the filling: “Then a cloud covered the tent of the congregation, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle.” And so full was the filling that the man who built the tent could not go into it: “And Moses was not able to enter into the tent of the congregation, because the cloud abode thereon, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle.”

Read that ending closely, because it corrects two errors at once. The glory is God’s, not the craftsmen’s — the book ends not by admiring Israel’s obedience but by watching God take up residence. And the glory is portable: from now on the cloud, not the people’s mood, sets the itinerary: “And when the cloud was taken up from over the tabernacle, the children of Israel went onward in all their journeys.” Spurgeon, preaching on the parallel scene at the temple’s dedication in “Temple Glories,” begins from exactly this pattern and notes that in the wilderness God showed his glorious presence in the midst of the camp of Israel — presence in the middle, not power at a distance, is the point of both scenes. The camp now has a centre, and the centre is not Israel. Every march, every encampment, every tribe’s place is measured from a tent Israel did not fill and cannot manage.

Leviticus then answers the question the filling raises: how do sinful, ordinary people live this close to a holy God — neither presuming on him nor fleeing from him? Its opening command sets the register for everything that follows: “Ye shall be holy: for I the LORD your God am holy.” Holiness here is not first a feeling of reverence. It is a pattern of life copied from God’s own character, worked out in offerings and feasts, in clean and unclean, in how blood and body and speech are handled. Some of this week’s chapters will feel strange to a modern reader, and some severe. Read them as what they are: a holy God’s patient instruction for neighbours learning to share one camp with glory at its heart.

And in the middle of Leviticus, the holiness walks out of the tent and into the field and the marketplace. Chapter 19 commands the harvester: “thou shalt not wholly reap the corners of thy field, neither shalt thou gather the gleanings of thy harvest” — the edges of your plenty are not yours; they belong by law to the poor and the stranger. It commands the employer: “the wages of him that is hired shall not abide with thee all night until the morning” — holiness pays on time. And it lands on the sentence Jesus himself will later lift up as the law’s second great word: “Thou shalt not avenge, nor bear any grudge against the children of thy people, but thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself: I am the LORD.” Note the signature at the end: I am the LORD. Neighbour-love in Leviticus is not folk wisdom. It is the character of the God in the middle of the camp, enforced by his own name.

Doctrine stated plainly, then: God dwells among his people not to be admired from a distance but to make them holy as he is holy — and holiness, in this book, is always testable at the edges of the camp: the gleaner at your field’s corner, the worker waiting for his wage, the neighbour you are tempted to grudge. A modern sermon on Exodus 40 makes the same movement, observing that the cloud has come down off the mountain into the midst of the people, and that everything in Israel’s life is reordered around the glory now dwelling in their midst.

So order one corner of your life around the centre this week. Leave a margin on purpose: do not harvest one corner of your income, your time, or your table bare — set it aside, before you are asked, for the poor and the stranger near you. Pay what you owe promptly, this week, without being chased. And drop one grudge deliberately: name it, release the debt in prayer, and do the person one concrete good. The God in the middle of the camp is making a holy people at its edges.

God has moved into the middle — leave the corner of your field, pay promptly, and love the neighbour at the edge.
