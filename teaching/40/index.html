Sabbath Teaching · Week 40

The Book with Honest Gaps

Sabbath, July 24, 2027 · Readings: Josippon 72–104; Matthew 1–3

What do you do when the page itself gives out — when the story you have been reading for weeks reaches places where the parchment is simply gone? This week Josippon ends, and its ending teaches the year's hardest lesson about honesty: a gap explained is worth more than a smooth paragraph invented.

Josippon has carried us from Adam through the Second Temple and its fall, in the Ge'ez dress the Ethiopian Church gave it as the Zena Ayhud. For this edition it was translated anew from a single codex, British Library EAP704/2/3, copied in 1595–1605 and read from photographs, chapter by chapter. Its final stretch, chapters 78–104, is a discourse appendix standing after the chronicle — and that appendix, rendered anew here from that Ge'ez text, is a documented first at the level of this part of the book. Say it no wider than that. The chronicle itself has been in English since 1558, and no claim is made for it here. The narrow claim is the true one, and it is enough: these closing discourses had to be carried over fresh from the Ge'ez for you to read them in this place.

But the base witness is damaged, and three passages are physically lost: chapter 7, verses 12–14, which you met earlier in the year; chapter 84, verses 21–35; and chapter 104, verses 14–19, at the very end. Twenty-four verse units in all — the only physical losses in this entire edition. They were hunted: through the witness at Strasbourg, through other copies at the British Library, and through the catalogues of some 5,000 Ge'ez manuscripts. They were never found. The other witnesses that could be reached do not carry them at all. So they stand as honest blanks — not reconstructed, not bridged, not invented.

Sit with that refusal a moment. It would have been easy to guess what a doxology probably said, or how a history probably ended. Instead you are handed the truth: this much the parchment gives, and this much it does not. That is the same honesty you have met all through this canon, in witnesses checked and found not to carry a passage, and recorded as checked. Watch for it as a kind of reverence — the copyist's and the translator's fear of putting words in a book's mouth.

Then, at the week's close, a door opens. Matthew begins with a genealogy — name upon name, Abraham to David to Joseph — and with wise men asking, "Where is he that is born King of the Jews? for we have seen his star in the east, and are come to worship him." The history of the people Josippon traced now gathers into a child. Next week we read his Gospel whole.

We did our best with every word — and where the page gave out, we told you so.
