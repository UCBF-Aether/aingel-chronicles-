Sabbath Teaching · Week 40

The Book with Honest Gaps

Sabbath, July 24, 2027 · Readings: Josippon 72–104; Matthew 1–3

What do you do when a book you trust reaches a place where the page itself gives out? This week Josippon ends, and the way it ends is itself a teaching. Open its last movement slowly. Chapters 78–104 are not more chronicle; they are a discourse appendix standing after the history — sermons and treatises gathered behind the story, rendered anew here from that Ge'ez text. That is the claim, and it is deliberately narrow: the discourse appendix, rendered anew from the Ge'ez. The chronicle itself has been in English for centuries, and no wider claim is made. A true claim stated exactly is stronger than a large claim stated loosely, and this book, all year, has taught you to prefer the first.

Then notice what is not there. Three passages are physically lost in the base witness: chapter 7, verses 12–14, which you met earlier in the year; chapter 84, verses 21–35; and chapter 104, verses 14–19, at the very end of the book. They were hunted — through the Strasbourg witness, through other British Library copies, and through catalogues of some 5,000 Ge'ez manuscripts — and they were never found. The witnesses that could be reached do not carry the appendix at all. So the places stand blank. Not bridged, not guessed, not smoothed over with a plausible doxology. Twenty-four verse units are the only physical losses in this whole edition, and the edition would rather show you a gap than lend the book words it never said.

Hold that beside the other marks you learned two weeks ago. A [?] or a (?) in the workshop record is not a gap; it is an uncertain reading, a word hard to read, honestly flagged and then stripped from your page so the text reads clean. A blank is different. A blank means the parchment gives nothing. Confusing the two would flatter the edition and mislead the reader. Keeping them distinct is reverence in practice: we did our best with every word, and where the page gave out, we told you so.

John Chrysostom, preaching on Matthew's opening, lingered over a smaller honesty of the same kind — what the evangelist includes, what he passes over, and why. Chrysostom noticed that Matthew counts his genealogy in three fourteens and yet names only twelve in the last section, and instead of hiding the difficulty he worked at it in front of his hearers, even leaving one question, as he said, for them to examine themselves lest they grow lazy. That is the preacher's version of the translator's blank: do not pretend the text is smoother than it is; teach the people to look at what is actually on the page.

And what is on the page, at this week's close, is a beginning. Matthew opens with a title that is itself a sermon — "The book of the generation of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham." Then he does something Josippon has trained you to value: he names names, generation by generation, sinners included, and lets the long history gather into one child. The wise men arrive asking the question the whole canon has been asking in different words: "Where is he that is born King of the Jews? for we have seen his star in the east, and are come to worship him."

So practise the honesty this week in your own small sphere. Say what you know, say what you do not know, and do not fill a gap in a story — yours or another's — with invention because a smooth story feels kinder. Leave the blank blank, and worship the King who does not need our inventions to be found.

We did our best with every word — and where the page gave out, we told you so.
