Sabbath Teaching · Week 46

The Apostle as Pastor

Sabbath, September 4, 2027 · Readings: Romans 13–16; 1 Corinthians 1–16; 2 Corinthians 1–13; Galatians 1–6; Ephesians 1–5

What does an apostle do when he cannot be in the room? He writes — and this week you read a satchel of his letters at once: the close of Romans, both Corinthian letters whole, Galatians, and the opening of Ephesians. One thread runs through them all. Paul is not lecturing from a height. He is shepherding young, messy, beloved churches by letter, the way a pastor visits by post.

Romans ends its argument in the kitchen and the street: love without pretence, honouring one another, receiving the weak without quarrelling over scruples, and a long roll of greetings that names ordinary believers one by one. Paul's theology always lands in named people. Then Corinth — gifted, divided, proud, and in real trouble. Paul answers report by report and question by question: factions, immorality tolerated, lawsuits, marriage, food offered to idols, the ordering of worship, the gifts of the Spirit. And at the centre he sets the chapter you may already know by heart, in this edition's older wording: "Charity suffereth long, and is kind; charity envieth not." The King James word is charity — love with its sleeves rolled up. "And now abideth faith, hope, charity, these three; but the greatest of these is charity." A church can have every gift, Paul says, and without this it is noise.

Second Corinthians is the most personal letter of the set — comfort in affliction from a man who had despaired of life itself, a defence of a ministry carried in earthen vessels, and the astonishing claim that God's power is made perfect in weakness. If Romans shows the pastor's mind and 1 Corinthians his patience, this one shows his heart, bruises and all.

Galatians burns hotter. Teachers had followed Paul insisting that Gentile believers needed the law's full yoke to be complete, and Paul answers with his own story, with Abraham, and with a command to guard the gift: "Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free, and be not entangled again with the yoke of bondage." Freedom, though, is not licence — the same letter ends with the fruit of the Spirit and the charge to bear one another's burdens.

Then Ephesians lifts the eyes. One body, one Spirit, one hope; Jews and Gentiles made one new people; the mystery of Christ opened wide. Watch for the turn midway, where praise becomes practice: walk worthy, speak truth, put away bitterness, forgive as you were forgiven. Five letters, one shepherd. He corrects, comforts, argues, and blesses — and never treats a young church as a problem to be solved rather than a people to be loved into maturity.

A shepherd's care travels any distance a letter can go — read these as written to you.
