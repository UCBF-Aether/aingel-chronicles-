Sabbath Teaching · Week 46

The Apostle as Pastor

Sabbath, September 4, 2027 · Readings: Romans 13–16; 1 Corinthians 1–16; 2 Corinthians 1–13; Galatians 1–6; Ephesians 1–5

What does an apostle do when he cannot be in the room? He writes like a pastor, not a professor — and this week's satchel of letters proves it. Romans ends with love practised in kitchens and streets and a long roll of ordinary names greeted one by one. Then Corinth, twice; then Galatia, burning hot; then Ephesus, lifting the eyes. One man, five situations, and the shepherding changes its tone every time while never changing its aim: churches loved into maturity.

Stand in the centre of 1 Corinthians and read chapter 13 the way Paul placed it — not as a wedding decoration but as a rebuke to a gifted, divided church in the middle of an argument about spiritual gifts. "Charity suffereth long, and is kind; charity envieth not; charity vaunteth not itself, is not puffed up." The edition's older word is charity — love with its working clothes on. Paul measures every gift against it and lets the gifts fail the test: tongues, prophecy, knowledge, even faith to move mountains — without charity, "it profiteth me nothing." Spurgeon, preaching "Love's Labors" on 1 Corinthians 13:7 in 1881, refused to let his hearers admire that chapter from a distance: this charity, he insisted, is not a fancy virtue for special saints but, in his words, "the common, everyday livery of the people of God" — the uniform every believer is expected to wear, not the medal a few are decorated with. That is Paul's own pressure, faithfully carried: "And now abideth faith, hope, charity, these three; but the greatest of these is charity."

Second Corinthians shows you the pastor's own bruises, and its opening doxology is also its method: "Blessed be God, even the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of mercies, and the God of all comfort; Who comforteth us in all our tribulation, that we may be able to comfort them which are in any trouble, by the comfort wherewith we ourselves are comforted of God." Comfort, in Paul's economy, is never a terminal payment. It is received in order to be handed on, by the exact route it arrived. A ministry carried in earthen vessels, power made perfect in weakness — this is the most personal letter in the canon, and the most useful one for anyone who has ever tried to help people while hurting.

Galatians is hotter because the danger was worse: teachers had arrived requiring Gentile believers to take on the law's full yoke to be complete Christians. Paul answers with autobiography, with Abraham, and with a command: "Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free, and be not entangled again with the yoke of bondage." John Wesley's notes on this verse gloss the liberty precisely — freedom from the ceremonial law as a way of justification — and the same chapter guards the freedom from its opposite abuse: liberty is not an occasion to the flesh, because the whole law is fulfilled in loving your neighbour. And Ephesians closes the week by turning praise into practice, landing doctrine in the household: "And be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you."

So doctrine and practice here are one thing. This week, be a pastor by post, as Paul was: write one real letter — paper, not a message thread — to comfort someone with the specific comfort God once gave you in a specific trouble, naming both. And wear the livery once deliberately: one act of charity that suffers long and is kind toward the person in your church or house who is currently hardest to love. Paul says that act matters more than every gift you envy in others.

A shepherd's care travels any distance a letter can go — write yours this week, and wear the livery.
