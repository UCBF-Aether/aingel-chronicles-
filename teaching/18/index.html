Sabbath Teaching · Week 18

The Book Jude Quoted

Sabbath, February 20, 2027 · Readings: Jubilees 34–50; 1 Enoch 1–14

Where have you already met this week's new book — long before you opened it? Near the end of the New Testament you will finish later this year. Jude writes: “And Enoch also, the seventh from Adam, prophesied of these, saying, Behold, the Lord cometh with ten thousands of his saints, To execute judgment upon all.” Jude is quoting — 1 Enoch, chapter 1, almost word for word. His readers plainly knew this book and honoured it. Most canons, in time, did not keep it. Ethiopia did, and whole and complete 1 Enoch survives only in Ge'ez, which is why the English in this edition is R. H. Charles's translation, published by SPCK in 1917, made from the Ethiopic.

So open Enoch the way Jude's church opened it: as a book whose first page holds judgement and mercy in one hand. The opening line announces its audience: “The words of the blessing of Enoch, wherewith he blessed the elect” and righteous, who will be living in the day of tribulation. Then the vision: the Holy Great One comes forth, mountains shake, the earth is rent — and verse 8 turns the face of the chapter toward the people you might not expect to survive it: “But with the righteous He will make peace, And will protect the elect, And mercy shall be upon them.” Read the order carefully. Enoch does not begin with the Watchers' crimes or the giants' violence. He begins with a coming Judge — and with peace promised, in advance, to the righteous. Judgement in this book is not God's cruelty. It is God's answer to a world where the strong devour the weak and heaven itself has been corrupted.

Because that corruption is the centre of chapters 6–11. The angels, the children of heaven, see the daughters of men, choose wives, and bind themselves by a mutual oath on Mount Hermon — two hundred descending in the days of Jared. Notice what the text stresses: this was not a fall in a moment of passion. It was sworn, organised, deliberate — sin with a ceremony. From it flow the giants, the violence, the corruption of the earth, until the cry of the suffering rises to heaven and Enoch is sent to pronounce judgement on angels who left their appointed place. Jude remembered exactly this when he wrote of “the angels which kept not their first estate, but left their own habitation.”

The early church heard this book the same way. Tertullian defended Enoch's Scripture at length, arguing from Jude itself: an apostle quotes Enoch as a prophet, so the church should not lightly discard what an apostle honoured. Others in the early church disagreed, and the book's long history is honestly mixed — which is precisely why this edition tells you plainly where it survived and how it reached you, rather than pretending the question was never asked.

This week you also finished Jubilees: Joseph loved, betrayed, and raised up, and the Exodus retold to its very edge inside the covenant's calendar. Set the two closings side by side. Jubilees ends with a family becoming a people. Enoch opens with a man shown the end of all peoples. The shelf is widening from family history to the judgement of the world.

The doctrine: judgement is real, it begins with the highest rebels, and mercy for the righteous is announced before the charges are even read. The practice: examine one sworn, settled compromise in your own life — not a stumble, but an arrangement, something you have bound yourself to that you know leaves your first estate. Enoch's warning is aimed at arrangements, not accidents. Break the oath that heaven never made, this week, in one concrete act — and take verse 8 as your ground for hope while you do it: with the righteous he will make peace.

Judgement is real, and mercy is announced first — with the righteous he will make peace.
