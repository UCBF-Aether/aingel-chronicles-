Sabbath Teaching · Week 18

The Book Jude Quoted

Sabbath, February 20, 2027 · Readings: Jubilees 34–50; 1 Enoch 1–14

Where have you already met this week's new book — long before you ever opened it? In the New Testament itself. The letter of Jude, near the very end of the Bible you will finish later this year, quotes it by name: “And Enoch also, the seventh from Adam, prophesied of these, saying, Behold, the Lord cometh with ten thousands of his saints, to execute judgment upon all” (Jude 14–15). Jude's readers evidently knew the book of Enoch and honoured it. Most canons, in time, did not keep it. Ethiopia did — and whole and complete, 1 Enoch survives only in Ge'ez, the ancient language of Ethiopia, which is why the classic English translation printed in this edition, R. H. Charles's, published by SPCK in 1917, was made from the Ethiopic.

This week you first finished Jubilees, and its closing chapters carry the story through Joseph — loved, betrayed, raised up in Egypt — and retell the Exodus to its very edge, the family of Jacob becoming the people of the Exodus inside the covenant's calendar. Then the shelf turns, and a very different voice opens. “The words of the blessing of Enoch, wherewith he blessed the elect and righteous, who will be living in the day of tribulation, when all the wicked and godless are to be removed.” Enoch, the man of Genesis who walked with God and was taken, here speaks at length — and what he has seen is both glorious and grievous. The Holy Great One will come forth from his dwelling, the mountains will melt like wax before the flame, and there will be judgement upon all. But with the righteous, the opening promises, “He will make peace, and will protect the elect, and mercy shall be upon them.”

At the centre of these first fourteen chapters stands the fall of the Watchers. In chapters 6–11 the angels, the children of heaven, see the daughters of men, choose wives from among them, and bind themselves together by an oath on Mount Hermon — two hundred of them descending in the days of Jared. From that descent flow violence, corruption, and the cry of the earth rising to heaven, and Enoch is sent to pronounce judgement on angels who left their first estate — the same fall Jude remembers when he writes of angels who “kept not their first estate.”

Read these chapters as Jude's church read them: not as curiosity, but as solemn warning wrapped in mercy. Judgement is real in Enoch — and so, from its first page, is peace for the righteous.

Jude trusted this voice — read Enoch slowly, and let warning and mercy stand together.
