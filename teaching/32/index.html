Sabbath Teaching · Week 32

Buying a Field in a Dying City

Sabbath, May 29, 2027 · Readings: Jeremiah 32–52; Lamentations 1–4

What does hope look like when it has to sign paperwork? Jerusalem is besieged, Jeremiah is imprisoned in the court of the prison for prophesying the city's fall — a fall he has never softened or retracted — and there, with the end visible from the walls, his cousin arrives to sell him a field. Chapter 32 is told with a lawyer's patience, and the patience is the preaching. Nothing in it is vague, symbolic, or discounted.

Follow the deed step by step. "And I bought the field of Hanameel my uncle’s son, that was in Anathoth, and weighed him the money, even seventeen shekels of silver." Full weight, counted out. The deed is subscribed, sealed, witnessed; an open copy and a sealed copy are put in an earthen vessel, that they may continue many days. And Jeremiah himself states the absurdity honestly in prayer: "And thou hast said unto me, O Lord GOD, Buy thee the field for money, and take witnesses; for the city is given into the hand of the Chaldeans." He does not pretend the purchase makes worldly sense. He obeys a word, and the word interprets itself: "For thus saith the LORD of hosts, the God of Israel; Houses and fields and vineyards shall be possessed again in this land." And at the book's end the promise is repeated as settled policy for the future: "Men shall buy fields for money, and subscribe evidences, and seal them, and take witnesses in the land of Benjamin, and in the places about Jerusalem, and in the cities of Judah, and in the cities of the mountains, and in the cities of the valley, and in the cities of the south: for I will cause their captivity to return, saith the LORD."

The doctrine, stated plainly: hope, in Jeremiah, is not a feeling about the future and it is not denial of the present. The same prophet signs for land he will never farm and announces a captivity he will not live to see end. Judgment is true, and mercy is truer — but neither cancels the other, and hope is the act that holds both at once, at full price, in public, with witnesses.

The rest of the week shows what that hope had to survive. The fall is narrated, and then narrated again in chapter 52 like a bell tolling. Lamentations opens on the city personified and alone: "How doth the city sit solitary, that was full of people! how is she become as a widow! she that was great among the nations, and princess among the provinces, how is she become tributary!" Grief here is not rushed or decorated; the poems are acrostics, sorrow worked through letter by letter, as if honesty itself needed an alphabet to hold it.

And in the middle chapter, at the middle of the grief, the turn comes — and notice its grammar: "This I recall to my mind, therefore have I hope" is how the passage begins in the chapter's flow, and then: "It is of the LORD’s mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not." "They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness." "The LORD is my portion, saith my soul; therefore will I hope in him." Spurgeon's exposition on this chapter, lingering over those verses, pictured hope as the thought that swims when everything else is drowned. The text's own claim is simpler and stronger: the speaker is not consumed — still alive, still addressed by mercy — and mercy arrives on a morning schedule, not a mood schedule. Not great is our strength. Not great is our record. Great is thy faithfulness.

Live it this week in Jeremiah's two motions. First, do one deed of hope at full price: invest time, money, prayer, or forgiveness in a future you may not personally enjoy, because God's word — not the visible odds — warrants it. Second, take your mercies morning by morning: each day this week, name one mercy of that day before you name one trouble. The field-buyer and the lament-singer are the same man. Be both.

Hope is a deed signed at full price on God's word — and his mercies arrive every morning.
