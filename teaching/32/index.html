Sabbath Teaching · Week 32

Buying a Field in a Dying City

Sabbath, May 29, 2027 · Readings: Jeremiah 32–52; Lamentations 1–4

What does hope look like when it has to sign paperwork? This week Jeremiah gave the strangest answer in all the prophets. Jerusalem is besieged. The Chaldean army is at the walls, the king has thrown Jeremiah into prison for prophesying that the city will fall — and it will fall; the prophet has never pretended otherwise — and in that prison courtyard, with the end visibly coming, Jeremiah buys a field.

Notice how deliberately the deed is done, because the deliberateness is the teaching. His cousin Hanameel comes, as God had said he would, offering the family field at Anathoth, in Benjamin — land already, for practical purposes, behind enemy lines. Jeremiah weighs out the money in full: "And I bought the field of Hanameel my uncle's son, that was in Anathoth, and weighed him the money, even seventeen shekels of silver." He signs the deed, seals it, calls witnesses, and orders both copies put in an earthen vessel, that they may continue many days. Then comes the interpretation, and it is not Jeremiah's optimism; it is God's word: "Houses and fields and vineyards shall be possessed again in this land." The purchase is a sermon you can hold. A man who truly believed the exile would last, and that return would follow it, acted on both beliefs at once — he neither denied the judgment nor doubted the mercy. If you want to learn hope from Jeremiah, learn it here: hope is not a feeling about the future. It is a deed done in the present, at full price, on God's word alone.

The rest of the week tells you what that hope had to survive. Chapter 39 narrates the fall itself — the walls breached, the king captured, the temple burned; chapter 52 retells it at the book's close, like a tolling bell, with the temple vessels carried away piece by piece. And Lamentations, which follows, refuses to hurry past the grief. The city sits solitary, become as a widow; her tears are on her cheeks in the night. Grief is given a strict, alphabetical form, as if sorrow itself had to be worked through letter by letter, honestly, to the end.

And in the middle of the ruins — the middle chapter, the middle verses — stands the sentence this whole canon may be most loved for. "It is of the LORD's mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness." Not great is our strength. Not great is our record. Great is thy faithfulness. Jeremiah bought a field on that faithfulness. Lamentations found it again in the ashes. So can you.

Hope is a deed done at full price on God's word alone — great is his faithfulness, new every morning.
