Sabbath Teaching · Week 12

A House Filled, a Kingdom Torn

Sabbath, January 9, 2027 · Readings: 1 Kings 8–22; 2 Kings 1–5

How close can glory and ruin stand to each other in one story? In this week’s reading, about four chapters apart. First Kings 8 is one of the Bible’s great summits. The ark is carried up into the finished temple, the singers sound as one, and the dedication reaches the tabernacle’s climax, now in stone: “So that the priests could not stand to minister because of the cloud: for the glory of the LORD had filled the house of the LORD.” Spurgeon, preaching on this pattern in “Temple Glories,” draws out what the filled house meant for everyone who would ever pray toward it: the worshippers of that day went home assured that they might pray and be heard — in his phrase, “God had filled the house–now He would hear their prayers.” Solomon’s great prayer then spends itself on future failures: Israel defeated, Israel exiled, Israel praying from a far country toward this house — and the stranger, not of Israel, who comes and prays here too. The glory is real, and the king praying under it already knows the people will need a way home. The week proves the prayer necessary: Solomon himself turns in old age after other gods, and at his death his son takes the throne.

Rehoboam refuses the elders’ counsel, takes the young men’s, and answers a plea for a lighter yoke with a threat — his little finger, he tells them, shall be thicker than his father’s loins. In chapter 12 the kingdom tears: ten tribes walk away under Jeroboam. What David united divides in a single day, over a single sentence — a king treating his people as a burden to be increased rather than a trust to be carried. Here is a king who answers to no one, and a kingdom halved before supper.

Into the divided, idol-building world that follows steps Elijah, and chapter 18 stages the confrontation Shechem anticipated. Carmel is crowded: one prophet of the Lord, four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal, and between them a whole nation trying to keep a foot in both camps. Elijah’s question is aimed not at Baal’s priests but at the crowd: “How long halt ye between two opinions? if the LORD be God, follow him: but if Baal, then follow him. And the people answered him not a word.” Spurgeon, preaching this verse in “Elijah’s Appeal to the Undecided,” divides the mountain exactly so: the devoted servant of the Lord, the decided servants of Baal, and the vast third company — the halting, who were secret half-hearted followers of Jehovah and public worshippers of Baal — and he preaches, as Elijah did, to the third company only. Baal’s prophets cry from morning to evening and there is no answer. Elijah rebuilds the broken altar, drowns everything in water until the trench runs full, and prays a short prayer. The fire falls. The people fall on their faces with the confession that gives the week its doctrine: “The LORD, he is the God; the LORD, he is the God.”

Then Elisha succeeds Elijah, and in 2 Kings 5 the beneficiary of Israel’s God is an enemy commander. Naaman of Syria, a leper, hears from a captive Israelite servant girl that there is a prophet in Israel; he comes with horses and chariots, and the cure he is offered offends him by its smallness: wash in Jordan, seven times. His servants talk him down with the plainest logic in Kings: “My father, if the prophet had bid thee do some great thing, wouldest thou not have done it? how much rather then, when he saith to thee, Wash, and be clean?” Spurgeon’s sermon on that verse, “A Serious Remonstrance,” is built on the same offence: plain remedies are despised precisely because they are plain. Naaman goes down: “Then went he down, and dipped himself seven times in Jordan, according to the saying of the man of God: and his flesh came again like unto the flesh of a little child, and he was clean.”

Doctrine stated plainly: a house filled with glory does not guarantee a kingdom kept; God will not be managed by kings or impressed by difficulty. He answers the short prayer of a decided man, and he heals proud enemies by small obediences — and the halting middle is the crowd both sermons are actually addressed to.

Practise Carmel’s cure for halting this week. Name the one opinion you are limping between — the loyalty you keep public and the one you keep private — and decide it, in deed, once: the altar rebuilt, the prayer prayed, the small commanded thing done seven times if God asks seven. And do not despise the plain remedy God has actually given you because you were hoping for a great one.

Stop halting: the Lord, he is God — and his cures are often as small as Wash, and be clean.
