Sabbath Teaching · Week 12

A House Filled, a Kingdom Torn

Sabbath, January 9, 2027 · Readings: 1 Kings 8–22; 2 Kings 1–5

How close can glory and ruin stand to each other in a single story? In this week's reading, they stand about four chapters apart. First Kings 8 is one of the Bible's great high points: Solomon gathers Israel, the ark is brought up into the finished temple, the singers lift their voices, and “the glory of the LORD had filled the house of the LORD,” so that the priests could not stand to minister. Solomon kneels before the altar with his hands spread toward heaven and prays — for Israel, for the stranger, for the exile who will one day pray toward this house and ask to come home.

Read that prayer slowly, because the rest of the week will test almost every line of it. Solomon himself turns, in his old age, toward the gods of his many wives. At his death, his son Rehoboam takes the throne and is asked for a lighter yoke. He answers with arrogance — “my little finger shall be thicker than my father's loins” — and in chapter 12 the kingdom tears: ten tribes walk away under Jeroboam, and what David united stands divided for the rest of this long story.

Into that divided, idol-prone world steps Elijah, and chapter 18 gives you Mount Carmel. One prophet against four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal, a water-drenched altar, a short prayer — and fire falling, until a wavering people fall on their faces crying, “The LORD, he is the God; the LORD, he is the God.” Yet even Elijah ends his part of this week running for his life from Jezebel, asking under a juniper tree to die, and being fed instead, and sent on to anoint Elisha, who will carry the work further than he will.

It is Elisha, in 2 Kings, who brings this week to its tender close. Naaman, commander of Syria's army — a leper, and an enemy of Israel — is sent by a captive Israelite girl's words to wash in Jordan. Proud, furious at the smallness of the cure, he is persuaded by his own servants to try it: “Then went he down, and dipped himself seven times in Jordan… and his flesh came again like unto the flesh of a little child, and he was clean.”

Watch for the pattern that holds the glory and the tearing together. God fills houses, answers by fire, and heals enemies — and still will not be managed by kings. A house can be filled with glory in one generation and the kingdom torn in the next, and God goes on calling his people back through prophets, widows, servants, and little girls who remember, in a foreign house, that there is a prophet in Israel.

Kneel in the filled house and keep praying its prayer — God still hears strangers, exiles, and enemies made clean.
