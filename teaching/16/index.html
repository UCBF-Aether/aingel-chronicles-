Sabbath Teaching · Week 16

The Calendar of Jubilees

Sabbath, February 6, 2027 · Readings: Jubilees 2–15

Can a calendar be a sermon? Jubilees says yes — and preaches it for fourteen chapters. This week the book retells creation and the early generations, but everything arrives with a date attached: feasts fixed, seasons divided, years counted. To a modern ear that can sound like mere bookkeeping. To Jubilees it is the opposite. Time, in this book, is not neutral. Time is either kept as worship or lost as forgetfulness.

Go to the centre: chapter 6. Noah comes out of the ark, and the year is set out as heaven reckons it. The command, in Charles's translation: “command thou the children of Israel that they observe the years according to this reckoning — three hundred and sixty-four days, and (these) will constitute a complete year, and they will not disturb its time from its days and from its feasts.” Three hundred and sixty-four is fifty-two weeks exactly. Nothing drifts. Every feast lands where it belongs, every year, on the same day of the week, because the count is written, the book insists, on heavenly tables. Then hear the warning that follows, and notice what is actually at stake: if the count is disturbed, “they will go wrong as to the new moons and seasons and sabbaths and festivals.” Not merely wrong about arithmetic — wrong about Sabbaths. In Jubilees, to lose the calendar is to lose the appointments, and to lose the appointments is to forget the covenant the appointments remember. Keeping time rightly is keeping faith.

Now step back to chapter 2, where the pattern starts. The angel retells the six days, and the seventh arrives not as an Israelite custom but as a heavenly one: God gave his people “a great sign, the Sabbath day,” and the angels themselves keep Sabbath with him in heaven and on earth. Read that beside chapter 6 and the whole week's theology locks together. The Sabbath is older than Sinai, older than Israel, woven into the first week of the world; the calendar exists, in large part, to protect it — to make sure the seventh day is never swallowed, shifted, or forgotten by a drifting count. Rest is not what is left over after the important days are scheduled. In Jubilees, rest is what the scheduling is for.

Honest sermon research on Jubilees is thin — historic preachers rarely preached it, because most churches did not keep the book. What does exist is scholarly preaching and teaching on its calendar: the 364-day year as exactly fifty-two Sabbaths, a year in which no feast ever collides with a Sabbath, preserved also at Qumran. That observation is worth keeping, because it shows the calendar's intent: this is a system built so that holy time is never crowded out.

A word about the text in your hands. Jubilees is read here in R. H. Charles's 1902 translation from the Ethiopic. The scan of that text had been badly damaged by machine-reading errors and was repaired against Charles word by word — 598 repairs, every one logged. An ordered book, honestly handed on.

Watch, too, how the feasts in this week's chapters are never floating traditions. Again and again a feast is tied to a dated act of God — the ark, the deliverance, the covenant renewed — as if the book were saying that a people who cannot say when God acted will soon be unsure that he acted. Dates are Jubilees' way of nailing memory to the wall.

The doctrine is plain: God is Lord of time, not only of space and souls; the days themselves are his gift and his appointment. The practice: keep the seventh day this week the way Jubilees imagines heaven keeping it — deliberately, in advance, protected. Decide before Friday what the Sabbath will hold and what it will not. Put one appointment with God on the calendar with the same seriousness as any appointment with a king. Count the day as a gift received, not as time off taken.

Count the days as gifts — the Sabbath was written into the first week of the world.
