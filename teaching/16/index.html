Sabbath Teaching · Week 16

The Calendar of Jubilees

Sabbath, February 6, 2027 · Readings: Jubilees 2–15

What if time itself were a form of worship? That is the wonder at the heart of this week's reading. Jubilees retells creation and the early story of the world, but it tells everything by date — new moons, seasons, feasts, and years all fixed, the book insists, by heaven's own reckoning, written on heavenly tables and not left to human guesswork.

The centre of it is chapter 6, where the calendar is set out in earnest. The year is to be reckoned at three hundred and sixty-four days — “and (these) will constitute a complete year” — a year of exactly fifty-two weeks, in which, the text promises, nothing will be disturbed: “they will not disturb its time from its days and from its feasts; for everything will fall out in them according to their testimony.” The four seasons are marked by days of remembrance at the new moons of the first, fourth, seventh, and tenth months, “written and ordained as a testimony for ever.” Why such severity about counting? Because if the count slips, everything slips with it: “all the children of Israel will forget … and will forget the new moons, and seasons, and sabbaths, and they will go wrong as to all the order of the years.” In Jubilees, to lose the calendar is to lose the feasts, and to lose the feasts is to forget the covenant they remember. Keeping time rightly is keeping faith.

And the pattern reaches all the way back to creation. In chapter 2 the angel recounts the six days again — the heavens and the waters, the firmament, the seas and the garden, the sun and moon set “to give light upon all the earth” and to mark the order of days — and then the seventh: God “kept Sabbath on the seventh day and hallowed it for all ages, and appointed it as a sign for all His works.” The Sabbath is not an afterthought added at Sinai. In this telling it is woven into the fabric of the first week, kept in heaven before it was ever commanded on earth. Rest, too, is part of the calendar — the crown of the counting.

A word about the text in your hands, because this edition tells you plainly how it came to you. Jubilees is read here in R. H. Charles's translation from the Ethiopic, first published in 1902. The scan of that text had been badly damaged by machine-reading errors, and it was repaired against Charles, word by word — 598 repairs, every one logged. An ordered book, honestly handed on: that is a fitting way to read it.

Count the days as gifts — even time, kept rightly, is worship.
