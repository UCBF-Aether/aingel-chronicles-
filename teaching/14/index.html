Sabbath Teaching · Week 14

History Sung a Second Time

Sabbath, January 23, 2027 · Readings: 1 Chronicles 6–29; 2 Chronicles 1–8

Why tell a story your hearers already know? Because knowing and remembering are not the same thing. You have already walked through Samuel and Kings. This week Chronicles walks the same ground again, and the Chronicler changes the emphasis on purpose: long family lines, Levites and singers, and a king who will never build God's house spending his last strength preparing for someone else to build it. History sung a second time is not repetition. It is memory turned into worship.

Open the key passage slowly: 1 Chronicles 28–29. Notice first the charge. David speaks to Solomon in front of everyone: “know thou the God of thy father, and serve him with a perfect heart and with a willing mind.” Hold on to that word willing — the chapter will not let it go. David gives from his own treasure first. The leaders follow. The people follow. Then the narrator stops and tells you why the day mattered: “Then the people rejoiced, for that they offered willingly, because with perfect heart they offered willingly to the LORD: and David the king also rejoiced with great joy.” Count the repetitions. Willingly, willingly. Nobody is taxed into this. Nobody is shamed into it. The Chronicler is teaching a doctrine by repetition: the offering God delights in is the offering no one had to extract.

Then David prays, and the prayer states the doctrine outright. “Thine, O LORD is the greatness, and the power, and the glory, and the victory, and the majesty” — everything named, heaven and earth included, belongs to God before a single talent is laid down. So David asks the question that dismantles all pride in giving: “But who am I, and what is my people, that we should be able to offer so willingly after this sort? for all things come of thee, and of thine own have we given thee.” Giving, in this text, is not generosity toward a needy God. It is returning what was always his, with thanks. David adds one more line preachers have always lingered on: God “triest the heart, and hast pleasure in uprightness.” The gold can be counted; what God weighs is the heart that carried it.

Spurgeon, preaching through this part of Chronicles, fixed on the ground itself — the threshing floor where the angel sheathed his sword became the temple site — and on a church built where judgment stopped. That is the Chronicler's own instinct: the house rises where mercy has already happened, and the people's willing gifts are a response, not a purchase.

There is one more thing to watch before the prayer ends. Solomon is standing in the middle of all this giving, young and, by his father's own admission, tender — and David prays for him by name, asking God to give his son a perfect heart to keep the commandments and to build the house. The Chronicler's point is generational. A willing offering that is not handed on becomes a museum piece. David does not only empty his own treasure house; he prays the willingness itself down into his son, and the book then shows you Solomon building, in the chapters that close this week's reading, on ground his father's willing heart prepared. Memory becomes worship only when the next generation is taught to offer, not merely told that their fathers did.

So the doctrine is plain: God owns all, gives all, and receives back from his people only what he first put into their hands — and he delights when the returning is willing. Here is the practice for this week. Before you give anything — money, time, skill, forgiveness — name it first as God's. Say it in prayer, in David's words if you need words: of thine own have I given thee. Then give once this week without being asked, without announcement, and without keeping a record of your own generosity. And retell one story of God's carrying — your family's, this people's — out loud to someone younger. That is what Chronicles is for. The story told again, willingly, becomes the offering.

Of thine own have we given thee — remember rightly, and give willingly.
