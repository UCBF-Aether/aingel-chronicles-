Sabbath Teaching · Week 5

The Report That Closed the Land

Sabbath, November 21, 2026 · Readings: Leviticus 24–27; Numbers 1–19

How can a land stand wide open in front of a people, and still be shut to them for forty years? This week you closed Leviticus with some of its weightiest hope. In chapter 25 the land itself is given a Sabbath: rest every seventh year, and in the fiftieth year this proclamation: “And ye shall hallow the fiftieth year, and proclaim liberty throughout all the land unto all the inhabitants thereof: it shall be a jubile unto you; and ye shall return every man unto his possession, and ye shall return every man unto his family.” Hear what that assumes. The land is a gift, not a possession to be squeezed dry; no family’s loss is meant to be permanent; even the soil is owed rest.

Then Numbers opens with everything prepared. A census counts the tribes; the camp is arranged around the tabernacle; Levites are set apart; the priests bless the people with words still prayed over congregations today: “The LORD bless thee, and keep thee.” The trumpets sound, the cloud lifts, and the people march to the very border of the gift. Preparation, in this story, is not the problem. Israel arrives at the edge of the land with God’s blessing in its ears and God’s jubilee in its law.

And then twelve men walk through the land for forty days and come back carrying its fruit. Read Numbers 13 carefully and you will see that the ten and the two do not disagree about the facts. All twelve saw the same grapes, the same walls, the same tall men. The ten’s report ends in a sentence about themselves: “And there we saw the giants, the sons of Anak, which come of the giants: and we were in our own sight as grasshoppers, and so we were in their sight.” Mark the slide: from the land’s real dangers to a verdict on their own size, and then — the quiet catastrophe — to an assumption about how they looked in the giants’ eyes, which they could not possibly know. Fear had begun writing the report. Caleb answers with facts of a different order: “And Caleb stilled the people before Moses, and said, Let us go up at once, and possess it; for we are well able to overcome it.” Caleb is not denying the giants. He is denying that the giants are the largest fact in the story.

God’s own verdict on Caleb names what the two had and the ten lacked: “But my servant Caleb, because he had another spirit with him, and hath followed me fully, him will I bring into the land whereinto he went; and his seed shall possess it.” Spurgeon, preaching on this verse in “Caleb — The Man for the Times,” unpacks “fully” as obedience that is universal, undivided, and sincere, and he adds the sharp test this teaching borrows with credit: “One of the safest tests of sincerity is found in a willingness to suffer for the cause.” Caleb was willing to be stoned by his own congregation rather than sign the majority report. That willingness — not optimism, not temperament — is what “another spirit” looked like in public.

The people chose the ten. They wept all night, spoke of choosing a captain and returning to Egypt, and refused the very gift they had travelled so far to receive. So the land was closed — not by its walls, not by its giants, but by unbelief at its border. God pardons the people at Moses’ pleading, and the pardon is real; yet that generation will die in the wilderness, and the children they claimed would be a prey will be the ones to enter. The chapters that follow carry the long cost: murmuring, rebellion, Aaron’s rod budding in the night as God vindicates the leadership fear kept attacking.

Doctrine stated plainly: a report can be factually accurate and spiritually false. Truth about giants is not faithfulness; faithfulness is the same facts held inside a larger fact — the God who brought you out, blessed you, and promised the land. The jubilee country of Leviticus 25 did not move. The people simply would not enter it on God’s terms.

Practise Caleb’s discipline this week. First, write down the frightening facts of one situation facing you — honestly, giants included. Second, beneath them, write the larger facts: what God has already done, and what he has promised; then decide from the second list, not the first. Third, if you must dissent from a fearful majority — at work, at home, among friends — do it the Caleb way: still the noise, speak once, plainly, and accept the cost without contempt for the frightened. The land is entered by trust, or not at all.

Count the giants honestly — then count God, and go up at once.
