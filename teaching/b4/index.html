Bonus Teaching · From the Appendix

The Chapter that Survived Only in Ge'ez

Readings: Kebra Nagast 118–119

For more than a century, the Kebra Nagast in Budge's English has ended at the same page. E. A. Wallis Budge's 1922 translation — the text printed in this edition — closes with chapter 118, a colophon: the copyists' own sign-off, telling how the book was turned into Arabic from a Coptic manuscript belonging to the throne of Mark the Evangelist. It is a fitting last word for a translated book. It is not the last word of the book.

The Ge'ez has one more chapter. Chapter 119 turns the page after the colophon and simply continues the line the whole work has been drawing: “And after our Saviour was born, these kings reigned:” Then the names come, one after another, like beads on a string: Bazēn first; the two beloved brothers, ʾAbrəha and ʾAṣbəḥa; Kaleb, whose name rings through Ethiopia's own histories; Gäbrä Mäsqäl; and on, name upon name, until the line itself turns — “the kingdom was taken away, and given to a people who were not of the tribe of David” — and a new line begins, from Yəkʷəno ʾAmlak down to ʿAmədä Ṣəyon.

Why did its English readers wait so long for the ending? Because Budge's published translation ends short of it, and the chapter survived only in Ge'ez. For this edition it was translated fresh, directly from the Ge'ez — and no earlier published English translation of this chapter is known. The claim needs no inflating to be a wonder. A beloved book's last page was missing from its English life, and now it is not missing.

Even here, the edition kept its habits of honesty: a handful of the kings' names are hard to read in the surviving text, and the working translation marks those names rather than guess at them — a list of kings is only worth printing if every name in it is really there. What the parchment gives, we give, down to the very last page. And the last page turns out to be the argument in miniature: the Kebra Nagast tells of glory travelling in a line, from Solomon and Sheba's son through kings named one by one into Ethiopia's own story. A story like that cannot end at a colophon. It ends, rightly, in a genealogy.

Watch for the two brothers the list calls beloved, and for the quiet sentence where the kingdom changes hands, “as God saith.” The last chapter has no battles and no speeches. It has names — and in a book about a line of kings, names are the whole argument.

The book waited more than a century for its final page in English — read to the end of things; the end is part of the gift.
