Sabbath Teaching · Week 10

Kings Answerable

Sabbath, December 26, 2026 · Readings: 1 Samuel 12–31; 2 Samuel 1–9

What is a king for, if he is answerable to no one above him? Israel demanded a king, and this week the first two kings teach the answer from opposite directions: Saul, who falls by refusing to be answerable, and David, who rises by accepting it. Watch Saul’s fall closely, because it does not begin with a monstrous crime. It begins with pressure and impatience. In chapter 13, with his army scattering and Samuel delayed, Saul offers the sacrifice himself — a religious act, performed by the wrong man, because waiting had become unbearable. In chapter 15 the pattern completes itself. Sent against Amalek with a clear command, he spares King Agag and the best of the flocks, and explains the spoil as sacrifice: the people kept the best, he says, to offer to the Lord. Partial obedience, dressed in worship’s clothing.

Samuel’s answer is the sharpest sentence spoken to a king in the whole Bible, and it deserves to be read word by word: “Hath the LORD as great delight in burnt offerings and sacrifices, as in obeying the voice of the LORD? Behold, to obey is better than sacrifice, and to hearken than the fat of rams.” Then the diagnosis: “For rebellion is as the sin of witchcraft, and stubbornness is as iniquity and idolatry. Because thou hast rejected the word of the LORD, he hath also rejected thee from being king.” Hear what Samuel is and is not saying. He is not devaluing sacrifice — the same God commanded the sacrifices. He is exposing substitution: Saul offered God the thing God had not asked for, in place of the thing God had. A sacrifice chosen by the sinner, Spurgeon argues in his sermon “Obedience Better than Sacrifice,” is no excuse at all — his record of the sermon puts Samuel’s point this way: sacrifices were no excuse for an act of direct rebellion; and Spurgeon calls the sentence itself, in his Morning and Evening reading on this verse, “worthy to be printed in letters of gold, and to be hung up before the eyes of the present idolatrous generation.” Saul confesses — “I have sinned” — and in the same breath asks Samuel to honour him before the elders. It is confession as reputation management, and it fools no one. The kingdom is torn from him, and the rest of 1 Samuel watches a rejected king hunt the man who will replace him, growing lonelier and harder until he falls on Gilboa with his sons beside him.

Against Saul stands David, and the contrast the writer wants is not talent — Saul had every gift. It is hearkening. As a boy, David faces Goliath with the week’s other great confession: “the battle is the LORD’s, and he will give you into our hands” — trusting God, not weaponry, to decide. As a fugitive, twice he has the sleeping or trapped Saul in his power — once in a cave, once in the camp at night — and twice he refuses to take the crown by the sword: the Lord’s anointed is the Lord’s business. David will receive what God promised, or wait. That refusal is obedience in its most expensive form: obeying when disobedience would be quick, popular, and apparently justified.

When the crown finally comes and David brings the ark up to Jerusalem and plans to build God a house, the answer reverses his plan entirely: God will build David a house. “And thine house and thy kingdom shall be established for ever before thee: thy throne shall be established for ever.” It is the largest promise yet made to any man in Scripture — and the narrative places it after Saul’s whole sad education, as if to say: a promise this size is only safe in the hands of a king who has learned to be answerable.

Doctrine stated plainly: in God’s kingdom, authority is delegated and accountable. No office — king, parent, pastor, elder — lifts a person above the word that gave them the office. Worship is never an acceptable substitute for obedience, and the earliest symptom of a falling leader is not scandal but small, anxious, self-justifying disobedience, explained as devotion.

So examine one substitution this week. Ask, honestly: is there a command I know — about money, about speech, about reconciliation, about purity — that I have been replacing with religious activity: giving instead of forgiving, serving instead of repenting, attending instead of obeying? Name it, and then do the actual commanded thing, in its actual smallness, before the week ends. And if you hold any authority over others, invite answerability before you need it: give one trusted person permission to tell you when you are explaining instead of obeying.

To obey is better than sacrifice — stay answerable, especially when obedience is small and slow.
