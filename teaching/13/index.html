Sabbath Teaching · Week 13

The City Falls, the Story Refuses to End

Sabbath, January 16, 2027 · Readings: 2 Kings 6–25; 1 Chronicles 1–5

How does a story go on after its worst chapter — after the city itself has fallen? This week you read 2 Kings to the end, and the end is as sorrowful as Scripture gets. Elisha's ministry fills the early chapters: a borrowed axe head made to swim, a Shunammite son restored to life, a besieged Samaria delivered overnight, chariots of fire glimpsed by a frightened servant who is told, “they that be with us are more than they that be with them.” Elisha dies, and kings mostly worsen. Yet even in the long decline there are lights: Hezekiah, who trusts the Lord when Assyria surrounds Jerusalem and spreads the threatening letter before God in the temple; and Josiah, who finds the book of the law, tears his clothes, reforms the land from top to bottom, and keeps a Passover such as had not been held since the days of the judges.

They delay the end. They do not prevent it. In 2 Kings 25, Nebuchadnezzar's army returns, the city is besieged until famine drives mothers desperate, the walls are broken, King Zedekiah is captured and blinded, and “he burnt the house of the LORD, and the king's house, and all the houses of Jerusalem.” The temple Solomon dedicated, the ark's house, the city of David — burned, looted, emptied, its people carried to Babylon. If the Bible ended there, it would end in smoke.

It does not end there. After a small, quiet note — the captive King Jehoiachin lifted up, in Babylon, to eat at the king's table — the reader turns the page and meets Chronicles. And Chronicles does something astonishing: it begins with names. Adam, Seth, Enos. Chapter after chapter of genealogies, in this week's reading alone carrying the line from Adam through Abraham, Israel, Judah, and David's house. To a grieving reader this can look like the driest possible restart. Look closer, and it is the opposite.

The Chronicler is preaching with a list. To children born in exile, who might wonder whether the story that began in Eden had simply stopped in Babylon, he answers: here is your name's road home. Every generation is named because every generation was carried. The fall of the city was a chapter, even if it was the darkest chapter. It was not the end of the book.

Watch for that refusal to end in the pages you have just read. Even here, at the story's darkest close, the Chronicler is already retelling it from the beginning — because this God specialises, if the word may be allowed, in beginnings after endings. This week, let the names do their quiet work on you. The city fell. The story, stubbornly, prayerfully, refused to.

When a chapter ends in smoke, read the names and remember — God is still beginning again.
