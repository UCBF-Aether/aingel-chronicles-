Sabbath Teaching · Week 31

The Prophet of the New Covenant

Sabbath, May 22, 2027 · Readings: Jeremiah 2–31

What do you do when an arrangement has failed for centuries, in plain sight, with the failure documented by the very man sent to announce its replacement? That is Jeremiah's position, and this week's reading climbs toward chapter 31 the way a road climbs toward a pass. Before the summit, walk the three stations the week sets in your path, because the promise means little until you feel the weight it has to lift.

First, the temple sermon, chapter 7. Jeremiah stands in the gate and forbids the people their favourite sentence — the temple of the LORD, repeated as a charm. In its place he puts conditions with names on them: "Thus saith the LORD of hosts, the God of Israel, Amend your ways and your doings, and I will cause you to dwell in this place." "For if ye throughly amend your ways and your doings; if ye throughly execute judgment between a man and his neighbour;" "If ye oppress not the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow, and shed not innocent blood in this place, neither walk after other gods to your hurt:" Stone walls, Jeremiah insists, are not a substitute for justice at the gate and mercy to the stranger. A holy place cannot sanctify an unjust people; it can only testify against them.

Second, the potter's house, chapter 18, where the doctrine is shown to hands and eyes: "O house of Israel, cannot I do with you as this potter? saith the LORD. Behold, as the clay is in the potter’s hand, so are ye in mine hand, O house of Israel." Clay that spoils can be reworked — but Jeremiah's generation keeps spoiling. Third, the letter to the exiles, chapter 29, which is Jeremiah the pastor at his most surprising. To people longing for a quick rescue he writes: settle. "And seek the peace of the city whither I have caused you to be carried away captives, and pray unto the LORD for it: for in the peace thereof shall ye have peace." And he gives them God's own summary of his intentions: "For I know the thoughts that I think toward you, saith the LORD, thoughts of peace, and not of evil, to give you an expected end." Notice: that famous promise is not addressed to people in comfort. It is addressed to captives being told to plant gardens in the city of their captivity.

Then chapter 31, and the summit. "Behold, the days come, saith the LORD, that I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel, and with the house of Judah:" The contrast is drawn immediately: "Not according to the covenant that I made with their fathers in the day that I took them by the hand to bring them out of the land of Egypt; which my covenant they brake, although I was an husband unto them, saith the LORD:" Spurgeon preached on this text twice over — in God's Writing Upon Man's Heart and in his early sermon on "I will be their God" — and his central observation is the one the text forces on any careful reader: the old covenant ran on the principle of requirement, do and live; the new covenant, read from first word to last, runs on God's own "I will." Listen to the verbs in the promise itself, and count who acts: "But this shall be the covenant that I will make with the house of Israel; After those days, saith the LORD, I will put my law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts; and will be their God, and they shall be my people." "And they shall teach no more every man his neighbour, and every man his brother, saying, Know the LORD: for they shall all know me, from the least of them unto the greatest of them, saith the LORD: for I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more."

State it plainly. The new covenant is not the old one with better people, or better intentions, or a fresh start. It differs in location — the law moves from stone to inward parts and hearts — and in foundation: forgiveness comes first, "for I will forgive," and knowledge of God is given, least to greatest, not achieved. Live it this week. Stop treating one stubborn sin as a problem of trying harder at the surface; ask God, in Jeremiah's own words, to write his law inwardly, and sit daily with the text until it begins to be written. And practise chapter 29 where you actually live: pray by name for the peace of your own city, however exiled you feel in it.

A covenant of God's own "I will" — written on the heart, founded on forgiveness.
