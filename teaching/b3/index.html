Bonus Teaching · From the Appendix

Sheba's Journey

Readings: Kebra Nagast 21–34

The Book of Kings gives the Queen of Sheba a single page: she heard of Solomon's fame, came to prove him with hard questions, saw his wisdom and his house, gave him gold and spices, and went home. The Kebra Nagast — Ethiopia's own great telling, treasured as tradition — gives her what Kings does not: the journey itself, from the first rumour that reached her court to the son who carried the story into a nation's life.

It begins with a merchant. Tâmrîn, leader of the Queen's caravans — five hundred and twenty camels, the book counts them — trades as far as Jerusalem and comes home with a report no bale of goods could match: a king whose wisdom answers everything. The Queen — Mâkĕdâ, the book names her — addresses her people and sets out herself, arriving in Jerusalem with very many precious gifts. She blesses Solomon for the wisdom she finds in him, and she stays to learn: Solomon turns her questions toward the God “Who created the universe,” the maker of the sun and the moon, and sets his worship before her.

The book does not rush her home. There is the night of the famous oath — Solomon swearing he would not take her by force, the Queen swearing in return — and then the parting gift. Solomon took her aside, drew the ring from his own little finger, and gave it to her: “Take this so that thou mayest not forget me.” It was a sign, by which a son, if one were born, would one day be known to his father. And Solomon told her of his dream, the book says: a sun that had risen upon Israel, but snatched itself away and flew off to light up the country of Ethiopia. She went home rejoicing, and in her own country she brought forth a son.

Years later the journey runs backward. The young king of Ethiopia — Menyelek, as Budge's title names him — travels with Tâmrîn to the father he has never seen, carrying the sign. Solomon knows him, honours him, and when the young man turns for home he does not go alone: the children of Israel's nobles go with him — and with them, as the story of the Ark tells, goes Zion herself. The Queen's hard questions have become a nation's founding story.

Watch for the pattern of the journey: a report believed, a road taken, questions asked in person, a sign carried home. In Kings she is a visitor. In Ethiopia's telling she is a seeker — and the seeking, not the gold, is what the book treasures.

Come with your hard questions, as she did — the road to wisdom is walked, not wished.
