Sabbath Teaching · Week 21

Ezra's Questions

Sabbath, March 13, 2027 · Readings: Nehemiah 4–13; 1 Esdras 1–9; 2 Esdras 1–16

Why do the righteous suffer while the wicked flourish — and how many of the saved will there even be? This week holds three books, and the hardest of them, 2 Esdras, will not put that question down. The book speaks in Ezra's name — Salathiel, who is also Esdras — a generation after Jerusalem's fall, and it gives its Ezra no pat comfort. In chapters 3–7 he argues with the angel Uriel honestly and at length: Adam sinned and we are his children; Israel was chosen and Israel is crushed; the world is wide and the saved look few. Why?

Uriel's first answer is not an explanation. It is a test. “Go thy way, weigh me the weight of the fire, or measure me the blast of the wind, or call me again the day that is past.” Ezra cannot. No one born can. And the angel draws the conclusion without cruelty but without flinching: “How should thy vessel then be able to comprehend the way of the Highest?” — a vessel made of this corrupted world cannot hold the measures of the world to come. Watch what the book is doing. It does not rebuke Ezra for asking; the asking fills chapters, and God keeps sending the angel back to the conversation. What it rebukes is the demand that the answer arrive at Ezra's scale. What Ezra receives instead is a promise with a shape: this age is a seed-time, a harvest is fixed and coming, God has weighed the age and numbered its days, and the end will make sense of what the middle cannot. Generations of readers in times of loss have kept this book close for exactly that honesty — it asks the forbidden question out loud, in prayer, and stays in the room.

The week's other books answer in deeds and in debate. In Nehemiah the wall rises under threat, and the answer to danger is a sentence every preacher since Spurgeon has loved: “Nevertheless we made our prayer unto our God, and set a watch against them day and night, because of them.” Prayer and a watch. Then the unforgettable picture: “every one with one of his hands wrought in the work, and with the other hand held a weapon.” Spurgeon took Nehemiah 4:17–18 as the motto of his magazine, The Sword and the Trowel — combat with sin and labour for the Lord, in the same pair of hands, at the same hour. Nehemiah does not tell the builders to choose between trusting God and watching the road. Faith that will not set a watch is presumption; a watch without prayer is panic.

And in 1 Esdras the week stages its famous contest. Three guardsmen debate what is strongest — wine, the king, women — and young Zorobabel wins by naming a fourth thing, and the court shouts its verdict: “Great is Truth, and mighty above all things.” His speech ends, “Blessed be the God of truth” — and his prize is permission to rebuild Jerusalem. Truth, in this book, is not an abstraction that wins debates. It is the thing that gets cities rebuilt.

Hold the three together and the week preaches one sermon: bring God the hardest questions honestly, as Ezra does; pray and set a watch, as Nehemiah does; and let truth — not wine, not power, not flattery — be the strongest thing in the room, as Zorobabel does. The doctrine: God is not threatened by honest questions, but he answers them on his scale and in his season, and meanwhile his people are to build with both hands occupied. The practice: take your one hardest question to God in prayer this week, out loud, in Ezra's manner — state it fully, then sit for the part of the answer that is a promise rather than an explanation. And on the Nehemiah side, pair every anxious prayer this week with one watchful act: the message sent, the boundary set, the work done with your other hand.

Bring God the hardest question honestly — pray, set a watch, and keep building.
