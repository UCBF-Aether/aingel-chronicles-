Sabbath Teaching · Week 35

Daniel's Fourteenth Chapter

Sabbath, June 19, 2027 · Readings: Ezekiel 45–48; Daniel 1–14; Hosea 1–14; Amos 1–5

How many chapters does Daniel have? In most Bibles you would say twelve. In this canon you read fourteen this week, and the two extra chapters — and the long prayer hidden inside a familiar one — complete the book's own argument. This Sabbath, see the whole of Daniel standing together, as the Ethiopian church has always read it.

The familiar stories come first: Daniel and his friends refusing the king's food, the fiery furnace, the handwriting on the wall, the lions' den, and then the visions of beasts and kingdoms in chapters 7 to 12. But inside chapter 3, the furnace chapter, this edition restores what the King James Apocrypha kept: the Prayer of Azariah and the Song of the Three Children, standing here as verses 24 to 98 of the chapter. Azariah prays from inside the fire — confessing that God has executed true judgment, and pleading not on Israel's record but on Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and the holy covenant. Then the three children sing from inside the fire, calling on all the works of the Lord — sun and moon, showers and dew, frost and heat — to bless the Lord and praise him for ever. The furnace becomes a choir. Other traditions moved this singing to an appendix; here it never left the flames.

Chapter 13 is Susanna. It is a sobering courtroom story: a righteous woman, one that feared the Lord, is falsely accused by two elders whose word everyone is prepared to take. What saves her is young Daniel's refusal to accept testimony untested. He questions the witnesses apart, and their stories break against each other. False witness, this chapter teaches, cannot survive honest questions asked one witness at a time.

Chapter 14, Bel and the Dragon, is Daniel the detective of idols. Bel's priests secretly eat the god's offerings through a hidden door; Daniel scatters ashes on the temple floor and lets footprints tell the truth. The dragon, a living creature worshipped as a god, is fed and bursts — a god that can be killed by its dinner was never God. And keep the spelling straight: the prophet who appears in this chapter, carried by an angel to feed Daniel in the lions' den, is spelled Habbacuc — double b, single k — because that is the spelling the King James Apocrypha carries, from which this chapter was restored. His own book, which you will read next week, is spelled Habakkuk.

Your week's other readings frame him well. Ezekiel closed with a river flowing from the temple, deepening as it goes, until everything lives where the river comes. Hosea grieved through fourteen chapters — God a father who taught his child to walk and cannot give him up. Amos, in the chapters you have read so far, demanded that worship and justice meet: "let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream."

Sing inside the furnace, question false witness honestly, and let justice run down like waters.
