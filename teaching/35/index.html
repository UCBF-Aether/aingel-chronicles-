Sabbath Teaching · Week 35

Daniel's Fourteenth Chapter

Sabbath, June 19, 2027 · Readings: Ezekiel 45–48; Daniel 1–14; Hosea 1–14; Amos 1–5

How many chapters does Daniel have? Twelve, in most Bibles. Fourteen, in this canon — and the difference is not padding. The restored material completes Daniel's argument: that the living God, not the idols of empire, rules, delivers, and judges falsehood. Watch the whole book stand together, and pay closest attention to the three places most readers have never been given.

The first is inside a chapter you thought you knew. In the furnace chapter, chapter 3, this edition restores the Prayer of Azariah and the Song of the Three Children as verses 24 to 98, from the King James Apocrypha. The narrative hinge is one quiet verse: "And they walked in the midst of the fire, praising God, and blessing the Lord." Then Azariah stands and prays from inside the fire — confessing that God has executed true judgment, and pleading not on Israel's record but on the fathers and the covenant. Then the song: "Then the three, as out of one mouth, praised, glorified, and blessed, God in the furnace, saying," — and what follows is a roll-call of creation, works of the Lord summoned one kind at a time to bless him. Spurgeon preached on this chapter in Consolation in the Furnace, on the fourth figure walking in the fire, and his burden was integrity under loss: lose all, he urged, rather than lose your integrity; hold a clear conscience as the rarest jewel. The restored verses add what his text assumed — inside the furnace, the young men do not merely endure. They confess, and then they sing, and the singing is offered before any deliverance is visible.

The second restored chapter is Susanna, chapter 13: "And he took a wife, whose name was Susanna, the daughter of Chelcias, a very fair woman, and one that feared the Lord." Falsely accused by two elders whose office everyone trusts, she cries out to the God that knowest the secrets. Deliverance comes through a young judge's refusal to accept testimony untested: "Therefore when she was led to be put to death, the Lord raised up the holy spirit of a young youth whose name was Daniel:" Daniel stops the procession with a procedural demand: "So he standing in the midst of them said, Are ye such fools, ye sons of Israel, that without examination or knowledge of the truth ye have condemned a daughter of Israel?" "Return again to the place of judgment: for they have borne false witness against her." The witnesses are separated, questioned apart, and convicted out of their own mouths. The doctrine is practical: false witness cannot survive honest questions asked one witness at a time, and a community that rushes to condemn without examination has already sinned, whatever the verdict.

The third is Bel and the Dragon, chapter 14, which opens with Daniel's creed: "Who answered and said, Because I may not worship idols made with hands, but the living God, who hath created the heaven and the earth, and hath sovereignty over all flesh." Bel's food is eaten by priests through a secret door; ashes on a floor expose the footprints. The dragon, worshipped because it lives, is killed by what it is fed — a god that can be killed by its dinner was never God. And keep the spelling the text itself gives you: the prophet carried by an angel to feed Daniel in the den is Habbacuc — double b, single k — as the King James Apocrypha spells him: "Now there was in Jewry a prophet, called Habbacuc, who had made pottage, and had broken bread in a bowl, and was going into the field, for to bring it to the reapers." "Then the angel of the Lord took him by the crown, and bare him by the hair of his head, and through the vehemency of his spirit set him in Babylon over the den." His own book, spelled Habakkuk, waits for next week.

Your frame readings agree. Ezekiel closes with a river where everything lives whither the river cometh. Hosea grieves and cannot let go: "How shall I give thee up, Ephraim? how shall I deliver thee, Israel? how shall I make thee as Admah? how shall I set thee as Zeboim? mine heart is turned within me, my repentings are kindled together." And Amos, in the chapters read so far — Amos 7 is next week's, and not used here — demands: "But let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream."

Live it this week. When accused or when tempted to repeat an accusation, practise Susanna's chapter: examine separately, ask the second question, refuse the rushed verdict. When pressed to bend, keep the furnace integrity Spurgeon preached — and, with Azariah, confess first and sing before the outcome changes.

Confess and sing inside the furnace, examine before you condemn — and worship the living God, not idols made with hands.
