Sabbath Teaching · Week 41

The King and His Kingdom

Sabbath, July 31, 2027 · Readings: Matthew 4–28

What kind of king opens his reign by blessing people the world calls losers? Read Matthew 5:1–3 the way you would read a coronation speech, because that is what it is. Jesus sees the multitudes, goes up into a mountain, sits — the teacher's posture — and opens his mouth. His first public word is not a command, a threat, or a tax. It is "Blessed." And the first people blessed are not the strong: "Blessed are the poor in spirit: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven." Watch the tense. Not theirs will be. Theirs is. The kingdom is not a prize dangled at the top of a long climb; it is handed, at the foot of the mountain, to people who know they are spiritually bankrupt.

C. H. Spurgeon, preaching on this text in 1873 — in "The First Beatitude," companion to his survey "The Beatitudes" — made exactly that observation with a builder's picture: a ladder, if it is to be of any use, must have its first rung near the ground. Had the first blessing been given to the pure in heart, Spurgeon argued, beginners could never have mounted; the Saviour begins at the beginning, with poverty of spirit, so that the weakest climber can start. That is close reading, not decoration — it follows the order of the text itself. Mourning comes before comfort, meekness before inheritance, hunger before filling, in Matthew's order and in ours.

From the mountain, keep watching how this King rules. He touches lepers before he heals them. He eats with publicans. He stills a storm with a word, then asks his frightened men why they feared. In chapter 13 he describes his own kingdom in farm language — seed and soils, a mustard seed, treasure in a field — as if to say the kingdom grows the way living things grow, quietly and then all at once. Matthew also counts the cost in the same breath: the treasure and the pearl are each worth everything a man has, and the finder sells all with joy, not with regret. And five times Matthew gathers his teaching into great blocks, like a scribe who wants the lessons kept in order, because this King is a teacher before he is anything else in this Gospel.

Then the road narrows, and Matthew will not let you look away. The King rides into Jerusalem on a borrowed donkey, shares a Passover, and is betrayed by a friend for silver. At the cross the signs are public and cosmic — darkness, a torn veil, an earthquake — because Matthew wants you to know this death is not a private tragedy. It looks like the kingdom ending. It is the kingdom being purchased.

And the last chapter answers the first. The blessed poor in spirit were promised a kingdom that is theirs; the risen King now stands on another mountain and speaks in the present tense of total authority: "Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost" — and then the promise that closes the book, "lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world." Announced on a mountain, finished outside a city wall, and still being announced from a mountain: that is Matthew's shape, and you have now read it whole.

This week, live at the first rung. Begin each day by naming your poverty honestly in prayer — not as despair, but as the doorway Jesus blessed. Then take one Beatitude a day and obey it concretely: comfort one mourner, show mercy before you are asked, make one peace. The kingdom, Matthew says, is already theirs who start there.

The kingdom begins at the first rung — blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs it already is.
