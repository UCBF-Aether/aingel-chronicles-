Sabbath Teaching · Week 24

Meqabyan's Witness, Finished

Sabbath, April 3, 2027 · Readings: 2 Meqabyan 10–21; 3 Meqabyan 1–17

How does a book of suffering end — in bitterness, or in teaching? This week you finished the Meqabyan witness: the closing chapters of the second book, and the greater part of the third. And the closing voice of the set is, as this edition's introduction to 3 Meqabyan puts it, the most reflective of the three — less another chain of conflicts, more a sermon woven from Israel's long history, drawing the lessons of endurance out of the whole biblical story and speaking them directly to the reader.

Hear the threads the three books have been braiding, now pulled together. First, repentance. Meqabyan never treats suffering as a full stop; again and again its people turn, confess, and find that turning is the road home. The books are stern with proud power, but they are tender with the penitent — no one in these pages who turns back to God is turned away. Second, false teaching resisted. The danger in Meqabyan is not only the ruler who commands idolatry from the throne; it is also the plausible voice nearby that explains why bowing just this once would be wise, harmless, even faithful. The third book especially warns its readers against abandoning hope because the wicked seem to prosper — against envying the proud their brief season and quietly letting go of trust.

And third, hope beyond death. This hope runs through all three books like a spine. The confessors do not stand firm because this life is all they have; they stand firm because they believe it is not. Suffering borne in faith is seed, not waste. That is why these books can end, after all their persecution and loss, in direct, warm address — as if, the introduction says, the preacher were speaking to you alone, urging you not to feel small or forgotten, not to let the apparent victory of the proud talk you out of your hope.

It is worth pausing, this Sabbath, on the strangeness and gift of that. These are books most of the Christian world let go, preserved in Ge'ez by one church, and translated anew from that Ge'ez for this edition — and what they turn out to contain is not exotic novelty but a familiar, bracing pastoral word: repent, resist the smooth compromise, and hope past the grave. Schools of endurance do not date. Finish the set next week with a thankful heart: the Ethiopian church kept this witness for a long time, and now it has been handed, whole, to you.

Repent, resist the smooth compromise, and hope beyond death — that is Meqabyan's last word.
