Sabbath Teaching · Week 24

Meqabyan's Witness, Finished

Sabbath, April 3, 2027 · Readings: 2 Meqabyan 10–21; 3 Meqabyan 1–17

How does a book of suffering end — in bitterness, or in teaching? This week you finished the Meqabyan witness: the closing chapters of the second book and the greater part of the third. And the closing voice of the set is the most reflective of the three — less a chain of conflicts than a sermon woven from Israel's long history, turning the lessons of endurance over and speaking them, again and again, directly to the reader. Three threads have run through all three books. At the finish, pull them tight.

First, repentance. Meqabyan never treats suffering as a full stop, and it never treats the penitent as finished either. Its God is stern with proud, violent power and tender with people who turn. Again and again the books' logic runs the same way: the nation suffers, the people confess, God keeps and forgives. No one in these pages who turns back is turned away. That is not a small doctrine in a book about persecution. It means the confessor's strength is not sinlessness. It is the practiced habit of turning — quickly, honestly, without waiting to be caught.

Second, the resurrection, stated as a warning as much as a comfort. Hear the second book's own sentence, and notice it is aimed at deniers, not doubters: “And woe unto the evil doers who believed not the resurrection of the dead, and the law of God; and the day of the resurrection they remembered not.” And the third book answers the mockers it quotes — those who say the flesh shall not rise, for it shall become dust — with its own flat verdict: “they shall be judged at the resurrection of soul and flesh.” Soul and flesh. Meqabyan does not preach an escape of the soul from a worthless body. It preaches a God who raises the whole person, which is why its confessors can give their bodies to the fire without believing they are throwing away the part of themselves that matters. The hope beyond death is the spine of all three books: suffering borne in faith is seed, not waste.

Third, false teaching resisted. The danger in Meqabyan is never only the king on the throne commanding idolatry. It is also the smooth voice nearby explaining why bowing just this once would be wise, harmless, even faithful — and, in the third book especially, the quieter temptation to abandon hope because the wicked seem to prosper: to envy the proud their brief season and let go of trust a little at a time. Against that voice the books set their direct, warm address, as if the preacher were speaking to you alone: do not feel small, do not feel forgotten, do not let the apparent victory of the proud talk you out of the resurrection.

There is no historic sermon tradition on these books to borrow from, and this teaching will not pretend otherwise. What the Ethiopian church preserved, in Ge'ez, translated anew for this edition, turns out not to be exotic novelty but a familiar, bracing pastoral word that any honest preacher would recognise: repent, resist the smooth compromise, and hope past the grave.

The doctrine: the resurrection of soul and flesh is the ground of endurance — God raises the whole person, so nothing given to him in faith is wasted, and no one who turns to him is turned away. The practice this week has two parts. First, practice the turn quickly: confess one specific thing today, by name, without the softening clause. Second, answer the smooth voice once, out loud — when the reasonable compromise presents itself this week, say why you will not, in a sentence, to another person. Meqabyan's confessors strengthened one another by speaking. Silence is how the smooth voice wins.

Repent, resist the smooth compromise, and hope beyond death — the resurrection is of soul and flesh.
