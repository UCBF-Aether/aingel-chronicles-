Sabbath Teaching · Week 9

Loyalty in a Lawless Time

Sabbath, December 19, 2026 · Readings: Judges 8–21; Ruth 1–4; 1 Samuel 1–11

What does faithfulness look like when nobody around you is being faithful? Judges ends this week in darkness, and its final line is the book’s own verdict: “In those days there was no king in Israel: every man did that which was right in his own eyes.” The chapters before it earn that line. Gideon’s victory sours into a golden ephod. Abimelech murders his way to a crown. Jephthah’s rash vow costs him his daughter. Samson is governed entirely by his appetites. The last chapters descend into idolatry, unspeakable violence, and civil war in which Israel nearly exterminates one of its own tribes. Judges is what a covenant people looks like when every man’s own eyes become the law.

Set immediately after that darkness is a four-chapter story about two widows and a harvest field, and the little book dates itself in its first sentence: it happened “in the days when the judges ruled.” Same years. Same country. Famine drives Naomi’s family to Moab; death takes her husband and both her sons; and on the road home she urges her daughters-in-law to go back. Orpah kisses her and goes. Ruth clings to her, and says the words Israel’s judges never managed to say to God: “Intreat me not to leave thee, or to return from following after thee: for whither thou goest, I will go; and where thou lodgest, I will lodge: thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God.” Read the vow closely. It is not a sentiment; it is a series of renunciations — country, lodging, people, gods — bound up in a single loyalty. Ruth, a Moabitess with no legal claim and no visible future, attaches herself to an empty-handed widow and to the widow’s God. Loyalty, in this book, is not what you feel in a crisis. It is where you are still standing afterwards, gleaning in a stranger’s field to feed the woman you refused to leave.

And heaven notices the field. Boaz, the kinsman who owns it, has heard the whole story before he meets her, and his greeting names what Ruth has actually done: “The LORD recompense thy work, and a full reward be given thee of the LORD God of Israel, under whose wings thou art come to trust.” Under whose wings thou art come to trust — the vow on the road becomes shelter in the field. Spurgeon, preaching on Ruth in “Ruth’s Reward,” dwells on Boaz’s kindness to a stranger who had come among a people not her own, and turns it on his hearers: “Should not recognition, companionship and hospitality be extended to them to make them feel at home with us?” Ruth’s loyalty is met by a community’s loyalty — handfuls dropped on purpose in the field, a kinsman’s duty honoured publicly at the gate. The story ends with a marriage, a son, and a sentence that quietly detonates: “There is a son born to Naomi; and they called his name Obed: he is the father of Jesse, the father of David.” In the days when everyone did what was right in his own eyes, God carried the royal line of Israel forward through a foreign widow who simply would not let go.

Then 1 Samuel opens, and loyalty takes a second form. Hannah, childless and provoked, prays at Shiloh in tears — “I am a woman of a sorrowful spirit,” she says; she has “poured out my soul before the LORD” — and when the son she prayed for is weaned, she brings him back and lends him to the Lord for life. The boy grows up in a corrupt priest’s house, and in the night God calls him by name, and the child learns the answer on which the prophetic age will run: “Speak; for thy servant heareth.” Around those two faithful figures the institutions fail — the ark captured and returned, Eli’s house judged — until Samuel anoints Saul, found hiding among the baggage.

Doctrine stated plainly: in a lawless time, God does his saving work chiefly through kept vows in small rooms — a widow’s loyalty on a road, a mother’s prayer, a boy listening in the dark. History’s hinge this week is not an army. It is a sentence spoken to a mother-in-law.

Practise Ruth’s order this week. First, cling where love has called you: identify one loyalty that circumstances invite you to abandon quietly, and renew it in words, to their face. Second, be Boaz to one stranger: notice the newcomer in your church, street, or workplace, learn their name, and extend one concrete hospitality. Third, pray one prayer the Hannah way — poured out, specific, and surrendered: ask God for the thing, and give the outcome back to him before he answers.

Go where love calls you, and stay — God carries whole generations through one kept vow.
