Day 1: Follow and Obey

Day 1 · Day 2: Love Your Enemies · Day 3: Forgive · Day 4: Serve · Day 5: Give · Day 6: Pray in Secret · Day 7: Rest

Peter had fished all night and caught nothing. Jesus told him to try again anyway, and Peter answered with the words this whole section stands on: at thy word, I will. Following Jesus starts there — not with understanding everything, but with one yes.

The Word

Luke 5:4–5, 11 — Standard text:

4 Now when he had left speaking, he said unto Simon, Launch out into the deep, and let down your nets for a draught.

5 And Simon answering said unto him, Master, we have toiled all the night, and have taken nothing: nevertheless at thy word I will let down the net.

11 And when they had brought their ships to land, they forsook all, and followed him.

The Teaching

Peter’s night is over when Jesus speaks. The fishermen have left their boats and are washing their nets — the work is done, the catch is nothing, and the professional has his verdict: the lake gave nothing. Jesus borrows Peter’s boat to preach from, and when the preaching ends, He sends Peter back to his own trade with an order that makes no professional sense: launch out into the deep, in daylight, after a night that already failed, and let the nets down again.

Peter’s answer is the whole teaching in one sentence, and it holds two halves that most people try to separate. The first half is honest: we have toiled all the night, and have taken nothing. Gill notes the objection is honest — night was the best fishing time, and the men were wearied and empty. The second half does not cancel the first; it obeys through it: nevertheless at thy word I will let down the net. Jamieson-Fausset-Brown adds that letting down again was, in his reading, simply an act of faith, since as a fisherman Peter knew how hopeless it was. Obedience, in this story, is not pretending the objection is not real. It is letting the word of Christ outweigh a true objection.

That is why Thomas à Kempis puts obedience where he does: “It is verily a great thing to live in obedience, to be under authority, and not to be at our own disposal.” To be at our own disposal is the normal human state: we obey when the plan looks good. Peter obeys when it looks spent. And notice what Henry draws from the order itself: when preaching is done, Christ sends Peter back to his calling — time spent in public religion need not hinder worldly business but may further it. The command meets Peter at his nets, not away from them.

Then comes the catch, the breaking net, the sinking boats, and Peter on his knees: “Depart from me; for I am a sinful man, O Lord.” The miracle does not make Peter feel worthy; it makes him feel seen. Jesus answers fear with a calling: “Fear not; from henceforth thou shalt catch men.” And verse 11 lands the hardest part of the story — Henry stresses the point: they forsook all when their calling prospered. It is one thing to leave empty nets. It is another to leave full ones. Following Jesus starts with one yes spoken before the evidence arrives, and it ends, for Peter, with leaving even the catch behind.

How It Looks

What follows is not a set of steps to complete. It shows the manner of the teaching — what it looks like in an ordinary day, what it looks like when it gets hard, and what it looks like when you fail at it and begin again — so you can carry a way of acting into whatever your own day brings. The particulars stay open to you. This is guidance drawn from the sources named below, not Scripture.

A day lived this way has a particular shape, and Peter gives it its form. There is the thing you have been circling for a week — the apology, the phone call, the task that makes your stomach tighten — and there is the honest objection, which is usually true: you already tried, the time is wrong, it will fail again. Peter does not argue the objection away. He says it out loud, and then he says the second half: nevertheless at thy word. The obedience is not in feeling confident. It is in letting the net down while the objection is still standing there.

And when the net comes up empty again — because the story does not promise the catch, only the following — the day is not graded and thrown away. À Kempis gives the evening its work: the day is examined, the failure named plainly, and the next morning the vow is renewed as if that day were the first day of conversion. A failed morning is not permission to quit by noon. It is material for the evening’s honest look, and the morning after is a new beginning.

The Day’s Rhythm

The practices on this page are guidance drawn from the sources named below, not Scripture. Only the words under “The Word” are Scripture, quoted word for word from this site’s Standard text.

Morning: one word to carry

Carry this word with you: “nevertheless at thy word I will let down the net.” Think of one right thing you have been putting off, and decide this morning that today is the day.

Midday: one act to live

Do that one thing before the day ends — small is fine. Obedience does not need an audience; it needs a yes.

Night: one honest look back

Look back honestly. Where did you obey today, and where did you argue? No scoring, just notice — and begin again tomorrow.
