Walk Like Him

A church visit can end at the door. You sit, you listen, you walk out the same person you walked in as. This section is for the rest of the week — a way to leave with something and actually live it, one day at a time.

Thomas à Kempis wrote the book this whole section takes its shape from: The Imitation of Christ. In its first chapter he says the work plainly — let him “strive to conform his whole life to that mind of Christ,” for “it is a good life which maketh a man dear to God.” These seven days are that striving, made small enough to do: one teaching of Jesus, one practice, one day.

The Daily Rhythm

Every day follows the same three beats, morning to night. À Kempis gives the pattern himself: “In the morning make thy resolves, and in the evening inquire into thy life, how thou hast sped to-day in word, deed, and thought.”

Morning: one word of Jesus to carry. Midday: one act, one person, one moment to live it. Night: one honest look back.

The practices on this page are guidance drawn from the sources named below, not Scripture. Only the words under “The Word” are Scripture, quoted word for word from this site’s Standard text.

Sources

Every practice in this section is drawn from a real, freely available, public-domain source — nothing here is invented modern self-help, and nothing is quoted from a copyrighted work. The verse on each day is Scripture, quoted word for word from this site’s own Standard text. The guidance on each day comes from the sources below, each cited on its day’s page:

Thomas à Kempis, The Imitation of Christ (written c. 1418–1427; William Benham translation, Project Gutenberg ebook 1653) — the classic book on conforming one’s whole life to Christ. Public domain.

Brother Lawrence, The Practice of the Presence of God, First Letter (17th century; Brother Lawrence died 1691) — public domain.

Matthew Henry, Complete Commentary on the Whole Bible (1706–1710; Henry died 1714) — public domain, read at the Internet Sacred Text Archive and in this site’s Study Notes.

Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, Commentary Critical and Explanatory on the Whole Bible (1871) — public domain, in this site’s Study Notes.

John Gill, Exposition of the Entire Bible (1746–1766; Gill died 1771) — public domain, in this site’s Study Notes.

The Study Notes themselves were built only from these public-domain commentators; their source ledger stands at the Study Notes sources page. If a practice could not be tied to one of these sources, it was reworked until it could, or left out.
