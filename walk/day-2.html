Day 2: Love Your Enemies

Day 1: Follow and Obey · Day 2 · Day 3: Forgive · Day 4: Serve · Day 5: Give · Day 6: Pray in Secret · Day 7: Rest

Jesus does not ask His followers to like everyone. He asks for something harder and simpler: love them, do them good, bless them, pray for them. That is a thing you can do today, even toward someone you will never be friends with.

The Word

Luke 6:27–28 — Standard text:

27 But I say unto you which hear, Love your enemies, do good to them which hate you,

28 Bless them that curse you, and pray for them which despitefully use you.

The Teaching

Jesus is speaking to “you which hear” — to disciples, not to a crowd looking for a slogan. And He gives not one command but four, each one a replacement. Love replaces hatred. Doing good replaces doing harm. Blessing replaces cursing. Prayer replaces spiteful use. The Study Notes on this passage record Henry’s plain warning: these are hard lessons to flesh and blood, made easy only by being thoroughly grounded in Christ’s love. That is the first honest thing to say about them. They are hard. The teaching does not pretend otherwise.

The passage then explains why the standard is set this high. “For if ye love them which love you, what thank have ye? for sinners also love those that love them.” Loving the lovable proves nothing and changes nothing. The difference Jesus is after is love where nothing comes back: “love ye your enemies, and do good, and lend, hoping for nothing again.” The reason given is not that enemies deserve it. It is that “he is kind unto the unthankful and to the evil,” and therefore: “Be ye therefore merciful, as your Father also is merciful.” We love enemies because that is what the Father is like, and we are learning to be like Him.

Thomas à Kempis turns the same teaching toward the daily friction of living with people: “But now hath God thus ordained, that we may learn to bear one another’s burdens, because none is without defect, none without a burden, none sufficient of himself, none wise enough of himself; but it behoveth us to bear with one another, to comfort one another, to help, instruct, admonish one another.” Nobody in this picture is the finished article. The person you find hardest to love is carrying a burden you cannot see, and so are you.

So the teaching is not “feel warmly toward your enemy.” Feelings are not commanded anywhere in these two verses. What is commanded is doable: love as action, good as deed, blessing as word, prayer as act. You can bless someone you do not like. You can pray for someone you do not trust. That is the whole point — the teaching is built for people whose feelings have not caught up yet.

How It Looks

What follows is not a set of steps to complete. It shows the manner of the teaching — what it looks like in an ordinary day, what it looks like when it gets hard, and what it looks like when you fail at it and begin again — so you can carry a way of acting into whatever your own day brings. The particulars stay open to you. This is guidance drawn from the sources named below, not Scripture.

In an ordinary day this teaching rarely looks dramatic. It looks like the hard person at work, or at home, or in the family group chat — and one of the four verbs, chosen as the day allows. Love as action might be a chore done without mention. Doing good might be a message of thanks for something real. Blessing might be one genuine good word spoken about them to someone else. Prayer might be sixty seconds with their name before God, meant. The teaching does not ask for all four at once. It asks for one verb, lived, in the direction of the person you would rather avoid.

When the injury replays — and it does, sometimes within the hour — the manner of this teaching is to repeat the blessing instead of rehearsing the grievance. Not because the grievance was unreal, but because rehearsal is how hatred keeps itself fed. À Kempis is blunt about the other side of it: you also have things which have need to be borne with by others. And when you fail and answer evil with evil, in word or only in thought, the practice is not to declare the day ruined. It is to bless once before sleep, and begin again. The day is not a test you pass or fail. It is a field you keep walking.

The Day’s Rhythm

The practices on this page are guidance drawn from the sources named below, not Scripture. Only the words under “The Word” are Scripture, quoted word for word from this site’s Standard text.

Morning: one word to carry

Name one person who has been hard to love. Carry their name with you today, not as a grudge but as a prayer.

Midday: one act to live

Do one quiet good for them if you can — or, if you cannot reach them, bless them by name in prayer and mean it.

Night: one honest look back

Did you repay anything evil with good today? If the answer is no, there is still tomorrow. Love is a practice, not a feeling.
