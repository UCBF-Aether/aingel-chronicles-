Joel

Joel is only three chapters long, and it begins with a disaster you can hear: a locust plague stripping the land bare, leaf, bark, and branch, until nothing is left. The prophet reads the swarm as a warning of a greater day — the day of the Lord — and calls everyone, from elders to nursing children, to a fast and a return to God. Then the book turns: God will restore the years the locust has eaten, pour out his Spirit on all flesh — sons and daughters prophesying, old men dreaming dreams — and judge the nations in the valley of decision. The text here is the King James Version, printed as received. Joel stands among the twelve minor prophets in the Ethiopian canon this edition follows, as in other canons, and the early church reached for it on the day of Pentecost, when Peter quoted its Spirit promise to explain what the crowd was seeing. Watch for that promise in chapter 2 — it is the hinge the whole book, and much of the New Testament, swings on.
