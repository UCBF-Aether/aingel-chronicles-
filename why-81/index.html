Why 81? The Story of the Canon

Most English Bibles on a shelf today hold sixty-six books. The Bible of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church holds eighty-one — the largest canon in Christendom. It is not that Ethiopia added books of its own invention. It is that Ethiopia kept books other churches let go: ancient writings that were read, copied, and prayed with for centuries while elsewhere they slipped out of the canon, and in some cases out of existence. The Aingel Chronicles, assembled by Cory Brent, gathers that whole canon in one place. This page explains its shape, and how it survived.

The wider shelf

Start with the familiar sixty-six, and the Ethiopian Bible holds them all. Around them stands the broader company the Ethiopian church kept. There is Jubilees, retelling Genesis through the keeping of the calendar, and 1 Enoch, the visions of Noah's great-grandfather. There are 1 and 2 Esdras, Tobit, and Judith; the Wisdom of Solomon and Sirach; Baruch, with the Letter of Jeremiah as its sixth chapter, and 4 Baruch beside it.

Some familiar books are also fuller here. Esther runs to seventeen chapters, because the Greek additions stand after the Hebrew book as chapters 11–17. Daniel has fourteen chapters: the Prayer of Azariah and the Song of the Three Children stand inside chapter 3, Susanna is chapter 13, and Bel and the Dragon is chapter 14. The Psalter runs from Psalm 1 to Psalm 151, with the Prayer of Manasseh printed after it as the Psalter's appendix.

One absence surprises many readers. This canon has no 1–2 Maccabees. What it has instead are the three books of Meqabyan — a different work with a similar-sounding name, translated anew, directly from the Ge'ez, for this edition. The wider shelf also holds Josippon, a history of the Jewish people in 104 chapters, and a set of church books that most Bibles never carry at all: the Sinodos, the Gessew, the two Books of the Covenant, the Qalementos, and the Didascalia — the teaching and ordering of the early church, kept as Scripture's companions.

Finally, two beloved Ethiopian classics ride along as a bonus appendix, clearly marked as not part of the canon: the Kebra Nagast and the Teaching of the Mysteries.

How eighty-one becomes eighty-nine

One honest puzzle: why does this site speak of 81 books and 89 reading units, with 2,082 chapters in all? The Church's count of eighty-one groups some paired works as single books. Samuel, Kings, and Chronicles, for example, are each counted once in the eighty-one, but they are easier to read apart, so here each appears as two units. The Prayer of Manasseh is a unit of the site but an appendix, not a book of the count. And the two bonus books are units you can read, but they stand outside the eighty-one entirely.

Guarded in Ge'ez

How did these books survive in Ethiopia when they faded elsewhere? A large part of the answer is a language. Ge'ez is the ancient liturgical tongue of Ethiopia, and its church went on copying, chanting, and teaching its Scriptures in Ge'ez across the centuries. Ethiopia's church also lived with a long continuity of its own, at a relative distance from the empires and disputes that narrowed other canons. Books that were set aside elsewhere simply never stopped being the church's books there.

Enoch is the famous case. The New Testament's letter of Jude quotes Enoch as prophecy, yet most canons did not keep the book. Whole and complete, it survived in Ethiopia — the classic English translation by R. H. Charles, printed in this edition, was made from the Ethiopic. Jubilees survived the same way. Other keepers of the canon survive in English only in rare, scattered, or long-unavailable editions, and several had to be translated anew, directly from the Ge'ez, for this edition: the three books of Meqabyan, the Qalementos, the Sinodos, the Gessew, 4 Baruch, Josippon, and the Teaching of the Mysteries. For that last book the record is exact: its 1940 edition prints the Ge'ez with a French translation only, and no earlier published English translation is known.

The Ark, as tradition tells it

Ethiopian tradition holds that Ethiopia's guardianship began with the Ark itself. The Kebra Nagast — the story as the church tells it — recounts how the Queen of Sheba visited Solomon and how her son Menyelek brought the Ark of the Covenant to Ethiopia, where Ethiopian tradition holds that it rests to this day. That is tradition, told as tradition, not established history. We print the Kebra Nagast in the bonus appendix, marked as non-canonical, because it is a treasure of Ethiopian Christian literature — and because a church that understands itself as the Ark's guardian has never treated the guarding of holy books as a small thing either.

What was lost elsewhere

Loss, where it happened, was usually quiet. Enoch was quoted and then not kept. The additions to Daniel and Esther slipped out of most English Bibles, though they survived in the King James Apocrypha, from which this edition restored them. And loss still marks the text honestly: three passages of Josippon are gone from every witness that could be reached, and they stand in the working text as blanks. What the manuscript gives, this edition gives. What it does not, it does not invent.
