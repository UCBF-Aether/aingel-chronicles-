The Gallery

Everything on these walls is old, real, and freely given. This is the art the canon traveled with — Gospel illuminations from Ethiopia, the first church paintings, Psalters lit by hand, and the engravings that taught ordinary homes to see the Bible. Every piece hangs with its brief history and the name of the institution that keeps it, because a gift should carry its giver’s name. The full record — where each piece was found, and the license it travels under — stands in Sources & Method.

Two spaces — on the first wall and on the fifth — are empty on purpose. They are labeled, like everything else here.

Wall One — Ethiopia

The canon’s own homeland: Gospel books painted for Ethiopian churches, an icon worn at the neck, a cross carried in procession.

Temple Frontispiece from a Gospel Book

Late 14th–early 15th century · Ethiopia (Amhara peoples, Lake Tana region)

This page opens the Gospel book’s prefatory cycle: a curtained sanctuary beneath a cross, peacocks in roundels above, gazelles at its feet. The book was painted at a monastic center in Ethiopia’s Lake Tana region, and its twenty full-page miniatures were made to be viewed during liturgical processions. The Met notes it may have been commissioned by a member of the ruling elite, possibly at Dabra Hayq Estifanos monastery, for a church dedicated to the Archangel Michael.

The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York (Book of the Gospels, 1998.66) · Public domain — Met Open Access

Saint Luke — Leaf from a Gospel Book

c. 1440–1480 · Central Ethiopia

Evangelist portraits like this one introduced each Gospel in Ethiopian Gospel books — the writer seated, frontal and hieratic, in ink and tempera on vellum, painted in Central Ethiopia in the later fifteenth century. Such books were the most treasured possessions of their churches, gifts of royal and noble patrons; this leaf has come down to us detached from its parent volume. The museum photograph is released under CC0 through Wikimedia Commons.

The Cleveland Museum of Art · CC0 — photograph released by the photographer; artwork public domain

The Crucifixion — Gunda Gunde Gospels

First half of the 16th century · Gunda Gunde monastery, Tigray, Ethiopia

Christ on the cross between the spear-bearer and the sponge-bearer, the sun and moon witnessing above — a full-page miniature from the Gunda Gunde Gospels. The Walters dates the book to the first half of the sixteenth century, made at Gunda Gunde monastery in Tigray’s Agame district. Its eleven full-page miniatures, six canon tables, and five ornamented headpieces mark what the museum calls the golden age of the Gunda Gunde style: bold blocks of color bound in fine linear pattern.

The Walters Art Museum, Baltimore (W.850, fol. 197r) · Public domain — Walters Open Access

Canon Table — Gunda Gunde Gospels

First half of the 16th century · Ethiopia

Before print, readers navigated the four Gospels with the canon tables of Eusebius of Caesarea, a fourth-century cross-reference system sorting parallel passages into ten canons. Ethiopian scribes set the tables inside painted architecture — here an arched tempietto with interlaced curtains and hanging lamps. This table stands among the six canon tables of the Gunda Gunde Gospels (Walters W.850), which in Ethiopian practice follow the Gospel text they index.

The Walters Art Museum, Baltimore (W.850, fol. 209r) · Public domain — Walters Open Access

Double Diptych Icon Pendant

Early 18th century · Ethiopia (Amhara or Tigrinya peoples)

A pendant for private prayer, small enough to hang from the neck: opened, it shows Saint George spearing the dragon on one wing and the Virgin and Child on the other. The Met catalogs it as early eighteenth-century work of Amhara or Tigrinya makers, in wood and tempera, just fifteen centimeters wide when open. Such double diptychs carried the sanctuary into daily life in Gondarine-era Ethiopia, folding shut to travel with their owner.

The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York (1997.81.1) · CC0 — Met Open Access

Processional Cross

Gondarine period · Ethiopia

Gondarine processional crosses were no longer cast whole by the lost-wax method; this one’s components were cut from flat brass sheet, welded, and riveted to the shaft, its lower arms once supporting the fabric dressing processional crosses required. The front shows Our Lady Mary with her Son, Saint George, and Saint Täklä Haymanot — depicted with the leg he broke standing too long in prayer — before a prostrate donor; the back carries the Crucifixion, the Entombment, and the Resurrection.

The Walters Art Museum, Baltimore (54.2893) · Public domain — Walters Open Access

Processional Cross (12th–13th Century)

12th–13th century · Ethiopia (Solomonic)

Cast in one piece — a considerable technical feat — with the shaft, perhaps later, riveted to the base. The foliate decoration suggests birds and vegetation at once; in Ethiopian art birds herald the Resurrection, and the tendril-like projections speak of the cross’s rejuvenating power, three smaller crosses springing like buds at the terminals. A different cross from the Gondarine processional cross beside it: different accession number, different century, different construction.

The Walters Art Museum, Baltimore (54.2891) · CC0 — Walters Open Access

Processional Cross (15th Century)

15th century · Ethiopia (Solomonic)

Nine interlocking circles in a quatrelobe frame, ribbon work, serpentine terminal curves, and perforated crosses flanking the shaft that once held the procession dressings. The Walters calls it one of the most important crosses to exist outside of Ethiopia. Its serpentine edges may reference the brazen serpent of Moses (Numbers 21:4–9), read by Christians as a prefiguration of the Cross, and its sprouting terminals the Tree of Paradise.

The Walters Art Museum, Baltimore (54.2894) · CC0 — Walters Open Access

Mäsqälä qəddase — Liturgical Cross

12th–13th century · Lasta, Ethiopia

A bronze liturgical cross from Lasta, Lalibela’s own region: nested pattée crosses, diamond and geometric interlace, seven smaller pattée crosses as finials, and openwork looped arms that held streaming textiles — Christ’s shroud, Mary’s veil. Set at the summit of a staff carried by an Ethiopian Orthodox priest, it is contemporary with the later rock-hewn churches at Lalibela and shares their split-palmette motif: cross-design travelled between metalwork and stone in the same region.

The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York (2008.502) · Public domain — Met Open Access

Processional Cross (14th–15th Century, Lasta)

14th–15th century · Lasta, Ethiopia

A brass processional cross from Lasta, its central square inscribed with a pattée cross, its stepped arms ending in cruciform openings, its socket and loops made for staff-mounting and for the cloths symbolizing the veil of Mary. Processional crosses predating the seventeenth century are rare — sixteenth-century incursions devastated the region’s churches — and its stepped-cross motif reaches back to Aksumite architecture and forward to Lalibela’s rock churches.

The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York (1998.37) · Public domain — Met Open Access

Icon Triptych of Ewosṭatewos and Eight Disciples

Late 17th century · Southern Lake Tana region, Ethiopia

St. Ewosṭatewos at the summit of the central panel, prayer staff in one hand, censer and blessing cross in the other, eight named disciples below in swirling robes. The only known icon devoted to the genealogy of a monastic order — the “House of Ewost’atewos,” one of the two great orders of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church, the monastic world that kept the 81 books. A panel painting was regarded as the spiritual embodiment of the saint portrayed.

The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York (2006.98) · Public domain — Met Open Access

Hand Cross

13th–15th century · Ethiopia

Hammered, not cast: Ethiopian metal hand crosses were shaped by hammering silver, brass, or iron, imitating the processional crosses of great religious centers such as Lalibela in portable form. Because priests used them daily, they show the wear of that service — the scratches on this cross testify to its life in its owner's hands. It is the working tool of the liturgy, not a showpiece: the Ethiopian wall's working cross beside its processional cross. Numbers 21:8–9 (the lifted bronze serpent, the cross prefigured); John 3:14; 1 Corinthians 1:18 (the word of the cross).

The Walters Art Museum (52.296) · CC0

The Virgin and Child with Archangels, Scenes from the Life of Christ, and Saints — Ethiopian, Early Gondarine

Early 17th century · Ethiopia

A triptych for devotion, painted in early Gondarine Ethiopia. The central panel carries a Virgin and Child modeled on the famous icon of Santa Maria Maggiore in Rome, believed in the Middle Ages to have been painted by Saint Luke: prints of that Roman icon reached Ethiopia with Jesuit missionaries around 1600 and remade Ethiopian Marian imagery. Here the Roman model takes an Ethiopian form — Mary's cloak stretches out like an umbrella to embrace the scene of Christ teaching the apostles below, so that she appears as both protector and personification of the Church. The painters of this school used blue grounds and fine black linear detail, and set large figures across the top of all three panels with smaller figures below. The Walters dates it to the early seventeenth century; the makers are unidentified.

The Walters Art Museum, Baltimore (36.4) · CC0 — Walters Open Access

Diptych with Virgin and Child Flanked by Archangels; Scenes from the Lives of Christ and the Virgin, and Saints — Ethiopian, Early Gondarine

Late 17th–early 18th century · Ethiopia

A diptych that is really a triptych with a wing missing: the paired holes along the outer edge of the right panel show a third panel is lost. What survives as the right wing carries standard central-panel iconography — a Virgin and Child after the Santa Maria Maggiore icon, with Christ teaching the apostles below — while the left wing gathers the Anastasis and the Crucifixion together with other scenes, stressing the Virgin's share in Christ's Passion as a counterpoint to her joy with the infant Son. Because the left wing ends with the Last Judgment, the Walters notes the missing wing likely opened with early scenes from the life of Christ, such as the Nativity. The exuberant color is characteristic of early Gondarine painting, and the alternating horizontal and vertical bands of color connect the work to the region of Lasta. The painter is unidentified, and the Lasta connection is the museum's stylistic reading, not a documented workshop.

The Walters Art Museum, Baltimore (36.3) · CC0 — Walters Open Access

Diptych Icon with Saint George and the Virgin Mary and Infant Christ — Ethiopian

Early 15th century · Ethiopia

One of the earliest Ethiopian panel icons to survive in such good condition, and one of the smallest: a two-panel diptych made for personal use in the early fifteenth century, when panel painting was still new in Ethiopia. Saint George is paired with Mary because, as the Walters puts it, he more than any other saint was considered her special messenger — his presence was a guarantee that the owner's petitions would reach Mary and Christ. The brightly painted interior, seen when the little book of panels opens, was meant to deepen that personal connection. The painter is unidentified. This is a different object from the Met's early-eighteenth-century double diptych pendant already on the Ethiopia wall, and two centuries older.

The Walters Art Museum, Baltimore (36.16) · CC0 1.0 — Walters image, terms pinned on the Look and Learn page

Saint George Slaying the Dragon — Leaf from a Gondarine Sensul — Ethiopian

Late 17th century (Gondarine) · Ethiopia

A single small leaf — only about nine centimeters square — from a Gondarine sensul, the folding chain-book of pictures carried for private devotion. This panel shows the subject Ethiopians never tired of: Saint George on horseback slaying the dragon, the soldier-saint who stands at the Virgin's side. It is ink and pigments on parchment, backed with heavier parchment, from the late seventeenth century. The painter is unidentified. This leaf belongs to the Walters sensul 36.10, whose whole-strip open view was never hung on the Gallery walls; this single panel is a different view of that object, not a re-hang of a wall piece.

The Walters Art Museum, Baltimore (36.10.2V, leaf from the Gondarine sensul 36.10) · CC0 — Walters Open Access

Wall Two — The Ancient Church

The first centuries, in paint, wax, and gold glass: a baptistery wall, a burial vault, a desert icon, and the mosaics of Ravenna.

The Good Shepherd and Adam and Eve — Dura-Europos Baptistery

c. 240s CE · Roman Syria

Around 240 CE, Christians in the frontier garrison town of Dura-Europos, on the Euphrates in Roman Syria, converted a house into a church and painted its baptistery. Above the font stood the Good Shepherd carrying his sheep, with Adam and Eve below — among the oldest Christian images to survive anywhere. The city fell to a Sasanian siege in 256/257, and the paintings slept beneath its own defensive earthworks until excavators uncovered them in the 1930s.

Yale University Art Gallery (object 34493) · Public domain

Catacomb Ceiling — Good Shepherd, Jonah, Adam and Eve, Orans

4th century CE · Rome (Coemeterium Maius)

The vault of a burial chamber in Rome’s Coemeterium Maius gathers the first Christians’ picture-language of rescue: the Good Shepherd at the center, Jonah delivered from the great fish, Adam and Eve at the tree, and a believer praying with raised hands — the orans. This image comes from Joseph Wilpert’s Die Malereien der Katakomben Roms (1903), whose plates, photographed under his direction, remain a standard record of catacomb painting.

In situ, Rome; plate from Joseph Wilpert, Die Malereien der Katakomben Roms (1903) · Public domain — 1903 publication

Christ Pantocrator of Sinai

6th century · Byzantine (encaustic icon)

Painted in hot wax — encaustic — in the sixth century, this is among the oldest Byzantine icons in existence, preserved because Saint Catherine’s Monastery at Sinai lay beyond the reach of imperial iconoclasm. Christ blesses with one hand and holds a jeweled Gospel book in the other; viewers have long noticed that the face’s two halves differ, one sterner, one gentler. The icon remains in the monastery that has kept it for some fourteen centuries.

Saint Catherine’s Monastery, Mount Sinai · Public domain

Emperor Justinian (detail) — San Vitale, Ravenna

Consecrated 547 · Byzantine mosaic

In the apse of San Vitale in Ravenna, consecrated in 547, the emperor Justinian stands at the center of the north wall’s mosaic panel, haloed, carrying a golden paten toward the altar, flanked by Bishop Maximianus — the only figure labeled by name — clergy, courtiers, and soldiers. Justinian never visited Ravenna; the mosaic is presence by portrait, imperial power set into the sanctuary wall in glass and stone tesserae. This image is a detail of the panel.

Basilica of San Vitale, Ravenna (in situ) · Public domain — Yorck Project scan

Empress Theodora and Her Attendants — San Vitale, Ravenna

547 · Byzantine mosaic

Facing her husband across the apse of San Vitale, the empress Theodora carries a golden chalice in the south panel, attended by her ladies and two officials, a doorway and fountain at her side marking her procession’s entry into the sanctuary. Set in 547, the paired panels place the imperial couple permanently inside the liturgy of a church in Ravenna — a city Theodora, like Justinian, never saw.

Basilica of San Vitale, Ravenna (in situ) · Public domain — Yorck Project scan

The Good Shepherd — Mausoleum of Galla Placidia, Ravenna

c. 425–450 · Late Roman / early Byzantine mosaic

In a lunette of the small cruciform building known as the Mausoleum of Galla Placidia, Christ the Good Shepherd sits in a rocky paradise among his sheep, youthful and beardless, draped in gold and purple, a slender cross-staff in his hand. It is among the finest mosaics to survive from the fifth century, in a building whose deep-blue, starry vault still startles visitors to Ravenna.

Mausoleum of Galla Placidia, Ravenna (in situ) · Public domain — Yorck Project scan

The Healing of the Paralytic — Dura-Europos Baptistery

c. 233 CE · Roman Syria

From the earliest identified Christian house church, a converted home in the Euphrates garrison town of Dura-Europos: Christ points to the healed man, who carries his pallet away on his shoulders. Painted before the city fell to siege in 256/257, it is the oldest known depiction of any of Jesus’ miracles. A different wall panel from the Good Shepherd and Adam and Eve piece already on this wall, and a baptism image in its own right — rise, take up your pallet, walk (Mark 2:1–12 and parallels).

Yale University Art Gallery · Public domain

Apse Mosaic — Sant’Apollinare in Classe, Ravenna

Consecrated 549 · Ravenna, Italy

The first known apse to put a saint rather than Christ in Majesty at its center: Saint Apollinaris in the orans pose in a green paradise meadow, twelve lambs — his flock — around him, beneath a jewelled cross on a starry blue field bearing Christ’s face, flanked by Moses and Elijah. The upper zone is a symbolic Transfiguration (Matthew 17): Christ as the cross, Peter, James, and John as three lambs. The same bishop, Maximianus, consecrated San Vitale, but this is a different church and a different mosaic from the San Vitale panels on this wall.

Basilica of Sant’Apollinare in Classe, Ravenna (in situ) · CC BY-SA 4.0 — photograph by Terragio67, shared under the same license

Moses Found in the River — Dura-Europos Synagogue

c. 244–255 CE · Dura-Europos, Roman Syria

The infancy of Moses (Exodus 2), painted on the wall of a synagogue in the Euphrates frontier city of Dura-Europos around the middle of the third century — part of the earliest continuous surviving cycle of biblical narrative painting. A Jewish community in a Roman garrison town told its own story on its walls, in the flat, frontal, story-telling style of Roman Syria: the child drawn from the water who would draw a whole people out of Egypt. The synagogue's paintings are preserved at the National Museum of Damascus; the exact identification of every figure in the damaged cycle is uncertain in places, but this panel is catalogued as Moses found in the river.

Dura-Europos synagogue paintings, National Museum of Damascus · Public domain (PD-Art) — source

The Crossing of the Red Sea — Dura-Europos Synagogue

c. 244–256 CE · Dura-Europos, Roman Syria

Exodus 14, painted while the Exodus was still a recent memory in the community's own terms: the Israelites cross the sea on foot while Pharaoh's army pursues behind them. It is a different panel from the Moses-in-the-river fresco above, from the same synagogue walls at Dura-Europos — the congregation's liberation story, set beside the shrine that held the Torah itself. The city fell to siege in 256/257, and the paintings slept under the city's own defensive earthworks until their discovery in the 1930s; the panels are preserved at the National Museum of Damascus.

Dura-Europos synagogue paintings, National Museum of Damascus · Public domain (PD-Art) — source

Wall Three — The Illuminated Word

Scripture made visible, page by page — from Iona to Constantinople to Cilicia, the painted books the words traveled in.

Cross-Carpet Page — Lindisfarne Gospels

c. 715–720 · Northumbria (Insular)

Each Gospel in the Lindisfarne Gospels opens with a “carpet page” — a full page of interlace and pattern organized around a cross, made for contemplation before the text begins. The book was written and painted on Holy Island off the Northumbrian coast around 715–720, in honor of Saint Cuthbert, by a scribe-artist whom tradition associates with Eadfrith, later bishop of Lindisfarne. It survived Viking raids and centuries of wandering to rest in the British Library.

The British Library, London (Cotton MS Nero D.IV) · Public domain — Yorck Project scan

The Chi Rho Page — Book of Kells

c. 800 · Ireland / Iona (Insular), fol. 34r

The most famous page in Irish art: the Chi Rho page (fol. 34r) of the Book of Kells, painted around 800 by Columban monks — in Iona, Kells, or both — opening Matthew’s account of Christ’s birth with the Greek letters of his name, Chi and Rho, swollen into a universe of spirals, beasts, and tiny watching faces. A note on the image: Trinity College Dublin licenses its own modern photography, so this gallery hangs the older public-domain reproduction long published from early facsimiles.

Trinity College Dublin (MS 58) · Public domain — older reproduction

The Feeding of the Five Thousand — T’oros Roslin Gospels

1262 · Armenian Cilicia (Hromkla)

T’oros Roslin, the great Armenian illuminator of Cilicia, painted the Feeding of the Five Thousand into this Gospel book in 1262, working at the scriptorium of Hromkla under Catholicos Constantine I. The Walters notes that Roslin broke with the tradition of evangelist portraits alone and gave the Gospels a whole cycle of narrative images. This copy, Walters W.539, is released under CC0, and Roslin’s colophon survives at the book’s end.

The Walters Art Museum, Baltimore (W.539, fol. 67r) · CC0 — Walters Art Museum

A Page of the Utrecht Psalter

c. 820–835 · Reims region, Francia (Carolingian)

The Utrecht Psalter, made near Reims around 820–835, illustrates all 150 psalms with wiry pen drawings that take the poetry literally — the instruments, weapons, and creatures named in a verse appear in the drawing acting it out. Its restless, sketch-like line made it one of the most influential picture-books of the early Middle Ages, studied and copied by generations of artists. It rests today in Utrecht University Library as Ms. 32.

Utrecht University Library (Ms. 32) · Public domain — Utrecht University Library

David Composing the Psalms — Paris Psalter

c. 940–960 · Constantinople (Byzantine)

The Paris Psalter’s full-page miniatures are the glory of tenth-century Byzantine painting — classicizing pictures that look back across five centuries to late antiquity. On fol. 1v, David composes the Psalms on his lyre, attended by the personification Melodia, while Echo listens from the rocks and a figure below personifies the Mount of Bethlehem. Painted in Constantinople around 940–960, the manuscript is now Bibliothèque nationale de France, ms. gr. 139, digitized on Gallica.

Bibliothèque nationale de France, Paris (ms. gr. 139, fol. 1v) · Public domain — BnF Gallica scan

The Ascension — Rabbula Gospels

586 CE · Mesopotamia (Syriac), fol. 13v

In 586 CE, at the monastery of Saint John of Zagba in Mesopotamia, a scribe who signed himself Rabbula finished this Syriac Gospel book — the earliest illuminated Gospel manuscript with a secure date. Its Ascension (fol. 13v) is among the oldest surviving pictures of the scene: Christ in a mandorla borne by angels, above the Virgin and the apostles. The book is kept by the Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana in Florence (Plut. 1.56), which performed this scan.

Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Florence (Plut. 1.56) · Public domain — scan by the Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana

The Temptation of Joseph — Vienna Genesis

6th century · Vienna Genesis (Österreichische Nationalbibliothek, Cod. Theol. gr. 31)

Joseph resisting Potiphar’s wife (Genesis 39), from the oldest well-preserved illustrated biblical manuscript: a luxury purple-parchment codex from sixth-century Syria or Constantinople, painted in the same world as the Rossano Gospels. Its Genesis cycle is the ancestor of all illustrated Bibles, and this is the Gallery’s oldest Genesis picture.

Österreichische Nationalbibliothek, Vienna (Cod. Theol. gr. 31) · Public domain

The Ladder of Divine Ascent — Russian Miniature

Early 1500s · Russian State Library, Moscow

Monks climbing a ladder toward Christ in heaven, demons dragging at them, angels aiding them — a Russian book-miniature of John Climacus’ (c. 600) thirty-rung Ladder, the Orthodox classic of ascetic life, read in Lent, its thirty rungs its thirty chapters. Labelled here as Climacus, not Jacob: the ladder’s scriptural root is Jacob’s ladder (Genesis 28:12), and its way is the narrow way (Matthew 7:13–14). This is the Russian miniature tradition’s version, not the twelfth-century Sinai icon.

Russian State Library, Moscow · Public domain

The Nativity — T'oros Roslin Gospels

1262 · Armenian Cilicia (Hromkla)

Roslin, the great Armenian illuminator, broke with evangelist-portraits-only and gave the Gospels a narrative cycle; W.539 is the most sumptuous of his seven signed books, made for the priest T'oros, nephew of Catholicos Constantine I. This Nativity (fol. 208v) is the second Roslin miniature on this wall — a different miniature from the Feeding of the Five Thousand (fol. 67r) in the same book. Luke 2 (the Nativity); Matthew 2 (the Magi, in Roslin's cycle).

The Walters Art Museum (W.539.208V) · CC0

Ezra the Scribe — Codex Amiatinus

c. 700, before 716 · Monkwearmouth–Jarrow, Northumbria

The scribe Ezra, haloed, seated and writing in a book on his lap, before a great open cupboard holding nine bound volumes of Scripture — the frontispiece to the Old Testament in the Codex Amiatinus, the earliest surviving complete single-volume Latin Bible. It was made around 700 at the twin monastery of Monkwearmouth–Jarrow in Northumbria, one of three giant pandects commissioned by Abbot Ceolfrith, and carried in 716 as a gift for Pope Gregory II; Ceolfrith died on the journey. The portrait, modeled on a late-antique author portrait, is the oldest English painting to which an absolute date can be assigned, since the book must predate the 716 journey. The book now rests in Florence as Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Amiatino 1, fol. 5r.

Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Florence (Amiatino 1, fol. 5r) · Public domain (PD-art) — source

Saint Luke — Gospel Book of Aristakes

1475 · Lake Van region, Armenia

Luke the evangelist, seated with his Gospel, painted in the brilliant colors and decorative patterning of the Lake Van school. This Gospel book was executed in 1475 by a scribe identified as Aristakes, for a priest named Hakob. It carries sixteen images on the life of Christ before the Gospel text, the traditional evangelist portraits, and marginal illustrations throughout — a different book from the T’oros Roslin Gospels already on the Gallery wall, and a century and a school apart from it. Its later history is written on its covers: a rebinding with jeweled silver plaques from Kayseri, about 1700, showing the Adoration of the Magi and the Ascension.

The Walters Art Museum, Baltimore (W.540, fol. 125v) · Public domain (PD-art) — source

Saint Matthew and the Initial L — Leaf from a Gospel Book

c. 1190 · Helmarshausen Abbey, Germany

The verso of a single leaf that once opened the Gospel of Matthew: the evangelist beside the great initial L of Liber generationis, the book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, in ink, tempera, silver, and gold on vellum. The leaf was written and illuminated at Helmarshausen Abbey around 1190; the volume it was cut from is preserved today in the Trier Cathedral Library (Codex 142), and the leaf is said, uncertainly, to have been made for the monks at Corvey. Its recto carries the Nativity — the same leaf, turned over — so this hang shows one side only, and names the other.

The Cleveland Museum of Art (1933.445.b) · CC0 1.0 — source

Initial I with the Marriage at Cana — Leaf from a Latin Bible

c. 1260–1270 · Cambrai, northeastern France

A ladder initial: the letter I that opens John’s Gospel — In principio erat verbum, in the beginning was the Word — built upward from three small scenes of the Marriage at Cana, one above another beneath cusped gables. At the top Christ and the Virgin speak together; at the center Christ tells the attendant to fill the jars; below, the banquet proceeds after water has become wine. The leaf is a fragment, a tall strip 42 by 15.3 centimeters, cut from a large multi-volume lectern Bible of the kind kept for refectory reading or library reference in the 1200s, in ink, tempera, and gold on vellum.

The Cleveland Museum of Art (1952.565) · CC0 1.0 — source

Saint Matthew — Leaf from the Lectionary of Empress Katherine Komnene

1057–1063 · Constantinople, Byzantium

Matthew the evangelist, with the tools of the scribe’s trade laid out before him: stylus, dividers, pens, knife, burnisher, and inkpots. This leaf comes from a lectionary — a book of Gospel readings for church services — presented to the Holy Trinity Monastery at Chalke in Constantinople by the Empress Katherine Komnene in 1063, which dates the book to 1057–1063. The museum notes why evangelist portraits stand at the head of Gospel books everywhere: understood as eyewitnesses to the texts they wrote, their presence served to authenticate the Gospels. In older exhibition literature the parent book is also called the Phanar Gospels.

The Cleveland Museum of Art (1942.1512) · CC0 1.0 — source

Christ in Gethsemane (Agony in the Garden) — Rossano Gospels

6th century · Byzantine, purple-vellum Gospel book

A miniature from the Rossano Gospels, a Greek Gospel book written in silver and gold on purple-dyed vellum and kept at Rossano in Calabria — believed to be the oldest surviving illustrated New Testament manuscript. The holding museum counts this Gethsemane scene among the book's twelve illuminations of the life of Christ; four psalm verses are inscribed below the miniature on the page. The scene identification rests on the file title and that museum cycle list, and the book's date is given as the 6th century on the file page.

Museo Diocesano d'Arte Sacra, Rossano (Archepiscopal Treasury, s.n.) · Public domain (PD-art) — source

The Baptism of Christ — T'oros Roslin

1262 · Armenian Cilicia (Hromkla)

Christ baptized in the Jordan, painted by T'oros Roslin — the great Armenian illuminator of Cilicia — into the Gospel book he made in 1262 at the scriptorium of Hromkla for the priest T'oros, nephew of Catholicos Constantine I. It is a different miniature from the two Roslin miniatures already on the Gallery's third wall (the Feeding of the Five Thousand, fol. 67r, and the Nativity, fol. 208v): this is fol. 23r of the same book, W.539. Roslin's narrative cycle, rather than evangelist portraits alone, is what the Walters singles out about this book.

The Walters Art Museum, Baltimore (W.539.23R) · CC0 — Walters Open Access, source

An Illuminated Text Page — Gospel Book with Commentaries

c. 1000–1100 · Constantinople, Byzantium

An illuminated text page from a 428-leaf Greek Gospel book with commentaries, made in Constantinople around 1000–1100. The page carries a decorated gold-and-blue headpiece with a Greek title, two birds flanking a fountain at the top — the fountain of life — and dense Greek text below, with a small painted figure in the left margin. The book's four Gospel openings carry full evangelist portraits with peacocks and fountains; this page is a text opening, not one of those portrait pages, and no evangelist is named for it. Wall Four — The Engravers

The Bible in black and white — Doré and Blake, and the masters before them, Dürer and Rembrandt.

The Deluge — Gustave Doré

1866 · France (wood engraving after Doré)

Gustave Doré’s illustrated Bible of 1866 carried his designs into millions of homes; the plates were cut in wood by the engravers of his Paris workshop. The Deluge stands first in this set: the last survivors cling to a rock as the waters take the world below them — Doré’s gift for vast, theatrical catastrophe at its most famous. No artist did more to fix the visual imagination of the Bible for ordinary readers.

From the 1866 illustrated Bible (La Sainte Bible, Mame) · Public domain

Jacob’s Dream — Gustave Doré

1866–67 · France (wood engraving after Doré)

From Doré’s English Bible (Cassell, 1866–67): Jacob asleep in the wilderness of Bethel, stones for his pillow, while the ladder rises from beside him into a heaven crowded with ascending and descending angels (Genesis 28:10–15). Doré’s plates, engraved on wood after his drawings by his Paris workshop, were reprinted in Bibles and picture books across the world for a century.

From Doré’s English Bible (Cassell) · Public domain

Job and His Family — William Blake

Engraved 1825 (published 1826) · England

William Blake engraved his Illustrations of the Book of Job in 1823–25, each plate ringed with handwritten scripture. The series opens in deceptive calm: Job and his family at prayer beneath the tree, children and flocks around them, musical instruments hanging silent in the branches — a plenty the surrounding texts quietly shadow with the loss to come. This impression is the Yale Center for British Art’s, released under CC0.

Yale Center for British Art, New Haven (B1978.43.1503) · CC0 — Yale Center for British Art

Job and His Family Restored to Prosperity — William Blake

Engraved 1825 (published 1826) · England

The last plate answers the first: “Job and His Family Restored to Prosperity.” The tree is the same, but now the instruments are down from the branches and played, and the children dance the story back to their father amid doubled flocks. Blake, who engraved the set in his sixties, in poverty and near-obscurity, ends the Book of Job not in explanation but in music.

Yale Center for British Art, New Haven (B1978.43.1523) · CC0 — Yale Center for British Art

The Four Horsemen — Albrecht Dürer

1498 · Nuremberg (woodcut, from The Apocalypse)

Albrecht Dürer published his Apocalypse in Nuremberg in 1498, in Latin and German at once — fifteen woodcuts that made his name across Europe. The Four Horsemen charge out of Revelation 6: conquest, war, famine, and death ride down bishop and peasant alike while the cut lines themselves seem to gallop. No image of the apocalypse has been copied more often. This impression is the National Gallery of Art’s, released under CC0.

Christ Healing the Sick (“The Hundred Guilder Print”) — Rembrandt

c. 1648–49 · Amsterdam (etching)

Rembrandt’s largest etching gathers a whole Gospel into one crowd: Christ preaching at the center, the sick carried in, children brought to be blessed, a rich young man turning thoughtful at the edge — episodes from Matthew 19 woven into a single scene. Tradition says an impression once sold for a hundred guilders, and the name stuck: the Hundred Guilder Print. This impression is the Rijksmuseum’s, released under CC0.

Water in the wilderness: Moses strikes the rock at Horeb and the spring bursts out for a thirsty people (Exodus 17:6). Wall Six — Byzantium: Icons and Relics

The Byzantine world in gold and mosaic: the tenderness icons, the Resurrection image of the East, and a small box that holds a whole Gospel.

Our Lady of Vladimir (Vladimirskaya)

12th century · Constantinople, now Tretyakov Gallery, Moscow

Painted in Constantinople in the late eleventh or early twelfth century and brought to Russia, the most venerated icon in Russian Orthodoxy, credited with delivering Moscow from invasion (feasts 3 June, 6 June, 8 September). Faces and hands are the surviving twelfth-century paint; the icon has been restored at least five times, once thought led by Andrei Rublev. The Virgin holding the Christ child cheek-to-cheek, the child reaching for her, her solemn gaze on the viewer — the tenderness type (Eleousa) against the Gallery’s hieratic icons (Luke 1–2).

Tretyakov Gallery, Moscow · Public domain

The Anastasis (Harrowing of Hell) — Chora Church

14th century · Chora Church, Constantinople

Painted c. 1315–1321 under Theodore Metochites’ rebuilding of Chora, plastered and whitewashed after the sixteenth-century conversion to a mosque, and restored in the twentieth century. Christ strides through the broken gates of Hell, grasping Adam by one wrist and Eve by the other, lifting them from their tombs. The Eastern Church’s Resurrection image — there is no empty-tomb Easter icon in the East; this IS the Resurrection icon (Matthew 27:52–53; 1 Peter 3:19; the Creed’s descent).

Church of the Saviour in Chora, Istanbul (in situ) · Public domain

The Fieschi Morgan Staurotheke — Lower Side

Early 9th century · Byzantine

Hang this honestly: the verified file shows the LOWER side of the reliquary — Saints Panteleimon, Eustratios, and Merkourios — not the famous lid Crucifixion. Made in Constantinople (?) c. 800 to hold a fragment of the True Cross, in gilded silver, gold, cloisonné enamel, and niello, a whole Gospel in a box ten centimetres long: the lid carries the Crucifixion flanked by Mary and John, fourteen saints around, and worked in niello on the UNDERSIDE OF THE LID are four scenes from the life of Christ — the Annunciation, the Nativity, the Crucifixion, and the Anastasis. The interior itself houses the cross-shaped relic container. Gift of J. Pierpont Morgan, 1917. The museum’s first true relic-container (John 19; Hebrews 9).

The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York (17.190.715a, b) · CC0 — Met Open Access

Portable Icon with the Virgin Eleousa

Early 1300s · Probably Constantinople

A pocket icon in miniature mosaic — set in wax on a wood panel, with gold, multicoloured stones, and gilded copper, just 11.2 by 8.6 centimetres — a luxury devotional object small enough to travel, made as the Eleousa type was reshaping Western Madonnas. A fifteenth-century Latin inscription on the reverse identifies it as the icon that converted St Catherine of Alexandria. The Eleousa type pairs with the Vladimirskaya on this wall as the two great tenderness icons, one painted, one mosaic (Luke 1–2).

Wall Seven — Sacred Architecture

The buildings themselves: churches, monasteries, and holy places where the canon has been kept and prayed.

The Interior of Hagia Sophia — Istanbul

Church consecrated 537 · Constantinople (Istanbul), Turkey

The church Emperor Justinian raised in Constantinople and consecrated in 537, its dome floating on a ring of forty arched windows above a nave wider than any built before it — for nearly a thousand years the largest cathedral in Christendom, and the model every later Orthodox church measured itself against. Tradition gives Justinian the words on entering it, "Solomon, I have surpassed thee." The building has been cathedral, mosque, museum, and mosque again across its fourteen centuries; what this photograph shows is the space itself — dome, half-domes, and hanging lamps — rather than any one mosaic, so it stands apart from the Gallery's existing Ravenna and Chora mosaics. The dome's present slightly elliptical shape is the result of centuries of repair, including rebuilds after earthquakes.

Photograph by José Luiz Bernardes Ribeiro (13 September 2011) · CC BY-SA 3.0 — source

The Monasteries of Meteora — Greece

Monasteries founded 14th–16th centuries · Meteora, Thessaly, Greece

Monasteries built on top of rock pillars in Thessaly, where hermits first sheltered in the cliffs and, from the 14th century onward, whole communities raised their churches, cells, and refectories hundreds of feet in the air, reached for centuries only by rope ladders and nets. The name means "suspended in the air," and the photograph earns it: Rousanou monastery in the foreground, Agios Nikolaos behind it, and part of the Grand Meteora — the largest and oldest of the houses — in the upper right. Six of the original twenty-four monasteries are still inhabited by monks and nuns today. No monastery complex as architecture yet stands in the Gallery; this is the monastic life as a skyline.

Photograph by Takeaway (5 October 2007) · CC BY-SA 3.0 — source

The Church of Saint George (Bet Giyorgis) — Lalibela, Ethiopia

13th century (traditionally Zagwe dynasty) · Lalibela, Ethiopia

The most famous of Lalibela's eleven rock-hewn churches, and the one building in this haul that was never built at all — it was carved downward out of living volcanic rock, roof first, until a free-standing cross-shaped block stood in a deep trench, entered only by a tunnel. Tradition says King Lalibela built the group as a New Jerusalem after a vision, and that Saint George himself reproached the king for leaving him no house, so this church — a nearly perfect cross in plan, its roof a relief of three Greek crosses within each other — was made for him, the most refined of the group. It is still a living church of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, and the existing Gallery's Ethiopian wall shows the books and crosses of this same tradition but no Lalibela building itself; this is the architecture those books were made for. Its exact building date is uncertain — the 13th-century dating is traditional, and some accounts place its completion after Lalibela's death.

Photograph by Giustino (20 August 2005) · CC BY 2.0 — source

Saint Catherine's Monastery — Mount Sinai, Egypt

Monastery founded 6th century (traditionally under Justinian) · South Sinai, Egypt

The oldest continuously inhabited Christian monastery in the world, walled against the desert at the foot of the mountain where Moses is held to have received the Law. Founded in the 6th century — tradition names Justinian — around the Chapel of the Burning Bush, it survived fourteen centuries of empires because it lay beyond the reach of iconoclasm and invasion alike, which is why the Gallery's Sinai Pantocrator icon still lives there. Its library is second only to the Vatican's among early Christian manuscript collections, and its monks still keep the hours in the same church. The Gallery already hangs the monastery's icon; this is the monastery itself, the walls that kept it.

Photograph by Pastor Sam (27 May 2009) · CC BY 3.0 Unported — source

The Catholicon of the Church of the Holy Sepulchre — Jerusalem

Present church chiefly Crusader (12th century), on a 4th-century site · Jerusalem

The heart of the church held to stand over the Crucifixion and the tomb: the Greek Orthodox Catholicon, the great domed nave at the building's center, with its iconostasis and the stone of anointing nearby. The present church is chiefly the Crusader rebuilding of the 12th century, raised over Constantine's 4th-century basilica, which itself was raised over a Roman temple — three layers of sacred building on one ground, shared today, sometimes tensely, by six Christian communities. The identification of this ground with Golgotha and the tomb rests on the tradition Constantine's builders followed, not on archaeology that can prove it; the church's claim is stated here as tradition, honestly.

Photograph by Deror avi (26 March 2009) · CC BY-SA 3.0 Unported — source

The Procession of the Virgins and Martyrs — Sant'Apollinare Nuovo, Ravenna

Mosaics completed before 526 (processions later, Byzantine period) · Ravenna, Italy

A different Ravenna church from the two already on the Gallery's Ancient Church wall, and a different kind of witness: Sant'Apollinare Nuovo was built by the Ostrogothic king Theoderic as his Arian palace church, then reconsecrated for Orthodox use after Justinian's reconquest, and its nave walls still carry the two great processions — twenty-two virgins led by the Magi moving from the port of Classe toward the Madonna and Child, and twenty-six martyrs moving from Theoderic's palace toward Christ enthroned. The processions themselves are Byzantine-period work, later than Theoderic's upper mosaics, and the Magi here carry the names Balthasar, Melchior, and Gaspar — thought to be the earliest example of those names assigned to the Magi in Christian art. It hangs as church architecture in use: the walls of a working basilica, not a detached panel. This crop shows five figures of the procession; the Madonna and Child they move toward stands off-frame.

Basilica of Sant'Apollinare Nuovo, Ravenna (in situ); Yorck Project scan · Public domain — Yorck Project scan — source

Wall Eight — Panel Paintings

Paintings on panel and in fresco: the great biblical pictures, from Babel to the Baptist.

The Tower of Babel — Pieter Bruegel the Elder

1563 · Flanders

The tower that was meant to reach heaven, still unfinished and already crumbling at its crown: Bruegel paints Genesis 11 as a construction site, with cranes, scaffolds, and ant-small labourers swarming the spiralling ramps above a harbour city. Signed on a stone block at the base, BRVEGEL FE MCCCCCLXIII, it is the great Vienna version of the subject — Bruegel painted a smaller one, now in Rotterdam. The file page lists Nimrod, the tower's king, among the people depicted. The panel passed from its first owner, Nicolaas Jongelinck, through the city of Antwerp into the collection of Emperor Rudolf II.

The Creation of Adam — Michelangelo

c. 1511 · Rome (Sistine Chapel ceiling, Vatican)

The opening of the canon's story, at the summit of Renaissance art: God, borne up by angels, reaches out his hand to the newly formed Adam (Genesis 1:27), their fingers nearly touching across the centre of the Sistine Chapel ceiling. Michelangelo painted the ceiling's Genesis panels around 1511, working on scaffolding high above the chapel floor. This file is, plainly, a perspective-corrected photograph of the ceiling fresco, not a scan of a portable object — the work itself cannot travel, and stays in situ in the Vatican. It is hung here because no Old Testament wall is complete without the moment humanity itself begins.

The Annunciation — Sandro Botticelli

c. 1485 · Florence

A small panel, only 19.1 by 31.4 centimetres, in tempera and gold on wood: the archangel Gabriel bringing the announcement to Mary. Botticelli painted it in Florence around 1485, when the Annunciation was the city's own feast-day subject. Its size is the point — this is a picture for close, private looking, gold ground and all, not a church wall.

Saint Nicholas of Myra — Fra Angelico

Daniel in the Lions' Den — Peter Paul Rubens

c. 1614–1616 · Flanders

Morning after the night in the den (Daniel 6): Daniel sits unharmed in prayer among nine lions, painted almost life-size on a canvas more than three metres wide, with the bones of earlier victims in the foreground to say what the lions could have done. Rubens, who by his own account painted the picture entirely himself rather than leaving it to assistants, makes the miracle quiet — no angel is shown, only a man kept alive through the night. The canvas has a long road behind it: acquired from the artist in 1618 by Sir Dudley Carleton, presented to Charles I, sold through the Hamilton Palace sales, and bought for Washington in 1965.
