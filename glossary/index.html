Glossary — Words for the Wider Canon

4 Baruch

A short book that stands beside Baruch in this canon, in nine chapters. Where no usable English existed for it, it was translated anew, directly from the Ge'ez, for this edition.

Abtilis

The fourth of the four parts of the Sinodos, as the Sinodos is traditionally divided. In this edition the Sinodos is printed as one book of thirty-seven chapters, so you will meet Abtilis as the name of its final part rather than as a separate book on the shelf.

Anaphora

The central prayer of the Eucharistic liturgy — the great prayer of thanksgiving and consecration at the heart of the service. The word turns up in this project's church books, including the Sinodos, which carries an anaphora among its early chapters.

Apocrypha

In this edition, the word usually points to the King James Apocrypha: the collection of books printed with the King James Bible, outside its sixty-six. Several books were received from that text here, and the additions to Daniel and Esther and the Prayer of Manasseh were restored or supplied from it.

Ark of the Covenant

The gold-covered chest of ancient Israel, holding the tablets of the covenant. Ethiopian tradition holds that the Ark was brought to Ethiopia in the days of Solomon's son Menyelek and that it rests in Ethiopia to this day — that is tradition, told as tradition, and the Kebra Nagast is its great telling.

Bel and the Dragon

The closing story of Daniel in its Ethiopian form, standing as Daniel chapter 14, restored here from the King James Apocrypha. Daniel exposes two idols in Babylon — Bel, whose priests secretly eat the god's offerings, and a living dragon worshipped as a god. The prophet Habbacuc appears in this chapter; see his entry below.

Books of the Covenant

Two church books of this canon, the 1 Book of the Covenant (forty-seven chapters) and the 2 Book of the Covenant (twenty-seven chapters). The English printed here is Cooper and Maclean's 1902 translation of the work known in Latin as the Testamentum Domini, the Testament of Our Lord, received as a public-domain text.

Broader canon

A name for the wider list of Scripture kept by the Ethiopian church, set beside the narrower canons kept elsewhere. Where most traditions settled on sixty-six books, the broader canon holds eighty-one — the largest canon in Christendom.

Canon

The list of books a church receives as Scripture — the books that belong on the shelf, read, copied, and prayed with as holy. Canons differ between traditions, which is why one Bible can hold sixty-six books and another eighty-one.

Deuterocanon

The "second canon": books received as Scripture by some churches — among them several that stand in the King James Apocrypha — but not kept in every tradition's list. The Ethiopian canon is broader still, holding books beyond even this second shelf.

Didascalia

A book of church teaching and order, handed down under the apostles' name. The English printed here is J. M. Harden's Ethiopic Didascalia of 1920, received as a public-domain text and carefully repaired where the scan had tangled its footnotes into the text; it runs to forty-three chapters.

Enoch (1 Enoch)

The visions handed down under the name of Enoch, Noah's great-grandfather, in one hundred and eight chapters. The book was quoted in the New Testament's letter of Jude, yet most canons did not keep it; whole and complete, it survived in Ethiopia, and the classic English translation by R. H. Charles printed here was made from the Ethiopic.

Ge'ez

The ancient liturgical tongue of Ethiopia — the language in which its church copied, chanted, and taught its Scriptures across the centuries. Several books in this edition had to be translated fresh, directly from the Ge'ez, because no usable English existed for them.

Gessew

A book of church order in fifty-seven chapters: the fifty-six apostolic canons and a closing epilogue counted as canon 57. It is traditionally the third part of the Sinodos — you will also meet its name spelled Gitsew — and it is printed here as its own reading unit, translated anew from the Ge'ez of W. Fell's 1871 edition.

Habbacuc

The prophet who, in Bel and the Dragon (Daniel 14), is carried by an angel to feed Daniel in the lions' den. He is spelled with a double b and a single k because that is the spelling carried in the King James Apocrypha text from which chapter 14 was restored; the prophet's own book in this Bible is spelled Habakkuk.

Josippon

A history of the Jewish people in one hundred and four chapters, translated anew for this edition from a Ge'ez codex copied around 1600 (British Library EAP704/2/3). Its Ge'ez name is Zena Ayhud. Three passages the manuscript itself has lost were searched for in every other witness that could be reached and not found, so they stand in the working text as honest blanks.

Jubilees

A retelling of Genesis and the opening of Exodus, ordered around jubilee cycles of years and the keeping of the calendar — sometimes called "Little Genesis," which is a nickname, not its name. It survived whole in Ethiopia, and R. H. Charles's translation from the Ethiopic is printed here, repaired word by word where its scan had been damaged.

Kebra Nagast

A beloved classic of Ethiopian Christian literature, printed here in the bonus appendix and marked as not part of the eighty-one-book canon. In E. A. Wallis Budge's 1922 telling it recounts the Queen of Sheba's visit to Solomon and how, as Ethiopian tradition tells it, her son Menyelek brought the Ark of the Covenant to Ethiopia; its final chapter survived only in Ge'ez and was translated fresh.

Lacuna

A gap — a place where the text itself is physically gone: a torn leaf, a destroyed foot of page. A lacuna is a loss to be recorded, not bridged; this edition marks such places in its working files and does not invent words to fill them.

Maccabees

The books about the Maccabean revolt, found in some canons and in the King James Apocrypha. They are not in this canon — do not confuse them with the Meqabyan, which is a different work entirely, as the next entry explains.

Meqabyan

Not the Maccabees — these are entirely different books that happen to sound alike, and this canon has the three Meqabyan and no Maccabees. The three books (thirty-six, twenty-one, and twenty-one chapters) were translated anew, directly from the Ge'ez, for this edition. You can begin at 1 Meqabyan.

Prayer of Manasseh

A short prayer of repentance in fifteen verses, handed down under the name of Manasseh, king of Judah. In this edition it is printed from the King James Apocrypha after Psalm 151, as a labelled appendix to the Psalter — a companion to the Psalms rather than a Psalm itself.

Psalter

The book of the Psalms, taken as one collection. Here the Psalter runs from Psalm 1 to Psalm 151 — the 151st Psalm, which is not in the King James Version, is printed from Charles Thomson's 1808 English Septuagint — with the Prayer of Manasseh following as its appendix.

Pseudepigrapha

Ancient writings that circulate under the name of an earlier figure — Enoch, Baruch, and others. Scholars use the word as a shelf-label for such books, some of which, like 1 Enoch and 4 Baruch, this canon kept as Scripture.

Qalementos

The Ethiopic Clement: a church book in sixty-seven chapters, handed down in Clement's voice — Clement speaking as the helper of Peter, chief of the apostles, as its own opening lines present him. It was translated anew, directly from the Ge'ez, for this edition.

Septuagint

The ancient Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures, made in the centuries before Christ and read widely by the early church. This edition draws on it directly in one place: Psalm 151 is printed from Charles Thomson's English Septuagint of 1808.

Sinodos

The great book of church order in this canon: canons, rulings, and the ordering of the church's life, in thirty-seven chapters, translated anew from the Ge'ez printed in G. Horner's 1904 edition. As traditionally divided it has four parts — Sirate Tsion, Tizaz, Gitsew, and Abtilis — though this edition prints it, as its source does, as a single work.

Sirate Tsion

The first of the four parts of the Sinodos, as the Sinodos is traditionally divided. In this edition the Sinodos stands as one book of thirty-seven chapters, so Sirate Tsion names its opening part.

Susanna

The story of Susanna, a righteous woman falsely accused and vindicated by the young Daniel's questioning. In the Ethiopian form of Daniel it stands as chapter 13, restored here from the King James Apocrypha.

Tabot

The consecrated tablet kept in the sanctuary of an Ethiopian church, representing the tablets of the covenant and the Ark that held them. In Ethiopian tradition the tabot is what makes a church a church — the holy centre around which its worship gathers.

Teaching of the Mysteries (Temherta Hebuat)

A short work of five chapters and twenty-seven verses, printed here in the bonus appendix and marked as not part of the canon; its Ge'ez name is given in the records as Temherta Hebu'at. Its 1940 edition prints the Ge'ez with a French translation only, and no earlier published English translation is known — the English here was translated anew from the Ge'ez.

Tizaz

The second of the four parts of the Sinodos, as the Sinodos is traditionally divided. In this edition the Sinodos is printed whole, in thirty-seven chapters, and Tizaz names its second part.

Wax and gold

The Ge'ez tradition of layered meaning, sometimes named from the craft of the goldsmith: a text has its surface sense, the "wax," and a hidden, spiritual sense beneath it, the "gold." This edition translates the wax — the words as they stand, as faithfully as we can render them. The gold belongs to the church's commentary tradition, a separate layer kept distinct from the text itself, never blended into it.

Witness

A manuscript or edition that carries a text — one surviving copy standing as evidence for what the book says. To check a passage against other witnesses is to ask what the copies agree on; and a witness checked and found not to carry a passage is still evidence, honestly recorded.
