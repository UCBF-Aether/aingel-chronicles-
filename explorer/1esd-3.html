The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — 1 Esdras 3 (King James Version, Apocrypha; public-domain text as received)

1 Now when Darius reigned, he made a great feast unto all his subjects, and unto all his household, and unto all the princes of Media and Persia,

2 And to all the governors and captains and lieutenants that were under him, from India unto Ethiopia, of an hundred twenty and seven provinces.

3 And when they had eaten and drunken, and being satisfied were gone home, then Darius the king went into his bedchamber, and slept, and soon after awaked.

4 Then three young men, that were of the guard that kept the king's body, spake one to another;

5 Let every one of us speak a sentence: he that shall overcome, and whose sentence shall seem wiser than the others, unto him shall the king Darius give great gifts, and great things in token of victory:

6 As, to be clothed in purple, to drink in gold, and to sleep upon gold, and a chariot with bridles of gold, and an headtire of fine linen, and a chain about his neck:

7 And he shall sit next to Darius because of his wisdom, and shall be called Darius his cousin.

8 And then every one wrote his sentence, sealed it, and laid it under king Darius his pillow;

9 And said that, when the king is risen, some will give him the writings; and of whose side the king and the three princes of Persia shall judge that his sentence is the wisest, to him shall the victory be given, as was appointed.

10 The first wrote, Wine is the strongest.

11 The second wrote, The king is strongest.

12 The third wrote, Women are strongest: but above all things Truth beareth away the victory.

13 Now when the king was risen up, they took their writings, and delivered them unto him, and so he read them:

14 And sending forth he called all the princes of Persia and Media, and the governors, and the captains, and the lieutenants, and the chief officers;

15 And sat him down in the royal seat of judgment; and the writings were read before them.

16 And he said, Call the young men, and they shall declare their own sentences. So they were called, and came in.

17 And he said unto them, Declare unto us your mind concerning the writings. Then began the first, who had spoken of the strength of wine;

18 And he said thus, O ye men, how exceeding strong is wine! it causeth all men to err that drink it:

19 It maketh the mind of the king and of the fatherless child to be all one; of the bondman and of the freeman, of the poor man and of the rich:

20 It turneth also every thought into jollity and mirth, so that a man remembereth neither sorrow nor debt:

21 And it maketh every heart rich, so that a man remembereth neither king nor governor; and it maketh to speak all things by talents:

22 And when they are in their cups, they forget their love both to friends and brethren, and a little after draw out swords:

23 But when they are from the wine, they remember not what they have done.

24 O ye men, is not wine the strongest, that enforceth to do thus? And when he had so spoken, he held his peace.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

What the chapter says, in plain language

King Darius makes a great feast for his household and the princes of Media and Persia, and three young bodyguards agree to a contest: each will write a sentence on what is strongest, and the king and the three princes of Persia will judge the wisest. The first speaker argues at length that wine is the strongest, and the chapter closes with his question, 'is not wine the strongest?' Interpreted Chapter text and study notes for this chapter

Unfamiliar terms

  • Bodyguards. the three young men of the king's guard who compete in the contest of wise sentences (verses 4-9). Interpreted Study note for this chapter
  • Princes of Persia. the judges of the contest alongside the king (verse 9). Interpreted Study note for this chapter

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Bissell notes the Greek for making a great feast is exactly reproduced in Luke 5:29, and that the household servants are literally those born in the house. Interpreted Study note, summarizing E. C. Bissell
  • Verse 2. Bissell compares the extent of the empire, from India unto Ethiopia, with the same formula in Esther 1:1 and 8:9 and in Daniel 6:1. Interpreted Study note, summarizing E. C. Bissell
  • Verse 3. Bissell notes the Greek word may mean either awoke or slept profoundly, though awoke is the natural and usual meaning, and that Josephus gives the narrative quite a different coloring, making the contest turn on an oration agreeable to truth and wisdom and attributing to Darius a prior vow to return the vessels of God to Jerusalem. Interpreted Study note, summarizing E. C. Bissell
  • Verse 4. Bissell notes this debate before Darius is the book's only independent portion, in which three young men of the king's body-guard dispute the question of what is mightiest, and that it became the compiler's principal point of attraction. Interpreted Study note, summarizing E. C. Bissell
  • Verse 5. Bissell notes the comparative here is used for the superlative, so that wiser than the other means wisest of all, and the victor was to receive great gifts in token of victory. Interpreted Study note, summarizing E. C. Bissell
  • Verse 7. Bissell notes being called the king's kinsman was simply an honorary title, as in the Maccabean books where the same title is bestowed as a mark of royal favor. Interpreted Study note, summarizing E. C. Bissell
  • Verse 9. Bissell notes the three princes of Persia correspond to the leading princely families of Persia, whose heads were supposed to form the king's council, and that three may have been selected here with reference to the number of contestants. Interpreted Study note, summarizing E. C. Bissell
  • Verse 12. Bissell notes this contest is the book's only independent portion, and its verdict, that truth bears away the victory above all things, is what secures the third speaker's victory and, with it, the king's consent to the Jews' return. Interpreted Study note, summarizing E. C. Bissell
  • Verse 14. Bissell notes the chief officers are the hypatoi, a term used by Latin writers to designate consuls and prefects, and that one critic thinks the term furnishes a key to the time of the book's composition. Interpreted Study note, summarizing E. C. Bissell
  • Verse 18. Bissell connects the wording with the pointing of the Greek text, so that wine seduces or deceives the mind of every man who drinks it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing E. C. Bissell
  • Verse 19. Bissell notes the fatherless child is literally the orphan, used figuratively for what is weak, in contradistinction to the king, so that wine levels the strongest and the weakest minds alike. Interpreted Study note, summarizing E. C. Bissell
  • Verse 20. Bissell compares the sentiment of the verse with Psalm 104:15, Ecclesiastes 10:19, Wisdom 2:9, and Ecclesiasticus 13:8, on wine gladdening the heart and turning thought to mirth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing E. C. Bissell
  • Verse 21. Bissell explains the phrase about speaking by talents to mean speaking as though one had to do only with talents, that is, talking like a millionaire, as Wahl renders it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing E. C. Bissell
  • Verse 23. Bissell explains the phrase about being from the wine as rising from the sleep produced by wine, that is, sobering up after drinking. Interpreted Study note, summarizing E. C. Bissell
