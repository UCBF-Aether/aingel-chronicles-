The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 24 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Why, seeing times are not hidden from the Almighty, do they that know him not see his days?

2 Some remove the landmarks; they violently take away flocks, and feed thereof.

3 They drive away the ass of the fatherless, they take the widow’s ox for a pledge.

4 They turn the needy out of the way: the poor of the earth hide themselves together.

5 Behold, as wild asses in the desert, go they forth to their work; rising betimes for a prey: the wilderness yieldeth food for them and for their children.

6 They reap every one his corn in the field: and they gather the vintage of the wicked.

7 They cause the naked to lodge without clothing, that they have no covering in the cold.

8 They are wet with the showers of the mountains, and embrace the rock for want of a shelter.

9 They pluck the fatherless from the breast, and take a pledge of the poor.

10 They cause him to go naked without clothing, and they take away the sheaf from the hungry;

11 Which make oil within their walls, and tread their winepresses, and suffer thirst.

12 Men groan from out of the city, and the soul of the wounded crieth out: yet God layeth not folly to them.

13 They are of those that rebel against the light; they know not the ways thereof, nor abide in the paths thereof.

14 The murderer rising with the light killeth the poor and needy, and in the night is as a thief.

15 The eye also of the adulterer waiteth for the twilight, saying, No eye shall see me: and disguiseth his face.

16 In the dark they dig through houses, which they had marked for themselves in the daytime: they know not the light.

17 For the morning is to them even as the shadow of death: if one know them, they are in the terrors of the shadow of death.

18 He is swift as the waters; their portion is cursed in the earth: he beholdeth not the way of the vineyards.

19 Drought and heat consume the snow waters: so doth the grave those which have sinned.

20 The womb shall forget him; the worm shall feed sweetly on him; he shall be no more remembered; and wickedness shall be broken as a tree.

21 He evil entreateth the barren that beareth not: and doeth not good to the widow.

22 He draweth also the mighty with his power: he riseth up, and no man is sure of life.

23 Though it be given him to be in safety, whereon he resteth; yet his eyes are upon their ways.

24 They are exalted for a little while, but are gone and brought low; they are taken out of the way as all other, and cut off as the tops of the ears of corn.

25 And if it be not so now, who will make me a liar, and make my speech nothing worth?

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Job continues with a catalogue of unpunished wrong: men remove landmarks, seize flocks, drive away the orphan’s donkey, take the widow’s ox, and turn the needy out of the way, while murderers, adulterers, and thieves work in darkness. Times of punishment are not hidden from the Almighty, he asks, so why do those who know him not see his days? The wicked are exalted for a little while, he grants, but then they are gone, cut off like the tops of the ears of corn. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Why, seeing times are not hidden from the Almighty, do they that know him not see his days? JFB explains times as times of punishment, and days as days of vengeance: Job does not doubt they are appointed, but wishes God would let all now see it is so. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Some remove the landmarks, violently take away flocks, and feed thereof. JFB introduces verses 2-24 as instances of the wicked doing the worst deeds with seeming impunity, with landmarks as boundaries between pastures. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. They drive away the ass of the fatherless and take the widow's ox for a pledge. JFB notes others really do, with impunity, what Eliphaz falsely charged the afflicted Job with. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. They turn the needy out of the way, and the poor of the earth hide themselves together. JFB explains it literally as pushing the poor out of their road, taking advantage by force, with the poor hiding from oppressors who robbed them of all and drove them to unfrequented places. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Behold, as wild asses in the desert, the poor go forth to their work, rising betimes for a prey, the wilderness yielding food for them and their children. JFB explains the wild asses as Bedouin robbers with unbridled desert wildness, whose lawless work is robbery, rising early as Eastern travel begins before heat. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. They reap every one his corn in the field, and gather the vintage of the wicked. JFB explains reaping metaphorically as the robbers' various grain, and the vintage as the vintage of robbery, not honest industry, gathered late in autumn. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. They cause the naked to lodge without clothing, having no covering in the cold. JFB notes the robbers are regardless of life's comforts, passing the night naked and uncovered, with frost not uncommon at night in those regions. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. They are wet with the showers of the mountains, and embrace the rock for want of a shelter. JFB explains the plundered travellers embracing the rock as taking refuge under it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. They pluck the fatherless from the breast, and take a pledge of the poor. JFB notes kidnapping children for slaves, passing from desert wrongs to wrongs among habitations, with pledge as the poor debtor's garment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. They cause the naked to go without clothing, taking away the sheaf from the hungry. JFB contrasts open desert robbery of garments with refined robbery in civilized life under a pledge, where the poor labour in harvest fields and are denied even the corn they carry. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Which make oil within their walls, and tread their winepresses, and suffer thirst. JFB explains the poor pressing oil within the oppressor's walled vineyards and olive gardens, yet not allowed to quench thirst with grapes and olives. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Men groan from out of the city, and the soul of the wounded cries out, yet God lays not folly to them. JFB reads men as the dying, answering to the wounded, oppressed sufferers in the city crying in vain, with God taking no account by punishing their oppressors' sin, the gist of the whole list. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. They are of those that rebel against the light, knowing not its ways nor abiding in its paths. JFB notes the turn from open sins to sins done in the dark, with light both literal and figurative, and paths as places where light shines. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. The murderer rising with the light kills the poor and needy, and in the night is as a thief. JFB explains the robber lying in wait at early dawn, when travellers set out and labourers go to work, while thieves steal at night: the same man steals at night and murders at dawn to escape detection. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. The eye of the adulterer waits for the twilight, saying no eye shall see me, and disguises his face. JFB notes the veil put on to escape detection. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. In the dark they dig through houses, which they had marked for themselves in the daytime, knowing not the light. JFB explains Eastern houses of sun-dried mud bricks dug through, and marked as sealed up in their houses by day for their own ends, shunning light. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. For the morning is to them even as the shadow of death; if one knows them, they are in the terrors of the shadow of death. JFB notes they shrink from morning light as other men shrink from blackest darkness, familiar with the terrors of darkness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. He is swift as the waters, his portion is cursed in the earth, and he beholds not the way of the vineyards. JFB notes Job here quotes his adversaries ironically, as before: the sinner swiftly passes away like a thing floating on waters, cursed by witnesses, turning not to enjoy his pleasant possessions. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. Drought and heat consume the snow waters, so does the grave those which have sinned. JFB gives the Arabian image: melted snow quickly dries in sunburnt sand leaving no trace, expressing the swift, utter destruction of the godless. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. The womb shall forget him, the worm shall feed sweetly on him, and he shall be no more remembered, with wickedness broken as a tree. JFB notes even the mother who last forgets her child shall dismiss him, the worm feeding sweetly as on a delicate morsel, and wickedness broken utterly like a staff. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. He evil entreats the barren that bears not, and does not good to the widow. JFB explains the friends' reason for the sinner's fate: the barren without sons to protect her, and the widow without a husband to support her, are his victims. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. He draws also the mighty with his power, rising up though no man is sure of life. JFB gives Job's reply to the friends' opinion: experience proves the contrary, for God prolongs the mighty with his power, and the wicked rises from his sickbed though he had given up hope of life. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. Though it is given him to be in safety, whereon he rests, yet God's eyes are upon their ways. JFB notes God gives the wicked safety and security, and Job's wonder is that God so favours them while his eyes are open to their wicked ways. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. They are exalted for a little while, but are gone and brought low, taken out of the way as all others, and cut off as the tops of the ears of corn. JFB repeats Job's point that sinners die in exalted positions by a quick, easy, natural death, gathered as ripe corn in full age, not prematurely. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. And if it be not so now, who will make Job a liar, and make his speech nothing worth? The challenge closes the catalogue: disprove the observed facts, or the friends' theory falls. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
