The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Isaiah 53 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Who hath believed our report? and to whom is the arm of the LORD revealed?

2 For he shall grow up before him as a tender plant, and as a root out of a dry ground: he hath no form nor comeliness; and when we shall see him, there is no beauty that we should desire him.

3 He is despised and rejected of men; a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief: and we hid as it were our faces from him; he was despised, and we esteemed him not.

4 Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows: yet we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted.

5 But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed.

6 All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned every one to his own way; and the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all.

7 He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth: he is brought as a lamb to the slaughter, and as a sheep before her shearers is dumb, so he openeth not his mouth.

8 He was taken from prison and from judgment: and who shall declare his generation? for he was cut off out of the land of the living: for the transgression of my people was he stricken.

9 And he made his grave with the wicked, and with the rich in his death; because he had done no violence, neither was any deceit in his mouth.

10 Yet it pleased the LORD to bruise him; he hath put him to grief: when thou shalt make his soul an offering for sin, he shall see his seed, he shall prolong his days, and the pleasure of the LORD shall prosper in his hand.

11 He shall see of the travail of his soul, and shall be satisfied: by his knowledge shall my righteous servant justify many; for he shall bear their iniquities.

12 Therefore will I divide him a portion with the great, and he shall divide the spoil with the strong; because he hath poured out his soul unto death: and he was numbered with the transgressors; and he bare the sin of many, and made intercession for the transgressors.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Who has believed our report? The servant grows up like a tender plant, despised and rejected, a man of sorrows — yet he was wounded for our transgressions and bruised for our iniquities, led as a lamb to the slaughter without opening his mouth. He is cut off, buried with the rich, and then, because he poured out his soul unto death and bore the sin of many, he divides the spoil with the strong. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Who has believed our report? And to whom is the arm of the Lord revealed? This chapter treats man's unbelief, Messiah's vicarious sufferings, and final triumph for man. The speaker, according to some, personates the repenting Jews in the latter ages of the world coming over to the faith of the Redeemer; the whole is their penitent confession. This view suits the context in chapter 52 verses 7 to 9, which is not to be fully realised until Israel is restored. However, primarily, it is the abrupt exclamation of the prophet: Who hath believed our report, that of Isaiah and the other prophets, as to Messiah? The infidel's objection from the unbelief of the Jews is anticipated and hereby answered: that unbelief and the cause of it, Messiah's humiliation, whereas they looked for One coming to reign, were foreseen and foretold. Report is literally, the thing heard, referring to which sense Paul says, So, then, faith cometh by hearing. Arm means power, as in chapter 40, exercised in miracles and in saving men. The prophet, as if present during Messiah's ministry on earth, is deeply moved to see how few believed on Him, as in chapter 49 and Mark and Acts. Two reasons are given why all ought to have believed: first, the report of the ancient prophets; second, the arm of Jehovah exhibited in Messiah while on earth. In the other view, this will be the penitent confession of the Jews, How few of our nation, in Messiah's days, believed in Him! Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. For he will grow up before him as a tender plant, and as a root out of a dry ground; he has no form nor comeliness, and when we will see him, there is no beauty that we should desire him. Tender plant means Messiah grew silently and insensibly, as a sucker from an ancient stock, seemingly dead, namely, the house of David, then in a decayed state, as in chapter 11. Shall grow, hath is rather, grew up, had. Before him means before Jehovah; though unknown to the world, as in John, Messiah was observed by God, who ordered the most minute circumstances attending His growth. Root means that is, sprout from a root. Form means beautiful form: sorrow had marred His once beautiful form. And when we shall see is rather, joined with the previous words, Nor comeliness, attractiveness, that we should look, with delight, on Him. There is is rather, was. The studied reticence of the New Testament as to His form, stature, colour, etc., was designed to prevent our dwelling on the bodily, rather than on His moral beauty, holiness, love, etc., also a providential protest against the making and veneration of images of Him. The letter of P. Lentulus to the emperor Tiberius, describing His person, is spurious; so also the story of His sending His portrait to Abgar, king of Edessa; and the alleged impression of His countenance on the handkerchief of Veronica. The former part of this verse refers to His birth and childhood; the latter to His first public appearance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. He is despised and rejected of men, a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief, and we hid as it were our faces from him; he was despised, and we esteemed him not. Rejected means forsaken of men; most abject of men. Literally, He who ceases from men, that is, is no longer regarded as a man, as in chapters 52 and 49. Man of sorrows means that is, whose distinguishing characteristic was sorrows. Acquainted with means familiar by constant contact with. Grief is literally, disease, figuratively for all kinds of calamity, as in Jeremiah; leprosy especially represented this, being a direct judgment from God. It is remarkable Jesus is not mentioned as having ever suffered under sickness. And we hid faces is rather, as one who causes men to hide their faces from Him, in aversion, or, He was as an hiding of the face before it, that is, as a thing before which a man covers his face in disgust, or, as one before whom is the covering of the face, before whom one covers the face in disgust. We means the prophet identifying himself with the Jews, as in verse 1. Esteemed not means negative contempt; the previous words express positive. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Surely he has borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows, yet we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted. Our griefs is literally, But yet He hath taken, or borne, our sicknesses, that is, they who despised Him because of His human infirmities ought rather to have esteemed Him on account of them; for thereby Himself took our infirmities, bodily diseases, so Matthew quotes it. In the Hebrew for borne, or took, there is probably the double notion, He took on Himself vicariously, as in verses 5, 6, 8, and 12, and so He took away; His perfect humanity whereby He was bodily afflicted for us, and in all our afflictions, as in chapters 63 and Hebrews, was the ground on which He cured the sick; so that Matthew's quotation is not a mere accommodation. The Hebrew there may mean to overwhelm with darkness; Messiah's time of darkness was temporary, as in Matthew, answering to the bruising of His heel; Satan's is to be eternal, answering to the bruising of his head, as in chapter 50. Carried sorrows means the notion of substitution strictly: Carried, namely, as a burden. Sorrows, that is, pains of the mind, as griefs refer to pains of the body, as in Psalms. Matthew might seem to oppose this: And bare our sicknesses. But he uses sicknesses figuratively for sins, the cause of them. Christ took on Himself all man's infirmities, so as to remove them; the bodily by direct miracle, grounded on His participation in human infirmities; those of the soul by His vicarious suffering, which did away with the source of both. Sin and sickness are ethically connected as cause and effect, as in chapters 33 and Psalm 103 and Matthew and John and James. We did esteem him stricken means judicially, namely, for His sins; whereas it was for ours. We thought Him to be a leper, as in the Vulgate, leprosy being the direct divine judgment for guilt, as in Leviticus, Numbers, and Chronicles. Smitten means by divine judgments. Afflicted means for His sins; this was the point in which they so erred, as in Luke, Acts, and Corinthians. He was, it is true, afflicted, but not for His sins. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities; the chastisement of our peace was upon him, and with his stripes we are healed. Wounded means a bodily wound, not mere mental sorrow; literally, pierced, minutely appropriate to Messiah, whose hands, feet, and side were pierced, as in Psalm 22. The margin, wrongly, from a Hebrew root, translates, tormented. For, for means, as in Romans, Corinthians, Hebrews, and Peter, the cause for which He suffered not His own, but our sins. Bruised means crushing inward and outward suffering, as in verse 10. Chastisement is literally, the correction inflicted by a parent on children for their good, as in Hebrews; not punishment strictly, for this can have place only where there is guilt, which He had not; but He took on Himself the chastisement whereby the peace, reconciliation with our Father, as in Romans and Ephesians, of the children of God was to be effected, as in Hebrews. Upon him means as a burden, parallel to hath borne and carried. Stripes means minutely prophetical of His being scourged, as in Matthew and 1 Peter. Healed means spiritually, as in Psalm 41 and Jeremiah. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned every one to his own way, and the Lord has laid on him the iniquity of us all. This is the penitent confession of believers and of Israel in the last days, as in Zechariah. Sheep astray recalls Psalm 119 and 1 Peter: the antithesis is, In ourselves we were scattered; in Christ we are collected together; by nature we wander, driven headlong to destruction; in Christ we find the way to the gate of life, as Calvin. True, also, literally of Israel before its coming restoration, as in Ezekiel and Zechariah, also Matthew. Laid means hath made to light on Him, rather, hath made to rush upon Him. The iniquity means that is, its penalty; or rather, as in 2 Corinthians, He was not merely a sin offering, which would destroy the antithesis to righteousness, but sin for us; sin itself vicariously; the representative of the aggregate sin of all mankind; not sins in the plural, for the sin of the world is one, as in Romans; thus we are made not merely righteous, but righteousness, even the righteousness of God. The innocent was punished as if guilty, that the guilty might be rewarded as if innocent. This verse could be said of no mere martyr. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth; he is brought as a lamb to the slaughter, and as a sheep before her shearers is dumb, so he opens not his mouth. Oppressed means, some translate, It was exacted, and He was made answerable. The verb means, to have payment of a debt sternly exacted, as in Deuteronomy, and so to be oppressed in general; the exaction of the full penalty for our sins in His sufferings is probably alluded to. And afflicted means or, and yet He suffered, or bore Himself patiently, etc. Some translation, He was made answerable, is hardly admitted by the Hebrew. Opened not mouth recalls Jeremiah and David in Psalms, prefiguring Messiah, as in Matthew and 1 Peter. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. He was taken from prison and from judgment, and who will declare his generation? For he was cut off out of the land of the living; for the transgression of my people was he stricken. From, not by, by. But prison is not true of Jesus, who was not incarcerated; restraint and bonds, as in John, more accord with the Hebrew. Acts translates as the Septuagint: In His humiliation His judgment, legal trial, was taken away; the virtual sense of the Hebrew as rendered by Lowth and sanctioned by the inspired writer of Acts; He was treated as one so mean that a fair trial was denied Him, as in Matthew and Mark. Some translate, After condemnation and judgment He was accepted. Who declare generation means who can set forth the wickedness of His generation? that is, of His contemporaries, as in Acts, which suits best the parallelism, the wickedness of His generation corresponding to oppressive judgment. But Luther, His length of life, that is, there shall be no end of His future days, as in verse 10 and Romans. Calvin includes the days of His church, which is inseparable from Himself. Hengstenberg, His posterity. He, indeed, shall be cut off, but His race shall be so numerous that none can fully declare it. Chrysostom, etc., His eternal sonship and miraculous incarnation. Cut off means implying a violent death, as in Daniel. My people means Isaiah, including himself among them by the word my; rather, Jehovah speaks in the person of His prophet, My people, by the election of grace, as in Hebrews. Was he stricken is Hebrew, the stroke was laid upon Him; some say the Hebrew means them, the collective body, whether of the prophets or people, to which the Jews refer the whole prophecy. But Jerome, the Syriac, and Ethiopic versions translate it Him; so it is singular in some passages, as in Psalm 11, His, Job 27, Him, chapter 44, thereto. The Septuagint, the Hebrew, lamo, upon Him, read the similar words, lamuth, unto death, which would at once set aside the Jewish interpretation, upon them. Origen, who laboriously compared the Hebrew with the Septuagint, so read it, and urged it against the Jews of his day, who would have denied it to be the true reading if the word had not then really so stood in the Hebrew text. If his sole authority be thought insufficient, perhaps lamo may imply that Messiah was the representative of the collective body of all men; hence the equivocal plural-singular form. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. He made his grave with the wicked, and with the rich in his death, because he had done no violence, neither was any deceit in his mouth. Rather, His grave was appointed, or they appointed Him His grave; that is, they intended, by crucifying Him with two thieves, as in Matthew, that He should have His grave with the wicked. Compare John, the denial of honourable burial being accounted a great ignominy, as in chapters 14 and Jeremiah. And with rich is rather, but He was with a rich man, etc. Some, for the parallelism to the wicked, translate ungodly, the effect of riches being to make one ungodly, but the Hebrew everywhere means rich, never by itself ungodly; the parallelism, too, is one of contrast, namely, between their design and the fact, as it was ordered by God, as in Matthew, Mark, and John; two rich men honoured Him at His death, Joseph of Arimathaea, and Nicodemus. In his death is Hebrew, deaths; some translate, His tomb, bamoth, from a different root, meaning high places, and so mounds for sepulture, as in Ezekiel. But all the versions oppose this, and the Hebrew hardly admits it. Rather translate, after His death, as we say, at His death. The plural, deaths, intensifies the force; as Adam by sin dying died, that is, incurred death, physical and spiritual, so Messiah, His substitute, endured death in both senses; spiritual, during His temporary abandonment by the Father; physical, when He gave up the ghost. Because is rather, as the sense demands, as in Job, although He had done no, etc., as in 1 Peter and 1 John. Violence means that is, wrong. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Yet it pleased the Lord to bruise him; he has put him to grief. When you will make his soul an offering for sin, he will see his seed, he will prolong his days, and the pleasure of the Lord will prosper in his hand. This is a transition from His humiliation to His exaltation. Pleased the Lord means the secret of His sufferings. They were voluntarily borne by Messiah, in order that thereby He might do Jehovah's will, as in John and Hebrews, as to man's redemption; so at the end of the verse, the pleasure of the Lord shall prosper in His hand. Bruise recalls verse 5 and Genesis 3, which was hereby fulfilled, though the Hebrew word for bruise, there, is not the one used here. The word Himself, in Matthew, implies a personal bearing on Himself of our maladies, spiritual and physical, which included as a consequence His ministration to our bodily ailments: these latter are the reverse side of sin; His bearing on Him our spiritual malady involved with it His bearing sympathetically, and healing, the outward, which is its fruits and its type. Some rightly object to Magee's translation, taken away, instead of borne, that the parallelism to carried would be destroyed. Besides, the Hebrew word elsewhere, when connected with sin, means to bear it and its punishment, as in Ezekiel. Matthew, elsewhere, also sets forth His vicarious atonement, as in Matthew 20. When thou, etc., is rather, as in the margin, when His soul, that is, He, shall have made an offering, etc. In the English version the change of person is harsh: from Jehovah, addressed in the second person in verse 10, to Jehovah speaking in the first person in verse 11. The margin rightly makes the prophet in the name of Jehovah Himself to speak in this verse. Offering for sin recalls Romans and 1 John. His seed means His spiritual posterity shall be numerous, as in Psalm 22; nay, more, though He must die, He shall see them. A numerous posterity was accounted a high blessing among the Hebrews; still more so, for one to live to see them, as in Genesis and Psalm 128. Prolong days means also esteemed a special blessing among the Jews, as in Psalm 91. Messiah shall, after death, rise again to an endless life, as in Hosea and Romans. Prosper recalls chapter 52, margin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. He will see of the travail of his soul, and will be satisfied; by his knowledge will my righteous servant justify many, for he will bear their iniquities. Jehovah is still speaking. See of the travail means He shall see such blessed fruits resulting from His sufferings as amply to repay Him for them, as in chapters 49 and 50. The satisfaction, in seeing the full fruit of His travail of soul in the conversion of Israel and the world, is to be realised in the last days, as in chapter 2. His knowledge is rather, the knowledge, experimentally, of Him, as in John and Philippians. My servant means Messiah, as in chapters 42 and 52. Righteous means the ground on which He justifies others, His own righteousness, as in 1 John. Justify means treat as if righteous, forensically, on the ground of His meritorious suffering, not their righteousness. Bear iniquities recalls verses 4 and 5, as the sinner's substitute. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Therefore will I divide him a portion with the great, and he will divide the spoil with the strong, because he has poured out his soul unto death, and he was numbered with the transgressors, and he bare the sin of many, and made intercession for the transgressors. Divide means as a conqueror dividing the spoil after a victory, as in Psalm 2 and Luke. Him means for Him. With great means some translate, I will give Him the mighty for a portion, so the Septuagint. But the parallel clause, with the strong, favours the English version. His triumphs shall be not merely among the few and weak, but among the many and mighty. Spoil strong recalls Colossians, compare Proverbs: With the great, with the mighty, may mean, as a great and mighty hero. Poured out soul means that is, His life, which was considered as residing in the blood, as in Leviticus and Romans. Numbered with, etc., means not that He was a transgressor, but He was treated as such, when crucified with thieves, as in Mark and Luke. Made intercession, etc., means this office He began on the cross, as in Luke, and now continues in heaven, as in chapters 59 and Hebrews and 1 John. Understand because before He was numbered, He bare, made intercession. His meritorious death and intercession are the cause of His ultimate triumph. Some, for the parallelism, translate, He was put on the same footing with the transgressors. But the English version agrees better with the Hebrew, and with the sense and fact as to Christ. That translation would make a tautology after He was numbered with the transgressors; parallelism does not need so servile a repetition. He made intercession for, etc., answers to the parallel, He was numbered with, etc., as effect answers to cause, His intercession for sinners being the effect flowing from His having been numbered with them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

  • Verse 7. He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth; he is brought as a lamb to the slaughter, and as a sheep before her shearers is dumb, so he opens not his mouth. Oppressed means, some translate, It was exacted, and He was made answerable. The verb means, to have payment of a debt sternly exacted, as in Deuteronomy, and so to be oppressed in general; the exaction of the full penalty for our sins in His sufferings is probably alluded to. And afflicted means or, and yet He suffered, or bore Himself patiently, etc. Some translation, He was made answerable, is hardly admitted by the Hebrew. Opened not mouth recalls Jeremiah and David in Psalms, prefiguring Messiah, as in Matthew and 1 Peter. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. He was taken from prison and from judgment, and who will declare his generation? For he was cut off out of the land of the living; for the transgression of my people was he stricken. From, not by, by. But prison is not true of Jesus, who was not incarcerated; restraint and bonds, as in John, more accord with the Hebrew. Acts translates as the Septuagint: In His humiliation His judgment, legal trial, was taken away; the virtual sense of the Hebrew as rendered by Lowth and sanctioned by the inspired writer of Acts; He was treated as one so mean that a fair trial was denied Him, as in Matthew and Mark. Some translate, After condemnation and judgment He was accepted. Who declare generation means who can set forth the wickedness of His generation? that is, of His contemporaries, as in Acts, which suits best the parallelism, the wickedness of His generation corresponding to oppressive judgment. But Luther, His length of life, that is, there shall be no end of His future days, as in verse 10 and Romans. Calvin includes the days of His church, which is inseparable from Himself. Hengstenberg, His posterity. He, indeed, shall be cut off, but His race shall be so numerous that none can fully declare it. Chrysostom, etc., His eternal sonship and miraculous incarnation. Cut off means implying a violent death, as in Daniel. My people means Isaiah, including himself among them by the word my; rather, Jehovah speaks in the person of His prophet, My people, by the election of grace, as in Hebrews. Was he stricken is Hebrew, the stroke was laid upon Him; some say the Hebrew means them, the collective body, whether of the prophets or people, to which the Jews refer the whole prophecy. But Jerome, the Syriac, and Ethiopic versions translate it Him; so it is singular in some passages, as in Psalm 11, His, Job 27, Him, chapter 44, thereto. The Septuagint, the Hebrew, lamo, upon Him, read the similar words, lamuth, unto death, which would at once set aside the Jewish interpretation, upon them. Origen, who laboriously compared the Hebrew with the Septuagint, so read it, and urged it against the Jews of his day, who would have denied it to be the true reading if the word had not then really so stood in the Hebrew text. If his sole authority be thought insufficient, perhaps lamo may imply that Messiah was the representative of the collective body of all men; hence the equivocal plural-singular form. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. He made his grave with the wicked, and with the rich in his death, because he had done no violence, neither was any deceit in his mouth. Rather, His grave was appointed, or they appointed Him His grave; that is, they intended, by crucifying Him with two thieves, as in Matthew, that He should have His grave with the wicked. Compare John, the denial of honourable burial being accounted a great ignominy, as in chapters 14 and Jeremiah. And with rich is rather, but He was with a rich man, etc. Some, for the parallelism to the wicked, translate ungodly, the effect of riches being to make one ungodly, but the Hebrew everywhere means rich, never by itself ungodly; the parallelism, too, is one of contrast, namely, between their design and the fact, as it was ordered by God, as in Matthew, Mark, and John; two rich men honoured Him at His death, Joseph of Arimathaea, and Nicodemus. In his death is Hebrew, deaths; some translate, His tomb, bamoth, from a different root, meaning high places, and so mounds for sepulture, as in Ezekiel. But all the versions oppose this, and the Hebrew hardly admits it. Rather translate, after His death, as we say, at His death. The plural, deaths, intensifies the force; as Adam by sin dying died, that is, incurred death, physical and spiritual, so Messiah, His substitute, endured death in both senses; spiritual, during His temporary abandonment by the Father; physical, when He gave up the ghost. Because is rather, as the sense demands, as in Job, although He had done no, etc., as in 1 Peter and 1 John. Violence means that is, wrong. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Yet it pleased the Lord to bruise him; he has put him to grief. When you will make his soul an offering for sin, he will see his seed, he will prolong his days, and the pleasure of the Lord will prosper in his hand. This is a transition from His humiliation to His exaltation. Pleased the Lord means the secret of His sufferings. They were voluntarily borne by Messiah, in order that thereby He might do Jehovah's will, as in John and Hebrews, as to man's redemption; so at the end of the verse, the pleasure of the Lord shall prosper in His hand. Bruise recalls verse 5 and Genesis 3, which was hereby fulfilled, though the Hebrew word for bruise, there, is not the one used here. The word Himself, in Matthew, implies a personal bearing on Himself of our maladies, spiritual and physical, which included as a consequence His ministration to our bodily ailments: these latter are the reverse side of sin; His bearing on Him our spiritual malady involved with it His bearing sympathetically, and healing, the outward, which is its fruits and its type. Some rightly object to Magee's translation, taken away, instead of borne, that the parallelism to carried would be destroyed. Besides, the Hebrew word elsewhere, when connected with sin, means to bear it and its punishment, as in Ezekiel. Matthew, elsewhere, also sets forth His vicarious atonement, as in Matthew 20. When thou, etc., is rather, as in the margin, when His soul, that is, He, shall have made an offering, etc. In the English version the change of person is harsh: from Jehovah, addressed in the second person in verse 10, to Jehovah speaking in the first person in verse 11. The margin rightly makes the prophet in the name of Jehovah Himself to speak in this verse. Offering for sin recalls Romans and 1 John. His seed means His spiritual posterity shall be numerous, as in Psalm 22; nay, more, though He must die, He shall see them. A numerous posterity was accounted a high blessing among the Hebrews; still more so, for one to live to see them, as in Genesis and Psalm 128. Prolong days means also esteemed a special blessing among the Jews, as in Psalm 91. Messiah shall, after death, rise again to an endless life, as in Hosea and Romans. Prosper recalls chapter 52, margin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. He will see of the travail of his soul, and will be satisfied; by his knowledge will my righteous servant justify many, for he will bear their iniquities. Jehovah is still speaking. See of the travail means He shall see such blessed fruits resulting from His sufferings as amply to repay Him for them, as in chapters 49 and 50. The satisfaction, in seeing the full fruit of His travail of soul in the conversion of Israel and the world, is to be realised in the last days, as in chapter 2. His knowledge is rather, the knowledge, experimentally, of Him, as in John and Philippians. My servant means Messiah, as in chapters 42 and 52. Righteous means the ground on which He justifies others, His own righteousness, as in 1 John. Justify means treat as if righteous, forensically, on the ground of His meritorious suffering, not their righteousness. Bear iniquities recalls verses 4 and 5, as the sinner's substitute. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Therefore will I divide him a portion with the great, and he will divide the spoil with the strong, because he has poured out his soul unto death, and he was numbered with the transgressors, and he bare the sin of many, and made intercession for the transgressors. Divide means as a conqueror dividing the spoil after a victory, as in Psalm 2 and Luke. Him means for Him. With great means some translate, I will give Him the mighty for a portion, so the Septuagint. But the parallel clause, with the strong, favours the English version. His triumphs shall be not merely among the few and weak, but among the many and mighty. Spoil strong recalls Colossians, compare Proverbs: With the great, with the mighty, may mean, as a great and mighty hero. Poured out soul means that is, His life, which was considered as residing in the blood, as in Leviticus and Romans. Numbered with, etc., means not that He was a transgressor, but He was treated as such, when crucified with thieves, as in Mark and Luke. Made intercession, etc., means this office He began on the cross, as in Luke, and now continues in heaven, as in chapters 59 and Hebrews and 1 John. Understand because before He was numbered, He bare, made intercession. His meritorious death and intercession are the cause of His ultimate triumph. Some, for the parallelism, translate, He was put on the same footing with the transgressors. But the English version agrees better with the Hebrew, and with the sense and fact as to Christ. That translation would make a tautology after He was numbered with the transgressors; parallelism does not need so servile a repetition. He made intercession for, etc., answers to the parallel, He was numbered with, etc., as effect answers to cause, His intercession for sinners being the effect flowing from His having been numbered with them.
