The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Numbers 5 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying,

2 Command the children of Israel, that they put out of the camp every leper, and every one that hath an issue, and whosoever is defiled by the dead:

3 Both male and female shall ye put out, without the camp shall ye put them; that they defile not their camps, in the midst whereof I dwell.

4 And the children of Israel did so, and put them out without the camp: as the LORD spake unto Moses, so did the children of Israel.

5 And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying,

6 Speak unto the children of Israel, When a man or woman shall commit any sin that men commit, to do a trespass against the LORD, and that person be guilty;

7 Then they shall confess their sin which they have done: and he shall recompense his trespass with the principal thereof, and add unto it the fifth part thereof, and give it unto him against whom he hath trespassed.

8 But if the man have no kinsman to recompense the trespass unto, let the trespass be recompensed unto the LORD, even to the priest; beside the ram of the atonement, whereby an atonement shall be made for him.

9 And every offering of all the holy things of the children of Israel, which they bring unto the priest, shall be his.

10 And every man’s hallowed things shall be his: whatsoever any man giveth the priest, it shall be his.

11 And the LORD spake unto Moses, saying,

12 Speak unto the children of Israel, and say unto them, If any man’s wife go aside, and commit a trespass against him,

13 And a man lie with her carnally, and it be hid from the eyes of her husband, and be kept close, and she be defiled, and there be no witness against her, neither she be taken with the manner;

14 And the spirit of jealousy come upon him, and he be jealous of his wife, and she be defiled: or if the spirit of jealousy come upon him, and he be jealous of his wife, and she be not defiled:

15 Then shall the man bring his wife unto the priest, and he shall bring her offering for her, the tenth part of an ephah of barley meal; he shall pour no oil upon it, nor put frankincense thereon; for it is an offering of jealousy, an offering of memorial, bringing iniquity to remembrance.

16 And the priest shall bring her near, and set her before the LORD:

17 And the priest shall take holy water in an earthen vessel; and of the dust that is in the floor of the tabernacle the priest shall take, and put it into the water:

18 And the priest shall set the woman before the LORD, and uncover the woman’s head, and put the offering of memorial in her hands, which is the jealousy offering: and the priest shall have in his hand the bitter water that causeth the curse:

19 And the priest shall charge her by an oath, and say unto the woman, If no man have lain with thee, and if thou hast not gone aside to uncleanness with another instead of thy husband, be thou free from this bitter water that causeth the curse:

20 But if thou hast gone aside to another instead of thy husband, and if thou be defiled, and some man have lain with thee beside thine husband:

21 Then the priest shall charge the woman with an oath of cursing, and the priest shall say unto the woman, The LORD make thee a curse and an oath among thy people, when the LORD doth make thy thigh to rot, and thy belly to swell;

22 And this water that causeth the curse shall go into thy bowels, to make thy belly to swell, and thy thigh to rot: And the woman shall say, Amen, amen.

23 And the priest shall write these curses in a book, and he shall blot them out with the bitter water:

24 And he shall cause the woman to drink the bitter water that causeth the curse: and the water that causeth the curse shall enter into her, and become bitter.

25 Then the priest shall take the jealousy offering out of the woman’s hand, and shall wave the offering before the LORD, and offer it upon the altar:

26 And the priest shall take an handful of the offering, even the memorial thereof, and burn it upon the altar, and afterward shall cause the woman to drink the water.

27 And when he hath made her to drink the water, then it shall come to pass, that, if she be defiled, and have done trespass against her husband, that the water that causeth the curse shall enter into her, and become bitter, and her belly shall swell, and her thigh shall rot: and the woman shall be a curse among her people.

28 And if the woman be not defiled, but be clean; then she shall be free, and shall conceive seed.

29 This is the law of jealousies, when a wife goeth aside to another instead of her husband, and is defiled;

30 Or when the spirit of jealousy cometh upon him, and he be jealous over his wife, and shall set the woman before the LORD, and the priest shall execute upon her all this law.

31 Then shall the man be guiltless from iniquity, and this woman shall bear her iniquity.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

This chapter keeps the camp clean and deals with wrongs between neighbours. Every leper, everyone with an issue, and everyone defiled by the dead must be put outside the camp. A person who wrongs another must confess, restore the principal, and add a fifth to it. The second half gives the law of jealousy: when a husband suspects his wife, the priest sets her before the LORD with a jealousy offering, writes curses in a book and washes them into bitter water, and she drinks it, so that guilt or innocence is shown by the outcome the chapter describes. Interpreted Editorial overview, grounded in the chapter text and its study notes

Unfamiliar terms

  • Jealousy offering. The offering brought when a husband suspects his wife, set in her hands by the priest as part of the ordeal before the LORD. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 2. Israel was commanded to put out of the camp every leper, everyone with an issue, and everyone defiled by the dead. Henry notes the camp was newly modelled and put in order, and cleansing completed the reformation: the purity of the church must be kept as carefully as its peace and order. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 3. Both male and female were put outside the camp, so that they would not defile the camps in the midst of which God dwelt. The rule admits no exception of sex or standing: where God dwells, defilement must be kept far from his tabernacle. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 4. Israel did so at once, putting them out without the camp as the Lord spoke to Moses. The immediate execution matters: the order was not debated or delayed once the camp had been ordered and the tabernacle fixed in its midst. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 6. When a man or woman commits any sin that men commit, doing a trespass against the Lord, that person is guilty. Henry stresses that overreaching or defrauding a brother is a trespass against the Lord himself, who strictly charges us to do justly. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 7. The offender must confess his sin and recompense the trespass with its principal, adding a fifth part, to the one wronged. Henry insists confession must be to God and to the neighbour, with shame taken to oneself, and restitution alone is not enough without faith and repentance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 8. If the wronged man has no kinsman to receive recompense, the trespass is recompensed to the Lord, that is, to the priest, besides the ram of atonement. Satisfaction was thus made both for the loss sustained by the neighbour and for the offence done to God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 9. Every offering of the holy things of Israel, brought to the priest, shall be his. The priesthood lived by the holy things, a standing provision that freed them for the sanctuary's service. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 10. Every man's hallowed things shall be his: whatever any man gives the priest, it shall be his. Henry's group comment covers both verses: the offerings belonged to the priests as God's appointed portion, not as a gift at the giver's later disposal. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 12. If a man's wife goes aside and commits a trespass against him, the law of jealousy applies. Henry calls adultery an exceedingly sinful sin: going aside from God and virtue, robbing the husband of honour, alienating his right, and violating her covenant with him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 13. The case supposes a man lying with her carnally, hidden from her husband's eyes, kept close, with no witness against her and she not taken in the act. Henry notes the sin is taken to be one sinners take great care to conceal, since it would not covet secrecy if it were not shameful. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 14. The spirit of jealousy may come on a husband whether his wife is defiled or not defiled. Henry quotes Solomon: jealousy is the rage of a man, and cruel as the grave, which is why the law had to govern it rather than leave it to private revenge. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 15. The man must bring his wife to the priest, with her offering, a tenth of an ephah of barley meal, without oil or frankincense. Henry explains the coarse offering, an offering of memorial, suited the afflicted state of the family, whether the jealousy had cause or not, and marked the trial as a religious appeal to God's omniscience and justice. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 16. The priest brought her near and set her before the Lord. The trial was not a private ordeal but a solemn public appeal to a heart-searching God, in whose presence the truth would be declared. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 17. The priest took holy water in an earthen vessel, with dust from the floor of the tabernacle in it. Henry explains the plain earthen vessel suited the occasion's coarseness, the dust signified the reproach she lay under and the shame she ought to take to herself, and dust from the tabernacle floor honoured the place God had chosen for his name. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 18. The priest set the woman before the Lord, uncovered her head, and put the jealousy offering in her hands. The uncovered head and the offering in her hands made the trial public and solemn: she stood exposed before God, holding the memorial of the charge against her. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 19. The priest charged her by oath: if no man had lain with her and she had not gone aside to uncleanness, she would be free from the bitter water. The oath first offered innocence its clearing, so that a guiltless wife was not condemned by suspicion alone. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 20. But if she had gone aside to another instead of her husband and been defiled, the curse would apply. The conditional form is the heart of the trial: the same water and the same oath would clear the innocent and condemn the guilty. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 21. The priest charged her with an oath of cursing: the Lord would make her thigh rot and her belly swell. The curse was pronounced in advance and in detail, so that no guilty woman could afterwards claim she had not understood what she invoked. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 22. The water that causes the curse would go into her bowels, to make her belly swell and her thigh rot, and the woman answered, Amen, amen. Henry observes no guilty woman could say Amen to the adjuration and drink afterwards, unless she disbelieved God's truth or defied his justice. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 23. The priest wrote the curses in a book and blotted them out with the bitter water. Henry notes the priest first urged confession, warning her not to let the great name described in the sacred ceremony be blotted out with the bitter water, so the writing joined her fate to the water she would drink. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 24. The priest caused the woman to drink the bitter water that causes the curse, and the water entered into her. The water is called bitter because it caused the curse, and Henry draws the moral: sin is an evil and bitter thing, and forbidden pleasures will be bitterness in the latter end. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 25. The priest took the jealousy offering out of the woman's hand and waved it before the Lord. The memorial offering, waved after the drinking, kept the whole trial in the realm of worship: the case had been laid before God, not settled by the husband's hand. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 26. The priest took a handful of the offering, its memorial, burned it on the altar, and afterward caused the woman to drink the water. The burning of the memorial sealed the appeal: God had been called to remember the case, and the water would now declare it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 27. If she was defiled, the water that caused the curse entered her, her belly swelled, her thigh rotted, and she became a curse among her people. Henry's lesson is that secret sins are known to God and sometimes strangely brought to light in this life, and that whoremongers and adulterers God will surely judge. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 28. If the woman was not defiled but clean, she was free and would conceive seed. The same providence that hurt the guilty cleared the innocent, and Henry notes God will manifest the innocency of the innocent, answering the purposes he intends. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 29. This is the law of jealousies, when a wife goes aside to another instead of her husband and is defiled. The summary gathers the whole trial into a standing law, so that no future case of suspicion needed a new rule. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 30. The same law applied when the spirit of jealousy came on a man over his wife, whether she was defiled or not. The law thus covered both outcomes in advance, restraining the husband from cruel treatment on mere suspicion while still leaving guilt no escape. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 31. Then the man was guiltless from iniquity, and the woman bore her iniquity. Gill explains the husband, having proceeded as the law directed on just ground of suspicion, was chargeable with no sin or blame whatever the outcome, while the guilty woman bore the punishment the bitter waters brought on her. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, John Gill
