The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 15 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Then answered Eliphaz the Temanite, and said,

2 Should a wise man utter vain knowledge, and fill his belly with the east wind?

3 Should he reason with unprofitable talk? or with speeches wherewith he can do no good?

4 Yea, thou castest off fear, and restrainest prayer before God.

5 For thy mouth uttereth thine iniquity, and thou choosest the tongue of the crafty.

6 Thine own mouth condemneth thee, and not I: yea, thine own lips testify against thee.

7 Art thou the first man that was born? or wast thou made before the hills?

8 Hast thou heard the secret of God? and dost thou restrain wisdom to thyself?

9 What knowest thou, that we know not? what understandest thou, which is not in us?

10 With us are both the grayheaded and very aged men, much elder than thy father.

11 Are the consolations of God small with thee? is there any secret thing with thee?

12 Why doth thine heart carry thee away? and what do thy eyes wink at,

13 That thou turnest thy spirit against God, and lettest such words go out of thy mouth?

14 What is man, that he should be clean? and he which is born of a woman, that he should be righteous?

15 Behold, he putteth no trust in his saints; yea, the heavens are not clean in his sight.

16 How much more abominable and filthy is man, which drinketh iniquity like water?

17 I will shew thee, hear me; and that which I have seen I will declare;

18 Which wise men have told from their fathers, and have not hid it:

19 Unto whom alone the earth was given, and no stranger passed among them.

20 The wicked man travaileth with pain all his days, and the number of years is hidden to the oppressor.

21 A dreadful sound is in his ears: in prosperity the destroyer shall come upon him.

22 He believeth not that he shall return out of darkness, and he is waited for of the sword.

23 He wandereth abroad for bread, saying, Where is it? he knoweth that the day of darkness is ready at his hand.

24 Trouble and anguish shall make him afraid; they shall prevail against him, as a king ready to the battle.

25 For he stretcheth out his hand against God, and strengtheneth himself against the Almighty.

26 He runneth upon him, even on his neck, upon the thick bosses of his bucklers:

27 Because he covereth his face with his fatness, and maketh collops of fat on his flanks.

28 And he dwelleth in desolate cities, and in houses which no man inhabiteth, which are ready to become heaps.

29 He shall not be rich, neither shall his substance continue, neither shall he prolong the perfection thereof upon the earth.

30 He shall not depart out of darkness; the flame shall dry up his branches, and by the breath of his mouth shall he go away.

31 Let not him that is deceived trust in vanity: for vanity shall be his recompence.

32 It shall be accomplished before his time, and his branch shall not be green.

33 He shall shake off his unripe grape as the vine, and shall cast off his flower as the olive.

34 For the congregation of hypocrites shall be desolate, and fire shall consume the tabernacles of bribery.

35 They conceive mischief, and bring forth vanity, and their belly prepareth deceit.

Every substantive historical claim in the editorial panels below carries one of three labels, plus a source reference:

  • Documented — directly supported by a cited source.
  • Interpreted — a reasoned explanation or proposed relationship.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Eliphaz answers a second time, now sharper: should a wise man utter vain knowledge and fill his belly with the east wind? He charges that Job’s own words condemn him, and repeats in substance the night revelation of chapter 4 — God puts no trust in his saints, and the heavens are not clean in his sight. The rest of the speech paints the wicked man’s lot: terror, desolate cities, and a life that reaps what it sowed. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Eliphaz the Temanite answers a second time, now sharper than before. The second series of speeches abandons the mild opening for direct accusation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Should a wise man utter vain knowledge and fill his belly with the east wind? JFB explains vain knowledge as windy knowledge, and the east wind as the most destructive wind of the region — empty violence filling the inward parts. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Should he reason with unprofitable talk, or speeches with which he can do no good? Eliphaz charges Job's words as useless as well as empty. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Job casts off fear and restrains prayer before God. JFB explains fear as reverence, and prayer as devotion: if God disregarded the righteous and prospered the wicked, all devotion would end. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Job's mouth teaches his iniquity, and he chooses the tongue of the crafty. JFB reads this as Job's own sophistry proving his guilt. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Job's own mouth condemns him, not Eliphaz, and his own lips testify against him. JFB notes no pious man would utter such sentiments. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Was Job the first man born, or made before the hills? JFB asks whether Job is wisdom personified: wisdom existed before the hills, the eternal Son, and the farther back one existed the nearer he was to eternal wisdom. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Has Job heard the secret of God, and does he restrain wisdom to himself? JFB renders secret as a listener in God's secret council, on whose cushions Eastern counsellors sit, and asks whether Job borrowed his wisdom thence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. What do the friends know that Job does not know, or understand that is not in them? JFB notes Eliphaz retorts Job's words upon himself, claiming the friends are equally aware. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. With the friends are the greyheaded and very aged men, much elder than Job's father. JFB notes the aged are on Eliphaz's side, and Eliphaz himself seems older than Job. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Are the consolations of God small with Job, or is there any secret thing with him? JFB explains the consolations as Eliphaz's revelatory reproof, and asks whether Job despises a gentle word addressed to him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Why does Job's heart carry him away, and what do his eyes wink at? JFB explains winking as eyes evincing pride. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Job turns his spirit against God and lets such words go out of his mouth. JFB renders it as fretting against God and letting fall rash words. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. What is man that he should be clean, and he born of a woman that he should be righteous? JFB notes Eliphaz repeats the revelation of chapter 4 in substance, using Job's own words to strike him with his own weapons. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Behold, God puts no trust in his saints, and the heavens are not clean in his sight. JFB notes the saints here are holy angels, as servants were in chapter 4. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. How much more abominable and filthy is man, who drinks iniquity like water? JFB explains filthy as sour in Arabic, corrupted from original purity. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. Eliphaz will show Job, hear him, and declare what he has seen. Against Job's claim that the wicked prosper most, Eliphaz appeals to his own experience and the wisdom of the ancients. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. Which wise men have told from their fathers, and have not hidden it. JFB renders it as wisdom handed down from fathers and not concealed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. Unto whom alone the earth was given, and no stranger passed among them. JFB notes Eliphaz boasts like a genuine Arab that his ancestors possessed the land unmixed with foreigners, opposing Job's claim that the earth is given into the wicked's hand. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. The wicked man travails with pain all his days, and the number of his years is hidden to the oppressor. JFB renders travaileth as trembling of himself with no real danger, because he knows not when his life must end. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. A dreadful sound is in the wicked man's ears, and in prosperity the destroyer shall come upon him. JFB explains an evil conscience conceiving alarm at every sudden sound even in a time of peace. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. He believes he shall not return out of darkness, and he is waited for by the sword. JFB explains darkness as danger or calamity, glancing at Job's despair of restoration, and the sword as his destiny, every sword seeming drawn against him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. He wanders abroad for bread, saying where is it, knowing the day of darkness is ready at his hand. JFB notes famine depicts sore need, and ready at his hand is an Arabic phrase for complete readiness, as if in the hand. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. Trouble and anguish make him afraid, prevailing against him as a king ready to battle. JFB explains prevail as breaking upon him suddenly and terribly, as a king ready for battle. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. Because he stretches out his hand against God and strengthens himself against the Almighty. JFB gives the image of wielding a spear as a bold rebel against God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. He runs upon God with neck outstretched, upon the thick bosses of his bucklers. JFB explains the rebel and his fellows joining shields into a compact covering against weapons hurled from a fortress. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. Because he covers his face with fatness and makes collops of fat on his flanks. JFB notes the well-nourished body is the sign of prosperity, pampered by sensual indulgence until it rebels against God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. He dwells in desolate cities and in houses which no man inhabits, ready to become heaps. JFB identifies this class as robbers who plunder cities and seize houses of banished citizens, the class Job himself had chosen in chapter 12. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. He shall not be rich, neither shall his substance continue, nor shall he prolong perfection on the earth. JFB renders it as not increasing his riches: he has reached his highest point, and his acquired wealth shall not be extended. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. He shall not depart out of darkness, the flame shall dry up his branches, and by the breath of God's mouth he shall go away. JFB explains the branches as offspring, the flame as the sultry eastern wind shrivelling sappy plants, and the mouth as God's wrath. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 31. Let not him that is deceived trust in vanity, for vanity shall be his recompense. JFB renders it as not trusting in vanity or being deceived by it, since sin is its own punishment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 32. It shall be accomplished before his time, and his branch shall not be green. JFB explains the tree shall not be filled up in its time, but ended before its time, with the withered tree imaging childless extinction. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 33. He shall shake off his unripe grape as the vine, and cast off his flower as the olive. JFB calls these images of incompleteness, the vine's own act poetically expressing that the sinner's ruin is the fruit of his own conduct. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 34. For the congregation of hypocrites shall be desolate, and fire shall consume the tabernacles of bribery. JFB explains the binding together of hypocrites as fruitless, and the tabernacles as dwellings of unjust judges, insinuating Job's fire may have come from bribery as an emir. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 35. They conceive mischief and bring forth vanity, and their belly prepares deceit. JFB calls this bitter irony on the unfruitfulness of the wicked: their conceptions and birthgivings consist solely in mischief. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
