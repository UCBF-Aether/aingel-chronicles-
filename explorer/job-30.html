The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 30 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 But now they that are younger than I have me in derision, whose fathers I would have disdained to have set with the dogs of my flock.

2 Yea, whereto might the strength of their hands profit me, in whom old age was perished?

3 For want and famine they were solitary; fleeing into the wilderness in former time desolate and waste.

4 Who cut up mallows by the bushes, and juniper roots for their meat.

5 They were driven forth from among men, (they cried after them as after a thief;)

6 To dwell in the cliffs of the valleys, in caves of the earth, and in the rocks.

7 Among the bushes they brayed; under the nettles they were gathered together.

8 They were children of fools, yea, children of base men: they were viler than the earth.

9 And now am I their song, yea, I am their byword.

10 They abhor me, they flee far from me, and spare not to spit in my face.

11 Because he hath loosed my cord, and afflicted me, they have also let loose the bridle before me.

12 Upon my right hand rise the youth; they push away my feet, and they raise up against me the ways of their destruction.

13 They mar my path, they set forward my calamity, they have no helper.

14 They came upon me as a wide breaking in of waters: in the desolation they rolled themselves upon me.

15 Terrors are turned upon me: they pursue my soul as the wind: and my welfare passeth away as a cloud.

16 And now my soul is poured out upon me; the days of affliction have taken hold upon me.

17 My bones are pierced in me in the night season: and my sinews take no rest.

18 By the great force of my disease is my garment changed: it bindeth me about as the collar of my coat.

19 He hath cast me into the mire, and I am become like dust and ashes.

20 I cry unto thee, and thou dost not hear me: I stand up, and thou regardest me not.

21 Thou art become cruel to me: with thy strong hand thou opposest thyself against me.

22 Thou liftest me up to the wind; thou causest me to ride upon it, and dissolvest my substance.

23 For I know that thou wilt bring me to death, and to the house appointed for all living.

24 Howbeit he will not stretch out his hand to the grave, though they cry in his destruction.

25 Did not I weep for him that was in trouble? was not my soul grieved for the poor?

26 When I looked for good, then evil came unto me: and when I waited for light, there came darkness.

27 My bowels boiled, and rested not: the days of affliction prevented me.

28 I went mourning without the sun: I stood up, and I cried in the congregation.

29 I am a brother to dragons, and a companion to owls.

30 My skin is black upon me, and my bones are burned with heat.

31 My harp also is turned to mourning, and my organ into the voice of them that weep.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

But now, Job says, they that are younger than he have him in derision — men whose fathers he would have disdained to set with the dogs of his flock mock him in the streets and abhor him. His pain gnaws through the nights, his bones are burned with heat, and his harp is turned to mourning. He cries to God and is not heard, and knows where it ends: God will bring him to death, to the house appointed for all living. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. But now they that are younger than Job have him in derision, whose fathers he would have disdained to set with the dogs of his flock. JFB notes the deriders are not the three friends but a low, base-born brood, whose juniors ought to reverence him, with dogs regarded in the East as unclean, living on offal outside houses. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Yea, whereto might the strength of their hands profit Job, in whom old age was perished? JFB notes that if their fathers could be no profit, much less the feebler sons, in whom hope of old age is gone, and even strength of hands would be useless, since all Job wants now is sympathy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. For want and famine they were solitary, fleeing into the wilderness in former time desolate and waste. JFB explains solitary as dried up and emaciated with hunger, describing the rudest Bedouins, gnawers of the wilderness in the yesternight of desolation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Who cut up mallows by the bushes, and juniper roots for their meat. JFB explains mallows as salt-wort eaten as salad by the poor, and juniper as broom, whose bitter roots the poor eat. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. They were driven forth from among men, and they cried after them as after a thief. JFB explains the cry raised as contempt from civilized Arabs, driving wild vagabonds away as thieves when they raid villages. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. To dwell in the cliffs of the valleys, in caves of the earth, and in the rocks. JFB explains the gloomy valleys or wadies, where dwelling marks wretchedness, with troglodytes living in caves. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Among the bushes they brayed, and under the nettles they were gathered together. JFB explains braying like the wild ass for food, with inarticulate tones little above beasts, gathered as sprinkled here and there behind thorn bushes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. They were children of fools, children of base men, viler than the earth. JFB explains fools as impious and abandoned, base as nameless low-born rabble, driven out of the land like the Horites, cave-dwellers of Mount Seir. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. And now Job is their song, yea, he is their byword. JFB notes the derision Christ also underwent, with Job become a song of derision to such as these. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. They abhor Job, flee far from him, and spare not to spit in his face. JFB explains spitting before his face as deliberate contempt and abhorrence in the East. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Because God has loosed Job's cord and afflicted him, they have also let loose the bridle before him. JFB explains He as God, and cord as a bow unstrung, with each letting loose his reins in unrestrained insult. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Upon Job's right hand rise the youth, pushing away his feet, and raising up against him the ways of their destruction. JFB explains youth as a low brood, rising on the right as accusers in court, jostling him out of the way, and raising siege ways against him like an army approaching a city. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. They mar Job's path, set forward his calamity, and have no helper. JFB continues the fortress image: they tear up the path by which succour might reach him, with no helper as an Arabic proverb for contemptible persons, yet even such afflict him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. They came upon Job as a wide breaking in of waters, and in the desolation they rolled themselves upon him. JFB explains it as through a wide breach made by besiegers in a fortress wall, amid the crash of falling masonry. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Terrors are turned upon Job, pursuing his soul as the wind, and his welfare passes away as a cloud. JFB explains terrors as pursuing his dignity, with welfare as prosperity passing like a cloud. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. And now Job's soul is poured out upon him, and the days of affliction have taken hold upon him. JFB notes Job's outward calamities now affect his mind, with poured out as irrepressible complaints. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. His bones are pierced in him in the night season, and his sinews take no rest. JFB explains night personified as piercing his bones so they fall from him, with sinews as gnawers, gnawing pains from elephantiasis never ceasing. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. By the great force of his disease Job's garment is changed, binding him about as the collar of his coat. JFB explains the garment changed from a robe of honour to mourning, with affliction as both outer and inner garment, binding his throat as a coat collar. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. God has cast Job into the mire, and he has become like dust and ashes. JFB notes God is poetically said to do what the mourner did to himself, lying in ashes until he became like them in dirty colour. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Job cries unto God, but God does not hear him; he stands up, but God regards him not. JFB explains standing as the reverential attitude of a suppliant before a king, with God not only refusing aid but regarding him with a frown. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. God has become cruel to Job, with his strong hand opposing himself against him. The charge is direct: the strong hand once preserving now opposes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. God lifts Job up to the wind, causing him to ride upon it, and dissolving his substance. JFB explains lifting to wind as a leaf or stubble, or pillars of sand raised to the clouds, with substance dissolved in the whirlwind's crash. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. For Job knows God will bring him to death, and to the house appointed for all living. JFB notes this shows chapter 30 cannot be restricted to hope of temporal deliverance: death is the realm of the dead, the house appointed for all. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. Howbeit God will not stretch out his hand to the grave, though they cry in his destruction. JFB explains Job's faith after death: though one goes to the grave, God will no more afflict in the ruin of the body if one cried to him when destroyed, with stretching the hand answering to raising the cry. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. Did not Job weep for him that was in trouble, and was not his soul grieved for the poor? JFB asks whether he may not be allowed to complain and beg relief, seeing he himself sympathized with those in trouble, those who had a hard time. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. When Job looked for good, then evil came unto him, and when he waited for light, there came darkness. JFB notes he may be allowed to crave help, seeing that when he looked for good on account of piety and charity, evil came instead. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. Job's bowels boiled and rested not, and the days of affliction prevented him. JFB explains bowels as the seat of deep feeling, boiled as violently heated and agitated, with prevented as unexpectedly surprising him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. Job went mourning without the sun, and stood up and cried in the congregation. JFB explains mourning as moving blackened, though not by the sun, but by the heat of God's wrath and elephantiasis blackness, standing as an innocent man crying for justice in court. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. Job is a brother to dragons, and a companion to owls. JFB renders dragons and owls as jackals and ostriches, uttering dismal screams amid solitudes, whose desolation Job resembles. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. Job's skin is black upon him, and his bones are burned with heat. JFB explains skin black and falling away from him, with bones as in chapter 19, burned with fever heat. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 31. Job's harp also is turned to mourning, and his organ into the voice of them that weep. JFB explains organ as pipe, with instruments properly for joy now used in sorrow, sadder by contrast. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
