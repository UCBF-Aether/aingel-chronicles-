The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Isaiah 36 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Now it came to pass in the fourteenth year of king Hezekiah, that Sennacherib king of Assyria came up against all the defenced cities of Judah, and took them.

2 And the king of Assyria sent Rabshakeh from Lachish to Jerusalem unto king Hezekiah with a great army. And he stood by the conduit of the upper pool in the highway of the fuller’s field.

3 Then came forth unto him Eliakim, Hilkiah’s son, which was over the house, and Shebna the scribe, and Joah, Asaph’s son, the recorder.

4 And Rabshakeh said unto them, Say ye now to Hezekiah, Thus saith the great king, the king of Assyria, What confidence is this wherein thou trustest?

5 I say, sayest thou, (but they are but vain words) I have counsel and strength for war: now on whom dost thou trust, that thou rebellest against me?

6 Lo, thou trustest in the staff of this broken reed, on Egypt; whereon if a man lean, it will go into his hand, and pierce it: so is Pharaoh king of Egypt to all that trust in him.

7 But if thou say to me, We trust in the LORD our God: is it not he, whose high places and whose altars Hezekiah hath taken away, and said to Judah and to Jerusalem, Ye shall worship before this altar?

8 Now therefore give pledges, I pray thee, to my master the king of Assyria, and I will give thee two thousand horses, if thou be able on thy part to set riders upon them.

9 How then wilt thou turn away the face of one captain of the least of my master’s servants, and put thy trust on Egypt for chariots and for horsemen?

10 And am I now come up without the LORD against this land to destroy it? the LORD said unto me, Go up against this land, and destroy it.

11 Then said Eliakim and Shebna and Joah unto Rabshakeh, Speak, I pray thee, unto thy servants in the Syrian language; for we understand it: and speak not to us in the Jews’ language, in the ears of the people that are on the wall.

12 But Rabshakeh said, Hath my master sent me to thy master and to thee to speak these words? hath he not sent me to the men that sit upon the wall, that they may eat their own dung, and drink their own piss with you?

13 Then Rabshakeh stood, and cried with a loud voice in the Jews’ language, and said, Hear ye the words of the great king, the king of Assyria.

14 Thus saith the king, Let not Hezekiah deceive you: for he shall not be able to deliver you.

15 Neither let Hezekiah make you trust in the LORD, saying, The LORD will surely deliver us: this city shall not be delivered into the hand of the king of Assyria.

16 Hearken not to Hezekiah: for thus saith the king of Assyria, Make an agreement with me by a present, and come out to me: and eat ye every one of his vine, and every one of his fig tree, and drink ye every one the waters of his own cistern;

17 Until I come and take you away to a land like your own land, a land of corn and wine, a land of bread and vineyards.

18 Beware lest Hezekiah persuade you, saying, the LORD will deliver us. Hath any of the gods of the nations delivered his land out of the hand of the king of Assyria?

19 Where are the gods of Hamath and Arphad? where are the gods of Sepharvaim? and have they delivered Samaria out of my hand?

20 Who are they among all the gods of these lands, that have delivered their land out of my hand, that the LORD should deliver Jerusalem out of my hand?

21 But they held their peace, and answered him not a word: for the king’s commandment was, saying, Answer him not.

22 Then came Eliakim, the son of Hilkiah, that was over the household, and Shebna the scribe, and Joah, the son of Asaph, the recorder, to Hezekiah with their clothes rent, and told him the words of Rabshakeh.

Every substantive historical claim in the editorial panels below carries one of three labels, plus a source reference:

  • Documented — directly supported by a cited source.
  • Interpreted — a reasoned explanation or proposed relationship.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

The historical appendix begins: in the fourteenth year of Hezekiah, Sennacherib king of Assyria takes all the fortified cities of Judah, then sends Rabshakeh with a great army from Lachish to Jerusalem. Standing by the conduit of the upper pool, Rabshakeh taunts the people in their own language, urging them not to trust Hezekiah or the Lord — and Jerusalem's envoys return to the king with their clothes rent. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Now it came to pass in the fourteenth year of king Hezekiah, that Sennacherib king of Assyria came up against all the defenced cities of Judah, and took them. This and chapters 37 to 39 form the historical appendix closing the first division of Isaiah's prophecies, added to make the parts referring to Assyria more intelligible, occurring almost word for word in 2 Kings 18 and 19, with 2 Kings 18:14-16 as additional matter and Hezekiah's writing in Isaiah alone. From 2 Chronicles we know Isaiah wrote the acts of Hezekiah, so it is probable his record here was incorporated into the Book of Kings by its compiler. Sennacherib lived, according to Assyrian inscriptions, more than twenty years after his invasion, but Isaiah survived Hezekiah, who lived upwards of fifteen years after the invasion, so the record of Sennacherib's death in chapter 37 is no objection to this section coming from Isaiah. Pul was probably the last of the old dynasty, and Sargon, a powerful satrap who contrived to possess himself of supreme power and found a new dynasty; no attempt was made by Judah to throw off the Assyrian yoke during his vigorous reign. The accession of his son Sennacherib was thought by Hezekiah the opportune time to refuse the long-paid tribute; Egypt and Ethiopia promised help to secure an ally against Assyria on their Asiatic frontier; Isaiah, while opposed to submission to Assyria, advised reliance on Jehovah and not on Egypt, but his advice was disregarded, and so Sennacherib invaded Judea in 712 B.C. He was the builder of the largest of the excavated palaces, that of Koyunjik, and his inscriptions agree strikingly with the Bible: the capture of the defenced cities of Judah, the devastation of the country and deportation of its inhabitants, and the increased tribute imposed on Hezekiah, thirty talents of gold, the exact number given in both, though the silver is set down at eight hundred talents in the inscriptions and three hundred in the Bible, the latter perhaps the actual amount carried off and the larger sum including silver from the temple doors and pillars. Fourteenth is the third of Sennacherib's reign; his ultimate object was Egypt, Hezekiah's ally, hence he, with the great body of his army, advanced towards the Egyptian frontier in southwest Palestine and did not approach Jerusalem. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. The king of Assyria sent Rabshakeh from Lachish to Jerusalem unto king Hezekiah with a great army. And he stood by the conduit of the upper pool in the highway of the fuller's field. Rab-shakeh was probably the chief leader, joined in Kings with Tartan and Rab-saris; Rab is a title of authority, chief-cup-bearer. Lachish was a frontier town southwest of Jerusalem in Judah, represented as a great fortified city in a hilly and fruitful country in the Koyunjik bas-reliefs now in the British Museum, and its name is found on a slab over a figure of Sennacherib on his throne. The upper pool is the side on which the Assyrians would approach Jerusalem coming from the southwest. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Then came forth unto him Eliakim, Hilkiah's son, which was over the house, and Shebna the scribe, and Joah, Asaph's son, the recorder. Eliakim was successor to Shebna, who had been over the household, that is, chief minister of the king, as foretold in chapter 22. The scribe is secretary, recorder, literally one who reminds, a remembrancer to keep the king informed on important facts and to act as historiographer; in Kings the additional fact is given that the Assyrian envoys called to the king, in consequence of which Eliakim and the others came out to them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Rabshakeh said unto them: Say now to Hezekiah: Thus says the great king, the king of Assyria: What confidence is this wherein you trust? The great king is the usual title of the Persian and Assyrian kings, as they had many subordinate princes or kings under them over provinces. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. I say, sayest thou (but they are but vain words) I have counsel and strength for war: now on whom do you trust, that you rebel against me? Counsel refers to Egypt, famed for its wisdom, which Hezekiah trusted for counsel and strength for war. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Lo, you trust in the staff of this broken reed, on Egypt, whereon if a man lean, it will go into his hand, and pierce it: so is Pharaoh king of Egypt unto all that trust in him. It was a similar alliance with So, that is, Sabacho or else Sevechus, the Ethiopian king of Egypt, which provoked the Assyrian to invade and destroy Israel, the northern kingdom, under Hoshea; so Egypt proves a broken reed to all who trust in it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. But if you say to me: We trust in the Lord our God, is it not he, whose high places and whose altars Hezekiah has taken away, and said to Judah and to Jerusalem: You will worship before this altar? The Assyrian mistakes Hezekiah's religious reforms, whereby he took away the high places, as directed against Jehovah. Some of the high places may have been dedicated to Jehovah but worshipped under the form of an image in violation of the second commandment, and the brazen serpent, broken in pieces by Hezekiah and called Nehushtan, a piece of brass, because it was worshipped by Israel, was originally set up by God's command; hence the Assyrian's allegation has a specious colour, that you cannot look for help from Jehovah, for your king has taken away His altars. To Jerusalem recalls Deuteronomy and John's gospel, that worship is before this altar at Jerusalem. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Now therefore give pledges, I pray you, to my master the king of Assyria, and I will give you two thousand horses, if you be able on your part to set riders upon them. Give pledges is a taunting challenge: only give the guarantee that you can supply as many as two thousand riders, and I will give you two thousand horses. But seeing that you have not even this small number, how can you stand against the hosts of Assyrian cavalry? The Jews tried to supply their weakness in this arm from Egypt, as in chapter 31. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. How then will you turn away the face of one captain of the least of my master's servants, and put your trust on Egypt for chariots and for horsemen? A captain is a governor under a satrap, and even he commands more horsemen than this, so how can Judah turn away his face and yet trust Egypt for chariots and horsemen? Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Am I now come up without the Lord against this land to destroy it? The Lord said unto me: Go up against this land, and destroy it. This is a boastful inference from Assyria's past successes, designed to influence the Jews to surrender, for their own principles bound them to yield to Jehovah's will; Rab-shakeh may have heard from partisans in Judah what Isaiah had foretold in chapter 10. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Then said Eliakim and Shebna and Joah unto Rabshakeh: Speak, I pray you, unto your servants in the Syrian language, for we understand it, and speak not to us in the Jews' language, in the ears of the people that are on the wall. Syrian is rather Aramean, the language spoken north and east of Palestine and understood by the Assyrians as belonging to the same family of languages as their own, nearly akin to Hebrew also, though not intelligible to the multitude; Aram means a high land, and includes parts of Assyria as well as Syria. The Jews' language is Hebrew, which the men of Judah since the disruption of Israel claimed as their own peculiarly, as if they were now the only true representatives of the whole Hebrew twelve tribes. The ears of the people on the wall matter because the interview is within hearing distance of the city, the people crowd on the wall curious to hear the Assyrian message, and the Jewish rulers fear it will terrify the people, so they beg Rab-shakeh to speak Aramean. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. But Rabshakeh said: Has my master sent me to your master and to you to speak these words? Has he not sent me to the men that sit upon the wall, that they may eat their own dung, and drink their own piss with you? Is it to thy master and thee that I am sent? Nay, it is to the men on the wall, to let them know, so far am I from wishing them not to hear as you would wish, that unless they surrender they will be reduced to the direst extremities of famine in the siege, namely to eat their own excrements; or, connecting that they may eat with sit upon the wall, they who hold the wall knowingly expose themselves to the direst extremities. Isaiah, as a faithful historian, records the filthy and blasphemous language of the Assyrians to mark aright the true character of the attack on Jerusalem. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Then Rabshakeh stood, and cried with a loud voice in the Jews' language, and said: Hear the words of the great king, the king of Assyria. Rab-shakeh speaks louder and plainer than ever to the men on the wall, in the Jews' language itself. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Neither let Hezekiah make you trust in the Lord, saying: The Lord will surely deliver us; this city will not be delivered into the hand of the king of Assyria. The foes of God's people cannot succeed against them unless they can shake their trust in Him, which is Rab-shakeh's aim here, as in verse 10. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Hearken not to Hezekiah, for thus says the king of Assyria: Make an agreement with me by a present, and come out to me, and eat every man of his vine, and every one of his fig tree, and drink every one the waters of his own cistern. By a present is rather make peace with me, literally blessing, so called from the mutual congratulations attending the ratification of peace, or else do homage to me. Come out means surrender to me, then you may remain in quiet possession of your lands till my return from Egypt, when I will lead you away to a land fruitful as your own; Rab-shakeh tries to soften, in the Jews' eyes, the well-known Assyrian policy of weakening the vanquished by deporting them to other lands. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. Where are the gods of Hamath and Arphad? Where are the gods of Sepharvaim? And have they delivered Samaria out of my hand? Arphad is as in chapter 10, and Sepharvaim literally means the two scribes, now Sipphara on the east of Euphrates above Babylon. It was a just retribution: Israel worshipped the gods of Sepharvaim, and so colonists of Sepharvaim were planted in the land of Israel, thenceforth called Samaria, by the Assyrian conqueror. Samaria's fall recalls that Shalmaneser began the siege against Hoshea because of his conspiring with So of Egypt, Sargon finished it, and in his palace at Khorsabad he mentioned the number of Israelites carried captive, 27,280. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Who are they among all the gods of these lands, that have delivered their land out of my hand, that the Lord should deliver Jerusalem out of my hand? Compare chapter 10 and 2 Chronicles 32: here he contradicts his own assertion in verse 10 that he had come up against the land with the Lord. Liars need good memories. He classes Jehovah with the idols of the other lands, nay, thinks Him inferior in proportion as Judah, under His tutelage, was less than the lands under the tutelage of the idols. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. But they held their peace, and answered him not a word: for the king's commandment was, saying: Answer him not. Not a word, so as not to enter into a war of words with the blasphemer, as in Exodus and Jude. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Then came Eliakim, the son of Hilkiah, that was over the household, and Shebna the scribe, and Joah, the son of Asaph, the recorder, to Hezekiah with their clothes rent, and told him the words of Rabshakeh. Clothes rent means in grief and horror at the blasphemy, as in Matthew 26, and they told Hezekiah the words of Rab-shakeh. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Disclaimer. This section investigates a parallel between two passages; it does not conclude that one depends on the other, that they share an author, or that any historical claim about their composition is established. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Isaiah 36–39 and 2 Kings 18–19

Parallels. The study note on Isaiah 36:1 states that chapters 36 to 39 form the historical appendix closing the first division of Isaiah's prophecies, and that the narrative occurs almost word for word in 2 Kings 18 and 19: Sennacherib's invasion in Hezekiah's fourteenth year, Rabshakeh's embassy and taunts, Hezekiah's prayer, the destruction of the Assyrian army, and Sennacherib's murder. Interpreted Study note on Isaiah 36:1, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Differences. The same note records material found in one account and not the other: 2 Kings 18:14–16 is additional matter not in Isaiah, while Hezekiah's writing (his song in Isaiah 38) appears in Isaiah alone. The note also says that, from 2 Chronicles, Isaiah is known to have written the acts of Hezekiah, and that it is probable his record here was incorporated into the Books of Kings. Interpreted Study note on Isaiah 36:1, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
