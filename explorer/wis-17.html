The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Wisdom of Solomon 17 (King James Version, Apocrypha; public-domain text as received)

1 For great are thy judgments, and cannot be expressed: therefore unnurtured souls have erred.

2 For when unrighteous men thought to oppress the holy nation; they being shut up in their houses, the prisoners of darkness, and fettered with the bonds of a long night, lay [there] exiled from the eternal providence.

3 For while they supposed to lie hid in their secret sins, they were scattered under a dark veil of forgetfulness, being horribly astonished, and troubled with [strange] apparitions.

4 For neither might the corner that held them keep them from fear: but noises [as of waters] falling down sounded about them, and sad visions appeared unto them with heavy countenances.

5 No power of the fire might give them light: neither could the bright flames of the stars endure to lighten that horrible night.

6 Only there appeared unto them a fire kindled of itself, very dreadful: for being much terrified, they thought the things which they saw to be worse than the sight they saw not.

7 As for the illusions of art magick, they were put down, and their vaunting in wisdom was reproved with disgrace.

8 For they, that promised to drive away terrors and troubles from a sick soul, were sick themselves of fear, worthy to be laughed at.

9 For though no terrible thing did fear them; yet being scared with beasts that passed by, and hissing of serpents,

10 They died for fear, denying that they saw the air, which could of no side be avoided.

11 For wickedness, condemned by her own witness, is very timorous, and being pressed with conscience, always forecasteth grievous things.

12 For fear is nothing else but a betraying of the succours which reason offereth.

13 And the expectation from within, being less, counteth the ignorance more than the cause which bringeth the torment.

14 But they sleeping the same sleep that night, which was indeed intolerable, and which came upon them out of the bottoms of inevitable hell,

15 Were partly vexed with monstrous apparitions, and partly fainted, their heart failing them: for a sudden fear, and not looked for, came upon them.

16 So then whosoever there fell down was straitly kept, shut up in a prison without iron bars,

17 For whether he were husbandman, or shepherd, or a labourer in the field, he was overtaken, and endured that necessity, which could not be avoided: for they were all bound with one chain of darkness.

18 Whether it were a whistling wind, or a melodious noise of birds among the spreading branches, or a pleasing fall of water running violently,

19 Or a terrible sound of stones cast down, or a running that could not be seen of skipping beasts, or a roaring voice of most savage wild beasts, or a rebounding echo from the hollow mountains; these things made them to swoon for fear.

20 For the whole world shined with clear light, and none were hindered in their labour:

21 Over them only was spread an heavy night, an image of that darkness which should afterward receive them: but yet were they unto themselves more grievous than the darkness.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Over Egypt a heavy night fell: shut in their houses, the oppressors were scattered by fear, monstrous apparitions, and noises they could not escape, while the whole world besides shone with clear light. Their magicians' illusions failed, their promised deliverances sickened, and fear itself is named for what it is: a betraying of the succours which reason offers, and a witness of wickedness against itself. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing George Leo Haydock

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Great are your judgments, and cannot be expressed; therefore unnurtured souls have erred. Haydock's opening is that judgments too great for expression mislead the unnurtured. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 2. When unrighteous men thought to oppress the holy nation, they, shut up in their houses, prisoners of darkness and fettered with bonds of a long night, lay exiled from the eternal providence. Haydock pictures Egypt as prisoners of darkness, exiled from providence while thinking to oppress. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 3. While they supposed to lie hid in their secret sins, they were scattered under a dark veil of forgetfulness, being horribly astonished and troubled with strange apparitions. Haydock notes secret sins do not hide; they scatter under forgetfulness and apparitions. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 4. The cave that held them did not protect them from fear, for sounds on every side troubled them, and sad visions appeared with heavy countenances. Haydock observes that no hiding place protects from fear when darkness itself is the prison. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 5. No power of fire could give them light, nor could bright flames of stars endure to lighten that horrible night. Haydock notes fire and stars both fail to lighten this night. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 6. Only there appeared to them a fire kindled of itself, very dreadful, for being much terrified, they thought the things seen to be worse than the sight was. Haydock notes the self-kindled fire made terror worse than sight. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 7. As for the illusions of art magic, they were put down, and their vaunting in wisdom was reproved with disgrace. Haydock observes magic's illusions collapse and vaunted wisdom is disgraced. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 8. They who promised to drive away terrors and troubles from a sick soul were sick themselves of fear, worthy to be laughed at. Haydock's irony is that the promised drivers of fear are themselves sick with fear. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 9. Though no terrible thing frightened them, yet, scared by beasts passing by and hissing of serpents, they were troubled. Haydock notes passing beasts and hissing serpents suffice to terrify in darkness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 10. They died for fear, denying that they saw the air, which could of no side be avoided. Haydock observes they died of fear while denying the very air around them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 11. Wickedness, condemned by her own witness, is very timorous, and, pressed with conscience, always forecasts grievous things. Haydock's maxim is that wickedness makes fear, and conscience condemns. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 12. Fear is nothing else but a betraying of the succours which reason offers. Haydock defines fear as reason's succours betrayed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 13. Expectation from within, being less, counts the ignorance more than the cause which brings the torment. Haydock notes inward expectation magnifies ignorance above the torment's cause. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 14. They slept the same sleep that night, which was indeed intolerable, and came upon them out of the depths of inevitable hell. Haydock pictures the intolerable night coming from the depths below. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 15. They were partly vexed with monstrous apparitions, and partly fainted, their heart failing them, for a sudden fear, not looked for, came upon them. Haydock notes apparitions, fainting hearts, and sudden fear. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 16. Whoever there fell down was straitly kept, shut up in a prison without iron bars. Haydock notes the prison needs no bars; darkness itself holds them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 17. Whether he were husbandman, or shepherd, or a labourer in the field, he was overtaken, and endured that necessity which could not be avoided, for all were bound with one chain of darkness. Haydock stresses all are bound by one chain, whatever their trade. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 18. Whether it were a whistling wind, or a melodious noise of birds among spreading branches, or a pleasing fall of waters, all made them swoon for fear. Haydock's catalogue begins with ordinary sounds turned to terrors. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 19. Or a terrible sound of stones cast down, or a running that could not be seen of skipping beasts, or a roaring of wild beasts, or a rebounding echo from hollow mountains, all made them swoon for fear. Haydock continues the catalogue: stones, unseen beasts, roaring, and echo. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 20. The whole world shined with clear light, and none were hindered in their labour. Haydock's contrast is stark: the world works in light, while Egypt alone lies in darkness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 21. Over them only was spread a heavy night, an image of that darkness which should afterward receive them, but yet they were unto themselves more grievous than the darkness. Haydock notes the night's grievousness is that it images the final darkness, and the sufferers are worse to themselves than darkness itself. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
