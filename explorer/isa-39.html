The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Isaiah 39 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 At that time Merodachbaladan, the son of Baladan, king of Babylon, sent letters and a present to Hezekiah: for he had heard that he had been sick, and was recovered.

2 And Hezekiah was glad of them, and shewed them the house of his precious things, the silver, and the gold, and the spices, and the precious ointment, and all the house of his armour, and all that was found in his treasures: there was nothing in his house, nor in all his dominion, that Hezekiah shewed them not.

3 Then came Isaiah the prophet unto king Hezekiah, and said unto him, What said these men? and from whence came they unto thee? And Hezekiah said, They are come from a far country unto me, even from Babylon.

4 Then said he, What have they seen in thine house? And Hezekiah answered, All that is in mine house have they seen: there is nothing among my treasures that I have not shewed them.

5 Then said Isaiah to Hezekiah, Hear the word of the LORD of hosts:

6 Behold, the days come, that all that is in thine house, and that which thy fathers have laid up in store until this day, shall be carried to Babylon: nothing shall be left, saith the LORD.

7 And of thy sons that shall issue from thee, which thou shalt beget, shall they take away; and they shall be eunuchs in the palace of the king of Babylon.

8 Then said Hezekiah to Isaiah, Good is the word of the LORD which thou hast spoken. He said moreover, For there shall be peace and truth in my days.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

When Merodach-baladan, king of Babylon, sends letters and a present after Hezekiah's recovery, Hezekiah shows the envoys all his treasures — and Isaiah announces that all of it will one day be carried to Babylon, with his own sons serving in the Babylonian palace. Hezekiah accepts the word: there will be peace and truth in his days. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. At that time Merodachbaladan, the son of Baladan, king of Babylon, sent letters and a present to Hezekiah, for he had heard that he had been sick, and was recovered. This chapter treats Hezekiah's error in the display of his riches to the Babylonian ambassador. Merodach-baladan ruled Babylon among a succession of rulers, mostly viceroys of Assyria, for a hundred and fifty years before the overthrow of Nineveh by Cyaxares the Mede, from the time of Nabonassar, 747 B.C., called the Era of Nabonassar. Pul or Phallukha was then expelled and a new dynasty set up at Nineveh under Tiglath-pileser; Semiramis, Pul's wife, retired to Babylon with Nabonassar, her son, whose advent to the throne of Babylon after the overthrow of the old line at Nineveh marked a new era. Sometimes the viceroys of Babylon made themselves for a time independent of Assyria; thus Merodach-baladan at this time did so, encouraged by the Assyrian disaster in the Jewish campaign. He had done so before and was defeated in the first year of Sennacherib's reign, as recorded in cuneiform characters in that monarch's palace of Koyunjik. Nabopolassar was the first who established permanently his independence; his son Nebuchadnezzar raised Babylon to the position Nineveh once occupied, but from the want of stone near the Lower Euphrates, Babylon's buildings, formed of sun-dried brick, have not stood the wear of ages as Nineveh has. Merodach is an idol, the same as the god of war and planet Mars, and often kings took their names from their gods, as if peculiarly under their tutelage, so Belshazzar from Bel. Baladan means Bel is his lord; the chronicle of Eusebius contains a fragment of Berosus stating that Acises, an Assyrian viceroy, usurped the supreme command at Babylon, and Merodach-baladan murdered him and succeeded to the throne. Sennacherib conquered Merodach-baladan and left Esar-haddon, his son, as governor of Babylon. Merodach-baladan would naturally court the alliance of Hezekiah, who like himself had thrown off the yoke of the Assyrian king, and who would be equally glad of the Babylonian alliance against Assyria; hence arose the excessive attention he paid to the usurper. Sick gives an additional reason in Chronicles: the princes of Babylon sent to enquire of the wonder done in the land, namely the recession of the shadow on Ahaz's sundial, which to Chaldean astronomers would be especially interesting, the dial having been invented at Babylon. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Hezekiah was glad of them, and showed them the house of his precious things, the silver, and the gold, and the spices, and the precious ointment, and all the house of his armour, and all that was found in his treasures; there was nothing in his house, nor in all his dominion, that Hezekiah showed them not. Glad means it was not the mere act but the spirit of it which provoked God, for Hezekiah rendered not again according to the benefit done unto him, for his heart was lifted up; God tries His people at different times by different ways, bringing out all that is in their heart, to show them its varied corruptions, as with David in a similar case. Precious things are rather the house of his aromatic spices, from a Hebrew root to break to pieces, as is done to aromatics. Silver and gold were partly obtained from the Assyrian camp, partly from presents. Precious ointment was used for anointing kings and priests. Armour is or else vessels in general, but the parallel passage, treasuries for shields, favours the English version: his arsenal. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Then came Isaiah the prophet unto king Hezekiah, and said unto him: What said these men? And from whence came they unto you? And Hezekiah said: They are come from a far country unto me, even from Babylon. What and whence imply that any proposition coming from the idolatrous enemies of God, with whom Israel was forbidden to form alliance, should have been received with anything but gladness; reliance on Babylon rather than on God was a similar sin to the previous reliance on Egypt. Far country implies he had done nothing more than was proper in showing attention to strangers from a far country. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Then said he: What have they seen in your house? And Hezekiah answered: All that is in my house have they seen; there is nothing among my treasures that I have not showed them. All is a frank confession of his whole fault; the king submits his conduct to the scrutiny of a subject, because that subject was accredited by God, in contrast to Asa. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Then said Isaiah to Hezekiah: Hear the word of the Lord of hosts. Lord of hosts means who has all thy goods at His disposal. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Behold, the days come, that all that is in your house, and that which your fathers have laid up in store until this day, will be carried to Babylon; nothing will be left, says the Lord. Days come means one hundred and twenty years afterwards; this is the first intimation that the Jews would be carried to Babylon, the first designation of their place of punishment. The general prophecy of Moses, the more particular one of Ahijah in Jeroboam's time, beyond the river, and of Amos, captivity beyond Damascus, are now concentrated in this specific one as to Babylon. It was an exact retribution in kind, that as Babylon had been the instrument of Hezekiah and Judah's sin, so also it should be the instrument of their punishment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Of your sons that will issue from you, which you will beget, they will take away, and they will be eunuchs in the palace of the king of Babylon. From thee means the sons which Hezekiah, as Josephus tells us, wished to have, as in the note on wept sore in chapter 38, will be among the foremost in suffering; eunuchs was fulfilled in Daniel. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Then said Hezekiah to Isaiah: Good is the word of the Lord which you have spoken. He said moreover: For there will be peace and truth in my days. In my days means the punishment was not, as in David's case, sent in his time; true repentance acquiesces in all God's ways and finds cause of thanksgiving in any mitigation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
