The Text — Esther 11 (King James Version (1611) for chs 1–10; King James Version, Apocrypha (the Rest of Esther) for the Greek additions)

1 In the second year of the reign of Artexerxes the great, in the first day of the month Nisan, Mardocheus the son of Jairus, the son of Semei, the son of Cisai, of the tribe of Benjamin, had a dream;

2 Who was a Jew, and dwelt in the city of Susa, a great man, being a servitor in the king's court.

3 He was also one of the captives, which Nabuchodonosor the king of Babylon carried from Jerusalem with Jechonias king of Judea; and this was his dream:

4 Behold a noise of a tumult, with thunder, and earthquakes, and uproar in the land:

5 And, behold, two great dragons came forth ready to fight, and their cry was great.

6 And at their cry all nations were prepared to battle, that they might fight against the righteous people.

7 And lo a day of darkness and obscurity, tribulation and anguish, affliction and great uproar, upon earth.

8 And the whole righteous nation was troubled, fearing their own evils, and were ready to perish.

9 Then they cried unto God, and upon their cry, as it were from a little fountain, was made a great flood, even much water.

10 The light and the sun rose up, and the lowly were exalted, and devoured the glorious.

11 Now when Mardocheus, who had seen this dream, and what God had determined to do, was awake, he bare this dream in mind, and until night by all means was desirous to know it.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

In the second year of Artexerxes the great, on the first day of the month Nisan, Mordecai, a Jew of the tribe of Benjamin dwelling in Susa who had been carried captive from Jerusalem with Jechonias, dreams a dream of tumult: two great dragons come forth ready to fight, all nations prepare to battle against the righteous people, and a day of darkness and anguish falls on the righteous nation, troubled and ready to perish. The people cry to God, and as it were from a little fountain comes a great flood of much water; light and sun rise, the lowly are exalted and devour the glorious; Mordecai wakes, keeps the dream in mind, and desires until night to understand it. Unfamiliar terms

  • Nisan. The month named in the first verse, on whose first day Mordecai dreams; the chapter gives the name and no further explanation of it. Documented Chapter text, verse 1
  • Two great dragons. The dream's two great combatants, ready to fight with a great cry; what they stand for is not explained in this chapter, but chapter 17 names them as Mordecai and Aman. Documented Chapter text, verse 5; chapter 17, verse 4
  • The righteous people / the righteous nation. The people in the dream against whom all nations prepare to battle, troubled and ready to perish until they cry to God. Documented Chapter text, verses 6-8

Investigation: Mordecai's dream and the story of Esther

This section investigates a parallel; it does not conclude one. Nothing here claims a source relationship between the dream and the story, shared authorship, or a historically proven order of composition: the sources consulted for this page do not establish any of those things. Interpreted Chapter text

Parallels. The dream's two great dragons fight while all nations prepare to battle against the righteous people, the righteous nation is troubled and ready to perish, the people cry to God, a little fountain becomes a great flood, light and sun rise, and the lowly are exalted and devour the glorious. The story in chapters 3 to 9 runs a matching course: Haman against Mordecai, a decree against all the Jews, Esther's intercession, Haman hanged on his own gallows, and the Jews delivered. Chapter 17 itself supplies a mapping: the fountain become a river is Esther, the two dragons are Mordecai and Aman, and the nations are those assembled to destroy the name of the Jews. Interpreted Chapter text, Esther 11; Esther 17; Esther 3-9

Differences. The dream is symbolic throughout, dragons, flood, sun and darkness, while chapters 1 to 10 are a court narrative of decrees, banquets, a sceptre and a gallows; the dream is dated to the second year of Artexerxes and stands among the additions, chapters 11 to 17, while chapters 1 to 10 never mention the dream; and the dream's exaltation is collective, the lowly devouring the glorious, while the story's exaltation is personal, Mordecai honoured in the streets and Esther raised to queen. Interpreted Chapter text, Esther 11; Esther 1-10
