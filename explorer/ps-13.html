The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Psalms 13 (King James Version (1611) for Psalms 1–150; Charles Thomson, The Septuagint (1808) for Psalm 151)

1 How long wilt thou forget me, O LORD? for ever? how long wilt thou hide thy face from me?

2 How long shall I take counsel in my soul, having sorrow in my heart daily? how long shall mine enemy be exalted over me?

3 Consider and hear me, O LORD my God: lighten mine eyes, lest I sleep the sleep of death;

4 Lest mine enemy say, I have prevailed against him; and those that trouble me rejoice when I am moved.

5 But I have trusted in thy mercy; my heart shall rejoice in thy salvation.

6 I will sing unto the LORD, because he hath dealt bountifully with me.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Fourfold 'how long': felt desertion, inward sorrow, and an enemy's triumph, answered not by a deliverance already seen but by trust already stated, 'I have trusted in your mercy,' ending in song for bounty already received. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown; chapter text

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. How long will you forget me, Lord, for ever? How long will you hide your face? Repeated 'how long' voices the pain of felt desertion, not a charge that God has truly forgotten. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. How long shall I take counsel in my soul, with daily sorrow, while my enemy is exalted over me? Inward debate and outward triumph combine to weary the sufferer. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Consider and hear me, Lord my God; lighten my eyes, lest I sleep the sleep of death: he asks for reviving light, for despair darkens toward death. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Lest my enemy say, I have prevailed, and my troublers rejoice when I am moved: God's honour is pleaded — an enemy's boast would dishonour God's care. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. But I have trusted in your mercy, and my heart shall rejoice in your salvation: trust stated in the past tense steadies a present that still hurts. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. I will sing to the Lord because he has dealt bountifully with me: the psalm ends in song before the answer is seen, because mercy already received is ground enough for praise. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
