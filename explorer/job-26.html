The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 26 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 But Job answered and said,

2 How hast thou helped him that is without power? how savest thou the arm that hath no strength?

3 How hast thou counselled him that hath no wisdom? and how hast thou plentifully declared the thing as it is?

4 To whom hast thou uttered words? and whose spirit came from thee?

5 Dead things are formed from under the waters, and the inhabitants thereof.

6 Hell is naked before him, and destruction hath no covering.

7 He stretcheth out the north over the empty place, and hangeth the earth upon nothing.

8 He bindeth up the waters in his thick clouds; and the cloud is not rent under them.

9 He holdeth back the face of his throne, and spreadeth his cloud upon it.

10 He hath compassed the waters with bounds, until the day and night come to an end.

11 The pillars of heaven tremble and are astonished at his reproof.

12 He divideth the sea with his power, and by his understanding he smiteth through the proud.

13 By his spirit he hath garnished the heavens; his hand hath formed the crooked serpent.

14 Lo, these are parts of his ways: but how little a portion is heard of him? but the thunder of his power who can understand?

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Job answers Bildad with irony before outdoing him: how have you helped him that is without power, or saved the arm that has no strength? He then gives his own, grander account of God’s majesty — the dead tremble beneath the waters, Sheol is naked before him, he stretches the north over empty space and hangs the earth on nothing, and binds the waters in thick clouds. Yet even this, he says, is but the edges of his ways; the thunder of his power, who can understand? Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Job answers Bildad, turning Bildad's majesty speech back on him with irony. The reply first exposes the friends' unhelpfulness, then outdoes them in describing God's greatness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. How has Bildad helped him that is without power, and saved the arm that has no strength? JFB explains the negatives as designedly used for powerlessness: granting Job is powerlessness itself, how has Bildad helped such a one, with savest as supportest. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. How has Bildad counselled him that has no wisdom, and plentifully declared the thing as it is? JFB renders plentifully as abundantly wisdom, asking how Bildad's great pretensions to abundant wisdom have shown themselves. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. To whom has Bildad uttered words, and whose spirit came from him? JFB asks for whose instruction the words were meant, since Job knows God's omnipotence better than his instructor, and whose spirit is not God's but the borrowed sentiment of Eliphaz. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Dead things are formed from under the waters, and the inhabitants thereof. JFB renders it as the souls of the dead trembling, with Rephaim from a root meaning weak, hence deceased, under the waters where the dead dwell, showing God's power reaches hell as well as heaven. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Hell is naked before God, and destruction has no covering. JFB explains destruction as Abaddon, the abode of lost souls, with no covering from God's eyes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. God stretches out the north over the empty place, and hangs the earth upon nothing. JFB notes the hint of the true theory of the earth: suspension in empty space, with the north as the highest part and the northern vault as a stretched canopy, consistent with the earth's globular form. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. God binds up the waters in his thick clouds, and the cloud is not rent under them. JFB compares the clouds to airy vessels which, though light, do not burst with the weight of water in them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. God holds back the face of his throne, and spreads his cloud upon it. JFB renders it as encompassing or closing: God makes clouds a veil screening even the exterior of his throne from profane eyes, his agency everywhere yet himself invisible. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. God has compassed the waters with bounds, until the day and night come to an end. JFB renders it as drawing a circular bound round the waters, the horizon as a circle, indicating the earth's globular form, to the confines of light and darkness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. The pillars of heaven tremble and are astonished at his reproof. JFB explains pillars poetically as mountains seeming to bear up the sky, astonished from terror, with reproof as thunder reverberating from cliff to cliff. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. God divides the sea with his power, and by his understanding smites through the proud. JFB explains divides as stilling or moving, perhaps at creation or the flood's assuaging, with the proud as the sea's pride. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. By his spirit God has garnished the heavens, and his hand has formed the crooked serpent. JFB explains the spirit as breath or wind dissipating clouds to revive the heavens, and the crooked serpent as the oblique, fleeing constellation, representing the splendour of all stars. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Lo, these are parts of his ways, but how little a portion is heard of him? The thunder of his power who can understand? JFB renders parts as only the extreme boundaries, and the portion heard as a faint whisper against the fullness of thunder. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Where the speakers disagree

Bildad holds up God’s dominion and terror, and man’s uncleanness before him, as reason Job should not seek to come before God. Job answers that such majesty speech helps no one without power — and then outdoes it, describing a God before whom Sheol is naked and the earth hangs on nothing — yet insists even that is only the edges of God’s ways. They agree on God’s greatness and differ on what it means for Job’s plea. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
