The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 14 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Man that is born of a woman is of few days and full of trouble.

2 He cometh forth like a flower, and is cut down: he fleeth also as a shadow, and continueth not.

3 And doth thou open thine eyes upon such an one, and bringest me into judgment with thee?

4 Who can bring a clean thing out of an unclean? not one.

5 Seeing his days are determined, the number of his months are with thee, thou hast appointed his bounds that he cannot pass;

6 Turn from him, that he may rest, till he shall accomplish, as an hireling, his day.

7 For there is hope of a tree, if it be cut down, that it will sprout again, and that the tender branch thereof will not cease.

8 Though the root thereof wax old in the earth, and the stock thereof die in the ground;

9 Yet through the scent of water it will bud, and bring forth boughs like a plant.

10 But man dieth, and wasteth away: yea, man giveth up the ghost, and where is he?

11 As the waters fail from the sea, and the flood decayeth and drieth up:

12 So man lieth down, and riseth not: till the heavens be no more, they shall not awake, nor be raised out of their sleep.

13 O that thou wouldest hide me in the grave, that thou wouldest keep me secret, until thy wrath be past, that thou wouldest appoint me a set time, and remember me!

14 If a man die, shall he live again? all the days of my appointed time will I wait, till my change come.

15 Thou shalt call, and I will answer thee: thou wilt have a desire to the work of thine hands.

16 For now thou numberest my steps: dost thou not watch over my sin?

17 My transgression is sealed up in a bag, and thou sewest up mine iniquity.

18 And surely the mountains falling cometh to nought, and the rock is removed out of his place.

19 The waters wear the stones: thou washest away the things which grow out of the dust of the earth; and thou destroyest the hope of man.

20 Thou prevailest for ever against him, and he passeth: thou changest his countenance, and sendest him away.

21 His sons come to honour, and he knoweth it not; and they are brought low, but he perceiveth it not of them.

22 But his flesh upon him shall have pain, and his soul within him shall mourn.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Job turns from his own case to the general lot of man: born of a woman, of few days and full of trouble, he comes forth like a flower and is cut down, and flees as a shadow. A tree cut down sprouts again, but man dies and wastes away — and Job asks whether, if a man die, he shall live again, and whether God will remember him. The chapter leaves that hope as a question, not an answer. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Man born of a woman is of few days and full of trouble. JFB notes woman means feeble and, in the East, looked down upon, so man born of one so frail must be frail himself, and few days is literally short of days. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Man comes forth like a flower and is cut down, flees as a shadow and continues not. The flower and shadow give his brevity two images: beauty cut down, and a shape that does not stay. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Does God open his eyes upon such a one and bring him into judgment with himself? JFB explains open eyes not as graciousness but as sharply fixing eyes upon one so frail, asking whether he is worthy of such constant watching by the Almighty. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Who can bring a clean thing out of an unclean? Not one. JFB calls this a plea in mitigation, resting on the doctrine of original sin held from the first: man unclean from birth cannot be expected to present perfect cleanness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Seeing man's days are determined, the number of his months with God, and he has appointed bounds he cannot pass. The limits are fixed by God, not negotiable by man. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Turn from man, that he may rest, till he accomplishes as a hireling his day. JFB renders turn as turning God's eyes from jealous watching, and accomplish as enjoy: at least the hireling's measure of rest and reward. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. For there is hope of a tree, if it is cut down, that it will sprout again and its tender branch will not cease. Man may claim a peaceful life the more, JFB notes, since death separates him from it without return to the present condition — yet this does not deny a future life. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Though its root waxes old in the earth and its stock dies in the ground, yet through the scent of water it will bud and bring forth boughs like a plant. JFB notes the scent is the exhalation causing germination, and the tree is personified with volition in antithesis to man. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Yet through the scent of water it will bud, and bring forth boughs like a plant. The tree's renewal stands against man's finality in this present life. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. But man dies and wastes away, gives up the ghost, and where is he? JFB contrasts two Hebrew words: the mighty man dies, the man of earth gives up the ghost, reduced to nothing, unable to revive in the present state as the tree does. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. As waters fail from the sea and the flood decays and dries up, so man lies down and rises not. JFB explains sea as a lake or pool from a river's spreading, like the Euphrates, utterly dried up, answering to the outstretched corpse of the once living man. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. So man lies down and rises not till the heavens be no more, nor is he raised out of his sleep. JFB notes this implies only no hope of living again in the present order, not no hope in a new order, since the resurrection is preceded by the dissolution of the heavens. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Oh that God would hide Job in the grave until his wrath is past, appointing a set time and remembering him. JFB notes Job wishes to be kept hidden until God's wrath passes, with a decreed set time. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. If a man dies, shall he live again? All the days of Job's appointed time he will wait till his change comes. JFB explains the implied answer as hope of living, though not in the present order, and change as release, like a soldier relieved from duty by the guard. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. God shall call, and Job will answer; God will have a desire to the work of his hands. JFB reads this of the resurrection, and desire literally as becoming pale with anxious longing, implying God would not leave his wonderfully made creature in oblivion. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. For now God numbers Job's steps, but does not watch over his sin. JFB renders it as a future hope: God will number his steps to guard him from wandering, not jealously watch over his sin as now. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. Job's transgression is sealed up in a bag, and God sews up his iniquity. JFB explains sealed purses in the East, and sewing as covering: to cover sins is to forgive them completely and think of them no more. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. And surely the mountain falling comes to naught, and the rock is removed out of its place. JFB notes the turn back to gloomy bodings: even the solid mountain fades, so man cannot hope to escape decay in the present world. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. The waters wear the stones, and floods wash away the dust of the earth, and God destroys the hope of man. JFB traces the gradation from mountains to rocks to stones to dust, until the solid mountain disappears utterly. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. God prevails forever against man, passes him, changes his countenance, and sends him away. JFB explains prevail as overpowering by superior strength, passeth as dying, and changed countenance as the visage altered at death. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. Job's sons come to honour and he knows it not, or are brought low and he perceives it not. JFB selects the severance of parents and children as the striking trait of the dead's separation from all that passes in the world. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. But his flesh upon him shall have pain, and his soul within him shall mourn. JFB notes flesh and soul describe the whole man, and that in his gloomy frame Job anticipates the dead limited to thought of their own misery, with pain attributed by personification to the dead. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
