Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — 2 Samuel 1 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Now it came to pass after the death of Saul, when David was returned from the slaughter of the Amalekites, and David had abode two days in Ziklag;

2 It came even to pass on the third day, that, behold, a man came out of the camp from Saul with his clothes rent, and earth upon his head: and so it was, when he came to David, that he fell to the earth, and did obeisance.

3 And David said unto him, From whence comest thou? And he said unto him, Out of the camp of Israel am I escaped.

4 And David said unto him, How went the matter? I pray thee, tell me. And he answered, That the people are fled from the battle, and many of the people also are fallen and dead; and Saul and Jonathan his son are dead also.

5 And David said unto the young man that told him, How knowest thou that Saul and Jonathan his son be dead?

6 And the young man that told him said, As I happened by chance upon mount Gilboa, behold, Saul leaned upon his spear; and, lo, the chariots and horsemen followed hard after him.

7 And when he looked behind him, he saw me, and called unto me. And I answered, Here am I.

8 And he said unto me, Who art thou? And I answered him, I am an Amalekite.

9 He said unto me again, Stand, I pray thee, upon me, and slay me: for anguish is come upon me, because my life is yet whole in me.

10 So I stood upon him, and slew him, because I was sure that he could not live after that he was fallen: and I took the crown that was upon his head, and the bracelet that was on his arm, and have brought them hither unto my lord.

11 Then David took hold on his clothes, and rent them; and likewise all the men that were with him:

12 And they mourned, and wept, and fasted until even, for Saul, and for Jonathan his son, and for the people of the LORD, and for the house of Israel; because they were fallen by the sword.

13 And David said unto the young man that told him, Whence art thou? And he answered, I am the son of a stranger, an Amalekite.

14 And David said unto him, How wast thou not afraid to stretch forth thine hand to destroy the LORD’s anointed?

15 And David called one of the young men, and said, Go near, and fall upon him. And he smote him that he died.

16 And David said unto him, Thy blood be upon thy head; for thy mouth hath testified against thee, saying, I have slain the LORD’s anointed.

17 And David lamented with this lamentation over Saul and over Jonathan his son:

18 (Also he bade them teach the children of Judah the use of the bow: behold, it is written in the book of Jasher.)

19 The beauty of Israel is slain upon thy high places: how are the mighty fallen!

20 Tell it not in Gath, publish it not in the streets of Askelon; lest the daughters of the Philistines rejoice, lest the daughters of the uncircumcised triumph.

21 Ye mountains of Gilboa, let there be no dew, neither let there be rain, upon you, nor fields of offerings: for there the shield of the mighty is vilely cast away, the shield of Saul, as though he had not been anointed with oil.

22 From the blood of the slain, from the fat of the mighty, the bow of Jonathan turned not back, and the sword of Saul returned not empty.

23 Saul and Jonathan were lovely and pleasant in their lives, and in their death they were not divided: they were swifter than eagles, they were stronger than lions.

24 Ye daughters of Israel, weep over Saul, who clothed you in scarlet, with other delights, who put on ornaments of gold upon your apparel.

25 How are the mighty fallen in the midst of the battle! O Jonathan, thou wast slain in thine high places.

26 I am distressed for thee, my brother Jonathan: very pleasant hast thou been unto me: thy love to me was wonderful, passing the love of women.

27 How are the mighty fallen, and the weapons of war perished!

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

News of Saul and Jonathan's deaths reaches David at Ziklag, brought by an Amalekite who claims he finished Saul off and carries the crown and bracelet as proof. David has the man executed for striking the LORD's anointed, then leads his men in mourning and sings the lament known as the Song of the Bow. Interpreted Plain-language summary of the chapter text and its study notes

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. The blow that opened David's way to the throne came while he was himself sorely distressed at Ziklag. Henry notes that those who commit their concerns to the Lord will quietly abide his will rather than grasp at advancement. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. The messenger arrives in torn clothes with earth on his head, the outward marks of defeat and mourning. His obeisance shows he comes to David as the man now looked to for leadership. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. David asks where the man comes from, and learns he escaped from Israel's camp. JFB observes that David's eager question sprang from generous patriotism, not from ambition for the throne. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. The report is unrelieved disaster: the army fled, many fell, and Saul and Jonathan are dead. David hears in one sentence both national defeat and personal loss. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. David presses the messenger for how he knows this, testing the story before he acts on it. His caution shows he desired not Saul's death and was not impatient to take the throne. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. The young man claims he chanced upon Gilboa and found Saul leaning on his spear with chariots closing in. JFB warns this tale is a fiction invented to ingratiate himself with David, since the inspired account of Saul's death stands elsewhere. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Saul, looking behind him, calls the stranger, who answers that he is at hand. The invented detail is meant to make the messenger an eyewitness and so win David's trust. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. The man names himself an Amalekite, a people Israel was at war with and David had just fought. His identity makes his claim to have touched the king all the more perilous. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. In the man's story Saul begs to be slain because anguish has come upon him while life remains. The plea is framed to make the killing look like mercy rather than murder. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. The man says he slew Saul and brought the crown and bracelet as proof. JFB explains the crown as a small metallic cap or wreath and the bracelet as an armlet, both marks of royal dignity. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. David tears his clothes, and all his men do likewise, in sincere mourning. Henry stresses that David was sincere in grieving for Saul, not merely performing grief for advantage. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. They mourn, weep, and fast until evening for Saul, Jonathan, the people of the Lord, and Israel. Their grief humbles them under God's heavy hand on the nation, not only over two men. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. David formally asks the young man who he is, and he answers that he is the son of a stranger, an Amalekite. JFB notes the question is now put judicially, as the opening of a trial. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. David asks how the man was not afraid to destroy the Lord's anointed. His reverence for Saul as God's anointed had governed him through great temptation, and he will not excuse another for violating it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. David orders one of his young men to fall upon the Amalekite, and he is slain. Public justice is done without regard to David's own private interest in the dead king's crown. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. David declares the man's blood is on his own head, because his own mouth testified that he slew the Lord's anointed. If he did as he said, he deserved death for treason; if he lied, his lie proved against himself. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. David then composes a lamentation over Saul and Jonathan. JFB notes it was customary in the East to celebrate great kings and warriors in funeral songs, and this elegy became a national song. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. David orders the children of Judah to be taught the use of the bow, as recorded in the book of Jasher. JFB reads this as David remedying Israel's inferiority in archery, which had helped cause the disaster, and Henry takes the song's title to be "the bow." Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. The song opens calling Israel's beauty slain on the high places, with the chorus "how are the mighty fallen." JFB explains "the beauty of Israel" as the gazelle or antelope, the Eastern type of beauty and graceful form. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. David forbids publishing the news in Gath or Askelon, lest Philistine daughters rejoice. The grief of God's people, Henry notes, is sharpened by the triumph it gives their enemies. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. David curses Gilboa with no dew or rain, because there the shield of the mighty was vilely cast away. JFB observes that casting away the shield was counted a national disgrace, and the mountains of Gilboa remain naked and sterile. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Jonathan's bow never turned back and Saul's sword never returned empty, praising their unflinching warfare. David commends Saul only for what he truly was, saying nothing of piety or goodness he did not have. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. Saul and Jonathan, lovely and pleasant in life, were undivided in death, swifter than eagles and stronger than lions. Henry notes Jonathan was a dutiful son and Saul an affectionate father, dear to each other. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. The daughters of Israel are called to weep over Saul, who clothed them in scarlet and gold. JFB notes the Eastern fondness for bright dress and ornaments, and that David celebrates Saul's real good qualities alongside Jonathan's. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. The chorus returns over Jonathan himself, slain in his high places. The burden of the poem, JFB observes, is the praise of Jonathan, with which it begins and ends. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. David calls Jonathan his brother and his love wonderful, passing the love of women. Henry observes that next to the love between Christ and his people, the affection springing from it produces the strongest friendship. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. The lament closes as it opened: the mighty are fallen and the weapons of war perished. The repeated chorus fixes the nation's loss in memory rather than softening it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise), Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
