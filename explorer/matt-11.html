The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Matthew 11 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 And it came to pass, when Jesus had made an end of commanding his twelve disciples, he departed thence to teach and to preach in their cities.

2 Now when John had heard in the prison the works of Christ, he sent two of his disciples,

3 And said unto him, Art thou he that should come, or do we look for another?

4 Jesus answered and said unto them, Go and shew John again those things which ye do hear and see:

5 The blind receive their sight, and the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, and the deaf hear, the dead are raised up, and the poor have the gospel preached to them.

6 And blessed is he, whosoever shall not be offended in me.

7 And as they departed, Jesus began to say unto the multitudes concerning John, What went ye out into the wilderness to see? A reed shaken with the wind?

8 But what went ye out for to see? A man clothed in soft raiment? behold, they that wear soft clothing are in kings’ houses.

9 But what went ye out for to see? A prophet? yea, I say unto you, and more than a prophet.

10 For this is he, of whom it is written, Behold, I send my messenger before thy face, which shall prepare thy way before thee.

11 Verily I say unto you, Among them that are born of women there hath not risen a greater than John the Baptist: notwithstanding he that is least in the kingdom of heaven is greater than he.

12 And from the days of John the Baptist until now the kingdom of heaven suffereth violence, and the violent take it by force.

13 For all the prophets and the law prophesied until John.

14 And if ye will receive it, this is Elias, which was for to come.

15 He that hath ears to hear, let him hear.

16 But whereunto shall I liken this generation? It is like unto children sitting in the markets, and calling unto their fellows,

17 And saying, We have piped unto you, and ye have not danced; we have mourned unto you, and ye have not lamented.

18 For John came neither eating nor drinking, and they say, He hath a devil.

19 The Son of man came eating and drinking, and they say, Behold a man gluttonous, and a winebibber, a friend of publicans and sinners. But wisdom is justified of her children.

20 Then began he to upbraid the cities wherein most of his mighty works were done, because they repented not:

21 Woe unto thee, Chorazin! woe unto thee, Bethsaida! for if the mighty works, which were done in you, had been done in Tyre and Sidon, they would have repented long ago in sackcloth and ashes.

22 But I say unto you, It shall be more tolerable for Tyre and Sidon at the day of judgment, than for you.

23 And thou, Capernaum, which art exalted unto heaven, shalt be brought down to hell: for if the mighty works, which have been done in thee, had been done in Sodom, it would have remained until this day.

24 But I say unto you, That it shall be more tolerable for the land of Sodom in the day of judgment, than for thee.

25 At that time Jesus answered and said, I thank thee, O Father, Lord of heaven and earth, because thou hast hid these things from the wise and prudent, and hast revealed them unto babes.

26 Even so, Father: for so it seemed good in thy sight.

27 All things are delivered unto me of my Father: and no man knoweth the Son, but the Father; neither knoweth any man the Father, save the Son, and he to whomsoever the Son will reveal him.

28 Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest.

29 Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart: and ye shall find rest unto your souls.

30 For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light.

How to read the labels

Every substantive historical claim in the editorial panels below carries one of three labels, plus a source reference:

  • Documented — directly supported by a cited source.
  • Interpreted — a reasoned explanation or proposed relationship.
  • Unresolved — evidence is insufficient to establish the claim.

A claim does NOT become Documented merely because a commentary states it. Commentary views are attributed to their authors and labeled Interpreted unless a primary textual source directly supports the claim. Where evidence runs out, the page says Unresolved and stops — it does not fill the gap.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

John the Baptist, in prison, sends two disciples to ask whether Jesus is he that should come, and Jesus answers by pointing to what they hear and see: blind see, lame walk, lepers cleansed, deaf hear, dead raised, and the poor have the gospel preached. Jesus then praises John as more than a prophet, the Elias who was to come, pronounces woe on Chorazin, Bethsaida and Capernaum for unbelief, and closes with thanks to the Father and the invitation to the weary: take my yoke, for my yoke is easy and my burden light. What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. When Jesus had made an end of commanding his twelve disciples, he departed thence to teach and to preach in their cities — scarcely a fourth circuit, but visits to places not reached or too rapidly passed, filling the time till the Twelve returned. Our Divine Redeemer never was weary of his labour of love, and we should not be weary of well-doing, for in due season we shall reap if we faint not. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. When John had heard in the prison the works of Christ, he sent two of his disciples. For the account of this imprisonment, see Mark 6:17-20; JFB treats the whole passage at Luke 7:18-35, so the notes that follow rest on Henry's concise exposition of Matthew's text. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Art thou he that should come, or do we look for another? Some think John sent for his own satisfaction: where there is true faith there may yet be a mixture of unbelief, and the remaining unbelief of good men may in an hour of temptation call in question the most important truths. Others think he sent his disciples for their satisfaction, that their faith might be strengthened and confirmed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 4. Jesus answered and said unto them, Go and shew John again those things which ye do hear and see. Christ points them to what they heard and saw, that things seen and heard, compared with the Scriptures, direct in what way salvation is to be found. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 5. The blind receive their sight, and the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, and the deaf hear, the dead are raised up, and the poor have the gospel preached to them. Christ's gracious condescensions and compassions to the poor show it was he that should bring to the world the tender mercies of our God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 6. Blessed is he, whosoever shall not be offended in me. It is difficult to conquer prejudices, and dangerous not to conquer them; but those who believe in Christ, their faith will be found so much the more to praise, honour and glory. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 7. As they departed, Jesus began to say unto the multitudes concerning John, What went ye out into the wilderness to see? A reed shaken with the wind? What Christ said concerning John was not only for his praise but for the people's profit; those who attend on the word will be called to give account of their improvements, and when the sermon is done, the greatest of the care begins. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 8. But what went ye out for to see? A man clothed in soft raiment? Behold, they that wear soft clothing are in kings' houses. John was a self-denying man, dead to all pomps of the world and pleasures of sense; it becomes people, in all appearances, to be consistent with their character and situation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 9. But what went ye out for to see? A prophet? yea, I say unto you, and more than a prophet. John was a great and good man, yet not perfect; therefore he came short of glorified saints, but as forerunner he stands above the prophets, the connecting link between the old and new economies. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 10. For this is he, of whom it is written, Behold, I send my messenger before thy face, which shall prepare thy way before thee. The prophecy of Malachi is fulfilled in John, the messenger preparing the way of the Lord. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 11. Verily I say unto you, Among them that are born of women there hath not risen a greater than John the Baptist: notwithstanding he that is least in the kingdom of heaven is greater than he. The least in heaven knows, loves and does more in praising God and receives more from him than the greatest in this world; but by the kingdom of heaven here is rather meant the kingdom of grace, the gospel dispensation in its power and purity, under whose advantages our lot is thankfully cast. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 12. From the days of John the Baptist until now the kingdom of heaven suffereth violence, and the violent take it by force. Multitudes were wrought upon by John's ministry and became his disciples, and those strove for a place in the kingdom that one would think had no right or title to it; it shows what fervency and zeal are required of all, for those who will have an interest in the great salvation will have it upon any terms. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 13. For all the prophets and the law prophesied until John. Self must be denied, and the bent, frame and temper of the mind altered; God requires no more from us than the right use of the faculties he has given us, and people are ignorant because they will not learn. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 14. And if ye will receive it, this is Elias, which was for to come. John came in the spirit and power of Elijah, as the predicted forerunner, if they would receive the identification. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 15. He that hath ears to hear, let him hear. The things of God are of great and common concern, and hearing aright is the duty laid on every hearer. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 16. But whereunto shall I liken this generation? It is like unto children sitting in the markets, and calling unto their fellows. Christ reflects on the scribes and Pharisees, who had a proud conceit of themselves, likening their behaviour to children's play, out of temper without reason, quarrelling with all attempts of their fellows to please them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 17. And saying, We have piped unto you, and ye have not danced; we have mourned unto you, and ye have not lamented. The cavils of worldly men are often very trifling and show great malice; something they have to urge against every one, however excellent and holy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 18. For John came neither eating nor drinking, and they say, He hath a devil. The austere forerunner, self-denying and dead to sense, was charged with demonic possession by those who would receive neither his mourning nor Christ's piping. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 19. The Son of man came eating and drinking, and they say, Behold a man gluttonous, and a winebibber, a friend of publicans and sinners. But wisdom is justified of her children. Christ, undefiled and separate from sinners, is here represented as in league with them and polluted by them; the most unspotted innocence will not always be a defence against reproach, but wisdom is vindicated by those who receive her. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 20. Then began he to upbraid the cities wherein most of his mighty works were done, because they repented not. This outburst of feeling was suggested by the result of his labours in Galilee, where the most favoured region responded with impenitence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. Woe unto thee, Chorazin! woe unto thee, Bethsaida! for if the mighty works, which were done in you, had been done in Tyre and Sidon, they would have repented long ago in sackcloth and ashes. Chorazin, not elsewhere mentioned, must have lain near Capernaum; Bethsaida, “fishing-house” on the western side of the lake, birthplace of Andrew, Peter and Philip. The two cities denote the whole region favoured above every other with his presence, teaching and works; Tyre and Sidon, wealthy commercial cities whose luxury bred irreligion, had done less violence to conscience and so were less criminal. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. But I say unto you, It shall be more tolerable for Tyre and Sidon at the day of judgment, than for you. Christ knew the Jews' hearts were more bitter and hardened against his miracles and doctrines than Tyre and Sidon's would have been, therefore their condemnation would be greater; yet the Lord punishes none more than they deserve, and never withholds truth from those who long after it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. And thou, Capernaum, which art exalted unto heaven, shalt be brought down to hell: for if the mighty works, which have been done in thee, had been done in Sodom, it would have remained until this day. Capernaum, where Jesus had his stated abode through his Galilean public life, was the most favoured spot on earth, the most exalted in privilege; Sodom, destroyed for its pollutions, had done no such violence to conscience and incurred speakably less guilt. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. But I say unto you, That it shall be more tolerable for the land of Sodom in the day of judgment, than for thee. Stanley notes it has been more tolerable even in earthly judgment for Sodom, whose name and perhaps remains are still found by the Dead Sea, while Capernaum's name on the lake has been utterly lost; but the judgment here spoken of is still future, on responsible inhabitants, final and irretrievable. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. At that time Jesus answered and said, I thank thee, O Father, Lord of heaven and earth, because thou hast hid these things from the wise and prudent, and hast revealed them unto babes. “Answered” refers to the melancholy results just lamented: yes, but there is a brighter side — the pride of rejecters' hearts blinded them, while thirsting souls drew water with joy from salvation's wells. “I thank thee” conveys full, cordial concurrence, adoring acquiescence in the divine procedure. The wise are men proud of speculative attainments, the prudent men of worldly shrewdness; to bring our own wisdom to questions of recovery is impertinent, and if truth be hid from us it is fitting retribution. Babes are men of unassuming docility, conscious they know nothing, determined simply to hear what God the Lord will speak. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. Even so, Father: for so it seemed good in thy sight. This is the emphatic term for any object of divine complacency, whether Christ himself or God's gracious eternal arrangements; a sublime echo, as if Jesus paused to reflect on the glory of God's absolute will that so it should be. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. All things are delivered unto me of my Father: and no man knoweth the Son, but the Father; neither knoweth any man the Father, save the Son, and he to whomsoever the Son will reveal him. “Delivered over” means the whole administration of the kingdom of grace, including all power necessary to execute it. That Father and Son are mutually and exclusively known to each other is a claim to equality with the Father beyond which nothing higher can be conceived; yet this sovereignty of gracious will is revealed not to paralyse souls in despair but to enhance the glory of grace once imparted, as what follows shows. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. Incomparable, ravishing sounds in this weary, groaning world: “Hither to Me”; in “all ye that toil and are burdened” the universal wretchedness of man is depicted in both its forms, active and passive. In some senses all men are so: worldly men burdened with fruitless cares for wealth and honours, the gay and sensual labouring after pleasures, the slave of Satan and his lusts the merest drudge, those labouring to establish their own righteousness labouring in vain, the convinced sinner heavy-laden with guilt and terror, the tempted believer with labours and burdens. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart: and ye shall find rest unto your souls. As Christ's willingness to empty himself to the uttermost of his Father's requirements was the spring of ineffable repose to his own Spirit, so in the same track he invites all to follow him with assurance of the same experience. In coming they must take his yoke and submit to his authority, learning of him all things as to comfort and obedience; he accepts the willing servant, however imperfect the services. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light. Matchless paradox: the rest the soul experiences once safe under Christ's wing makes all yokes easy, all burdens light. His commandments are holy, just and good; the yoke requires self-denial and exposes to difficulties, but is abundantly repaid even in this world by inward peace and joy — a yoke lined with love, and the way of duty is the way of rest. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

  • Verse 18. For John came neither eating nor drinking, and they say, He hath a devil. The austere forerunner, self-denying and dead to sense, was charged with demonic possession by those who would receive neither his mourning nor Christ's piping. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 19. The Son of man came eating and drinking, and they say, Behold a man gluttonous, and a winebibber, a friend of publicans and sinners. But wisdom is justified of her children. Christ, undefiled and separate from sinners, is here represented as in league with them and polluted by them; the most unspotted innocence will not always be a defence against reproach, but wisdom is vindicated by those who receive her. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry

Where the notes differ

  • Why did John send his disciples (verse 3)? Some think John sent for his own satisfaction, because even true faith may be mixed with unbelief in an hour of temptation. Others think he sent his disciples for their satisfaction, that their faith might be strengthened and confirmed. The note records both readings without settling between them. Investigation (editorial — a parallel question, not a conclusion)

Parallel. John's imprisonment and his question from prison are treated in Matthew, in Mark 6:17-20, and in Luke 7:18-35, as the note itself directs the reader. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Difference. JFB treats the whole passage at Luke 7:18-35, so the notes that follow in this chapter rest on Henry's concise exposition of Matthew's text. The chapter therefore does not present a single harmonised account; the Mark and Luke treatments are pointed to, not merged.
