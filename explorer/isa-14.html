The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Isaiah 14 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 For the LORD will have mercy on Jacob, and will yet choose Israel, and set them in their own land: and the strangers shall be joined with them, and they shall cleave to the house of Jacob.

2 And the people shall take them, and bring them to their place: and the house of Israel shall possess them in the land of the LORD for servants and handmaids: and they shall take them captives, whose captives they were; and they shall rule over their oppressors.

3 And it shall come to pass in the day that the LORD shall give thee rest from thy sorrow, and from thy fear, and from the hard bondage wherein thou wast made to serve,

4 That thou shalt take up this proverb against the king of Babylon, and say, How hath the oppressor ceased! the golden city ceased!

5 The LORD hath broken the staff of the wicked, and the sceptre of the rulers.

6 He who smote the people in wrath with a continual stroke, he that ruled the nations in anger, is persecuted, and none hindereth.

7 The whole earth is at rest, and is quiet: they break forth into singing.

8 Yea, the fir trees rejoice at thee, and the cedars of Lebanon, saying, Since thou art laid down, no feller is come up against us.

9 Hell from beneath is moved for thee to meet thee at thy coming: it stirreth up the dead for thee, even all the chief ones of the earth; it hath raised up from their thrones all the kings of the nations.

10 All they shall speak and say unto thee, Art thou also become weak as we? art thou become like unto us?

11 Thy pomp is brought down to the grave, and the noise of thy viols: the worm is spread under thee, and the worms cover thee.

12 How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning! how art thou cut down to the ground, which didst weaken the nations!

13 For thou hast said in thine heart, I will ascend into heaven, I will exalt my throne above the stars of God: I will sit also upon the mount of the congregation, in the sides of the north:

14 I will ascend above the heights of the clouds; I will be like the most High.

15 Yet thou shalt be brought down to hell, to the sides of the pit.

16 They that see thee shall narrowly look upon thee, and consider thee, saying, Is this the man that made the earth to tremble, that did shake kingdoms;

17 That made the world as a wilderness, and destroyed the cities thereof; that opened not the house of his prisoners?

18 All the kings of the nations, even all of them, lie in glory, every one in his own house.

19 But thou art cast out of thy grave like an abominable branch, and as the raiment of those that are slain, thrust through with a sword, that go down to the stones of the pit; as a carcase trodden under feet.

20 Thou shalt not be joined with them in burial, because thou hast destroyed thy land, and slain thy people: the seed of evildoers shall never be renowned.

21 Prepare slaughter for his children for the iniquity of their fathers; that they do not rise, nor possess the land, nor fill the face of the world with cities.

22 For I will rise up against them, saith the LORD of hosts, and cut off from Babylon the name, and remnant, and son, and nephew, saith the LORD.

23 I will also make it a possession for the bittern, and pools of water: and I will sweep it with the besom of destruction, saith the LORD of hosts.

24 The LORD of hosts hath sworn, saying, Surely as I have thought, so shall it come to pass; and as I have purposed, so shall it stand:

25 That I will break the Assyrian in my land, and upon my mountains tread him under foot: then shall his yoke depart from off them, and his burden depart from off their shoulders.

26 This is the purpose that is purposed upon the whole earth: and this is the hand that is stretched out upon all the nations.

27 For the LORD of hosts hath purposed, and who shall disannul it? and his hand is stretched out, and who shall turn it back?

28 In the year that king Ahaz died was this burden.

29 Rejoice not thou, whole Palestina, because the rod of him that smote thee is broken: for out of the serpent’s root shall come forth a cockatrice, and his fruit shall be a fiery flying serpent.

30 And the firstborn of the poor shall feed, and the needy shall lie down in safety: and I will kill thy root with famine, and he shall slay thy remnant.

31 Howl, O gate; cry, O city; thou, whole Palestina, art dissolved: for there shall come from the north a smoke, and none shall be alone in his appointed times.

32 What shall one then answer the messengers of the nation? That the LORD hath founded Zion, and the poor of his people shall trust in it.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

After Babylon falls, Israel is restored to its own land and strangers join the house of Jacob. The chapter sings a taunt over the fallen king of Babylon, who boasted of ascending to heaven and now lies unburied, then turns to the breaking of Assyria's yoke and the ruin of Philistia, ending with the answer to the nations: the Lord has founded Zion, and the poor of his people trust in it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. For the Lord will have mercy on Jacob and will yet choose Israel, setting them in their own land, and strangers will be joined with them and cleave to the house of Jacob. Their restoration is grounded on election, a deliberate predilection, and the strangers are proselytes — an earnest of the future effect on the heathen world of the Jews' spiritual restoration. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. The people will take them and bring them to their place, and the house of Israel will possess them in the land of the Lord for servants and handmaids, and they will take captive those whose captives they were and rule over their oppressors. The people are primarily the Babylonians and ultimately the whole Gentile world, their place is Judea, and the captivity is won not by physical but by moral might, the force of love and regard for Israel's God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. In the day the Lord gives Israel rest from sorrow and fear and the hard bondage in which they were made to serve, this rest will be the promised rest of the prophets. The verse closes the certainty of deliverance from Babylon in verses 1–3. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Then Israel will take up this proverb — a taunting song of triumph, as Orientals embodied thoughts in weighty, figurative gnomes — against the king of Babylon: How has the oppressor ceased, and the golden city ceased! The king is the ideal representative of Babylon, perhaps Belshazzar, with mystical Babylon ultimately meant, and the golden city is rather the exactress of gold. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. The Lord has broken the staff of the wicked and the sceptre of the rulers. The staff is not the sceptre but the staff with which one strikes others, since more tyrants than one are spoken of, and the rulers are tyrants, as the parallelism with the wicked proves. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. He who smote the people in wrath with a continual stroke, and ruled the nations in anger, is persecuted, and none hinders. The Hebrew is rather active: he who persecuted them without any to hinder him is now himself pursued, and the peoples subjected to Babylon are the ones he smote. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. The whole earth is at rest and quiet, and they break forth into singing. The once-subject nations of the whole earth, freed from the oppressor, rejoice in the quiet that follows his fall. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Yes, the fir trees rejoice at you, and the cedars of Lebanon, saying: Since you are laid down, no feller has come up against us. The fir trees, now left undisturbed, rejoice at his fall, for no feller comes as formerly when he was in power and felled Lebanon's cedars. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Hell from beneath is moved for you to meet you at your coming, stirring up the dead for you, even all the chief ones of the earth, and raising all the kings of the nations from their thrones. The scene changes from earth to hell, Hades the unseen abode of the departed, whose tenants, once mighty monarchs, rise in astonishment at the humbled king of Babylon's descent among them; the chief ones are literally goats, rams and leaders of the flock, princes, with gigantic wickedness included. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. All of them will speak and say to you: Are you also become weak as we? Are you become like unto us? They taunt him and derive from his calamity consolation under their own, for he is now weak as a shade bereft of blood and life. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Your pomp is brought down to the grave, and the noise of your viols; the worm is spread under you, and the worms cover you. Pomp and music, the accompaniment of Babylon's former feastings, give place to the corruption and stillness of the grave, and instead of a crimson coverlet over him there are worms, and instead of a gorgeous couch the maggot under him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. How are you fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning! How are you cut down to the ground, who did weaken the nations! The language applies to the Babylonian king primarily and at the same time shadows forth the great final enemy, the man of sin, who alone will exhaustively fulfil all its lineaments; Lucifer, day star, is a title truly belonging to Christ, the bright and morning star, and therefore hereafter to be assumed by Antichrist, and to weaken means to prostrate and discomfit. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. For you have said in your heart: I will ascend into heaven, I will exalt my throne above the stars of God, I will sit also upon the mount of the congregation, in the sides of the north. The stars express earthly potentates and heavenly principalities, and the mount of the congregation is the place of solemn meeting between God and his people in the temple at Jerusalem — or, in the Babylonian king's own terms, the northern mountain fabled as the common meeting-place of his gods, in the extreme parts of the north. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. I will ascend above the heights of the clouds, I will be like the Most High. As the Shekinah cloud was connected with the temple, this claim answers to Antichrist sitting as God in the temple of God, seated in Babylon, literal and spiritual, to which Isaiah refers. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Yet you will be brought down to hell, to the sides of the pit — antithetical to the sides of the north. He who said "I will ascend into heaven" falls to Sheol, to the sides of the sepulchre round which the dead were arranged in niches, or the innermost parts of the sepulchre. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Those who see you will narrowly look upon you and consider you, saying: Is this the man that made the earth to tremble, that did shake kingdoms? Passers-by contemplate with astonishment the body of the king cast out instead of lying in a splendid mausoleum, and can hardly believe their senses that it is he, looking narrowly to be certain they are not mistaken. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. Is this he who made the world as a wilderness and destroyed its cities, who opened not the house of his prisoners? He made the world a wilderness and destroyed its cities, and did not let his captives loose homewards, keeping them shut in prison instead of releasing them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. All the kings of the nations, even all of them, lie in glory, every one in his own house. This is the usual practice: kings lie in grand mausoleums, their sepulchres, whereas the Babylonian king will be excluded from the family sepulchre, a mark of infamy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. But you are cast out of your grave like an abominable branch, and as the raiment of those slain, thrust through with a sword, that go down to the stones of the pit, as a carcass trodden under foot. He is cast out without a grave such as might have been expected, like a useless sucker cut away by the husbandman, covered with the slain as with a garment of gore, unlike those buried in sepulchres excavated amidst stones. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. You will not be joined with them in burial, because you have destroyed your land and slain your people; the seed of evildoers will never be renowned. Whereas the princes slain with him will be buried, he will not, for Belshazzar oppressed his land with wars and tyranny and was much hated, and his seed will not be named forever: the Babylonian dynasty ends with him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. Prepare slaughter for his children for the iniquity of their fathers, that they do not rise, nor possess the land, nor fill the face of the world with cities. The charge goes to the Medes and Persians as if they were God's conscious instruments, against Belshazzar's children, lest they occupy their fathers' places and inundate the world with armies, or fill the land with such proud cities as Babylon was. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. For God will rise up against them, says the Lord of hosts, and cut off from Babylon the name, and remnant, and son, and nephew. Against the family of Babylon's king, God will cut off the name — all male representatives so the name becomes extinct — and the remnant, all that is left, so the dynasty ceases. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. God will make Babylon a possession for the bittern, and pools of water, and sweep it with the besom of destruction, says the Lord of hosts. The bittern is rather the hedgehog, found in enormous numbers in the Euphrates islands, the pools come from Cyrus turning the Euphrates over the country, and the besom is a sweep-net that leaves nothing behind. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. The Lord of hosts has sworn, saying: Surely as I have thought, so will it come to pass, and as I have purposed, so will it stand. This fragment on the destruction of the Assyrians under Sennacherib comforts the Jews in Babylon as a pledge: he who with such ease overthrew the Assyrian could likewise effect his purpose as to Babylon, and the Lord's thought stands against the Assyrians' thoughts. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. God will break the Assyrian in his land, and upon his mountains tread him under foot; then his yoke will depart from off them, and his burden depart from off their shoulders. The purpose is that Sennacherib's army, destroyed on the mountains near Jerusalem, will no longer yoke Judah, for God regards Judah as peculiarly his. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. This is the purpose purposed upon the whole earth, and this is the hand stretched out upon all the nations. The purpose embraces the present world of all ages in its scope, of which Babylon and Assyria, the then representatives of world power, are but a part, and the hand is stretched out in punishment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. For the Lord of hosts has purposed, and who will disannul it? And his hand is stretched out, and who will turn it back? No one can annul God's purpose or turn back his stretched-out hand, as Daniel declares that none can stay his hand. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. In the year that King Ahaz died was this burden. This prophecy against Philistia, given in 726 B.C., comforts the Jews lest they fear that people, for the Philistines, subdued by Uzziah, had taken several towns in south Judea under Ahaz, and Isaiah now denounces their final subjugation by Hezekiah. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. Rejoice not, whole Palestina, because the rod of him that smote you is broken; for out of the serpent's root will come forth a cockatrice, and his fruit will be a fiery flying serpent. Palestina is literally the land of sojourners; the broken rod is Uzziah's yoke thrown off under Ahaz, and though Uzziah as a biting serpent is gone, a more deadly viper awaits Philistia in Hezekiah. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. The firstborn of the poor will feed, and the needy will lie down in safety, and God will kill your root with famine, and he will slay your remnant. The firstborn of the poor means the most abject poor, as firstborn of death means the most fatal death; the Jews, heretofore exposed to Philistine invasions, will feed in safety like a flock, while Philistia's root is radically destroyed by famine. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 31. Howl, O gate; cry, O city; you, whole Palestina, are dissolved, for there will come from the north a smoke, and none will be alone in his appointed times. The gate is those who throng the gate, the chief place of concourse; the smoke is from the north, Judea north and east of Palestine, whether the Jews' signal-fire or the region devastated by fire, and there will not be a straggler among the enemy's levies — the Jewish host will advance in close array, none lagging from weariness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 32. What will one then answer the messengers of the nation? That the Lord has founded Zion, and the poor of his people will trust in it. The answer to the nations' messengers is that Zion is founded by the Lord himself, and the poor, the humble remnant, will find refuge and trust there. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

  • Verse 28. In the year that King Ahaz died was this burden. This prophecy against Philistia, given in 726 B.C., comforts the Jews lest they fear that people, for the Philistines, subdued by Uzziah, had taken several towns in south Judea under Ahaz, and Isaiah now denounces their final subjugation by Hezekiah. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. Rejoice not, whole Palestina, because the rod of him that smote you is broken; for out of the serpent's root will come forth a cockatrice, and his fruit will be a fiery flying serpent. Palestina is literally the land of sojourners; the broken rod is Uzziah's yoke thrown off under Ahaz, and though Uzziah as a biting serpent is gone, a more deadly viper awaits Philistia in Hezekiah. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. The firstborn of the poor will feed, and the needy will lie down in safety, and God will kill your root with famine, and he will slay your remnant. The firstborn of the poor means the most abject poor, as firstborn of death means the most fatal death; the Jews, heretofore exposed to Philistine invasions, will feed in safety like a flock, while Philistia's root is radically destroyed by famine. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 31. Howl, O gate; cry, O city; you, whole Palestina, are dissolved, for there will come from the north a smoke, and none will be alone in his appointed times. The gate is those who throng the gate, the chief place of concourse; the smoke is from the north, Judea north and east of Palestine, whether the Jews' signal-fire or the region devastated by fire, and there will not be a straggler among the enemy's levies — the Jewish host will advance in close array, none lagging from weariness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 32. What will one then answer the messengers of the nation? That the Lord has founded Zion, and the poor of his people will trust in it. The answer to the nations' messengers is that Zion is founded by the Lord himself, and the poor, the humble remnant, will find refuge and trust there. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
