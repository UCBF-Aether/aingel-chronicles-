The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Jeremiah 46 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 The word of the LORD which came to Jeremiah the prophet against the Gentiles;

2 Against Egypt, against the army of Pharaohnecho king of Egypt, which was by the river Euphrates in Carchemish, which Nebuchadrezzar king of Babylon smote in the fourth year of Jehoiakim the son of Josiah king of Judah.

3 Order ye the buckler and shield, and draw near to battle.

4 Harness the horses; and get up, ye horsemen, and stand forth with your helmets; furbish the spears, and put on the brigandines.

5 Wherefore have I seen them dismayed and turned away back? and their mighty ones are beaten down, and are fled apace, and look not back: for fear was round about, saith the LORD.

6 Let not the swift flee away, nor the mighty man escape; they shall stumble, and fall toward the north by the river Euphrates.

7 Who is this that cometh up as a flood, whose waters are moved as the rivers?

8 Egypt riseth up like a flood, and his waters are moved like the rivers; and he saith, I will go up, and will cover the earth; I will destroy the city and the inhabitants thereof.

9 Come up, ye horses; and rage, ye chariots; and let the mighty men come forth; the Ethiopians and the Libyans, that handle the shield; and the Lydians, that handle and bend the bow.

10 For this is the day of the Lord GOD of hosts, a day of vengeance, that he may avenge him of his adversaries: and the sword shall devour, and it shall be satiate and made drunk with their blood: for the Lord GOD of hosts hath a sacrifice in the north country by the river Euphrates.

11 Go up into Gilead, and take balm, O virgin, the daughter of Egypt: in vain shalt thou use many medicines; for thou shalt not be cured.

12 The nations have heard of thy shame, and thy cry hath filled the land: for the mighty man hath stumbled against the mighty, and they are fallen both together.

13 The word that the LORD spake to Jeremiah the prophet, how Nebuchadrezzar king of Babylon should come and smite the land of Egypt.

14 Declare ye in Egypt, and publish in Migdol, and publish in Noph and in Tahpanhes: say ye, Stand fast, and prepare thee; for the sword shall devour round about thee.

15 Why are thy valiant men swept away? they stood not, because the LORD did drive them.

16 He made many to fall, yea, one fell upon another: and they said, Arise, and let us go again to our own people, and to the land of our nativity, from the oppressing sword.

17 They did cry there, Pharaoh king of Egypt is but a noise; he hath passed the time appointed.

18 As I live, saith the King, whose name is the LORD of hosts, Surely as Tabor is among the mountains, and as Carmel by the sea, so shall he come.

19 O thou daughter dwelling in Egypt, furnish thyself to go into captivity: for Noph shall be waste and desolate without an inhabitant.

20 Egypt is like a very fair heifer, but destruction cometh; it cometh out of the north.

21 Also her hired men are in the midst of her like fatted bullocks; for they also are turned back, and are fled away together: they did not stand, because the day of their calamity was come upon them, and the time of their visitation.

22 The voice thereof shall go like a serpent; for they shall march with an army, and come against her with axes, as hewers of wood.

23 They shall cut down her forest, saith the LORD, though it cannot be searched; because they are more than the grasshoppers, and are innumerable.

24 The daughter of Egypt shall be confounded; she shall be delivered into the hand of the people of the north.

25 The LORD of hosts, the God of Israel, saith; Behold, I will punish the multitude of No, and Pharaoh, and Egypt, with their gods, and their kings; even Pharaoh, and all them that trust in him:

26 And I will deliver them into the hand of those that seek their lives, and into the hand of Nebuchadrezzar king of Babylon, and into the hand of his servants: and afterward it shall be inhabited, as in the days of old, saith the LORD.

27 But fear not thou, O my servant Jacob, and be not dismayed, O Israel: for, behold, I will save thee from afar off, and thy seed from the land of their captivity; and Jacob shall return, and be in rest and at ease, and none shall make him afraid.

28 Fear thou not, O Jacob my servant, saith the LORD: for I am with thee; for I will make a full end of all the nations whither I have driven thee: but I will not make a full end of thee, but correct thee in measure; yet will I not leave thee wholly unpunished.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

The oracles against the nations begin with Egypt: Pharaohnecho's army by the Euphrates at Carchemish is broken, Egypt rising like the Nile is beaten back, and a later invasion by Nebuchadnezzar is foretold. The chapter closes with comfort addressed to Jacob: fear not, for God is with his servant, and though he will make a full end of the nations, he will not make a full end of Jacob. Interpreted Chapter text and study notes for this chapter

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Henry notes this verse is the title of the part of the book concerning the neighbouring nations: it is the word of the LORD against the Gentiles, for God is King and Judge of nations who will call to account those who know him not, and Henry contrasts the Old Testament word against the Gentiles with the New Testament word for them, that those afar off are made nigh. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 2. Henry begins with Egypt because they were of old Israel's oppressors and of late their deceivers when Judah put confidence in them, and notes Pharaoh-necho's overthrow at Carchemish in the fourth year of Jehoiakim was so complete that Babylon recovered all that pertained to Egypt from the river of Egypt to the Euphrates, weakened Pharaoh so he came no more out of his land, and avenged Josiah, whom Necho had slain four years before. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 3. Henry notes the irony of human preparation: the prophet calls Egypt to order buckler and shield and draw near to battle, and Henry reflects on what care, trouble, and vast expense men spend to kill one another, as if they did not die fast enough of themselves. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 4. Henry notes Egypt was famous for horses, so the call to harness them and mount the cavalry well is the utmost of military preparation, yet Henry adds the moral that those going to war should not only order the buckler and harness the horses, but repent of their sins and pray for God's presence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 5. Henry contrasts the mighty preparations with the shameful retreat: when the Chaldean army faces them, even their mighty ones are dismayed and turned back, fleeing by consent in confusion with neither time nor heart to look back, because fear is round about them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 6. Henry stresses they gain the shame of flight without its benefit: the swift shall not flee away and the mighty shall not escape, but shall stumble and fall toward the north, toward their enemy's country, in such confusion that instead of making homeward they made forward, proving the race is not to the swift nor the battle to the strong. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 7. Henry compares Egypt's marching out to the rising of the Nile: Egypt rises up like a flood, scorning to keep within its own banks and threatening to overflow all neighbouring lands, a formidable army brought into the field. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 8. Henry notes Egypt's boast, I will go up and cover the earth, I will destroy the city and its inhabitants, and observes that they knew their own thoughts and God knew them, but they knew not the thoughts of the LORD, for he gathers them as sheaves into the floor. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 9. Henry notes the prophet challenges Egypt to bring all its confederate troops, the Ethiopians descended from the same stock and their neighbours and allies, and the Libyans and Lydians seated in Africa west of Egypt, yet all their art and interest shall be in vain, for God will fight against them and against him there is no wisdom nor counsel. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 10. Henry explains that the Egyptians meant a day for themselves, but God says it shall be his day, the day of the LORD God of hosts in which he will be exalted in their overthrow, a day of vengeance for Josiah's death and a day of sacrifice to divine justice to which multitudes of Egypt's sinners shall fall as victims. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 11. Henry pictures the daughter of Egypt, a damsel living in great pomp and state, sorely wounded by this defeat, and notes that though she seek balm in Gilead and use all the medicines her wise men can prescribe, it shall be in vain, for she shall never again bring such a powerful army into the field. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 12. Henry notes the shame needs no conqueror's triumph to spread it: the nations have heard of Egypt's shame, and the shrieks and outcries of the conquered fill the country, as in flight one mighty man stumbled upon another and dashed against another, so that both together became an easy prey to the pursuers. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 13. Henry marks this as a second, later prediction, fulfilled by the same hand, Nebuchadnezzar, many years after, at least twenty, and probably about the same time as the prediction of the same event in chapter 43, showing how Babylon should come and smite the land of Egypt and bring war into their own bosoms which they had carried into his borders. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 14. Henry notes the alarm must be proclaimed in all parts of Egypt, particularly Migdol, Noph, and Tahpanhes, precisely where the Jewish refugees had planted themselves in contempt of God's command, so they may hear what a sorry shelter Egypt is likely to be to them, since the sword is already devouring round about. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 15. Henry explains the valiant men are swept away as with a sweeping rain: none can stand their ground because the LORD drives them from their posts, driving them by his terrors and by enabling the Chaldeans to drive them, since those whom the wrath of God chases cannot fix. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 16. Henry notes that when their day comes to fall, the enemy need not throw them down, for they shall fall one upon another, every man a stumbling-block to his fellow, and in consternation the mercenary troops make homeward, saying, Arise, let us go again to our own people from the oppressing sword, since little confidence can be put in troops that fight purely for pay with no interest in those they fight for. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 17. Henry explains the cry against Pharaoh: he had probably promised to come within a set time with a gallant army to support his frontier troops, but failed them and left them abandoned to the invaders, so they desert crying that Pharaoh king of Egypt is but a noise, a great talker who brings nothing to pass and has passed the time appointed, and Henry notes those who make most noise are frequently but a noise. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 18. Henry stresses the certainty sworn by the King whose name is the LORD of hosts: as Tabor overtops the mountains and Carmel overlooks the sea, so shall the king of Babylon overpower all the force of Egypt, such command and sway shall he bear, before whom the mightiest kings on earth are but as grasshoppers. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 19. Henry draws the practical lesson to prepare for trouble: the daughter dwelling securely and delicately in Egypt must furnish herself to go into captivity, exchanging rich clothes that would tempt the enemy for plain warm clothes and fine shoes for strong ones, and inuring herself to hardship, for Noph shall be waste and desolate without an inhabitant, so general shall the slaughter and captivity be. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 20. Henry pictures Egypt as a very fair heifer, fat and shining and not accustomed to the yoke, wanton as a well-fed heifer, with a possible allusion to Apis, the calf the Egyptians worshipped from whom Israel learned the golden calf, yet destruction comes out of the north, as butchers come to kill and cut up this fair heifer. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 21. Henry compares Egypt's hired men to fatted bullocks, lusty, able-bodied, and high-spirited men likely for action, yet they are turned back and fled away together because the day of their calamity had come, and Henry adds that some read the comparison as luxury: they had wantoned in pleasures and were unfit for hardships, and therefore could not stand. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 22. Henry explains the serpent's voice as Egypt's voice grown low and submissive: no longer lowing like a fair heifer making a great noise, but hissing out of holes like serpents, venting grief in silent murmurs and begging for life with entreaties, while the Chaldeans march with an army and come against her with axes as hewers of wood. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 23. Henry notes Egypt shall be felled as a forest by hewers of wood: though populous like a forest whose trees cannot be searched or numbered and rich in hidden treasures, the Chaldeans, more than the grasshoppers and innumerable, shall make a great spoil, for the LORD of hosts has numberless hosts at his command. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 24. Henry notes the daughter of Egypt shall be confounded and delivered into the hand of the people of the north, her tune changed and her pride brought down, as the Egyptians are tamed rather than merely beaten. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 25. Henry stresses that no multitude or rank escapes: the multitude of No, called populous No, shall be punished, for though hand join in hand they shall not escape and God will be too many for them, and their kings and petty princes shall fall, and their gods too, their tutelar idols proving no protection, with Pharaoh brought down and all those that trust in him, particularly the Jews who sojourned in his country trusting in him rather than in God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 26. Henry notes all shall be delivered into the hand not only of Nebuchadnezzar but of his servants, according to the curse on Ham's posterity, of which the Egyptians were, that they should be servants of servants, into the hand of those who seek their lives, yet Henry also notes the intimation that in process of time Egypt shall recover: afterwards it shall be inhabited and peopled again, whereas this destruction almost dispeopled it, and Ezekiel foretells this at the end of forty years, a reminder that nations are emptied and increased again, so prospering nations should not be secure nor those in thraldom despair. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 27. Henry applies the comfort to the captives in Babylon, or more generally to all the people of God in difficult times when God's judgments are abroad among the nations, repeating chapter 30: God would not have his people timorous, but Jacob shall be found out and gathered though far off, redeemed though held fast in captivity, and shall return to rest and ease with none making him afraid. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 28. Henry stresses the distinguishing mercy: wherever Jacob is, God is with him, a very present help, and while a full end shall be made of the nations that oppressed God's Israel, as Egypt and Babylon, mercy is kept in store for Israel, which shall be corrected but not cast off, the correction in measure in degree and continuance, and in the gospel church, God's spiritual Israel, this promise has its full accomplishment, since though God correct it he will never make a full end of it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

Where the notes differ

On the fatted bullocks of verse 21, Matthew Henry reads Egypt's hired men as lusty, able-bodied and high-spirited men likely for action, yet turned back in flight; as his own note records, some read the same comparison as luxury — men who had wantoned in pleasures and were unfit for hardships, and therefore could not stand. The note does not name those other readers, and this page does not adjudicate between the readings. Interpreted Study note for Jeremiah 46:21, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
