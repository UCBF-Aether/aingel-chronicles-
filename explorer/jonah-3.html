The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Jonah 3 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 And the word of the LORD came unto Jonah the second time, saying,

2 Arise, go unto Nineveh, that great city, and preach unto it the preaching that I bid thee.

3 So Jonah arose, and went unto Nineveh, according to the word of the LORD. Now Nineveh was an exceeding great city of three days’ journey.

4 And Jonah began to enter into the city a day’s journey, and he cried, and said, Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown.

5 So the people of Nineveh believed God, and proclaimed a fast, and put on sackcloth, from the greatest of them even to the least of them.

6 For word came unto the king of Nineveh, and he arose from his throne, and he laid his robe from him, and covered him with sackcloth, and sat in ashes.

7 And he caused it to be proclaimed and published through Nineveh by the decree of the king and his nobles, saying, Let neither man nor beast, herd nor flock, taste any thing: let them not feed, nor drink water:

8 But let man and beast be covered with sackcloth, and cry mightily unto God: yea, let them turn every one from his evil way, and from the violence that is in their hands.

9 Who can tell if God will turn and repent, and turn away from his fierce anger, that we perish not?

10 And God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way; and God repented of the evil, that he had said that he would do unto them; and he did it not.

Every substantive historical claim in the editorial panels below carries one of three labels, plus a source reference:

  • Interpreted — a reasoned explanation or proposed relationship.

A claim does NOT become Documented merely because a commentary states it. Commentary views are attributed to their authors and labeled Interpreted unless a primary textual source directly supports the claim. Where evidence runs out, the page says Unresolved and stops — it does not fill the gap.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

The word of the LORD comes to Jonah a second time, and this time he goes to Nineveh, a city of three days' journey, and cries after one day's journey: yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown. The people believe God, proclaim a fast, and put on sackcloth from the greatest to the least, the king rises from his throne, sits in ashes, and decrees that man and beast fast and cry mightily to God and turn from violence. God sees their works and repents of the evil he said he would do, and does it not. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. The word of the Lord came to Jonah the second time, evidence of a thorough reconciliation after a controversy that had run high. Henry notes that employing Jonah again was equivalent to a pardon: when God forgives, he forgets, and restores the disobedient servant to his former service. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 2. Jonah must arise, go to Nineveh, and preach the preaching God bids him, the very message he had fled from, unaltered to suit him. Henry stresses that God's word will not bend to the humours of preachers or hearers; they must comply with it, and God would go along with him step by step. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 3. Jonah arose and went to Nineveh according to the word of the Lord, without murmuring or disputing. Henry reads this as repentance itself: a change of mind and way, and a return to the work and duty from which he had turned aside, with affliction having brought him back to his place. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 4. Entering the exceedingly great city, Jonah cried at once, Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown. Henry notes the forty days were a reprieve in which God waited to see if they would humble themselves, slow to wrath even when wickedness cried for vengeance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 5. The people of Nineveh believed God, and proclaimed a fast, and put on sackcloth from the greatest to the least. Henry calls this a wonder of grace: they believed there was one living and true God to whom they were accountable, that ruin would come at a fixed time unless prevented by timely repentance, and that he might yet be merciful. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 6. Word reached the king of Nineveh, and he rose from his throne, laid aside his royal robe, covered himself with sackcloth and sat in ashes. Henry notes the king acknowledged he had forfeited throne and robe by misusing his power, and that it well becomes the greatest of men to abase themselves before the great God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 7. By the king's decree and his nobles', a fast was proclaimed: neither man nor beast was to taste anything, and beasts too were covered with sackcloth. Henry notes the whole legislative power and the whole people concurred, making it a national act to prevent a national ruin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 8. The decree joined prayer and reformation to fasting: let them cry mightily to God, and turn every one from his evil way and from the violence in his hands. Henry stresses it is not enough to fast for sin; we must fast from sin, restoring what was unjustly taken and ceasing to oppress or defraud. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 9. Who can tell if God will turn and repent, and turn from his fierce anger, that we perish not? Henry notes they dared not presume, yet would not despair: a general sense of God's goodness gave them hope that mercy might rejoice against judgment, and hope of mercy is the great encouragement to repentance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 10. God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way, and he repented of the evil he had said he would do, and did it not. Henry notes God looked beyond good words to fruits meet for repentance: fasting and sackcloth alone would have been nothing without a general turning from sin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry

Unfamiliar terms

  • Nineveh. the great Assyrian city, three days' journey across, to which Jonah is sent and whose repentance spares it in Jonah 3, and whose fall Nahum announces. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry
