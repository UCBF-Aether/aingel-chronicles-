The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Nehemiah 5 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 And there was a great cry of the people and of their wives against their brethren the Jews.

2 For there were that said, We, our sons, and our daughters, are many: therefore we take up corn for them, that we may eat, and live.

3 Some also there were that said, We have mortgaged our lands, vineyards, and houses, that we might buy corn, because of the dearth.

4 There were also that said, We have borrowed money for the king’s tribute, and that upon our lands and vineyards.

5 Yet now our flesh is as the flesh of our brethren, our children as their children: and, lo, we bring into bondage our sons and our daughters to be servants, and some of our daughters are brought unto bondage already: neither is it in our power to redeem them; for other men have our lands and vineyards.

6 And I was very angry when I heard their cry and these words.

7 Then I consulted with myself, and I rebuked the nobles, and the rulers, and said unto them, Ye exact usury, every one of his brother. And I set a great assembly against them.

8 And I said unto them, We after our ability have redeemed our brethren the Jews, which were sold unto the heathen; and will ye even sell your brethren? or shall they be sold unto us? Then held they their peace, and found nothing to answer.

9 Also I said, It is not good that ye do: ought ye not to walk in the fear of our God because of the reproach of the heathen our enemies?

10 I likewise, and my brethren, and my servants, might exact of them money and corn: I pray you, let us leave off this usury.

11 Restore, I pray you, to them, even this day, their lands, their vineyards, their oliveyards, and their houses, also the hundredth part of the money, and of the corn, the wine, and the oil, that ye exact of them.

12 Then said they, We will restore them, and will require nothing of them; so will we do as thou sayest. Then I called the priests, and took an oath of them, that they should do according to this promise.

13 Also I shook my lap, and said, So God shake out every man from his house, and from his labour, that performeth not this promise, even thus be he shaken out, and emptied. And all the congregation said, Amen, and praised the LORD. And the people did according to this promise.

14 Moreover from the time that I was appointed to be their governor in the land of Judah, from the twentieth year even unto the two and thirtieth year of Artaxerxes the king, that is, twelve years, I and my brethren have not eaten the bread of the governor.

15 But the former governors that had been before me were chargeable unto the people, and had taken of them bread and wine, beside forty shekels of silver; yea, even their servants bare rule over the people: but so did not I, because of the fear of God.

16 Yea, also I continued in the work of this wall, neither bought we any land: and all my servants were gathered thither unto the work.

17 Moreover there were at my table an hundred and fifty of the Jews and rulers, beside those that came unto us from among the heathen that are about us.

18 Now that which was prepared for me daily was one ox and six choice sheep; also fowls were prepared for me, and once in ten days store of all sorts of wine: yet for all this required not I the bread of the governor, because the bondage was heavy upon this people.

19 Think upon me, my God, for good, according to all that I have done for this people.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

What the chapter says, in plain language

A great cry rises from the poor: famine, mortgaged fields and vineyards, borrowed money for the king's tribute, and children sold into bondage to their own brethren. Nehemiah rebukes the nobles and rulers for exacting usury, makes them swear to restore fields, vineyards, and the hundredth part of money and corn, and sets his own example: for twelve years as governor he took no governor's bread, and he prays, 'Think upon me, my God, for good.' Interpreted Chapter text and study notes for this chapter

Unfamiliar terms

  • Usury. interest charged by a lender to his own brethren, here on money and corn lent in their need (verse 7). Interpreted Study note for this chapter
  • The hundredth part. the portion Nehemiah orders restored, the interest already taken from the poor (verse 11). Interpreted Study note for this chapter

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. A great cry arose from the people and their wives against their brethren the Jews. Henry frames it as the tears of the oppressed brought before the governor whose office was to deliver the poor out of the hand of wicked oppressors. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 2. Some complained that they, their sons, and their daughters were many, and must take up corn to eat and live. Henry notes the hardest pinch: the most numerous families were the most necessitous, with many mouths and no meat. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 3. Others had mortgaged their lands, vineyards, and houses to buy corn in the dearth. Henry traces the dearth to God's chastisement for neglected dues, and notes the poor had come out of Babylon poor and spent heavily on houses before these burdens fell. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 4. Others had borrowed money for the king's tribute upon their lands and vineyards. Henry observes the double squeeze: as corn grew dear, taxes stayed high, and the more mouths they had to maintain, the more they had to pay. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 5. Their bitterest complaint was that their children were brought into bondage, some already, with no power to redeem them, while others held their lands and vineyards. Henry stresses the aggravation: their children were as dear as their brethren's, free-born Israelites bearing the covenant seal, yet made slaves. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 6. Nehemiah was very angry when he heard their cry and these words. Henry notes he did not reject the complaint as unseasonable amid the wall-building, nor adjourn it, because a city could not be safe while such abuses were tolerated within it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 7. He consulted with himself, then rebuked the nobles and rulers, saying they exacted usury every one of his brother, and set a great assembly against them. Henry notes his anger stayed within bounds: reproof must be given with consideration, or well-meant correction fails for want of management. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 8. He reasoned with them: they themselves had redeemed brethren sold to the heathen, so would they now sell their brethren to be sold to them? Henry calls this absurd: must the same trouble be spent redeeming them from their own rulers as from Babylon? They held their peace, finding nothing to answer. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 9. He pressed further: what they did was not good, for they ought to walk in the fear of God, especially because of the reproach of the heathen enemies. Henry notes nothing exposes religion more to its enemies' reproach than the worldliness and hard-heartedness of its professors. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 10. Nehemiah added his own example: he, his brethren, and his servants might have exacted money and corn, but he urged them to leave off this usury. Henry observes that those who insist rigorously on their own rights will with ill grace persuade others to recede from theirs. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 11. He pressed them to restore the people's lands, vineyards, oliveyards, and houses, and the hundredth part of the money, corn, wine, and oil they had exacted. Henry notes the interest ran at twelve percent, a hundredth part every month, and that he urged them to give up mortgages, remit interest, and give time for the principal. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 12. They promised to restore and require nothing, and Nehemiah called the priests to take their oath to perform it. Henry notes he bound them while their convictions were strong, so that resolution would be kept, not merely felt. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 13. Nehemiah shook his lap, saying God would likewise shake out and empty every man who did not perform this promise, and the congregation said Amen and praised the LORD. Henry notes they promised with joy, and better still, they did according to the promise, unlike ancestors who re-enslaved those they had freed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 14. From the day he was appointed governor, in the twentieth year of Artaxerxes until the thirty-second, twelve years, neither Nehemiah nor his brethren ate the bread of the governor. Henry notes he recorded this not in pride but as an inducement to successors and magistrates to be tender of the people's ease. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 15. Former governors had been chargeable, taking bread and wine besides forty shekels of silver, while their servants squeezed the people. Henry notes the contrast: Nehemiah did not do so, because of the fear of God, which restrained him from oppression purely for conscience' sake. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 16. He continued in the work of the wall, bought no land, and his servants were all gathered to the work. Henry observes he got nothing from office except the satisfaction of doing good, aiming not to feather his own nest. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 17. At his table sat a hundred and fifty Jews and rulers, besides those who came from the heathen round about. Henry notes he kept a constantly good table for honourable guests, using his place to do good rather than to enrich himself. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 18. The daily provision was an ox, six choice sheep, and fowls, with wine every ten days, yet he did not require the governor's bread because the bondage was heavy on the people. Henry's rule: in our demands we must consider not only their justice but the ability of those on whom we make them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 19. He closed with prayer: "Think upon me, my God, for good, according to all that I have done for this people." Henry notes he mentioned his deeds to God not as merit owed, but because he looked for no recompense from men; if God but thought on him for good, he had enough. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
