The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Baruch 6 (King James Version, Apocrypha; public-domain text as received)

1 Because of the sins which ye have committed before God, ye shall be led away captives into Babylon by Nabuchodonosor king of the Babylonians.

2 So when ye be come unto Babylon, ye shall remain there many years, and for a long season, namely, seven generations: and after that I will bring you away peaceably from thence.

3 Now shall ye see in Babylon gods of silver, and of gold, and of wood, borne upon shoulders, which cause the nations to fear.

4 Beware therefore that ye in no wise be like to strangers, neither be ye and of them, when ye see the multitude before them and behind them, worshipping them.

5 But say ye in your hearts, O Lord, we must worship thee.

6 For mine angel is with you, and I myself caring for your souls.

7 As for their tongue, it is polished by the workman, and they themselves are gilded and laid over with silver; yet are they but false, and cannot speak.

8 And taking gold, as it were for a virgin that loveth to go gay, they make crowns for the heads of their gods.

9 Sometimes also the priests convey from their gods gold and silver, and bestow it upon themselves.

10 Yea, they will give thereof to the common harlots, and deck them as men with garments, [being] gods of silver, and gods of gold, and wood.

11 Yet cannot these gods save themselves from rust and moth, though they be covered with purple raiment.

12 They wipe their faces because of the dust of the temple, when there is much upon them.

13 And he that cannot put to death one that offendeth him holdeth a sceptre, as though he were a judge of the country.

14 He hath also in his right hand a dagger and an ax: but cannot deliver himself from war and thieves.

15 Whereby they are known not to be gods: therefore fear them not.

16 For like as a vessel that a man useth is nothing worth when it is broken; even so it is with their gods: when they be set up in the temple, their eyes be full of dust through the feet of them that come in.

17 And as the doors are made sure on every side upon him that offendeth the king, as being committed to suffer death: even so the priests make fast their temples with doors, with locks, and bars, lest their gods be spoiled with robbers.

18 They light them candles, yea, more than for themselves, whereof they cannot see one.

19 They are as one of the beams of the temple, yet they say their hearts are gnawed upon by things creeping out of the earth; and when they eat them and their clothes, they feel it not.

20 Their faces are blacked through the smoke that cometh out of the temple.

21 Upon their bodies and heads sit bats, swallows, and birds, and the cats also.

22 By this ye may know that they are no gods: therefore fear them not.

23 Notwithstanding the gold that is about them to make them beautiful, except they wipe off the rust, they will not shine: for neither when they were molten did they feel it.

24 The things wherein there is no breath are bought for a most high price.

25 They are borne upon shoulders, having no feet whereby they declare unto men that they be nothing worth.

26 They also that serve them are ashamed: for if they fall to the ground at any time, they cannot rise up again of themselves: neither, if one set them upright, can they move of themselves: neither, if they be bowed down, can they make themselves straight: but they set gifts before them as unto dead men.

27 As for the things that are sacrificed unto them, their priests sell and abuse; in like manner their wives lay up part thereof in salt; but unto the poor and impotent they give nothing of it.

28 Menstruous women and women in childbed eat their sacrifices: by these things ye may know that they are no gods: fear them not.

29 For how can they be called gods? because women set meat before the gods of silver, gold, and wood.

30 And the priests sit in their temples, having their clothes rent, and their heads and beards shaven, and nothing upon their heads.

31 They roar and cry before their gods, as men do at the feast when one is dead.

32 The priests also take off their garments, and clothe their wives and children.

33 Whether it be evil that one doeth unto them, or good, they are not able to recompense it: they can neither set up a king, nor put him down.

34 In like manner, they can neither give riches nor money: though a man make a vow unto them, and keep it not, they will not require it.

35 They can save no man from death, neither deliver the weak from the mighty.

36 They cannot restore a blind man to his sight, nor help any man in his distress.

37 They can shew no mercy to the widow, nor do good to the fatherless.

38 Their gods of wood, and which are overlaid with gold and silver, are like the stones that be hewn out of the mountain: they that worship them shall be confounded.

39 How should a man then think and say that they are gods, when even the Chaldeans themselves dishonour them?

40 Who if they shall see one dumb that cannot speak, they bring him, and intreat Bel that he may speak, as though he were able to understand.

41 Yet they cannot understand this themselves, and leave them: for they have no knowledge.

42 The women also with cords about them, sitting in the ways, burn bran for perfume: but if any of them, drawn by some that passeth by, lie with him, she reproacheth her fellow, that she was not thought as worthy as herself, nor her cord broken.

43 Whatsoever is done among them is false: how may it then be thought or said that they are gods?

44 They are made of carpenters and goldsmiths: they can be nothing else than the workmen will have them to be.

45 And they themselves that made them can never continue long; how should then the things that are made of them be gods?

46 For they left lies and reproaches to them that come after.

47 For when there cometh any war or plague upon them, the priests consult with themselves, where they may be hidden with them.

48 How then cannot men perceive that they be no gods, which can neither save themselves from war, nor from plague?

49 For seeing they be but of wood, and overlaid with silver and gold, it shall be known hereafter that they are false:

50 And it shall manifestly appear to all nations and kings that they are no gods, but the works of men's hands, and that there is no work of God in them.

51 Who then may not know that they are no gods?

52 For neither can they set up a king in the land, nor give rain unto men.

53 Neither can they judge their own cause, nor redress a wrong, being unable: for they are as crows between heaven and earth.

54 Whereupon when fire falleth upon the house of gods of wood, or laid over with gold or silver, their priests will flee away, and escape; but they themselves shall be burned asunder like beams.

55 Moreover they cannot withstand any king or enemies: how can it then be thought or said that they be gods?

56 Neither are those gods of wood, and laid over with silver or gold, able to escape either from thieves or robbers.

57 Whose gold, and silver, and garments wherewith they are clothed, they that are strong take, and go away withal: neither are they able to help themselves.

58 Therefore it is better to be a king that sheweth his power, or else a profitable vessel in an house, which the owner shall have use of, than such false gods; or to be a door in an house, to keep such things therein, than such false gods. or a pillar of wood in a a palace, than such false gods.

59 For sun, moon, and stars, being bright and sent to do their offices, are obedient.

60 In like manner the lightning when it breaketh forth is easy to be seen; and after the same manner the wind bloweth in every country.

61 And when God commandeth the clouds to go over the whole world, they do as they are bidden.

62 And the fire sent from above to consume hills and woods doeth as it is commanded: but these are like unto them neither in shew nor power.

63 Wherefore it is neither to be supposed nor said that they are gods, seeing, they are able neither to judge causes, nor to do good unto men.

64 Knowing therefore that they are no gods, fear them not,

65 For they can neither curse nor bless kings:

66 Neither can they shew signs in the heavens among the heathen, nor shine as the sun, nor give light as the moon.

67 The beasts are better than they: for they can get under a cover and help themselves.

68 It is then by no means manifest unto us that they are gods: therefore fear them not.

69 For as a scarecrow in a garden of cucumbers keepeth nothing: so are their gods of wood, and laid over with silver and gold.

70 And likewise their gods of wood, and laid over with silver and gold, are like to a white thorn in an orchard, that every bird sitteth upon; as also to a dead body, that is east into the dark.

71 And ye shall know them to be no gods by the bright purple that rotteth upon them: and they themselves afterward shall be eaten, and shall be a reproach in the country.

72 Better therefore is the just man that hath none idols: for he shall be far from reproach. Go back to the Bible

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

The Letter of Jeremiah warns the exiles on their way to Babylon that they will remain there many years, and that they will see gods of silver, gold and wood borne upon shoulders — and must not fear or worship them. Case after case follows: the idols cannot speak, cannot save, cannot protect themselves from thieves or rust, are dressed and fed like men or like the dead, and the chapter ends that better is the just man who has no idols, for he will be far from reproach. Interpreted Chapter text and study notes for this chapter

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 2. Haydock's authorities read the 'seven generations' of the captivity as seventy years, with 'seven' often standing for a general or round number. Calmet adds that eighteen of those seventy years had already elapsed when the letter was written. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 3. The gods borne upon shoulders reflect a very ancient custom, which Haydock traces to the avarice of the priests, who begged on such occasions. The spectacle itself is meant to expose, not honour, the idols. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 4. Haydock glosses the warning 'fear' simply: worship not such things. The fear the Babylonians' idols inspire is not to be mistaken for reverence owed to a god. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 6. The angel promised to be with the captives is Michael, the conductor of Israel in the desert, cited from Daniel 10:13 and Exodus 33:2. Worthington adds that the same protector guarded them also in Babylon. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 8. The gold crowns made for the idols' heads are likened to dressing a maiden that loves to go gay, and Calmet remarks that nothing could be more despicable. The finery advertises vanity, not divinity. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 10. The priests give the idols' gold and silver even to harlots under the same roof, while adorning the idols like men with garments. Haydock contrasts this with the Lord, who rejected the harlot's hire: the idols suffer themselves to be despoiled or adorned at pleasure. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 12. The idols' faces must be wiped because of the dust of the temple, on account of the many votaries crowding in. Haydock cites Arnobius ridiculing such gods, and Worthington adds that these absurdities show how foolish it is to serve idols or take any image to be a god. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 13. The idol holding a sceptre as though a judge of the country merely carries a badge of power: the sceptre and spear were such badges, as the sword was for Mars and the club for Hercules. Calmet notes how little is actually known of the idols of Babylon behind the show. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 16. Haydock's note on the vessel explains the comparison: the word covers armour or any utensil, and as a broken vessel becomes useless, so are gods set up in a temple. A thing that cannot serve its use cannot be a god. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 18. The candles lit for the idols are literally lamps, and Haydock notes some Egyptian temples were famous for their numbers. The Lord prescribed lamps in his own worship, but no one ever imagined he needed them to see, as idolaters supposed their idols did. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 19. The creeping things said to gnaw the idols' hearts are explained plainly: the wood is worm-eaten. The gods cannot even feel what eats them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 24. Things with no breath in them are bought for a most high price, and Worthington's gloss is that buyers foolishly believe the images have divine power. The price measures the buyer's folly, not the idol's worth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 26. The gifts set before fallen idols as before the dead recall the Chaldean supposition that Bel could eat, cited from Daniel 14, and the custom of placing food on tombs, which Augustine abolished in Africa. Offerings that feed no one expose the idol. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 27. The Greek for the priests' taking reads 'salt': they lay up part of the sacrifices in salt and give none to the poor, for fear their imposture would be detected, though they allow even the unclean to eat. The charity the law required is exactly what the idol cult withholds. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 29. Calmet's remark on the women setting meat before the gods is blunt: they make the gods. A deity that must be fed by its worshippers is the worshippers' own work. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 30. The priests sit with clothes rent and heads and beards shaven, as in mourning, contrary to the custom of Israel in Leviticus 21:10. Haydock adds that the Jewish priests wore mitres or caps, not this mourning disarray. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 31. The roaring and crying before the gods, as at a feast when one is dead, recalls the worship of Adonis, which Calmet says is here ridiculed. At Byblos people bewailed his death and proclaimed him alive the next day, cutting off their hair, with the hire consecrated to Venus. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 34. The Greek for 'requite' reads 'brass': the idols cannot give riches nor requite evil, much less gold. A god that cannot return even base metal for a vow is no god at all. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 40. Even the Chaldeans themselves dishonour their idols, and Calmet observes that the priests despise them most, exposing them to contempt by pretending they work miracles. Haydock quotes Varro and Augustine: setting up representations of the gods deprived cities of reverential awe and increased the error. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 41. The Greek reads that the dumb man's presenters, reflecting on the idol's senselessness, cannot still abandon it, for the gods themselves have no sense. Calmet's gloss is that worshippers are so stupid they will not quit such impotent idols. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 42. The women with cords about them recall the Babylonian rite in honour of Venus, or Mylitta, in which women sat crowned in the temple until some stranger selected them and broke their cords, as Herodotus describes. Aristophanes called harlots 'corded bodies,' and Haydock cites the passage against the rite. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 44. The idols are made by carpenters and goldsmiths, and can be nothing else than the workmen will have them to be, as Isaiah 44:14 shows. Haydock quotes Horace's Priapus explaining his origin as a useless piece of fig-tree, which the workman, unsure whether to make a bench or a god, chose to make a god. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 51. The Greek has simply, 'Who then knows not that they are no gods?' The verse states the letter's whole argument as a question no reader can honestly answer otherwise. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 53. The idols can neither judge a cause nor redress a wrong, and Calmet says they have no greater influence than jackdaws between heaven and earth. Creatures of the air outrank them in power. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 58. The Septuagint repeats the comparison after 'satisfied' and adds 'or a pillar of wood in palaces, than' such false gods. A king who shows his power, a profitable vessel, or a door keeping things safe is better than an idol. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 59. The sun, moon, and stars are obedient to God, appointed to mark out the seasons according to Genesis 1:14, and Calmet notes that if any creature were worthy of adoration, they would be. The Babylonians adored them as well as statues, which is why their proper destination is specified here. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 69. Like a scarecrow in a garden of cucumbers, the idols keep nothing, and Haydock notes Priapus answered this same glorious purpose in the workmen's idol of verse 44. Calmet adds that once birds grow accustomed to such things, they mind them not. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 71. The purple and scarlet rotting upon the idols mark them: the Greek speaks of marble-coloured wood, and Grotius would read a pearl, which decays through age. The finery itself rots, and the idols are at last consumed and left a reproach. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 72. Better is the just man who has no idols, for he shall be far from reproach, provided, Haydock adds, he also avoids the idols of the mind and of the heart. The letter ends where it began: freedom from idols, outward and inward. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock

Investigation (editorial — a question, not a conclusion)

This section investigates a parallel; it does not conclude a relationship between the books. Interpreted Editorial framing for this section

Parallel. Baruch 6, the Letter of Jeremiah, mocks idols that are cut, decked with silver and gold, fastened so they cannot move, carried because they cannot walk, and unable to speak or save — the same polemic shape as Jeremiah 10, where idols are cut from a forest, decked, nailed fast, upright as a palm tree yet unable to speak or go, and must needs be borne. Both chapters set the helpless made thing against the living God. Interpreted Chapter text of Baruch 6 and of Jeremiah 10

Differences. The settings differ: Jeremiah 10 addresses the house of Israel against learning the way of the heathen, while Baruch 6 is framed as a letter to exiles about to enter Babylon, warning them about the gods they will see there, and it extends the mockery at far greater length — priests, offerings, thieves, rust and broken images. The letter's exilic frame and its seventy-year horizon are its own. Interpreted Chapter text of Baruch 6 and of Jeremiah 10

Unresolved. Whether the Letter of Jeremiah drew directly on Jeremiah 10, or both drew on a shared idol-polemic tradition, is not established by the sources consulted for this page, and no direct dependence, shared authorship, or historical certainty is claimed here.
