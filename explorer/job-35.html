The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 35 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Elihu spake moreover, and said,

2 Thinkest thou this to be right, that thou saidst, My righteousness is more than God’s?

3 For thou saidst, What advantage will it be unto thee? and, What profit shall I have, if I be cleansed from my sin?

4 I will answer thee, and thy companions with thee.

5 Look unto the heavens, and see; and behold the clouds which are higher than thou.

6 If thou sinnest, what doest thou against him? or if thy transgressions be multiplied, what doest thou unto him?

7 If thou be righteous, what givest thou him? or what receiveth he of thine hand?

8 Thy wickedness may hurt a man as thou art; and thy righteousness may profit the son of man.

9 By reason of the multitude of oppressions they make the oppressed to cry: they cry out by reason of the arm of the mighty.

10 But none saith, Where is God my maker, who giveth songs in the night;

11 Who teacheth us more than the beasts of the earth, and maketh us wiser than the fowls of heaven?

12 There they cry, but none giveth answer, because of the pride of evil men.

13 Surely God will not hear vanity, neither will the Almighty regard it.

14 Although thou sayest thou shalt not see him, yet judgment is before him; therefore trust thou in him.

15 But now, because it is not so, he hath visited in his anger; yet he knoweth it not in great extremity:

16 Therefore doth Job open his mouth in vain; he multiplieth words without knowledge.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Elihu turns to Job’s claim that his righteousness is more than God’s: look to the heavens, he says — if you sin, what do you do against God, and if you are righteous, what do you give him? Your wickedness may hurt a man like yourself, and your righteousness profit a son of man, but God is high above both. Men cry out under oppression yet do not ask, where is God my maker, who gives songs in the night — and God, Elihu says, will not answer empty cries or regard vanity. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Elihu spoke moreover, and said. This is Elihu's third speech, turning to Job's claim about the profit of righteousness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Thinkest thou this to be right, that thou saidst, my righteousness is more than God's? JFB renders it as I am righteous before God: Elihu means Job said so virtually, not in so many words. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. For thou saidst, what advantage will it be unto thee, and what profit shall I have if I be cleansed from my sin? JFB explains this as explanatory of verse 2: what advantage is thy integrity to thee, what profit more than if thou hadst sinned, words the wicked use which Job virtually sanctioned by saying they do not suffer for it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Elihu will answer Job, and his companions with him. JFB explains companions as those entertaining like sentiments with Job. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Look unto the heavens, and see, and behold the clouds which are higher than thou. JFB notes Elihu, like Eliphaz, shows God is too exalted to be susceptible of benefit or hurt from men's righteousness or sin: the clouds are higher than Job, yet not as high as God's seat, so God has no inducement to injustice, and when he afflicts it must be for the good of the sufferer. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. If Job sins, what does he against God? Or if his transgressions be multiplied, what does he unto him? JFB renders it as how canst thou affect him, that can hurt him? Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. If Job be righteous, what gives he unto God, or what receives he of Job's hand? God gains nothing from man's righteousness, as the Psalms and Proverbs affirm. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Job's wickedness may hurt a man as he is, and his righteousness may profit the son of man. It is themselves, not God, that men benefit by righteousness or hurt by sin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. By reason of the multitude of oppressions the oppressed cry out by reason of the arm of the mighty. JFB notes Elihu states in Job's words the difficulty: the cries of the oppressed not being heard might lead man to think wrongs are not punished by God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. But none says, where is God my maker, who gives songs in the night? JFB explains the reason: innocent sufferers often do not humbly seek God for succour, so pride is to blame for their ruin, and if the sufferer would apply to God with a humbled, penitent spirit, he would hear, with songs as joy at deliverance, and in the night as unexpectedly or in calamity. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Who teaches us more than the beasts of the earth, and makes us wiser than the fowls of heaven? JFB notes man's spirit, which distinguishes him from the brute, is the strongest proof of God's beneficence, by which we may understand God is the Almighty helper of all sufferers who humbly seek him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. There they cry, but none gives answer, because of the pride of evil men. JFB renders there as then, when none humbly casts himself on God: they cry proudly against God rather than humbly to God, so there can be no answer until pride gives place to humble, penitent prayer. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Surely God will not hear vanity, neither will the Almighty regard it. JFB renders vanity as cries uttered in an unhumbled spirit, which applies in some degree to Job's cries, and still more to those of the wicked. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Although Job says he shall not see God, yet judgment is before him, therefore let him trust in him. JFB explains judgment as thy cause, thy right, and trust as wait thou on him patiently until he take up thy cause, noting Elihu alludes to Job's words about not seeing God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. But now, because it is not so, God has visited in his anger, yet he knows it not in great extremity. JFB explains that because Job waited not trustingly and patiently, God has visited, yet still has not taken severe cognizance of the great multitude of sins, so Job should not complain of being punished with undue severity. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Therefore Job opens his mouth in vain, and multiplies words without knowledge. JFB calls this the apodosis to verse 15, with in vain as rashly. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
