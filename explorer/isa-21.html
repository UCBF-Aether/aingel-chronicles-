The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Isaiah 21 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 The burden of the desert of the sea. As whirlwinds in the south pass through; so it cometh from the desert, from a terrible land.

2 A grievous vision is declared unto me; the treacherous dealer dealeth treacherously, and the spoiler spoileth. Go up, O Elam: besiege, O Media; all the sighing thereof have I made to cease.

3 Therefore are my loins filled with pain: pangs have taken hold upon me, as the pangs of a woman that travaileth: I was bowed down at the hearing of it; I was dismayed at the seeing of it.

4 My heart panted, fearfulness affrighted me: the night of my pleasure hath he turned into fear unto me.

5 Prepare the table, watch in the watchtower, eat, drink: arise, ye princes, and anoint the shield.

6 For thus hath the LORD said unto me, Go, set a watchman, let him declare what he seeth.

7 And he saw a chariot with a couple of horsemen, a chariot of asses, and a chariot of camels; and he hearkened diligently with much heed:

8 And he cried, A lion: My lord, I stand continually upon the watchtower in the daytime, and I am set in my ward whole nights:

9 And, behold, here cometh a chariot of men, with a couple of horsemen. And he answered and said, Babylon is fallen, is fallen; and all the graven images of her gods he hath broken unto the ground.

10 O my threshing, and the corn of my floor: that which I have heard of the LORD of hosts, the God of Israel, have I declared unto you.

11 The burden of Dumah. He calleth to me out of Seir, Watchman, what of the night? Watchman, what of the night?

12 The watchman said, The morning cometh, and also the night: if ye will enquire, enquire ye: return, come.

13 The burden upon Arabia. In the forest in Arabia shall ye lodge, O ye travelling companies of Dedanim.

14 The inhabitants of the land of Tema brought water to him that was thirsty, they prevented with their bread him that fled.

15 For they fled from the swords, from the drawn sword, and from the bent bow, and from the grievousness of war.

16 For thus hath the LORD said unto me, Within a year, according to the years of an hireling, and all the glory of Kedar shall fail:

17 And the residue of the number of archers, the mighty men of the children of Kedar, shall be diminished: for the LORD God of Israel hath spoken it.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

A watchman stationed over Babylon sees its fall announced — 'Babylon is fallen, is fallen' — and the chapter adds shorter oracles: a night enquiry from Dumah in Seir, and the fading glory of Kedar's archers within a year. All three are assurances to Jews facing Babylonian captivity that their enemy will be destroyed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. The burden of the desert of the sea: As whirlwinds in the south pass through, so it comes from the desert, from a terrible land. This repeats the assurance of chapters 13–14 to the Jews about to be captives in Babylon, that their enemy will be destroyed and they delivered; the prophet supposes himself a watchman in Babylon beholding events as they pass. The desert is the plain between Babylon and Persia, once desert and to become so again, the sea is the plain covered with Euphrates water until Semiramis raised dams which Cyrus removed, and the terrible land is Media, whose south whirlwinds come unbroken across the plain. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. A grievous vision is declared to Isaiah: the treacherous dealer deals treacherously, and the spoiler spoils. Go up, O Elam; besiege, O Media; all the sighing thereof God has made to cease. The treachery refers to Cyrus's military stratagem in taking Babylon, or Babylon repaid with treachery in her own coin, and Isaiah abruptly recites God's order to the Persians, his instruments of vengeance; Elam is a province of Persia east of the Euphrates, and the sighing made to cease is the sighing caused by Babylon. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Therefore Isaiah's loins are filled with pain, pangs have taken hold of him as the pangs of a woman that travails, and he is bowed down at the hearing of it and dismayed at the seeing of it. Imagining himself among the exiles in Babylon, the prophet cannot help feeling moved by the calamities coming on it, as he was moved for Moab; the Hebrew may mean he was so bowed down he could not hear, and so dismayed he could not see. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. His heart panted, fearfulness affrighted him, and the night of his pleasure he has turned into fear unto him. His heart is bewildered, and he supposes himself one of the banqueters at Belshazzar's feast on the night Babylon was taken by surprise, hence "my pleasure" turned to fear. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Prepare the table, watch in the watchtower, eat, drink: arise, you princes, and anoint the shield. Isaiah first represents the king ordering the feast ready, during which Cyrus opened the dykes confining the Euphrates so he could enter Babylon by the river channel; then the language turns suddenly to alarm for the Babylonian princes, who had set the watch and thought they might feast in security, and are now called to anoint their shields — oiling the leather lest it crack — and make ready for instant self-defence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. For thus has the Lord said to Isaiah: Go, set a watchman, let him declare what he sees. This is God's direction to set a watchman to declare what he sees, and Isaiah himself is represented as the one who declares, or as the watchman on the tower reporting what he sees. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. When the watchman sees a chariot with a couple of horsemen, a chariot of asses, and a chariot of camels, he will hearken diligently with much heed. The chariots are rather a body of riders, some riding in pairs on horses two abreast, others on asses, others on camels, for the Persians used asses and camels for war, and the joint army of Medes and Persians appears under their respective leaders. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. And he cried, A lion: My lord, I stand continually upon the watchtower in the daytime, and I am set in my ward whole nights. The watchman cries, I am as a lion: the point of comparison is vigilance, for the lion's eyelids are short so that even asleep he seems on watch, and hence lions were painted on temple doors as symbols of watchfulness guarding the place. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Behold, here comes a chariot of men, with a couple of horsemen, and he answered and said: Babylon is fallen, is fallen, and all the graven images of her gods he has broken unto the ground. The first half describes what the watchman sees — the same body of riders two abreast — and the second what he says, for in the interval the overthrow is accomplished, needing announcement because of the city's great extent; the repetition, fallen, fallen, expresses emphasis and certainty, and the Persians, who had no images or temples, dashed Babylon's images of Bel, Merodach, and the rest to the ground. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. O my threshing, and the corn of my floor: that which I have heard of the Lord of hosts, the God of Israel, have I declared unto you. My threshing is God's people, the Jews trodden down by Babylon, and the corn of the floor is his people treated as corn laid on the floor for threshing, implying that by affliction a remnant, grain, would be separated from the ungodly, chaff; Isaiah addresses his countrymen, not the Babylonians. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. The burden of Dumah: He calls to Isaiah out of Seir, Watchman, what of the night? Watchman, what of the night? This is a prophecy to the Idumeans who taunted the afflicted Jews in the Babylonian captivity: one out of Seir asks what of the night, whether there is hope of dawn deliverance, and the repetition marks the Idumeans' anxiety. Dumah is a tribe and region of Ishmael in Arabia, a part put for the whole of Edom, and its name in Hebrew means silence, implying Edom will soon be reduced to silence; Seir is the principal mountain in Idumea, and the watchman is the prophet announcing future events from his tower. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. The watchman said: The morning comes, and also the night; if you will inquire, inquire, and return, come. The morning of prosperity comes, and soon after follows the night of adversity, for though the Idumeans may have a gleam of prosperity it will soon be followed by adversity again — or, prosperity comes to the Jews to be quickly followed by adversity to the Idumeans who exulted in Jerusalem's fall; and if they wish to ask again they may, but if they want an answer of peace they must return, be converted to God, and come. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. The burden upon Arabia: In the forest in Arabia you will lodge, O you travelling companies of Dedanim. This prophecy that Arabia would be overrun by a foreign foe within a year, probably in the wars between Assyria and Egypt, has the caravans driven through fear to unfrequented routes; the forest is not a grove of trees but a region of thick underwood, rugged and inaccessible, for Arabia has no forest of trees, and Dedanim is in North Arabia. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. The inhabitants of the land of Tema brought water to him that was thirsty, and they prevented with their bread him that fled. Tema is a kindred tribe, an oasis in that region, and the Temeans give water to the faint and thirsting Dedanites — the greatest act of hospitality in the burning East where water is scarce — and anticipate the fugitives' wants by supplying the bread due to them for support. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. For they fled from the swords, from the drawn sword, and from the bent bow, and from the grievousness of war. The fugitive Dedanites and other Arabs flee from the drawn sword, the bent bow, and the grievousness of war brought by the invading foe. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. For thus has the Lord said to Isaiah: Within a year, according to the years of a hireling, all the glory of Kedar will fail. As a hireling's fixed term can neither be added to nor taken from, so Kedar's fall is fixed within a year; Kedar is a wandering tribe north of Arabia-Petraea, put for Arabia in general. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. The residue of the number of archers, the mighty men of the children of Kedar, will be diminished, for the Lord God of Israel has spoken it. The remnant of Arab warriors, famous in the bow, left after the invasion will be small, and the sentence stands because the Lord God of Israel has spoken it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
