The Text — Psalms 106 (King James Version (1611) for Psalms 1–150; Charles Thomson, The Septuagint (1808) for Psalm 151)

1 Praise ye the LORD. O give thanks unto the LORD; for he is good: for his mercy endureth for ever.

2 Who can utter the mighty acts of the LORD? who can shew forth all his praise?

3 Blessed are they that keep judgment, and he that doeth righteousness at all times.

4 Remember me, O LORD, with the favour that thou bearest unto thy people: O visit me with thy salvation;

5 That I may see the good of thy chosen, that I may rejoice in the gladness of thy nation, that I may glory with thine inheritance.

6 We have sinned with our fathers, we have committed iniquity, we have done wickedly.

7 Our fathers understood not thy wonders in Egypt; they remembered not the multitude of thy mercies; but provoked him at the sea, even at the Red sea.

8 Nevertheless he saved them for his name’s sake, that he might make his mighty power to be known.

9 He rebuked the Red sea also, and it was dried up: so he led them through the depths, as through the wilderness.

10 And he saved them from the hand of him that hated them, and redeemed them from the hand of the enemy.

11 And the waters covered their enemies: there was not one of them left.

12 Then believed they his words; they sang his praise.

13 They soon forgat his works; they waited not for his counsel:

14 But lusted exceedingly in the wilderness, and tempted God in the desert.

15 And he gave them their request; but sent leanness into their soul.

16 They envied Moses also in the camp, and Aaron the saint of the LORD.

17 The earth opened and swallowed up Dathan and covered the company of Abiram.

18 And a fire was kindled in their company; the flame burned up the wicked.

19 They made a calf in Horeb, and worshipped the molten image.

20 Thus they changed their glory into the similitude of an ox that eateth grass.

21 They forgat God their saviour, which had done great things in Egypt;

22 Wondrous works in the land of Ham, and terrible things by the Red sea.

23 Therefore he said that he would destroy them, had not Moses his chosen stood before him in the breach, to turn away his wrath, lest he should destroy them.

24 Yea, they despised the pleasant land, they believed not his word:

25 But murmured in their tents, and hearkened not unto the voice of the LORD.

26 Therefore he lifted up his hand against them, to overthrow them in the wilderness:

27 To overthrow their seed also among the nations, and to scatter them in the lands.

28 They joined themselves also unto Baalpeor, and ate the sacrifices of the dead.

29 Thus they provoked him to anger with their inventions: and the plague brake in upon them.

30 Then stood up Phinehas, and executed judgment: and so the plague was stayed.

31 And that was counted unto him for righteousness unto all generations for evermore.

32 They angered him also at the waters of strife, so that it went ill with Moses for their sakes:

33 Because they provoked his spirit, so that he spake unadvisedly with his lips.

34 They did not destroy the nations, concerning whom the LORD commanded them:

35 But were mingled among the heathen, and learned their works.

36 And they served their idols: which were a snare unto them.

37 Yea, they sacrificed their sons and their daughters unto devils,

38 And shed innocent blood, even the blood of their sons and of their daughters, whom they sacrificed unto the idols of Canaan: and the land was polluted with blood.

39 Thus were they defiled with their own works, and went a whoring with their own inventions.

40 Therefore was the wrath of the LORD kindled against his people, insomuch that he abhorred his own inheritance.

41 And he gave them into the hand of the heathen; and they that hated them ruled over them.

42 Their enemies also oppressed them, and they were brought into subjection under their hand.

43 Many times did he deliver them; but they provoked him with their counsel, and were brought low for their iniquity.

44 Nevertheless he regarded their affliction, when he heard their cry:

45 And he remembered for them his covenant, and repented according to the multitude of his mercies.

46 He made them also to be pitied of all those that carried them captives.

47 Save us, O LORD our God, and gather us from among the heathen, to give thanks unto thy holy name, and to triumph in thy praise.

48 Blessed be the LORD God of Israel from everlasting to everlasting: and let all the people say, Amen. Praise ye the LORD.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

The companion history to Psalm 105, told from the other side: praise and thanks for a mercy that endures for ever, followed by confession that "we have sinned with our fathers." The fathers understood not God's wonders in Egypt and provoked him at the Red Sea, yet he saved them for his name's sake; in the wilderness they lusted, murmured, despised the pleasant land, and were saved again and again only for Moses to stand in the breach. In the land they mingled with the heathen, served idols and shed innocent blood, and were oppressed and brought low — yet many times God regarded their affliction, heard their cry, and remembered his covenant. It closes with a prayer to be saved and gathered from among the heathen to give thanks and triumph in praise. Interpreted Psalm text above; study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Praise ye the Lord, O give thanks unto the Lord, for he is good, for his mercy endureth for ever, opens the psalm in praise before the confession that follows: the speaker blesses God for his goodness and his mighty acts, which are too many and too mysterious to be numbered or exhausted, and sets the whole recital of Israel's sins inside thanksgiving — for even the remembrance of former sins, notwithstanding which God did not cast off his people, encourages hope that though justly corrected we shall not be utterly abandoned. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 2. Who can utter the mighty acts of the Lord, who can shew forth all his praise, confesses that God's mighty acts are too many and too mysterious to be numbered or exhausted: no tongue can utter them all, no praise can show them all forth — yet the attempt itself is the psalm's business, to bless God for goodness and mighty acts beyond reckoning. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 3. Blessed are they that keep judgment, and he that doeth righteousness at all times, places blessedness not in occasional right but in steady constancy: blessed are those who keep judgment and do righteousness at all times — steadily and constantly, not by fits and starts. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 4. Remember me, O Lord, with the favour that thou bearest unto thy people, O visit me with thy salvation, is a personal plea inside the national prayer: remember me with the favour borne to thy people, visit me with thy salvation — that the speaker may share the good of the chosen, the gladness of the nation, and the glory of the inheritance, as everlasting joy and glory. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 5. Seeing the good of the chosen, gladness of the nation, glory of the inheritance is Henry's definition of enough: a peculiar good that is both pleasure and praise, cheerfulness and boasting in God, with everlasting joy and glory at its end. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 6. We have sinned with our fathers, we have committed iniquity, we have done wickedly, begins a penitential confession seasonable now that the church was in distress: thus we justify God in all he brings upon us, acknowledging that therefore he has done right because we have done wickedly, and the remembrance of former sins, notwithstanding which God did not cast off his people, encourages us to hope that though justly corrected for our sins we shall not be utterly abandoned. God's afflicted people own themselves guilty: we have sinned with our fathers, like our fathers, after the similitude of their transgression, adding to the stock of hereditary guilt and filling up the measure of our fathers' iniquity, to augment yet the fierce anger of the Lord. And they lay a load upon themselves, as becomes penitents: we have committed iniquity, that which is in its own nature sinful, and we have done wickedly, sinning with a high hand presumptuously — or this is a confession not only of imitation of but interest in their fathers' sins, for we were in their loins and bear their iniquity. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 7. Our fathers understood not thy wonders in Egypt, remembered not the multitude of thy mercies, but provoked him at the sea, even at the Red Sea, shows the strange stupidity of Israel amid God's favours: they saw his wonders but did not rightly apprehend their meaning and design. Blessed are those that have not seen and yet have understood. They thought Egypt's plagues were intended only for their deliverance, whereas they were intended also for their instruction and conviction — not only to force them out of Egyptian slavery but to cure them of their inclination to Egyptian idolatry, by evidencing the sovereign power and dominion of Israel's God above all gods and his particular concern for them. We lose the benefit of providences for want of understanding them. And as their understandings were dull, so their memories were treacherous: though one would think such astonishing events should never have been forgotten, yet they remembered not the multitude of God's mercies in them, and therefore God is distrusted because his favours are not remembered. Their perverseness arising from this stupidity was provoking him at the sea: despair of deliverance because the danger was great, and wishing they had been left in Egypt still, quarrelling with God's providence and questioning his power, goodness and faithfulness, as great provocations as any. The place aggravated the crime — at the sea, at the Red Sea, when newly out of Egypt with God's wonders fresh in their minds, yet reproaching him as if all that power had no mercy in it, but he had brought them out on purpose to kill them in the wilderness. They never lay at God's mercy so immediately as in their passage through the Red Sea, yet there they affronted it and provoked his wrath. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 8. Nevertheless he saved them for his name's sake, that he might make his mighty power to be known, answers why God did not cover them as he did their enemies for their unbelief and murmuring: it was for his name's sake. Though they did not deserve this favour, he designed it, and their undeservings should not alter his designs, nor break his measures, nor make him withdraw his promise or fail in its performance. He did this for his own glory, that he might make his mighty power known, not only in dividing the sea but in doing it notwithstanding their provocations — as Moses prayed, let the power of my Lord be great and pardon the iniquity of this people. The power of the God of grace in pardoning sin and sparing sinners is as much to be admired as the power of the God of nature in dividing the waters. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 9. He rebuked the Red Sea also, and it was dried up, so he led them through the depths, as through the wilderness, shows God forcing a passage for them: he rebuked the Red Sea for standing in their way and retarding their march, and it was dried up immediately, as in creation at God's rebuke the waters fled. Nay, he not only prepared them a way, but by the pillar of cloud and fire he led them into the sea, and by Moses' conduct led them through it as readily as through the wilderness, encouraging them to take those steps and subduing their fears, when those were their most dangerous and threatening enemies. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 10. And he saved them from the hand of him that hated them, and redeemed them from the hand of the enemy, shows God interposing between them and their pursuers and preventing them from cutting them off as designed: the Israelites were all on foot and the Egyptians all had chariots and horses with which they were likely to overtake them quickly, but God saved them from the hand of him that hated them — Pharaoh, who never loved them but now hated them the more for the plagues suffered on their account. From the hand of his enemy, just ready to seize them, God redeemed them, interposing himself, as it were, in the pillar of fire, between the persecuted and the persecutors. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 11. And the waters covered their enemies, there was not one of them left, completes the mercy and turns the deliverance into a victory: the Red Sea, which was a lane to them, was a grave to the Egyptians — the waters covered their enemies so as to slay them, but not so as to conceal their shame, for the next tide they were thrown up dead upon the shore. There was not one of them left alive to bring tidings of what had become of the rest. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 12. Then believed they his words, they sang his praise, is the good impression this made upon them for the present: then they believed his words and acknowledged that God was with them of a truth and had in mercy brought them out of Egypt, not with any design to slay them in the wilderness; then they feared the Lord and his servant Moses, and sang his praise in Moses' song penned on this great occasion. See in what a gracious and merciful way God sometimes silences his people's unbelief and turns their fears into praises — and so it is written, those that erred in spirit shall come to understanding, and those that murmured shall learn doctrine. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 13. They soon forgat his works, they waited not for his counsel, is disregard of God's works and word: soon forgetting his works, making haste, waiting not for his counsel. They took up resolves about which they did not consult him and made demands without calling upon him; they would be in Canaan directly, and had not patience to tarry God's time. The delay was intolerable, and therefore the difficulties were looked upon as insuperable. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 14. But lusted exceedingly in the wilderness, and tempted God in the desert, is their first wilderness sin after forgetting God's works: they would have flesh, yet would not believe God could give it to them. They lusted a lust in the wilderness, where they had bread enough and to spare, yet nothing would serve them but flesh to eat. They were now purely at God's finding, supported entirely by miracles, so that this was a reflection upon the wisdom and goodness of their Creator; they were also, in all probability, within a step of Canaan, yet had not patience to stay for dainties till they came thither. They had flocks and herds of their own but would not kill them — God must give them flesh as he gave them bread, or they will never give him credit or their good word. They did not only wish for flesh, but lusted exceedingly after it: a desire, even of lawful things, when inordinate and violent, becomes sinful, and therefore this is called lusting after evil things, though the quails as God's gift were good things. Yet this was not all: they tempted God in the desert, where they had had such experience of his goodness and power, and questioned whether he could and would gratify them herein. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 15. And he gave them their request, but sent leanness into their soul, shows how God showed displeasure for their lusting: he gave them their request, but gave it in anger and with a curse, for he sent leanness into their soul — filling them with uneasiness of mind, terror of conscience and self-reproach, occasioned by their bodies being sick with the surfeit, such as drunkards experience after a great debauch. Or this is put for that great plague with which the Lord smote them while the flesh was yet between their teeth, the consumption of the life. Note: what is asked in passion is often given in wrath; many that fare deliciously every day, whose bodies are healthful and fat, have at the same time leanness in their souls — no love to God, no thankfulness, no appetite to the bread of life — and then the soul must needs be lean. Those wretchedly forget themselves who feast their bodies and starve their souls. Then God gives the good things of this life in love, when with them he gives grace to glorify him in their use, for then the soul delights itself in fatness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 16. And envied Moses in the camp, and Aaron the saint of the Lord, is their quarrel with the government God had set over them in church and state: they envied Moses his authority in the camp, as generalissimo of Israel's armies and chief justice in all their courts, and envied Aaron his power as saint of the Lord, consecrated to the office of high priest — Korah would needs put in for the pontificate, while Dathan and Abiram, as princes of Reuben, Jacob's eldest son, would claim to be chief magistrates by the so-much-admired right of primogeniture. Those are preparing ruin for themselves who envy those whom God has put honour upon and usurp dignities they were never designed for, and justly will contempt be poured upon those who put contempt upon any of the saints of the Lord. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 17. The earth opened and swallowed up Dathan, and covered the company of Abiram, is God's displeasure for flying in the face of civil authority: those that flew in the face of the civil authority were punished by the earth, which opened and swallowed them up, as not fit to go upon God's ground, because they would not submit to God's government. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 18. And a fire was kindled in their company, the flame burned up the wicked, is God's displeasure for usurping ecclesiastical authority: those who would usurp authority in things pertaining to God suffered the vengeance of heaven, for fire came out from the Lord and consumed them, and the pretending sacrificers were themselves sacrificed to divine justice. The flame burnt up the wicked — for though they vied with Aaron, the saint of the Lord, for holiness, yet God adjudged them wicked, and as such cut them off, as in due time he will destroy the man of sin, that wicked one, notwithstanding his proud pretensions to holiness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 19. They made a calf in Horeb, and worshipped the molten image, was done in Horeb, where the law was given and where God had expressly said, thou shalt neither make any graven image nor bow down to it — they did both, making a calf and worshipping it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 20. Thus they changed their glory into the similitude of an ox that eateth grass, bade defiance to human reason itself: they changed their glory — their God, at least the manifestation of him, which always had been in a cloud, either dark or bright, without any manner of visible similitude — into the similitude of Apis, one of the Egyptian idols, an ox that eateth grass, than which nothing could be more grossly and scandalously absurd. Idolaters are perfectly besotted, and put the greatest disparagement possible both upon God, in representing him by the image of a beast, and upon themselves, in worshipping it when they have so done. That changing of their glory is explained by St. Paul to be the changing of the glory of the incorruptible God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 21. They forgat God their saviour, which had done great things in Egypt, is the root of the calf sin: they forgot God their Saviour — that is, they forgot that he had been their Saviour, for those who forget the works of God forget God himself, who makes himself known by his works. They forgot what was done but a few days before, which we may suppose they could not but talk of, even then, when because they did not make a good use of it they are said to forget it: what God did for them in Egypt, in the land of Ham, and by the Red Sea — things which we at this distance cannot, or should not, be unmindful of. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 22. Wondrous works in the land of Ham, and terrible things by the Red Sea, are the works they forgot: they forgot God their Saviour, that is, they forgot that he had been their Saviour, for those who forget the works of God forget God himself, who makes himself known by his works. They forgot what was done but a few days before, which we may suppose they could not but talk of, even then, when because they did not make a good use of it they are said to forget it: what God did for them in Egypt, in the land of Ham, and by the Red Sea — things which we at this distance cannot, or should not, be unmindful of. They are called great things, for though the great God does nothing mean, yet he does some things in a special manner great; wondrous works, out of the common road of providence, therefore observable, therefore memorable; and terrible things, awful to them and dreadful to their enemies — and yet soon forgotten, for even miracles that were seen passed away with them as tales that are told. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 23. Moses standing in the breach turns a decree of cutting off into a type: Henry sees mercy easily turning anger, prayer's power, and Christ the chosen intercessor ever living to turn wrath from a provoking world. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 24. And they despised the pleasant land, they believed not his word, is their giving credit to the evil spies' report in contradiction to God's promise: Canaan was a pleasant land, yet they undervalued it when they thought it not worth venturing for, no, not under God's own guidance, and were for making a captain and returning to Egypt again. They believed not God's word concerning it, but murmured in their tents, basely charging God with a design upon them in bringing them thither that they might become a prey to the Canaanites. And when reminded of God's power and promise, they were so far from hearkening to the Lord's voice that they attempted to stone those who spoke to them. The heavenly Canaan is a pleasant land, a promise is left us of entering into it, but many despise it, neglecting and refusing the offer, preferring this world's wealth and pleasure before it, and grudging this life's pains and hazards to obtain it. This also so displeased God that he lifted up his hand against them in threatening, to destroy them in the wilderness, nay, in swearing, for he swore in his wrath that they should not enter into his rest — nay, he threatened that their children also should be overthrown and scattered, and the whole nation dispersed and disinherited, but Moses prevailed for mercy for their seed, that they might enter Canaan. Those who despise God's favours, and particularly the pleasant land, forfeit his favours and will be shut out for ever from the pleasant land. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 25. But murmured in their tents, and hearkened not unto the voice of the Lord, continues their unbelief: they believed not his word, his promise that he would make them masters of Canaan, and hearkened not to the voice of the Lord, who gave them counsel which they would not wait for, not only by Moses and Aaron but by Caleb and Joshua. Those who will not wait for God's counsel shall justly be given up to their own hearts' lusts, to walk in their own counsels. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 26. Therefore he lifted up his hand against them, to overthrow them in the wilderness, is God's threatening for despising the land: he lifted up his hand in threatening to destroy them in the wilderness, nay, in swearing, for he swore in his wrath that they should not enter into his rest. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 27. To overthrow their seed also among the nations, and to scatter them in the lands, extends the threat: he threatened that their children also should be overthrown and scattered, and the whole nation dispersed and disinherited — but Moses prevailed for mercy for their seed, that they might enter Canaan. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 28. They joined themselves also unto Baal-peor, and ate the sacrifices of the dead, was the sin of the new generation, when within a step of Canaan: they joined themselves to Baal-peor, and so were entangled both in idolatry and in adultery, in corporeal and in spiritual whoredom. Those that did often partake of the altar of the living God now ate the sacrifices of the dead, of Moab's idols, dead images or dead men canonized or deified, or sacrifices to infernal deities on behalf of their dead friends. Thus they provoked God to anger with their inventions, in contempt of him and his institutions, his commands and his threatenings. The iniquity of Peor was so great that, long after, it is said they were not cleansed from it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 29. Thus they provoked him to anger with their inventions, and the plague brake in upon them, is Peor's fruit: they provoked God to anger with their inventions, in contempt of him and his institutions, his commands, and his threatenings. The iniquity of Peor was so great that, long after, it is said they were not cleansed from it — and God testified his displeasure by sending a plague among them, which in a little time swept away 24,000 of those impudent sinners. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 30. Then stood up Phinehas, and executed judgment, and so the plague was stayed, shows God's displeasure answered by national justice: God testified displeasure by sending a plague which in a little time swept away 24,000 of those impudent sinners, and by stirring up Phinehas to use his power as magistrate for suppressing the sin and checking its contagion. He stood up in his zeal for the Lord of hosts and executed judgment upon Zimri and Cozbi, sinners of the first rank, genteel sinners, putting the law in execution upon them, and this was a service so pleasing to God that upon it the plague was stayed. By this and some other similar acts of public justice on that occasion, the guilt ceased to be national and the general controversy was let fall. When the proper officers did their duty, God left it to them and did not any longer keep the work in his own hands by the plague. Note: national justice prevents national judgments. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 31. And that was counted unto him for righteousness unto all generations, is Phinehas' special mark of honour: for signalizing himself, what he did was counted to him for righteousness to all generations, and in recompence the priesthood was entailed on his family. He shall make an atonement by offering up the sacrifices, who had so bravely made an atonement by offering up the sinners. Note: it is the honour of saints to be zealous against sin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 32. They angered him also at the waters of strife, so that it went ill with Moses for their sakes, continues their murmurings to the very last of their wanderings: in the fortieth year they angered God at the waters of strife, and what aggravated it now was that it went ill with Moses for their sakes. For though he was the meekest of all men in the earth, yet their clamours at that time were so peevish and provoking that they put him into a passion, and having now grown very old and off his guard, he spoke unadvisedly with his lips, and not as became him on that occasion — saying in a heat, hear now, ye rebels, must we fetch water out of this rock for you? This was Moses' infirmity, written for our admonition, that we may learn, when in the midst of provocation, to keep our mouth as with a bridle, and to take heed to our spirits, that they admit not resentments too much — for when the spirit is provoked, it is much ado, even for those with much wisdom and grace, not to speak unadvisedly. But it is charged upon the people as their sin: they provoked his spirit with that with which they angered God himself. We must answer not only for our own passions but for the provocation which by them we give to the passions of others, especially of those who, if not greatly provoked, would be meek and quiet. God showed displeasure against this sin by shutting Moses and Aaron out of Canaan for their misconduct on this occasion, by which he discovered his resentment of all such intemperate heats, even in the dearest of his servants — if he deals thus severely with Moses for one unadvised word, what does their sin deserve who have spoken so many presumptuous wicked words? If this was done in the green tree, what shall be done in the dry? — and by depriving them of the blessing of Moses' guidance and government at a time when they most needed it, so that his death was more a punishment to them than to himself. It is just with God to remove those relations from us that are blessings to us, when we are peevish and provoking to them and grieve their spirits. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 33. Because they provoked his spirit, so that he spake unadvisedly with his lips, is charged upon the people as their sin: they provoked his spirit with that with which they angered God himself. We must answer not only for our own passions, but for the provocation which by them we give to the passions of others, especially of those who, if not greatly provoked, would be meek and quiet. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 34. They did not destroy the nations, concerning whom the Lord commanded them, begins Israel's conduct in Canaan, of a piece with the wilderness: the miracles and mercies settling them in Canaan made no more deep and durable impressions than those fetching them out of Egypt, for by the time they were just settled in Canaan they corrupted themselves and forsook God. The first step of their apostasy was sparing the nations God had doomed to destruction: when they had got the good land promised them they had no zeal against the wicked inhabitants whom the Lord commanded them to extirpate, pretending pity — but so merciful is God that no man needs to be in any case more compassionate than he. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 35. But were mingled among the heathen, and learned their works, is the next step downhill: when they spared them they promised themselves that, notwithstanding this, they would not join in any dangerous affinity with them — but the way of sin is downhill, omissions make way for commissions. When they neglected to destroy the heathen, the next news is they were mingled among the heathen, making leagues with them and contracting an intimacy with them, so that they learned their works. That which is rotten will sooner corrupt that which is sound than be cured or made sound by it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 36. And they served their idols, which were a snare unto them, is the next degree: when they mingled with them and learned some of their works that seemed innocent diversions and entertainments, yet they thought they would never join with them in their worship — but by degrees they learned that too, serving their idols in the same manner and with the same rites that they served them, and they became a snare to them. That sin drew on many more and brought God's judgments upon them, which they themselves could not but be sensible of and yet knew not how to recover themselves. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 37. Yea, they sacrificed their sons and their daughters unto devils, is the last depth: when they joined with the heathen in some idolatrous services which they thought had least harm in them, they little thought they should ever be guilty of that barbarous and inhuman piece of idolatry, the sacrificing of their living children to their dead gods — but they came to that at last, in which Satan triumphed over his worshippers and regaled himself in blood and slaughter. They sacrificed their sons and daughters, pieces of themselves, to devils, and added murder, the most unnatural murder, to their idolatry — one cannot think of it without horror. They shed innocent blood, the most innocent, for it was infant-blood, the blood of their sons and daughters. See the power of the spirit that works in the children of disobedience, and see his malice. The beginning of idolatry and superstition, like that of strife, is as the letting forth of water, and there is no villany which those who venture upon it can be sure they shall stop short of, for God justly gives them up to a reprobate mind. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 38. And shed innocent blood, even the blood of their sons and daughters, whom they sacrificed unto the idols of Canaan, and the land was polluted with blood, is their sin as its own punishment upon the country: they shed innocent blood, the most innocent, for it was infant-blood, the blood of their sons and daughters — and by it they wronged their country, for the pleasant land, the holy land, was polluted with blood and rendered uncomfortable to themselves, and unfit to receive those kind tokens of God's favour and presence in it which were designed to be its honour. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 39. And were defiled with their own works, and went a whoring with their own inventions, is their sin as its own punishment in conscience: by it they wronged their consciences — they went a whoring with their own inventions, and so debauched their own minds, and were defiled with their own works, and rendered odious in the eyes of the holy God, and perhaps of their own consciences. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 40. Therefore was the wrath of the Lord kindled against his people, insomuch that he abhorred his own inheritance, follows necessarily, for his name is Jealous and he is a jealous God: he fell out with them for it, for his wrath, that consuming fire, was kindled against his people, taking it from them as more insulting and ungrateful than from the heathen that never knew him — nay, he was sick of them and abhorred his own inheritance, which once he had taken pleasure in, yet the change was not in him but in them. This is the worst thing in sin, that it makes us loathsome to God, and the nearer any are to God in profession the more loathsome are they if they rebel against him, like a dunghill at our door. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 41. And he gave them into the hand of the heathen, and they that hated them ruled over them, shows the punishment answering to the sin: their enemies then fell upon them, and their defence having departed, made an easy prey of them. They mingled with the heathen and learned their works, from them they willingly took the infection of sin, and therefore God justly made use of them as the instruments of their correction. Sinners often see themselves ruined by those by whom they have suffered themselves to be debauched — Satan, who is a tempter, will be a tormentor. The heathen hated them: apostates lose all the love on God's side and get none on Satan's, and when those that hated them ruled over them and they were brought into subjection under them, no marvel that they oppressed them and ruled them with rigour, and thus God made them know the difference between his service and the service of the kings of the countries. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 42. And their enemies oppressed them, and they were brought into subjection under their hand, continues that subjection: when those that hated them ruled over them, they oppressed them and ruled them with rigour, and thus God made them know the difference between his service and the service of the kings of the countries. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 43. Many times did he deliver them, but they provoked him with their counsel, and were brought low for their iniquity, refers to the days of the Judges: when God granted them some relief, yet they went on in their sins and their troubles also were continued, for God often raised up deliverers and wrought deliverances for them, and yet they relapsed to idolatry and provoked God with their counsel, their idolatrous inventions, to deliver them up to some other oppressor, so that at last they were brought very low for their iniquity. Those that by sin disparage themselves and will not by repentance humble themselves are justly debased, humbled and brought low by God's judgments. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 44. Nevertheless he regarded their affliction, when he heard their cry, shows them chastened but not destroyed, cast down but not cast off: at length they cried unto God and God returned in favour to them. God appeared for them as a God of mercy, who looked upon their grievances, regarded their affliction, beheld when distress was upon them, looked over their complaints, for he heard their cry with tender compassion and overlooked their provocations — for though he had said, and had reason to say it, that he would destroy them, yet he repented, according to the multitude of his mercies, and reversed the sentence. Though he is not a man that he should repent so as to change his mind, yet he is a gracious God, who pities us and changes his way. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 45. And he remembered for them his covenant, and repented according to the multitude of his mercies, shows God as a God of truth: he remembered for them his covenant and made good every word he had spoken, and therefore, bad as they were, he would not break with them, because he would not break his own promise. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 46. He made them also to be pitied of all those that carried them captives, shows God as a God of power, who has all hearts in his hand and turns them which way soever he pleases: he made them to be pitied even of those that carried them captives and hated them and ruled them with rigour. He not only restrained the remainder of their enemies' wrath, that it should not utterly consume them, but he infused compassion even into their stony hearts and made them relent, which was more than any art of man could have done with the utmost force of rhetoric. God can change lions into lambs, and when a man's ways please the Lord he will make even his enemies to pity him and be at peace with him. When God pities, men shall: a God at peace with us makes everything at peace. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 47. Save us, O Lord our God, and gather us from among the heathen, to give thanks unto thy holy name, and to triumph in thy praise, is prayer for completing their deliverance: even when the Lord brought back the captivity of his people, still there was occasion to pray, Lord, turn again our captivity — so here, save us and gather us from among the heathen. We may suppose many forced into foreign countries in the days of the Judges, as Naomi was, had not returned in the beginning of David's reign, Saul's time being discouraging, and therefore it was seasonable to pray that the dispersed Israelites be gathered from among the heathen, to give thanks to his holy name — not only that they may have cause to give thanks and hearts to give thanks, but that they may have opportunity to do it in the courts of the Lord's house, from which they were now banished, and so may triumph in his praise, over those who had in scorn challenged them to sing the Lord's song in a strange land. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 48. Blessed be the Lord God of Israel from everlasting to everlasting, and let all the people say, Amen, praise ye the Lord, is praise for the beginning and progress of their deliverance: he is a blessed God from eternity and will be so to eternity, and so let him be praised by all his worshippers. Let the priests say this, and then let all the people say, Amen, Hallelujah, in token of their cheerful concurrence in all these prayers, praises and confessions. According to this rubric we find that when this psalm, or at least its closing verses, was sung, all the people said Amen and praised the Lord by saying Hallelujah — and by these two comprehensive words it is very proper in religious assemblies to testify their joining with their ministers in the prayers and praises which, as their mouth, they offer up to God according to his will, saying Amen to the prayers and Hallelujah to the praises. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

Matthew Henry (Complete)

  • Verse 1. Praise ye the Lord, O give thanks unto the Lord, for he is good, for his mercy endureth for ever, opens the psalm in praise before the confession that follows: the speaker blesses God for his goodness and his mighty acts, which are too many and too mysterious to be numbered or exhausted, and sets the whole recital of Israel's sins inside thanksgiving — for even the remembrance of former sins, notwithstanding which God did not cast off his people, encourages hope that though justly corrected we shall not be utterly abandoned. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 2. Who can utter the mighty acts of the Lord, who can shew forth all his praise, confesses that God's mighty acts are too many and too mysterious to be numbered or exhausted: no tongue can utter them all, no praise can show them all forth — yet the attempt itself is the psalm's business, to bless God for goodness and mighty acts beyond reckoning. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 3. Blessed are they that keep judgment, and he that doeth righteousness at all times, places blessedness not in occasional right but in steady constancy: blessed are those who keep judgment and do righteousness at all times — steadily and constantly, not by fits and starts. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 4. Remember me, O Lord, with the favour that thou bearest unto thy people, O visit me with thy salvation, is a personal plea inside the national prayer: remember me with the favour borne to thy people, visit me with thy salvation — that the speaker may share the good of the chosen, the gladness of the nation, and the glory of the inheritance, as everlasting joy and glory. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 5. Seeing the good of the chosen, gladness of the nation, glory of the inheritance is Henry's definition of enough: a peculiar good that is both pleasure and praise, cheerfulness and boasting in God, with everlasting joy and glory at its end. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 6. We have sinned with our fathers, we have committed iniquity, we have done wickedly, begins a penitential confession seasonable now that the church was in distress: thus we justify God in all he brings upon us, acknowledging that therefore he has done right because we have done wickedly, and the remembrance of former sins, notwithstanding which God did not cast off his people, encourages us to hope that though justly corrected for our sins we shall not be utterly abandoned. God's afflicted people own themselves guilty: we have sinned with our fathers, like our fathers, after the similitude of their transgression, adding to the stock of hereditary guilt and filling up the measure of our fathers' iniquity, to augment yet the fierce anger of the Lord. And they lay a load upon themselves, as becomes penitents: we have committed iniquity, that which is in its own nature sinful, and we have done wickedly, sinning with a high hand presumptuously — or this is a confession not only of imitation of but interest in their fathers' sins, for we were in their loins and bear their iniquity. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 7. Our fathers understood not thy wonders in Egypt, remembered not the multitude of thy mercies, but provoked him at the sea, even at the Red Sea, shows the strange stupidity of Israel amid God's favours: they saw his wonders but did not rightly apprehend their meaning and design. Blessed are those that have not seen and yet have understood. They thought Egypt's plagues were intended only for their deliverance, whereas they were intended also for their instruction and conviction — not only to force them out of Egyptian slavery but to cure them of their inclination to Egyptian idolatry, by evidencing the sovereign power and dominion of Israel's God above all gods and his particular concern for them. We lose the benefit of providences for want of understanding them. And as their understandings were dull, so their memories were treacherous: though one would think such astonishing events should never have been forgotten, yet they remembered not the multitude of God's mercies in them, and therefore God is distrusted because his favours are not remembered. Their perverseness arising from this stupidity was provoking him at the sea: despair of deliverance because the danger was great, and wishing they had been left in Egypt still, quarrelling with God's providence and questioning his power, goodness and faithfulness, as great provocations as any. The place aggravated the crime — at the sea, at the Red Sea, when newly out of Egypt with God's wonders fresh in their minds, yet reproaching him as if all that power had no mercy in it, but he had brought them out on purpose to kill them in the wilderness. They never lay at God's mercy so immediately as in their passage through the Red Sea, yet there they affronted it and provoked his wrath. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 8. Nevertheless he saved them for his name's sake, that he might make his mighty power to be known, answers why God did not cover them as he did their enemies for their unbelief and murmuring: it was for his name's sake. Though they did not deserve this favour, he designed it, and their undeservings should not alter his designs, nor break his measures, nor make him withdraw his promise or fail in its performance. He did this for his own glory, that he might make his mighty power known, not only in dividing the sea but in doing it notwithstanding their provocations — as Moses prayed, let the power of my Lord be great and pardon the iniquity of this people. The power of the God of grace in pardoning sin and sparing sinners is as much to be admired as the power of the God of nature in dividing the waters. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 9. He rebuked the Red Sea also, and it was dried up, so he led them through the depths, as through the wilderness, shows God forcing a passage for them: he rebuked the Red Sea for standing in their way and retarding their march, and it was dried up immediately, as in creation at God's rebuke the waters fled. Nay, he not only prepared them a way, but by the pillar of cloud and fire he led them into the sea, and by Moses' conduct led them through it as readily as through the wilderness, encouraging them to take those steps and subduing their fears, when those were their most dangerous and threatening enemies. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 10. And he saved them from the hand of him that hated them, and redeemed them from the hand of the enemy, shows God interposing between them and their pursuers and preventing them from cutting them off as designed: the Israelites were all on foot and the Egyptians all had chariots and horses with which they were likely to overtake them quickly, but God saved them from the hand of him that hated them — Pharaoh, who never loved them but now hated them the more for the plagues suffered on their account. From the hand of his enemy, just ready to seize them, God redeemed them, interposing himself, as it were, in the pillar of fire, between the persecuted and the persecutors. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 11. And the waters covered their enemies, there was not one of them left, completes the mercy and turns the deliverance into a victory: the Red Sea, which was a lane to them, was a grave to the Egyptians — the waters covered their enemies so as to slay them, but not so as to conceal their shame, for the next tide they were thrown up dead upon the shore. There was not one of them left alive to bring tidings of what had become of the rest. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 12. Then believed they his words, they sang his praise, is the good impression this made upon them for the present: then they believed his words and acknowledged that God was with them of a truth and had in mercy brought them out of Egypt, not with any design to slay them in the wilderness; then they feared the Lord and his servant Moses, and sang his praise in Moses' song penned on this great occasion. See in what a gracious and merciful way God sometimes silences his people's unbelief and turns their fears into praises — and so it is written, those that erred in spirit shall come to understanding, and those that murmured shall learn doctrine. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 13. They soon forgat his works, they waited not for his counsel, is disregard of God's works and word: soon forgetting his works, making haste, waiting not for his counsel. They took up resolves about which they did not consult him and made demands without calling upon him; they would be in Canaan directly, and had not patience to tarry God's time. The delay was intolerable, and therefore the difficulties were looked upon as insuperable. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 14. But lusted exceedingly in the wilderness, and tempted God in the desert, is their first wilderness sin after forgetting God's works: they would have flesh, yet would not believe God could give it to them. They lusted a lust in the wilderness, where they had bread enough and to spare, yet nothing would serve them but flesh to eat. They were now purely at God's finding, supported entirely by miracles, so that this was a reflection upon the wisdom and goodness of their Creator; they were also, in all probability, within a step of Canaan, yet had not patience to stay for dainties till they came thither. They had flocks and herds of their own but would not kill them — God must give them flesh as he gave them bread, or they will never give him credit or their good word. They did not only wish for flesh, but lusted exceedingly after it: a desire, even of lawful things, when inordinate and violent, becomes sinful, and therefore this is called lusting after evil things, though the quails as God's gift were good things. Yet this was not all: they tempted God in the desert, where they had had such experience of his goodness and power, and questioned whether he could and would gratify them herein. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 15. And he gave them their request, but sent leanness into their soul, shows how God showed displeasure for their lusting: he gave them their request, but gave it in anger and with a curse, for he sent leanness into their soul — filling them with uneasiness of mind, terror of conscience and self-reproach, occasioned by their bodies being sick with the surfeit, such as drunkards experience after a great debauch. Or this is put for that great plague with which the Lord smote them while the flesh was yet between their teeth, the consumption of the life. Note: what is asked in passion is often given in wrath; many that fare deliciously every day, whose bodies are healthful and fat, have at the same time leanness in their souls — no love to God, no thankfulness, no appetite to the bread of life — and then the soul must needs be lean. Those wretchedly forget themselves who feast their bodies and starve their souls. Then God gives the good things of this life in love, when with them he gives grace to glorify him in their use, for then the soul delights itself in fatness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 16. And envied Moses in the camp, and Aaron the saint of the Lord, is their quarrel with the government God had set over them in church and state: they envied Moses his authority in the camp, as generalissimo of Israel's armies and chief justice in all their courts, and envied Aaron his power as saint of the Lord, consecrated to the office of high priest — Korah would needs put in for the pontificate, while Dathan and Abiram, as princes of Reuben, Jacob's eldest son, would claim to be chief magistrates by the so-much-admired right of primogeniture. Those are preparing ruin for themselves who envy those whom God has put honour upon and usurp dignities they were never designed for, and justly will contempt be poured upon those who put contempt upon any of the saints of the Lord. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 17. The earth opened and swallowed up Dathan, and covered the company of Abiram, is God's displeasure for flying in the face of civil authority: those that flew in the face of the civil authority were punished by the earth, which opened and swallowed them up, as not fit to go upon God's ground, because they would not submit to God's government. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 18. And a fire was kindled in their company, the flame burned up the wicked, is God's displeasure for usurping ecclesiastical authority: those who would usurp authority in things pertaining to God suffered the vengeance of heaven, for fire came out from the Lord and consumed them, and the pretending sacrificers were themselves sacrificed to divine justice. The flame burnt up the wicked — for though they vied with Aaron, the saint of the Lord, for holiness, yet God adjudged them wicked, and as such cut them off, as in due time he will destroy the man of sin, that wicked one, notwithstanding his proud pretensions to holiness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 19. They made a calf in Horeb, and worshipped the molten image, was done in Horeb, where the law was given and where God had expressly said, thou shalt neither make any graven image nor bow down to it — they did both, making a calf and worshipping it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 20. Thus they changed their glory into the similitude of an ox that eateth grass, bade defiance to human reason itself: they changed their glory — their God, at least the manifestation of him, which always had been in a cloud, either dark or bright, without any manner of visible similitude — into the similitude of Apis, one of the Egyptian idols, an ox that eateth grass, than which nothing could be more grossly and scandalously absurd. Idolaters are perfectly besotted, and put the greatest disparagement possible both upon God, in representing him by the image of a beast, and upon themselves, in worshipping it when they have so done. That changing of their glory is explained by St. Paul to be the changing of the glory of the incorruptible God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 21. They forgat God their saviour, which had done great things in Egypt, is the root of the calf sin: they forgot God their Saviour — that is, they forgot that he had been their Saviour, for those who forget the works of God forget God himself, who makes himself known by his works. They forgot what was done but a few days before, which we may suppose they could not but talk of, even then, when because they did not make a good use of it they are said to forget it: what God did for them in Egypt, in the land of Ham, and by the Red Sea — things which we at this distance cannot, or should not, be unmindful of. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 22. Wondrous works in the land of Ham, and terrible things by the Red Sea, are the works they forgot: they forgot God their Saviour, that is, they forgot that he had been their Saviour, for those who forget the works of God forget God himself, who makes himself known by his works. They forgot what was done but a few days before, which we may suppose they could not but talk of, even then, when because they did not make a good use of it they are said to forget it: what God did for them in Egypt, in the land of Ham, and by the Red Sea — things which we at this distance cannot, or should not, be unmindful of. They are called great things, for though the great God does nothing mean, yet he does some things in a special manner great; wondrous works, out of the common road of providence, therefore observable, therefore memorable; and terrible things, awful to them and dreadful to their enemies — and yet soon forgotten, for even miracles that were seen passed away with them as tales that are told. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 23. Moses standing in the breach turns a decree of cutting off into a type: Henry sees mercy easily turning anger, prayer's power, and Christ the chosen intercessor ever living to turn wrath from a provoking world. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 24. And they despised the pleasant land, they believed not his word, is their giving credit to the evil spies' report in contradiction to God's promise: Canaan was a pleasant land, yet they undervalued it when they thought it not worth venturing for, no, not under God's own guidance, and were for making a captain and returning to Egypt again. They believed not God's word concerning it, but murmured in their tents, basely charging God with a design upon them in bringing them thither that they might become a prey to the Canaanites. And when reminded of God's power and promise, they were so far from hearkening to the Lord's voice that they attempted to stone those who spoke to them. The heavenly Canaan is a pleasant land, a promise is left us of entering into it, but many despise it, neglecting and refusing the offer, preferring this world's wealth and pleasure before it, and grudging this life's pains and hazards to obtain it. This also so displeased God that he lifted up his hand against them in threatening, to destroy them in the wilderness, nay, in swearing, for he swore in his wrath that they should not enter into his rest — nay, he threatened that their children also should be overthrown and scattered, and the whole nation dispersed and disinherited, but Moses prevailed for mercy for their seed, that they might enter Canaan. Those who despise God's favours, and particularly the pleasant land, forfeit his favours and will be shut out for ever from the pleasant land. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 25. But murmured in their tents, and hearkened not unto the voice of the Lord, continues their unbelief: they believed not his word, his promise that he would make them masters of Canaan, and hearkened not to the voice of the Lord, who gave them counsel which they would not wait for, not only by Moses and Aaron but by Caleb and Joshua. Those who will not wait for God's counsel shall justly be given up to their own hearts' lusts, to walk in their own counsels. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 26. Therefore he lifted up his hand against them, to overthrow them in the wilderness, is God's threatening for despising the land: he lifted up his hand in threatening to destroy them in the wilderness, nay, in swearing, for he swore in his wrath that they should not enter into his rest. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 27. To overthrow their seed also among the nations, and to scatter them in the lands, extends the threat: he threatened that their children also should be overthrown and scattered, and the whole nation dispersed and disinherited — but Moses prevailed for mercy for their seed, that they might enter Canaan. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 28. They joined themselves also unto Baal-peor, and ate the sacrifices of the dead, was the sin of the new generation, when within a step of Canaan: they joined themselves to Baal-peor, and so were entangled both in idolatry and in adultery, in corporeal and in spiritual whoredom. Those that did often partake of the altar of the living God now ate the sacrifices of the dead, of Moab's idols, dead images or dead men canonized or deified, or sacrifices to infernal deities on behalf of their dead friends. Thus they provoked God to anger with their inventions, in contempt of him and his institutions, his commands and his threatenings.   • Verse 29.   • Verse 30. Then stood up Phinehas, and executed judgment, and so the plague was stayed, shows God's displeasure answered by national justice: God testified displeasure by sending a plague which in a little time swept away 24,000 of those impudent sinners, and by stirring up Phinehas to use his power as magistrate for suppressing the sin and checking its contagion. He stood up in his zeal for the Lord of hosts and executed judgment upon Zimri and Cozbi, sinners of the first rank, genteel sinners, putting the law in execution upon them, and this was a service so pleasing to God that upon it the plague was stayed. By this and some other similar acts of public justice on that occasion, the guilt ceased to be national and the general controversy was let fall. When the proper officers did their duty, God left it to them and did not any longer keep the work in his own hands by the plague. Note: national justice prevents national judgments. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 31. And that was counted unto him for righteousness unto all generations, is Phinehas' special mark of honour: for signalizing himself, what he did was counted to him for righteousness to all generations, and in recompence the priesthood was entailed on his family. He shall make an atonement by offering up the sacrifices, who had so bravely made an atonement by offering up the sinners. Note: it is the honour of saints to be zealous against sin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 32. They angered him also at the waters of strife, so that it went ill with Moses for their sakes, continues their murmurings to the very last of their wanderings: in the fortieth year they angered God at the waters of strife, and what aggravated it now was that it went ill with Moses for their sakes. For though he was the meekest of all men in the earth, yet their clamours at that time were so peevish and provoking that they put him into a passion, and having now grown very old and off his guard, he spoke unadvisedly with his lips, and not as became him on that occasion — saying in a heat, hear now, ye rebels, must we fetch water out of this rock for you? This was Moses' infirmity, written for our admonition, that we may learn, when in the midst of provocation, to keep our mouth as with a bridle, and to take heed to our spirits, that they admit not resentments too much — for when the spirit is provoked, it is much ado, even for those with much wisdom and grace, not to speak unadvisedly. But it is charged upon the people as their sin: they provoked his spirit with that with which they angered God himself. We must answer not only for our own passions but for the provocation which by them we give to the passions of others, especially of those who, if not greatly provoked, would be meek and quiet. God showed displeasure against this sin by shutting Moses and Aaron out of Canaan for their misconduct on this occasion, by which he discovered his resentment of all such intemperate heats, even in the dearest of his servants — if he deals thus severely with Moses for one unadvised word, what does their sin deserve who have spoken so many presumptuous wicked words? If this was done in the green tree, what shall be done in the dry? — and by depriving them of the blessing of Moses' guidance and government at a time when they most needed it, so that his death was more a punishment to them than to himself. It is just with God to remove those relations from us that are blessings to us, when we are peevish and provoking to them and grieve their spirits. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 33. Because they provoked his spirit, so that he spake unadvisedly with his lips, is charged upon the people as their sin: they provoked his spirit with that with which they angered God himself. We must answer not only for our own passions, but for the provocation which by them we give to the passions of others, especially of those who, if not greatly provoked, would be meek and quiet. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 34. They did not destroy the nations, concerning whom the Lord commanded them, begins Israel's conduct in Canaan, of a piece with the wilderness: the miracles and mercies settling them in Canaan made no more deep and durable impressions than those fetching them out of Egypt, for by the time they were just settled in Canaan they corrupted themselves and forsook God. The first step of their apostasy was sparing the nations God had doomed to destruction: when they had got the good land promised them they had no zeal against the wicked inhabitants whom the Lord commanded them to extirpate, pretending pity — but so merciful is God that no man needs to be in any case more compassionate than he. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 35. But were mingled among the heathen, and learned their works, is the next step downhill: when they spared them they promised themselves that, notwithstanding this, they would not join in any dangerous affinity with them — but the way of sin is downhill, omissions make way for commissions. When they neglected to destroy the heathen, the next news is they were mingled among the heathen, making leagues with them and contracting an intimacy with them, so that they learned their works. That which is rotten will sooner corrupt that which is sound than be cured or made sound by it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 36. And they served their idols, which were a snare unto them, is the next degree: when they mingled with them and learned some of their works that seemed innocent diversions and entertainments, yet they thought they would never join with them in their worship — but by degrees they learned that too, serving their idols in the same manner and with the same rites that they served them, and they became a snare to them. That sin drew on many more and brought God's judgments upon them, which they themselves could not but be sensible of and yet knew not how to recover themselves. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 37. Yea, they sacrificed their sons and their daughters unto devils, is the last depth: when they joined with the heathen in some idolatrous services which they thought had least harm in them, they little thought they should ever be guilty of that barbarous and inhuman piece of idolatry, the sacrificing of their living children to their dead gods — but they came to that at last, in which Satan triumphed over his worshippers and regaled himself in blood and slaughter. They sacrificed their sons and daughters, pieces of themselves, to devils, and added murder, the most unnatural murder, to their idolatry — one cannot think of it without horror. They shed innocent blood, the most innocent, for it was infant-blood, the blood of their sons and daughters. See the power of the spirit that works in the children of disobedience, and see his malice. The beginning of idolatry and superstition, like that of strife, is as the letting forth of water, and there is no villany which those who venture upon it can be sure they shall stop short of, for God justly gives them up to a reprobate mind. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 38. And shed innocent blood, even the blood of their sons and daughters, whom they sacrificed unto the idols of Canaan, and the land was polluted with blood, is their sin as its own punishment upon the country: they shed innocent blood, the most innocent, for it was infant-blood, the blood of their sons and daughters — and by it they wronged their country, for the pleasant land, the holy land, was polluted with blood and rendered uncomfortable to themselves, and unfit to receive those kind tokens of God's favour and presence in it which were designed to be its honour. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 39. And were defiled with their own works, and went a whoring with their own inventions, is their sin as its own punishment in conscience: by it they wronged their consciences — they went a whoring with their own inventions, and so debauched their own minds, and were defiled with their own works, and rendered odious in the eyes of the holy God, and perhaps of their own consciences. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 40. Therefore was the wrath of the Lord kindled against his people, insomuch that he abhorred his own inheritance, follows necessarily, for his name is Jealous and he is a jealous God: he fell out with them for it, for his wrath, that consuming fire, was kindled against his people, taking it from them as more insulting and ungrateful than from the heathen that never knew him — nay, he was sick of them and abhorred his own inheritance, which once he had taken pleasure in, yet the change was not in him but in them. This is the worst thing in sin, that it makes us loathsome to God, and the nearer any are to God in profession the more loathsome are they if they rebel against him, like a dunghill at our door. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 41. And he gave them into the hand of the heathen, and they that hated them ruled over them, shows the punishment answering to the sin: their enemies then fell upon them, and their defence having departed, made an easy prey of them. They mingled with the heathen and learned their works, from them they willingly took the infection of sin, and therefore God justly made use of them as the instruments of their correction. Sinners often see themselves ruined by those by whom they have suffered themselves to be debauched — Satan, who is a tempter, will be a tormentor. The heathen hated them: apostates lose all the love on God's side and get none on Satan's, and when those that hated them ruled over them and they were brought into subjection under them, no marvel that they oppressed them and ruled them with rigour, and thus God made them know the difference between his service and the service of the kings of the countries. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 42. And their enemies oppressed them, and they were brought into subjection under their hand, continues that subjection: when those that hated them ruled over them, they oppressed them and ruled them with rigour, and thus God made them know the difference between his service and the service of the kings of the countries. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 43. Many times did he deliver them, but they provoked him with their counsel, and were brought low for their iniquity, refers to the days of the Judges: when God granted them some relief, yet they went on in their sins and their troubles also were continued, for God often raised up deliverers and wrought deliverances for them, and yet they relapsed to idolatry and provoked God with their counsel, their idolatrous inventions, to deliver them up to some other oppressor, so that at last they were brought very low for their iniquity. Those that by sin disparage themselves and will not by repentance humble themselves are justly debased, humbled and brought low by God's judgments. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 44. Nevertheless he regarded their affliction, when he heard their cry, shows them chastened but not destroyed, cast down but not cast off: at length they cried unto God and God returned in favour to them. God appeared for them as a God of mercy, who looked upon their grievances, regarded their affliction, beheld when distress was upon them, looked over their complaints, for he heard their cry with tender compassion and overlooked their provocations — for though he had said, and had reason to say it, that he would destroy them, yet he repented, according to the multitude of his mercies, and reversed the sentence. Though he is not a man that he should repent so as to change his mind, yet he is a gracious God, who pities us and changes his way. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 45. And he remembered for them his covenant, and repented according to the multitude of his mercies, shows God as a God of truth: he remembered for them his covenant and made good every word he had spoken, and therefore, bad as they were, he would not break with them, because he would not break his own promise. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 46. He made them also to be pitied of all those that carried them captives, shows God as a God of power, who has all hearts in his hand and turns them which way soever he pleases: he made them to be pitied even of those that carried them captives and hated them and ruled them with rigour. He not only restrained the remainder of their enemies' wrath, that it should not utterly consume them, but he infused compassion even into their stony hearts and made them relent, which was more than any art of man could have done with the utmost force of rhetoric. God can change lions into lambs, and when a man's ways please the Lord he will make even his enemies to pity him and be at peace with him. When God pities, men shall: a God at peace with us makes everything at peace. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 47. Save us, O Lord our God, and gather us from among the heathen, to give thanks unto thy holy name, and to triumph in thy praise, is prayer for completing their deliverance: even when the Lord brought back the captivity of his people, still there was occasion to pray, Lord, turn again our captivity — so here, save us and gather us from among the heathen. We may suppose many forced into foreign countries in the days of the Judges, as Naomi was, had not returned in the beginning of David's reign, Saul's time being discouraging, and therefore it was seasonable to pray that the dispersed Israelites be gathered from among the heathen, to give thanks to his holy name — not only that they may have cause to give thanks and hearts to give thanks, but that they may have opportunity to do it in the courts of the Lord's house, from which they were now banished, and so may triumph in his praise, over those who had in scorn challenged them to sing the Lord's song in a strange land. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 48. Blessed be the Lord God of Israel from everlasting to everlasting, and let all the people say, Amen, praise ye the Lord, is praise for the beginning and progress of their deliverance: he is a blessed God from eternity and will be so to eternity, and so let him be praised by all his worshippers. Let the priests say this, and then let all the people say, Amen, Hallelujah, in token of their cheerful concurrence in all these prayers, praises and confessions. According to this rubric we find that when this psalm, or at least its closing verses, was sung, all the people said Amen and praised the Lord by saying Hallelujah — and by these two comprehensive words it is very proper in religious assemblies to testify their joining with their ministers in the prayers and praises which, as their mouth, they offer up to God according to his will, saying Amen to the prayers and Hallelujah to the praises. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
