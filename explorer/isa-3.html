The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Isaiah 3 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 For, behold, the Lord, the LORD of hosts, doth take away from Jerusalem and from Judah the stay and the staff, the whole stay of bread, and the whole stay of water.

2 The mighty man, and the man of war, the judge, and the prophet, and the prudent, and the ancient,

3 The captain of fifty, and the honourable man, and the counsellor, and the cunning artificer, and the eloquent orator.

4 And I will give children to be their princes, and babes shall rule over them.

5 And the people shall be oppressed, every one by another, and every one by his neighbour: the child shall behave himself proudly against the ancient, and the base against the honourable.

6 When a man shall take hold of his brother of the house of his father, saying, Thou hast clothing, be thou our ruler, and let this ruin be under thy hand:

7 In that day shall he swear, saying, I will not be an healer; for in my house is neither bread nor clothing: make me not a ruler of the people.

8 For Jerusalem is ruined, and Judah is fallen: because their tongue and their doings are against the LORD, to provoke the eyes of his glory.

9 The shew of their countenance doth witness against them; and they declare their sin as Sodom, they hide it not. Woe unto their soul! for they have rewarded evil unto themselves.

10 Say ye to the righteous, that it shall be well with him: for they shall eat the fruit of their doings.

11 Woe unto the wicked! it shall be ill with him: for the reward of his hands shall be given him.

12 As for my people, children are their oppressors, and women rule over them. O my people, they which lead thee cause thee to err, and destroy the way of thy paths.

13 The LORD standeth up to plead, and standeth to judge the people.

14 The LORD will enter into judgment with the ancients of his people, and the princes thereof: for ye have eaten up the vineyard; the spoil of the poor is in your houses.

15 What mean ye that ye beat my people to pieces, and grind the faces of the poor? saith the Lord GOD of hosts.

16 Moreover the LORD saith, Because the daughters of Zion are haughty, and walk with stretched forth necks and wanton eyes, walking and mincing as they go, and making a tinkling with their feet:

17 Therefore the LORD will smite with a scab the crown of the head of the daughters of Zion, and the LORD will discover their secret parts.

18 In that day the Lord will take away the bravery of their tinkling ornaments about their feet, and their cauls, and their round tires like the moon,

19 The chains, and the bracelets, and the mufflers,

20 The bonnets, and the ornaments of the legs, and the headbands, and the tablets, and the earrings,

21 The rings, and nose jewels,

22 The changeable suits of apparel, and the mantles, and the wimples, and the crisping pins,

23 The glasses, and the fine linen, and the hoods, and the vails.

24 And it shall come to pass, that instead of sweet smell there shall be stink; and instead of a girdle a rent; and instead of well set hair baldness; and instead of a stomacher a girding of sackcloth; and burning instead of beauty.

25 Thy men shall fall by the sword, and thy mighty in the war.

26 And her gates shall lament and mourn; and she being desolate shall sit upon the ground.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

God takes away every support from Jerusalem and Judah — bread, water, and capable leaders — leaving a city run by the inexperienced and the proud. The rulers who oppressed the poor are judged, the haughty women of Zion are stripped of their finery, and the chapter closes with the city sitting desolate on the ground, its gates lamenting. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. The Lord of hosts, able to do as he says, will take away from Jerusalem and Judah every stay and staff — every kind of support. The change from the luxuries of chapter 2 is total, and JFB sees it fulfilled in the sieges by Nebuchadnezzar and afterwards by Titus. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. The mighty man, the man of war, the judge, the prophet, the prudent, and the ancient will all be taken away. The "prudent" may mean diviners on whom the people relied, who will fail in that day, while the ancient are the old and experienced counsellors. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Even captains of fifty, men of dignified aspect, skilled workmen, and eloquent orators will fail. Artisans are no mean stay among a nation's safeguards, and their business will come to a standstill in siege and desolation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Children in ability for governing will rule, and babes in warlike might will hold sway — the antithesis of the ancient and the mighty just removed. Imbecile rulers are themselves a judgment on the nation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Under such rulers comes anarchy: unjust exactions on every side, and the forms of respect violated, with the base behaving proudly against the honourable. JFB compares these marks to those of the last days. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. So few men of wealth and ability will remain that people will seize the first man they meet who has clothing and press him to be ruler. Clothing, and changes of raiment, were wealth in the East, so the plea is simply: you have something, rule over our ruin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. The man pressed to rule will refuse with a lifted hand of solemn attestation: he will not be a healer of the body politic, incurably diseased, for he has neither bread nor clothing. A nation's state must be bad indeed when no ambitious man will accept office. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Jerusalem stumbles and Judah falls because their tongue and doings are against the Lord, provoking the eyes of his glory. This is the reason all shrink from government: the ruin is the fruit of open defiance of God's glorious majesty. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. The show of their countenance witnesses against them: their look answers to their inner character, and they declare their sin as Sodom did, glorying in it rather than hiding it. Having lost not only the substance of virtue but its colour, they reward evil to themselves. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Yet the righteous are to be told it will be well with them, for they will eat the fruit of their doings. The faithlessness of many is no proof that all are faithless — fruit-bearing faith, not salvation by works, is meant. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Woe to the wicked: it will be ill with him, for the reward of his hands, his conduct, will be given to him. "Ill" stands as the emphatic antithesis to the "well" of the righteous in the verse before. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Children are the people's oppressors and women rule over them — exacting princes who ought to be protectors, as unqualified as children. Their leaders, false prophets who flatter with promises of safety in sin, cause the people to err and swallow up the right way of the law. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. The Lord stands up to plead, no longer sitting in silence, indignant against a wicked people. The courtroom image is of God rising to judge his people and their rulers. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. The Lord enters into judgment with the ancients and princes of his people, who have eaten up his vineyard, the Jewish theocracy. By oppressive exactions they have burnt the vineyard and filled their houses with the spoil of the poor. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. The Lord demands what right they have to beat his people to pieces and grind the faces of the poor — openly and palpably, by exactions that leave them nothing. The question exposes rulers who crush those they were set to protect. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Because the daughters of Zion are haughty, with stretched-forth necks and wanton, glancing eyes, mincing and tinkling as they go, judgment is pronounced on their luxury. Such luxury had grown great in Uzziah's prosperous reign, and the ankle-rings joined by chains forced their short, tripping steps. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. The Lord will smite the daughters of Zion with a scab, making them bald by disease, and will discover their secret parts. This is the greatest indignity that can befall female captives: to be stripped naked and exposed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. In that day the Lord will take away the bravery of their tinkling ornaments, their cauls and round tires like the moon. The finery itself is catalogued piece by piece, to show how completely it will be stripped away. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. The chains or pendants hanging about the neck, and the mufflers — veils covering the face with apertures for the eyes — will be taken away. The veil's very name refers to the tremulous, changing effect of its spangles. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. The bonnets or turbans, the ornaments of the legs — short stepping-chains giving a measured gait — the headbands, tablets or smelling-boxes, and earrings, which were really amulets with magic formulas, will all go. Each ornament marks the luxury and superstition of the age. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. The rings and nose jewels will be taken away; the cartilage between the nostrils was bored to receive them, usually hanging from the left nostril. Even the smallest personal adornments are included in the stripping. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Here begin entire articles of apparel, as distinct from single ornaments: changeable suits of apparel for special occasions, mantles or fuller tunics reaching to the feet, wimples or broad cloaks, and crisping pins, which are really money bags. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. The glasses or mirrors of polished metal, the hoods or diadems, and the large veils covering head and person will be taken away. The large veil, distinct from the smaller mufflers, was a token of woman's subjection. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. Instead of sweet smell there will be stink from ulcers, instead of a girdle a rent or rope of poverty, instead of well-set hair baldness, instead of a stomacher sackcloth, and burning instead of beauty. Stripped of hoods and veils, the captives will labour under a scorching sun and grow sunburnt. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. Jerusalem's men will fall by the sword and her mighty in the war. The stripping of the women is completed by the slaughter of the men who might have protected them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. Her gates, the place of concourse personified, will lament and mourn, and she will sit desolate on the ground. Judea was pictured exactly so on medals after the destruction by Titus: a grieving female sitting under a palm tree, captioned Judea capta. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
