The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 17 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 My breath is corrupt, my days are extinct, the graves are ready for me.

2 Are there not mockers with me? and doth not mine eye continue in their provocation?

3 Lay down now, put me in a surety with thee; who is he that will strike hands with me?

4 For thou hast hid their heart from understanding: therefore shalt thou not exalt them.

5 He that speaketh flattery to his friends, even the eyes of his children shall fail.

6 He hath made me also a byword of the people; and aforetime I was as a tabret.

7 Mine eye also is dim by reason of sorrow, and all my members are as a shadow.

8 Upright men shall be astonied at this, and the innocent shall stir up himself against the hypocrite.

9 The righteous also shall hold on his way, and he that hath clean hands shall be stronger and stronger.

10 But as for you all, do ye return, and come now: for I cannot find one wise man among you.

11 My days are past, my purposes are broken off, even the thoughts of my heart.

12 They change the night into day: the light is short because of darkness.

13 If I wait, the grave is mine house: I have made my bed in the darkness.

14 I have said to corruption, Thou art my father: to the worm, Thou art my mother, and my sister.

15 And where is now my hope? as for my hope, who shall see it?

16 They shall go down to the bars of the pit, when our rest together is in the dust.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Job continues in the same speech: his breath is corrupt, his days extinct, and the graves ready for him, while mockers provoke him. He asks God to lay down a pledge, to be his surety, since no one else will strike hands with him — the courtroom image of a guarantor standing for the accused. The chapter is a plea for God himself to vouch for a man his friends have abandoned. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Job's breath is corrupt, his days are extinct, the graves are ready for him. JFB notes corrupt breath is the result of elephantiasis, extinct days are an expiring light, and the plural graves heightens the force. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Are there not mockers with Job, and does not his eye continue in their provocation? JFB renders it emphatically: had he only not to endure mockery amid their contentions, his eye would remain quiet, tarrying all night in undisturbed rest. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Lay down now, put Job in a surety with God; who is he that will strike hands with him? JFB explains the pledge both litigants laid down before trial, and the surety as a presage of the Mediator, since only God himself could strike hands as security. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. God has hidden the friends' heart from understanding, therefore he shall not exalt them. JFB explains heart as intellect, and renders exalt as imperative: exalt them not, allow them not to conquer. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. He that speaks flattery to his friends, even the eyes of his children shall fail. JFB explains flattery as smoothness, then a prey divided by lot with a smooth stone, so the sense is delivering up a friend as prey, retorting the friends' reproach about Job's cut-off children. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. God has made Job a byword of the people, and aforetime he was as a tabret. JFB notes God is reverentially unnamed, byword as an execrated name implying superlative badness, and the tabret reading as being treated to his face as an object to spit upon. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Job's eye is dim by reason of sorrow, and all his members are as a shadow. JFB notes members literally as figures, the individual forms of the body, opposed to a shadow without solidity. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Upright men shall be astonished at this, and the innocent shall stir up himself against the hypocrite. JFB explains astonied as at unmerited sufferings, and the upright's sense of justice wounded and indignant at the wicked's prosperity, glancing at the false friends. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. The righteous also shall hold on his way, and he that has clean hands shall be stronger and stronger. JFB notes religious principle is heightened by misfortune, and the pious take fresh courage from suffering Job, like a warrior gaining courage in action. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. But as for you all, do you return, for Job cannot find one wise man among you. JFB renders return as recommencing their speech if they have anything really wise to advance, which Job doubts. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Job's days are past, his purposes are broken off, even the thoughts of his heart. JFB explains broken off as threads cut from the loom, and thoughts as possessions, the feelings and fair hopes his heart once nourished. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. They change the night into day, and the light is short because of darkness. JFB explains the friends would persuade Job his misery will change into joy, which is impossible, and that any new light would be near only before the darkness of death. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. If Job waits for the grave as his house, and makes his bed in darkness, where is his hope? JFB renders it as waiting for Sheol as his house, with the apodosis at verse 15. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Job has said to corruption, you are my father, and to the worm, you are my mother and sister. JFB notes this expresses most intimate connection: his diseased state makes him closely akin to grave and worm. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. And where is now Job's hope? As for his hope, who shall see it? JFB notes the question is who shall see fulfilled the hope of restoration the friends held out. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. They shall go down to the bars of the pit, when our rest together is in the dust. JFB explains his hopes shall be buried with him, the bars as the wastes of Sheol, and the rest as his and his hope's together — both expire together. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
