The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Matthew 16 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 The Pharisees also with the Sadducees came, and tempting desired him that he would shew them a sign from heaven.

2 He answered and said unto them, When it is evening, ye say, It will be fair weather: for the sky is red.

3 And in the morning, It will be foul weather to day: for the sky is red and lowering. O ye hypocrites, ye can discern the face of the sky; but can ye not discern the signs of the times?

4 A wicked and adulterous generation seeketh after a sign; and there shall no sign be given unto it, but the sign of the prophet Jonas. And he left them, and departed.

5 And when his disciples were come to the other side, they had forgotten to take bread.

6 Then Jesus said unto them, Take heed and beware of the leaven of the Pharisees and of the Sadducees.

7 And they reasoned among themselves, saying, It is because we have taken no bread.

8 Which when Jesus perceived, he said unto them, O ye of little faith, why reason ye among yourselves, because ye have brought no bread?

9 Do ye not yet understand, neither remember the five loaves of the five thousand, and how many baskets ye took up?

10 Neither the seven loaves of the four thousand, and how many baskets ye took up?

11 How is it that ye do not understand that I spake it not to you concerning bread, that ye should beware of the leaven of the Pharisees and of the Sadducees?

12 Then understood they how that he bade them not beware of the leaven of bread, but of the doctrine of the Pharisees and of the Sadducees.

13 When Jesus came into the coasts of Caesarea Philippi, he asked his disciples, saying, Whom do men say that I the Son of man am?

14 And they said, Some say that thou art John the Baptist: some, Elias; and others, Jeremias, or one of the prophets.

15 He saith unto them, But whom say ye that I am?

16 And Simon Peter answered and said, Thou art the Christ, the Son of the living God.

17 And Jesus answered and said unto him, Blessed art thou, Simon Barjona: for flesh and blood hath not revealed it unto thee, but my Father which is in heaven.

18 And I say also unto thee, That thou art Peter, and upon this rock I will build my church; and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it.

19 And I will give unto thee the keys of the kingdom of heaven: and whatsoever thou shalt bind on earth shall be bound in heaven: and whatsoever thou shalt loose on earth shall be loosed in heaven.

20 Then charged he his disciples that they should tell no man that he was Jesus the Christ.

21 From that time forth began Jesus to shew unto his disciples, how that he must go unto Jerusalem, and suffer many things of the elders and chief priests and scribes, and be killed, and be raised again the third day.

22 Then Peter took him, and began to rebuke him, saying, Be it far from thee, Lord: this shall not be unto thee.

23 But he turned, and said unto Peter, Get thee behind me, Satan: thou art an offence unto me: for thou savourest not the things that be of God, but those that be of men.

24 Then said Jesus unto his disciples, If any man will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow me.

25 For whosoever will save his life shall lose it: and whosoever will lose his life for my sake shall find it.

26 For what is a man profited, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul? or what shall a man give in exchange for his soul?

27 For the Son of man shall come in the glory of his Father with his angels; and then he shall reward every man according to his works.

28 Verily I say unto you, There be some standing here, which shall not taste of death, till they see the Son of man coming in his kingdom.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Pharisees and Sadducees demand a sign from heaven and are given only the sign of Jonah; Jesus warns against the leaven, that is, the doctrine, of the Pharisees and Sadducees. At Caesarea Philippi, Peter confesses, “Thou art the Christ, the Son of the living God”, Jesus speaks of the rock, the keys and binding and loosing, then foretells his suffering, death and resurrection, rebukes Peter's “Be it far from thee, Lord”, and teaches that following him means denying oneself and taking up the cross. What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. The Pharisees also with the Sadducees came, and tempting desired him that he would shew them a sign from heaven. The Pharisees and Sadducees were opposed to each other in principles and conduct, yet they joined against Christ. They desired a sign of their own choosing: they despised those signs which relieved the necessity of the sick and sorrowful, and called for something else which would gratify the curiosity of the proud. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 2. He answered and said unto them, When it is evening, ye say, It will be fair weather: for the sky is red. They could read the weather in the sky, yet were blind to the signs of the times standing before them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 3. And in the morning, It will be foul weather to day: for the sky is red and lowring. O ye hypocrites, ye can discern the face of the sky; but can ye not discern the signs of the times? It is great hypocrisy, when we slight the signs of God's ordaining, to seek for signs of our own devising. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 4. A wicked and adulterous generation seeketh after a sign; and there shall no sign be given unto it, but the sign of the prophet Jonas. And he left them, and departed. As in chapter 12, the sign of Jonah — burial and rising — is the one sign appointed, and they are left to it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 5. And when his disciples were come to the other side, they had forgotten to take bread. Christ speaks of spiritual things under a similitude, and the disciples misunderstand him of carnal things. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 6. Then Jesus said unto them, Take heed and beware of the leaven of the Pharisees and of the Sadducees. Leaven works secretly and spreads through the whole lump; so false doctrine, once admitted, corrupts the whole profession. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 7. And they reasoned among themselves, saying, It is because we have taken no bread. Their thoughts ran at once to the forgotten loaves, as if their Master were as thoughtful about bread as they were. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 8. Which when Jesus perceived, he said unto them, O ye of little faith, why reason ye among yourselves, because ye have taken no bread? He took it ill that they should think him as thoughtful about bread as they were, and that they should be so little acquainted with his way of preaching. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 9. Do ye not yet understand, neither remember the five loaves of the five thousand, and how many baskets ye took up? The feeding of the five thousand should have taught them that bread was no care to him who multiplied it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 10. Neither the seven loaves of the four thousand, and how many baskets ye took up? A second feeding, a second proof, and still their faith was slow to reckon with either. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 11. How is it that ye do not understand that I spake it not to you concerning bread, that ye should beware of the leaven of the Pharisees and of the Sadducees? The rebuke is gentle but pointed: spiritual teaching was being dragged down to a question of provisions. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 12. Then understood they how that he bade them not beware of the leaven of bread, but of the doctrine of the Pharisees and of the Sadducees. Christ teaches by the Spirit of wisdom in the heart, opening the understanding to the Spirit of revelation in the word. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 13. When Jesus came into the coasts of Caesarea Philippi, he asked his disciples, saying, Whom do men say that I the Son of man am? Caesarea Philippi lay at the foot of Mount Lebanon, near the sources of the Jordan, originally Panium, renamed by Philip the tetrarch for the emperor with Philippi added to distinguish it from the other Caesarea. This quiet and distant retreat Jesus sought to talk over with the Twelve the fruit of his past labours, and to break to them for the first time the sad intelligence of his approaching death. He had now closed the first great stage of his ministry and was just entering the last dark one; his spirit, burdened, sought relief in retirement, pouring out his soul in supplications and prayers with strong crying and tears. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. And they said, Some say that thou art John the Baptist: some, Elias; and others, Jeremias, or one of the prophets. So Herod Antipas was not singular in his surmise; some thought him Elias, others Jeremias — was this suggested by a supposed resemblance between the Man of Sorrows and the weeping prophet? — or one of the old prophets risen again, a prophetical person resembling those of old. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. He saith unto them, But whom say ye that I am? He had never put this question before, but the crisis he was reaching made it fitting he should now have it from them. As Isaiah speaks in his name, “I have laboured in vain; I have spent my strength for naught, and in vain”: lo, these three years I come seeking fruit on this fig tree, and what is it? As the result of all, I am taken for John, for Elias, for Jeremias, for one of the prophets. Yet some there are that have beheld my glory, the glory as of the Only-begotten of the Father, and I shall hear their voice, for it is sweet. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. And Simon Peter answered and said, Thou art the Christ, the Son of the living God. Peter, for himself and his brethren, said they were assured of our Lord's being the promised Messiah, the Son of the living God — showing they believed Jesus to be more than man. He does not tame the acknowledgment into “I believe that thou art,” but breaks forth in the language of adoration: THOU ART THE CHRIST, THE SON OF THE LIVING GOD. He first owns him the promised Messiah, then rises higher, echoing the voice from heaven, and in “Son of the LIVING GOD” recognizes the essential and eternal life of God as in this his Son. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. And Jesus answered and said unto him, Blessed art thou, Simon Barjona: for flesh and blood hath not revealed it unto thee, but my Father which is in heaven. Our Lord declared Peter blessed, as the teaching of God made him differ from his unbelieving countrymen. Though Peter only expressed the conviction of all the Twelve, he alone had clear enough apprehensions to put it in proper words, courage to speak them out, and readiness to do it at the right time, and so communicated to the saddened soul of the Redeemer the balm needed to cheer him. “Simon-Barjona,” son of Jona, denoting his humble fleshly extraction, is purposely mentioned to contrast with the spiritual elevation to which divine illumination had raised him. In speaking of God, Jesus never calls him “our Father,” but either “your Father” or, as here, “my Father,” to signify some peculiar action of him as the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. And I say also unto thee, That thou art Peter, and upon this rock I will build my church; and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it. Christ added that he had named him Peter, in allusion to his stability or firmness in professing the truth. The word translated “rock” is not the same word as Peter, but is of a similar meaning. Nothing can be more wrong than to suppose Christ meant the person of Peter was the rock. Without doubt Christ himself is the Rock, the tried foundation of the church; and woe to him that attempts to lay any other! Peter's confession is this rock as to doctrine. As “Peter” and “Rock” are one word in the Aramaic familiarly spoken by our Lord, the play upon the word is fully seen only in languages with one word for both — in French, Pierre–pierre. “My Church,” says our Lord, calling the Church his OWN, an expression nowhere else occurring in the Gospel. The gates of hell — of Hades, the unseen world, the gates of Death — mean, “It shall never perish.” Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. And I will give unto thee the keys of the kingdom of heaven: and whatsoever thou shalt bind on earth shall be bound in heaven: and whatsoever thou shalt loose on earth shall be loosed in heaven. Our Lord next declared the authority with which Peter would be invested. He spoke in the name of his brethren, and this related to them as well as to him. They had no certain knowledge of the characters of men, and were liable to mistakes in their own conduct; but they were kept from error in stating the way of acceptance and salvation, the rule of obedience, the believer's character and experience, and the final doom of unbelievers and hypocrites. In such matters their decision was right and confirmed in heaven. But all pretensions of any man, either to absolve or retain men's sins, are blasphemous and absurd. None can forgive sins but God only. And this binding and loosing, in the common language of the Jews, signified to forbid and to allow, or to teach what is lawful or unlawful. Whatever this mean, it was soon expressly extended to all the apostles, so the claim of supreme authority in the Church made for Peter is baseless. As first in confessing Christ, Peter got this commission before the rest; and with these keys, on the day of Pentecost, he first opened the door of faith to the Jews, and then, in Cornelius, to the Gentiles. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Then charged he his disciples that they should tell no man that he was Jesus the Christ. Now that he had been so explicit, they might naturally think the time come for giving it out openly; but here they are told it had not. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. From that time forth began Jesus to shew unto his disciples, how that he must go unto Jerusalem, and suffer many things of the elders and chief priests and scribes, and be killed, and be raised again the third day. Christ reveals his mind to his people gradually. From that time, when the apostles had made the full confession of Christ, that he was the Son of God, he began to show them of his sufferings, with an explicitness and frequency he had never observed before, to set right their mistakes about the outward pomp and power of his kingdom. Those that follow Christ must not expect great or high things in this world. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Then Peter took him, and began to rebuke him, saying, Be it far from thee, Lord: this shall not be unto thee. Peter took him aside, apart from the rest, presuming on the distinction just conferred on him, showing how unexpected and distasteful to them all was the announcement; affectionately, yet with a certain generous indignation, he chided him — “If I can help it” — the same spirit that prompted him in the garden to draw the sword in his behalf. Peter would have Christ dread suffering as much as he did; but we mistake if we measure Christ's love and patience by our own. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. But he turned, and said unto Peter, Get thee behind me, Satan: thou art an offence unto me: for thou savourest not the things that be of God, but those that be of men. He turned in the hearing of the rest, perceiving Peter had but boldly uttered what others felt, and the check was needed by them also. The same words as he had addressed to the Tempter, for he felt in it a satanic lure, a whisper from hell, to move him from his purpose to suffer. How quickly has the “rock” turned to a devil! The fruit of divine teaching the Lord delighted to honour in Peter; but the mouthpiece of hell, which he had in a moment of forgetfulness become, the Lord shook off with horror. “Thou art an offence” — a stumbling-block — “thou playest the Tempter, casting a stumbling-block in my way to the Cross.” “Thou savourest not” — thou thinkest not — the things of God, but of men, carried away by human views of Messiah's kingdom, quite contrary to those of God. We do not read of anything said or done by any disciple, at any time, that Christ resented so much as this. Whoever takes us from that which is good, and would make us fear to do too much for God, speaks Satan's language; whatever appears a temptation to sin must be resisted with abhorrence, not parleyed with. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. Then said Jesus unto his disciples, If any man will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow me. Mark says, when he had called the people unto him with his disciples also, he said unto them — turning the rebuke of one into a warning to all. A true disciple is one that follows him in duty and shall follow him to glory, walking in the same way Christ walked, led by his Spirit, treading in his steps whithersoever he goes. “Let him deny himself.” If self-denial be a hard lesson, it is no more than what our Master learned and practised, to redeem us and to teach us. “Let him take up his cross.” The cross is here put for every trouble that befalls us. We are apt to think we could bear another's cross better than our own; but that is best which is appointed us, and we ought to make the best of it. We must not by rashness pull crosses down upon our own heads, but must take them up when they are in our way. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. For whosoever will save his life shall lose it: and whosoever will lose his life for my sake shall find it. “A suffering and dying Messiah liketh you ill; but what if his servants shall meet the same fate? They may not; but who follows me must be prepared for the worst.” Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. For what is a man profited, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul? or what shall a man give in exchange for his soul? If all worldly things are worthless when compared with the life of the body, how forcible the same argument with respect to the soul and its state of never-ending happiness or misery! Thousands lose their souls for the most trifling gain, or the most worthless indulgence, nay, often from mere sloth and negligence. Whatever is the object for which men forsake Christ, that is the price at which Satan buys their souls. Yet one soul is worth more than all the world. This is Christ's judgment upon the matter; he knew the price of souls, for he redeemed them; nor would he underrate the world, for he made it. How awful is the stake as here set forth! If a man makes the present world — riches, honours, pleasures — the object of supreme pursuit, be it that he gains the world; yet along with it he forfeits his own soul. But if not the whole world, yet possibly something else may be conceived as an equivalent for the soul. Well, what is it? Thus, in language the weightiest because the simplest, does our Lord shut up his hearers, and all who shall read these words to the end of the world, to the priceless value to every man of his own soul. Mark and Luke add: “Whosoever shall be ashamed of me and of my words in this adulterous and sinful generation, of him shall the Son of man be ashamed when he cometh in the glory of his Father, with the holy angels.” To be lost to shame is to be nearly past hope; but when Christ and his words are unpopular, the same instinctive desire to stand well with others begets that temptation to be ashamed of him which only the expulsive power of a higher affection can effectually counteract. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. For the Son of man shall come in the glory of his Father with his angels; and then he shall reward every man according to his works. In the splendour of his Father's authority and with all his angelic ministers, ready to execute his pleasure, the Judge comes, and reward follows works. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. Verily I say unto you, There be some standing here, which shall not taste of death, till they see the Son of man coming in his kingdom. Or, as in Mark, “till they see the kingdom of God come with power,” or, as in Luke, more simply, “till they see the kingdom of God.” The reference, beyond doubt, is to the firm establishment and victorious progress, in the lifetime of some then present, of that new kingdom of Christ, which was destined to work the greatest of all changes on this earth, and be the grand pledge of his final coming in glory. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Our Lord next declared the authority with which Peter would be invested. He spoke in the name of his brethren, and this related to them as well as to him. They had no certain knowledge of the characters of men, and were liable to mistakes in their own conduct; but they were kept from error in stating the way of acceptance and salvation, the rule of obedience, the believer's character and experience, and the final doom of unbelievers and hypocrites. In such matters their decision was right and confirmed in heaven. But all pretensions of any man, either to absolve or retain men's sins, are blasphemous and absurd. None can forgive sins but God only. And this binding and loosing, in the common language of the Jews, signified to forbid and to allow, or to teach what is lawful or unlawful. Whatever this mean, it was soon expressly extended to all the apostles, so the claim of supreme authority in the Church made for Peter is baseless. As first in confessing Christ, Peter got this commission before the rest; and with these keys, on the day of Pentecost, he first opened the door of faith to the Jews, and then, in Cornelius, to the Gentiles. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Then charged he his disciples that they should tell no man that he was Jesus the Christ. Now that he had been so explicit, they might naturally think the time come for giving it out openly; but here they are told it had not. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. From that time forth began Jesus to shew unto his disciples, how that he must go unto Jerusalem, and suffer many things of the elders and chief priests and scribes, and be killed, and be raised again the third day. Christ reveals his mind to his people gradually. From that time, when the apostles had made the full confession of Christ, that he was the Son of God, he began to show them of his sufferings, with an explicitness and frequency he had never observed before, to set right their mistakes about the outward pomp and power of his kingdom. Those that follow Christ must not expect great or high things in this world. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Then Peter took him, and began to rebuke him, saying, Be it far from thee, Lord: this shall not be unto thee. Peter took him aside, apart from the rest, presuming on the distinction just conferred on him, showing how unexpected and distasteful to them all was the announcement; affectionately, yet with a certain generous indignation, he chided him — “If I can help it” — the same spirit that prompted him in the garden to draw the sword in his behalf. Peter would have Christ dread suffering as much as he did; but we mistake if we measure Christ's love and patience by our own. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. But he turned, and said unto Peter, Get thee behind me, Satan: thou art an offence unto me: for thou savourest not the things that be of God, but those that be of men. He turned in the hearing of the rest, perceiving Peter had but boldly uttered what others felt, and the check was needed by them also. The same words as he had addressed to the Tempter, for he felt in it a satanic lure, a whisper from hell, to move him from his purpose to suffer. How quickly has the “rock” turned to a devil! The fruit of divine teaching the Lord delighted to honour in Peter; but the mouthpiece of hell, which he had in a moment of forgetfulness become, the Lord shook off with horror. “Thou art an offence” — a stumbling-block — “thou playest the Tempter, casting a stumbling-block in my way to the Cross.” “Thou savourest not” — thou thinkest not — the things of God, but of men, carried away by human views of Messiah's kingdom, quite contrary to those of God. We do not read of anything said or done by any disciple, at any time, that Christ resented so much as this. Whoever takes us from that which is good, and would make us fear to do too much for God, speaks Satan's language; whatever appears a temptation to sin must be resisted with abhorrence, not parleyed with. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. Then said Jesus unto his disciples, If any man will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow me. Mark says, when he had called the people unto him with his disciples also, he said unto them — turning the rebuke of one into a warning to all. A true disciple is one that follows him in duty and shall follow him to glory, walking in the same way Christ walked, led by his Spirit, treading in his steps whithersoever he goes. “Let him deny himself.” If self-denial be a hard lesson, it is no more than what our Master learned and practised, to redeem us and to teach us. “Let him take up his cross.” The cross is here put for every trouble that befalls us. We are apt to think we could bear another's cross better than our own; but that is best which is appointed us, and we ought to make the best of it. We must not by rashness pull crosses down upon our own heads, but must take them up when they are in our way. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. For whosoever will save his life shall lose it: and whosoever will lose his life for my sake shall find it. “A suffering and dying Messiah liketh you ill; but what if his servants shall meet the same fate? They may not; but who follows me must be prepared for the worst.” Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. For what is a man profited, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul? or what shall a man give in exchange for his soul? If all worldly things are worthless when compared with the life of the body, how forcible the same argument with respect to the soul and its state of never-ending happiness or misery! Thousands lose their souls for the most trifling gain, or the most worthless indulgence, nay, often from mere sloth and negligence. Whatever is the object for which men forsake Christ, that is the price at which Satan buys their souls. Yet one soul is worth more than all the world. This is Christ's judgment upon the matter; he knew the price of souls, for he redeemed them; nor would he underrate the world, for he made it. How awful is the stake as here set forth! If a man makes the present world — riches, honours, pleasures — the object of supreme pursuit, be it that he gains the world; yet along with it he forfeits his own soul. But if not the whole world, yet possibly something else may be conceived as an equivalent for the soul. Well, what is it? Thus, in language the weightiest because the simplest, does our Lord shut up his hearers, and all who shall read these words to the end of the world, to the priceless value to every man of his own soul. Mark and Luke add: “Whosoever shall be ashamed of me and of my words in this adulterous and sinful generation, of him shall the Son of man be ashamed when he cometh in the glory of his Father, with the holy angels.” To be lost to shame is to be nearly past hope; but when Christ and his words are unpopular, the same instinctive desire to stand well with others begets that temptation to be ashamed of him which only the expulsive power of a higher affection can effectually counteract. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. For the Son of man shall come in the glory of his Father with his angels; and then he shall reward every man according to his works. In the splendour of his Father's authority and with all his angelic ministers, ready to execute his pleasure, the Judge comes, and reward follows works. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. Verily I say unto you, There be some standing here, which shall not taste of death, till they see the Son of man coming in his kingdom. Or, as in Mark, “till they see the kingdom of God come with power,” or, as in Luke, more simply, “till they see the kingdom of God.” The reference, beyond doubt, is to the firm establishment and victorious progress, in the lifetime of some then present, of that new kingdom of Christ, which was destined to work the greatest of all changes on this earth, and be the grand pledge of his final coming in glory. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Investigation (editorial — a parallel question, not a conclusion)

Parallel. The call to deny oneself and take up the cross is told in Matthew and, in the note's terms, in Mark as well. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Difference. Mark says Jesus called the people unto him with his disciples also before speaking, turning the rebuke of one into a warning to all, while Matthew addresses the disciples. The note records the wider audience in Mark without further comment. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
