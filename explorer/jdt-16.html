Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Judith 16 (King James Version, Apocrypha; public-domain text as received)

1 Then Judith began to sing this thanksgiving in all Israel, and all the people sang after her this song of praise.

2 And Judith said, Begin unto my God with timbrels, sing unto my Lord with cymbals: tune unto him a new psalm: exalt him, and call upon his name.

3 For God breaketh the battles: for among the camps in the midst of the people he hath delivered me out of the hands of them that persecuted me.

4 Assur came out of the mountains from the north, he came with ten thousands of his army, the multitude whereof stopped the torrents, and their horsemen have covered the hills.

5 He bragged that he would burn up my borders, and kill my young men with the sword, and dash the sucking children against the ground, and make mine infants as a prey, and my virgins as a spoil.

6 But the Almighty Lord hath disappointed them by the hand of a woman.

7 For the mighty one did not fall by the young men, neither did the sons of the Titans smite him, nor high giants set upon him: but Judith the daughter of Merari weakened him with the beauty of her countenance.

8 For she put off the garment of her widowhood for the exaltation of those that were oppressed in Israel, and anointed her face with ointment, and bound her hair in a tire, and took a linen garment to deceive him.

9 Her sandals ravished his eyes, her beauty took his mind prisoner, and the fauchion passed through his neck.

10 The Persians quaked at her boldness, and the Medes were daunted at her hardiness.

11 Then my afflicted shouted for joy, and my weak ones cried aloud; but they were astonished: these lifted up their voices, but they were overthrown.

12 The sons of the damsels have pierced them through, and wounded them as fugatives' children: they perished by the battle of the Lord.

13 I will sing unto the Lord a new song: O Lord, thou art great and glorious, wonderful in strength, and invincible.

14 Let all creatures serve thee: for thou spakest, and they were made, thou didst send forth thy spirit, and it created them, and there is none that can resist thy voice.

15 For the mountains shall be moved from their foundations with the waters, the rocks shall melt as wax at thy presence: yet thou art merciful to them that fear thee.

16 For all sacrifice is too little for a sweet savour unto thee, and all the fat is not sufficient for thy burnt offering: but he that feareth the Lord is great at all times.

17 Woe to the nations that rise up against my kindred! the Lord Almighty will take vengeance of them in the day of judgment, in putting fire and worms in their flesh; and they shall feel them, and weep for ever.

18 Now as soon as they entered into Jerusalem, they worshipped the Lord; and as soon as the people were purified, they offered their burnt offerings, and their free offerings, and their gifts.

19 Judith also dedicated all the stuff of Holofernes, which the people had given her, and gave the canopy, which she had taken out of his bedchamber, for a gift unto the Lord.

20 So the people continued feasting in Jerusalem before the sanctuary for the space of three months and Judith remained with them.

21 After this time every one returned to his own inheritance, and Judith went to Bethulia, and remained in her own possession, and was in her time honourable in all the country.

22 And many desired her, but none knew her all the days of her life, after that Manasses her husband was dead, and was gathered to his people.

23 But she increased more and more in honour, and waxed old in her husband's house, being an hundred and five years old, and made her maid free; so she died in Bethulia: and they buried her in the cave of her husband Manasses.

24 And the house of Israel lamented her seven days: and before she died, she did distribute her goods to all them that were nearest of kindred to Manasses her husband, and to them that were the nearest of her kindred.

25 And there was none that made the children of Israel any more afraid in the days of Judith, nor a long time after her death. Go back to the Bible

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

What the chapter says, in plain language

Judith sings a thanksgiving in all Israel, and all the people sing after her: the Lord who breaks battles, whose sandals and beauty took Holofernes's mind prisoner and whose sword passed through his neck, who is great and glorious, wonderful in strength and invincible, and who will take vengeance on the nations that rise against his kindred. The spoils are dedicated, Judith lives honoured, and there is none to make Israel afraid in the days of Judith, nor a long time after her death. Interpreted Chapter text and study notes for this chapter

Unfamiliar terms

  • New song. the song Judith sings to the Lord, 'I will sing unto the Lord a new song' (verse 13). Interpreted Study note for this chapter
  • Breaker of battles. the title given to God in Judith's prayer and song, who breaks the battles of his enemies. Interpreted Study note for this chapter

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Then Judith began to sing this thanksgiving in all Israel, and all the people sang after her this song of praise; "sung" is in Greek, "Gave out this confession, in all Israel: and all the people echoed this praise," either repeating the whole after her, or singing the first verse as a chorus (Exodus 15:20; 1 Samuel 18:6; 1 Chronicles 16:8). Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 2. And Judith said, "Begin unto my God with timbrels, sing unto my Lord with cymbals: tune unto him a new psalm: exalt him, and call upon his name"; "saying" is in Greek, "And Judith said: Begin ye to my Lord" — it is time for us to break silence, and to sound forth the praises of God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 3. For God breaketh the battles: for among the camps in the midst of the people he hath delivered me out of the hands of them that persecuted me; "wars" means destroying armies, and God is often represented as a mighty warrior (Isaiah 1:24; Jeremiah 2:20; Romans 9:29). This passage is quoted by St. Ephrem as a part of Scripture. The Greek reads, "For he has drawn me from the hands of my persecutors, into his camp, in the midst of the people." Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 4. Assur came out of the mountains from the north, he came with ten thousands of his army, the multitude whereof stopped the torrents, and their horsemen have covered the hills; the mountains are those of Cilicia, and the defiles of Libanus and Hermon, on the north of Palestine, by which road they were forced to come, as the desert Arabia was impassable for a large army. "North" includes to the east (Isaiah 14:13; Jeremiah 1:13; Ezekiel 1:4). "Torrents" — drinking them up, as Sennacherib boasted (2 Kings 19:24), and the Greek asserts the same of Xerxes' army (Herodotus vii. 108); the Greek seems to insinuate that the Assyrians "stopped up the springs," to distress the country, as Philopator did. "Valleys" is in Greek "hills." Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 5. He bragged that he would burn up my borders, and kill my young men with the sword, and dash the sucking children against the ground, and make mine infants as a prey, and my virgins as a spoil; the Greek reads, "that he would throw my children at the breast, on the pavement, and make" them a prey and spoil. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 6. But the Almighty Lord hath disappointed them by the hand of a woman; "stuck" is literally "hurt him, overturning all his projects," as the Greek intimates, and "him" is in Greek "them, by the hand of a woman." Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 7. For the mighty one did not fall by the young men, neither did the sons of the Titans smite him, nor high giants set upon him: but Judith the daughter of Merari weakened him with the beauty of her countenance; "men" means soldiers (chapter 14:6; Genesis 14:24), and "Titan" is how the Septuagint renders Raphaim (2 Samuel 5:18). The Greek translator seems to have read the poets, who pretend that the Titans attempted to take heaven by storm; neither such giants as those who lived before the flood (Genesis 6) nor such as were seen after (Numbers 13) attacked Holofernes, and neither does the Vulgate give any authority to poetic fictions, but only adopts terms which are best understood. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 8. For she put off the garment of her widowhood for the exaltation of those that were oppressed in Israel, and anointed her face with ointment, and bound her hair in a tire, and took a linen garment to deceive him; "and put" is in Greek, "for the exaltation of those who laboured in Israel." The "crown" or tire is in Greek "mitre," or ribband ornamented with jewels (chapter 10:3), Syriac "net-work," and the "new" garment is in Greek "linen stole," a long robe usually of linen, worn both by men and women. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 9. Her sandals ravished his eyes, her beauty took his mind prisoner, and the fauchion passed through his neck; "sandals" — the bandages which tied the shoe-soles to the feet were most ornamental (Isaiah 3), and the city of Antylla was assigned to furnish sandals for the queens of Egypt (Herodotus xii. 98). "With" is in Greek, "a sword (acinace) passed through his neck." She deceived him, as he would make love to her, and thus give her an opportunity to perform what she had designed; she was not actuated by the desire of being admired, but sought to deliver her people. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 10. The Persians quaked at her boldness, and the Medes were daunted at her hardiness; "Medes" — this is the first mention of these two nations, who afterwards became so famous, and Nabuchodonosor had overcome Phraortes (chapter 1). Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 11. Then my afflicted shouted for joy, and my weak ones cried aloud; but they were astonished: these lifted up their voices, but they were overthrown; the Greek reads, "Than my lowly ones (she speaks thus contemptuously of the Assyrians) howled, my weak ones cried out through fear; they raised their voice, and were overthrown." Others think that she is speaking of her fellow-citizens, or both. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 12. The sons of the damsels have pierced them through, and wounded them as fugitives' children: they perished by the battle of the Lord; "damsels" means young boys, and Syriac reads, "they run them through, as if they had been damsels." "Children" is in Syriac "slaves" — being thus overtaken, what could they do but sue for pardon? "Before" is in Greek "of the Lord." Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 13. I will sing unto the Lord a new song: O Lord, thou art great and glorious, wonderful in strength, and invincible; "let" is in Greek, "I will sing a new hymn to the Lord. Lord, thou art great." Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 14. Let all creatures serve thee: for thou spakest, and they were made, thou didst send forth thy spirit, and it created them, and there is none that can resist thy voice; "spirit, and they" is in Greek, "he built" the world (Genesis 1:8; Psalm 32:9). Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 15. For the mountains shall be moved from their foundations with the waters, the rocks shall melt as wax at thy presence: yet thou art merciful to them that fear thee; "waters" are moved by earthquakes and storms (Job 9:5; Isaiah 5:25; Psalm 17:8). Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 16. For all sacrifice is too little for a sweet savour unto thee, and all the fat is not sufficient for thy burnt offering: but he that feareth the Lord is great at all times; the Greek reads, "treated mercifully by thee: for little with thee is all sacrifice, for the odour of sweetness; and all fat is but the least for thy holocausts. But he who fears the Lord, is ever great." This text is remarkable, as it shows that no sacrifice can please God without interior holiness; outward magnificence will be otherwise rejected with contempt. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 17. Woe to the nations that rise up against my kindred! the Lord Almighty will take vengeance of them in the day of judgment, in putting fire and worms in their flesh; and they shall feel them, and weep for ever; "he will visit them" is not in Greek, and only expresses the same again. "Flesh" is thus punished for ever (St. Augustine, City of God xxv. 4), while the soul is still more tormented with the loss of God's vision; the bodies of those who persecute God's people will not only be thrown out with ignominy, but the impenitent shall suffer eternal torments, and these worms and fires do not cease. They are not merely figurative, but real, according to the Fathers, though the damned have not yet their bodies, they are no less affected with pain, as people who have lost a member often seem to feel pain in it. The Jews spoke of eternal torments in similar terms (Ecclesiasticus 7:19; Isaiah 66:24; Mark 9:45). Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 18. Now as soon as they entered into Jerusalem, they worshipped the Lord; and as soon as the people were purified, they offered their burnt offerings, and their free offerings, and their gifts; the Greek reads, "But as soon as they arrived at Jerusalem, they adored God; and when the people were purified, they offered their holocausts, and free gifts, and presents." They stood in need of purification, as they had shed blood, and had touched so many things of the Assyrians, which were to them unclean (Numbers 31:24). Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 19. Judith also dedicated all the stuff of Holofernes, which the people had given her, and gave the canopy, which she had taken out of his bedchamber, for a gift unto the Lord; "an anathema of oblivion" is a gift or offering made to God, by way of an everlasting monument, to prevent the oblivion or forgetting of so great a benefit — yet some would read oblationis instead of oblivionis. Greek and Syriac say nothing of oblivion, and the Greek reads, "to the Lord, all the vessels," or furniture (chapter 15:14). Joseph called one of his sons Manasses, because God had caused him to forget his former toils (Genesis 41:51); Judith's husband had the same name, and this victory made her forget past sorrow. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 20. So the people continued feasting in Jerusalem before the sanctuary for the space of three months and Judith remained with them; "three" is in Syriac "one entire month," and "joy" is in Greek, "and Judith remained with them." Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 21. After this time every one returned to his own inheritance, and Judith went to Bethulia, and remained in her own possession, and was in her time honourable in all the country; the Greek reads, "returned also to Bethulia, and dwelt on her own possessions; and in her time, was honourable throughout all the land." Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 22. And many desired her, but none knew her all the days of her life, after that Manasses her husband was dead, and was gathered to his people; "chastity" is in Greek, "many desired to have her (in marriage) but no man knew her." She again practised the same mortifications which she had done before, and the Greek adds, "when he was removed to his people. And proceeding (to the temple, or advancing in age) she was very great, and she grew old in her" husband's house. She probably went up to Jerusalem at the great festivals. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 23. But she increased more and more in honour, and waxed old in her husband's house, being an hundred and five years old, and made her maid free; so she died in Bethulia: and they buried her in the cave of her husband Manasses; "five" is read "fifty" by Suidas alone. Some would suppose that Judith lived 105 years after her husband's death, so that she might have been in all 125 years old, but she would thus have survived the siege of Jerusalem under Nabuchodonosor, and the text does not require this sense. "Free" — as she had been her assistant in such a glorious work. "Died" is in Greek, "in Bethulia; and they buried her in the cave of her husband, Manasses" (see chapter 8:3). Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 24. And the house of Israel lamented her seven days: and before she died, she did distribute her goods to all them that were nearest of kindred to Manasses her husband, and to them that were the nearest of her kindred; "seven days" is the usual term (Ecclesiasticus 22:13), but it was extraordinary for the whole province to mourn for an individual. Greek and Syriac add, "And she divided all her possessions, before her death, among the nearest relations of her husband, Manasses, and among her own," which was very equitable, as she had no children, we may suppose. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 25. And there was none that made the children of Israel any more afraid in the days of Judith, nor a long time after her death; "years" is in Greek "days" — the duration of the peace after her death. Judith was a widow near seventy years, showing an excellent pattern to all in that state; notwithstanding the many inducements she might have had to marry again, she chose to abstain, for greater perfection (Matthew 19; 1 Corinthians 7). From the death of Holofernes (year of the world 3348) to that of Josias (year 3394) only forty-six years of peace would occur in Juda, and it is much more probable that the peace subsisted from the 11th of Manasses to the death of Josias, 73 (Greek, 78) years, and that she was not above forty when she performed her exploit, as she was then styled a beautiful girl (chapter 12:12) and many desired to marry her. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock

Investigation (editorial — a question investigated, not concluded)

Judith's song, and the song of Exodus 15

This section investigates a parallel; it does not conclude that one passage depends on the other, shares its authorship, or records the same event. Parallels and differences are both stated, and the question is left open where the evidence runs out. Interpreted Editorial framing for this section

Parallel. Both Judith 16 and Exodus 15 are victory songs sung after a deliverance, praising God as the breaker of battles, with women leading the rejoicing: Miriam with timbrels in Exodus, Judith with timbrels here (Judith's dance-leading is at 15:12–13). The study note on verse 1 of this chapter itself connects the song to Exodus 15:20. Interpreted Judith 16; Exodus 15 (Standard corpus); study note on verse 1, summarizing George Leo Haydock

Difference. The songs centre different deeds: Exodus celebrates the sea closing over Pharaoh's host, while Judith's song celebrates a deed done by one woman's hand in an enemy tent, and goes on to dedicate spoils and promise vengeance on the nations in the day of judgment. Interpreted Judith 16; Exodus 15 (Standard corpus)

Unresolved. Whether these passages are related beyond the parallel itself is not established by the sources consulted for this page, and no dependence, shared authorship, or historical certainty is claimed here. Unresolved No source consulted for this page establishes the relationship

They are not merely figurative, but real, according to the Fathers, though the damned have not yet their bodies, they are no less affected with pain, as people who have lost a member often seem to feel pain in it. The Jews spoke of eternal torments in similar terms (Ecclesiasticus 7:19; Isaiah 66:24; Mark 9:45). Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 18. Now as soon as they entered into Jerusalem, they worshipped the Lord; and as soon as the people were purified, they offered their burnt offerings, and their free offerings, and their gifts; the Greek reads, "But as soon as they arrived at Jerusalem, they adored God; and when the people were purified, they offered their holocausts, and free gifts, and presents." They stood in need of purification, as they had shed blood, and had touched so many things of the Assyrians, which were to them unclean (Numbers 31:24). Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 19. Judith also dedicated all the stuff of Holofernes, which the people had given her, and gave the canopy, which she had taken out of his bedchamber, for a gift unto the Lord; "an anathema of oblivion" is a gift or offering made to God, by way of an everlasting monument, to prevent the oblivion or forgetting of so great a benefit — yet some would read oblationis instead of oblivionis. Greek and Syriac say nothing of oblivion, and the Greek reads, "to the Lord, all the vessels," or furniture (chapter 15:14). Joseph called one of his sons Manasses, because God had caused him to forget his former toils (Genesis 41:51); Judith's husband had the same name, and this victory made her forget past sorrow. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 20. So the people continued feasting in Jerusalem before the sanctuary for the space of three months and Judith remained with them; "three" is in Syriac "one entire month," and "joy" is in Greek, "and Judith remained with them." Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 21. After this time every one returned to his own inheritance, and Judith went to Bethulia, and remained in her own possession, and was in her time honourable in all the country; the Greek reads, "returned also to Bethulia, and dwelt on her own possessions; and in her time, was honourable throughout all the land." Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 22. And many desired her, but none knew her all the days of her life, after that Manasses her husband was dead, and was gathered to his people; "chastity" is in Greek, "many desired to have her (in marriage) but no man knew her." She again practised the same mortifications which she had done before, and the Greek adds, "when he was removed to his people. And proceeding (to the temple, or advancing in age) she was very great, and she grew old in her" husband's house. She probably went up to Jerusalem at the great festivals. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 23. But she increased more and more in honour, and waxed old in her husband's house, being an hundred and five years old, and made her maid free; so she died in Bethulia: and they buried her in the cave of her husband Manasses; "five" is read "fifty" by Suidas alone. Some would suppose that Judith lived 105 years after her husband's death, so that she might have been in all 125 years old, but she would thus have survived the siege of Jerusalem under Nabuchodonosor, and the text does not require this sense. "Free" — as she had been her assistant in such a glorious work. "Died" is in Greek, "in Bethulia; and they buried her in the cave of her husband, Manasses" (see chapter 8:3). Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 24. And the house of Israel lamented her seven days: and before she died, she did distribute her goods to all them that were nearest of kindred to Manasses her husband, and to them that were the nearest of her kindred; "seven days" is the usual term (Ecclesiasticus 22:13), but it was extraordinary for the whole province to mourn for an individual. Greek and Syriac add, "And she divided all her possessions, before her death, among the nearest relations of her husband, Manasses, and among her own," which was very equitable, as she had no children, we may suppose. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 25. And there was none that made the children of Israel any more afraid in the days of Judith, nor a long time after her death; "years" is in Greek "days" — the duration of the peace after her death. Judith was a widow near seventy years, showing an excellent pattern to all in that state; notwithstanding the many inducements she might have had to marry again, she chose to abstain, for greater perfection (Matthew 19; 1 Corinthians 7). From the death of Holofernes (year of the world 3348) to that of Josias (year 3394) only forty-six years of peace would occur in Juda, and it is much more probable that the peace subsisted from the 11th of Manasses to the death of Josias, 73 (Greek, 78) years, and that she was not above forty when she performed her exploit, as she was then styled a beautiful girl (chapter 12:12) and many desired to marry her. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
