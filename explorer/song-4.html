The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Song of Songs 4 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Behold, thou art fair, my love; behold, thou art fair; thou hast doves’ eyes within thy locks: thy hair is as a flock of goats, that appear from mount Gilead.

2 Thy teeth are like a flock of sheep that are even shorn, which came up from the washing; whereof every one bear twins, and none is barren among them.

3 Thy lips are like a thread of scarlet, and thy speech is comely: thy temples are like a piece of a pomegranate within thy locks.

4 Thy neck is like the tower of David builded for an armoury, whereon there hang a thousand bucklers, all shields of mighty men.

5 Thy two breasts are like two young roes that are twins, which feed among the lilies.

6 Until the day break, and the shadows flee away, I will get me to the mountain of myrrh, and to the hill of frankincense.

7 Thou art all fair, my love; there is no spot in thee.

8 Come with me from Lebanon, my spouse, with me from Lebanon: look from the top of Amana, from the top of Shenir and Hermon, from the lions’ dens, from the mountains of the leopards.

9 Thou hast ravished my heart, my sister, my spouse; thou hast ravished my heart with one of thine eyes, with one chain of thy neck.

10 How fair is thy love, my sister, my spouse! how much better is thy love than wine! and the smell of thine ointments than all spices!

11 Thy lips, O my spouse, drop as the honeycomb: honey and milk are under thy tongue; and the smell of thy garments is like the smell of Lebanon.

12 A garden inclosed is my sister, my spouse; a spring shut up, a fountain sealed.

13 Thy plants are an orchard of pomegranates, with pleasant fruits; camphire, with spikenard,

14 Spikenard and saffron; calamus and cinnamon, with all trees of frankincense; myrrh and aloes, with all the chief spices:

15 A fountain of gardens, a well of living waters, and streams from Lebanon.

16 Awake, O north wind; and come, thou south; blow upon my garden, that the spices thereof may flow out. Let my beloved come into his garden, and eat his pleasant fruits.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

The lover praises his love from head to foot, doves' eyes, scarlet lips, a neck like David's tower, and then calls her from Lebanon as a locked garden, a sealed fountain, an orchard of pomegranates, spices and living waters. She answers by inviting the winds to blow on her garden so its spices may flow out. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Behold, thou art fair, my love, with doves' eyes within thy locks, and hair as a flock of goats from mount Gilead. Henry cautions that if each comparison has a meaning applicable to the graces of the church, it is not clearly known, and great mistakes are made by fanciful guesses; the sure point is that believers are fair and comely when justified in Christ's righteousness and adorned with spiritual graces. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 2. Thy teeth are like a flock of sheep even shorn, which came up from the washing, every one bearing twins, none barren among them. Henry declines to fix a fanciful meaning to each image, and instead points to the general picture: thoughts, words, and deeds, though imperfect, pure, manifesting a heart nourished by the gospel. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 3. Thy lips are like a thread of scarlet, thy speech comely, thy temples like a piece of pomegranate within thy locks. The comparisons belong to the same portrait of a believer made fair by grace, Henry says, rather than to any certain one-to-one reading. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 4. Thy neck is like the tower of David builded for an armoury, whereon hang a thousand bucklers, all shields of mighty men. As with the other images in this passage, Henry warns against fanciful guesses and rests on the general truth of the church adorned and defended by grace. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 5. Thy two breasts are like two young roes that are twins, which feed among the lilies. This image, like the rest of the passage, is part of the portrait of believers made comely by Christ's righteousness, Henry notes, without a clearly known particular meaning. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 6. Until the day break and the shadows flee away, I will get me to the mountain of myrrh and to the hill of frankincense. The mountain of myrrh appears to mean Mount Moriah, Henry says, where the temple stood and incense burned, his residence till the shadows of the law were dispersed by the breaking of the gospel day. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 7. Thou art all fair, my love; there is no spot in thee. How fair and comely are believers, Henry says, when justified in Christ's righteousness and adorned with spiritual graces. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 8. Come with me from Lebanon, my spouse, look from the top of Amana, from Shenir and Hermon, from the lions' dens, from the mountains of the leopards. Observe the gracious call Christ gives to the church, Henry says: as a precept, it is his call to come off from the world, for those pleasant hills hold lions' dens; as a promise, many shall be brought from every point, and the church shall be delivered from her persecutors in due time. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 9. Thou hast ravished my heart, my sister, my spouse, with one of thine eyes, with one chain of thy neck. Christ's heart is upon his church, Henry says; his treasure is therein, and he delights in the affection she has for him, its working in the heart and its works in the life. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 10. How fair is thy love, my sister, my spouse! How much better is thy love than wine, and the smell of thine ointments than all spices! The odours wherewith the spouse is perfumed are as the gifts and graces of the Spirit, Henry explains, and love and obedience to God are more pleasing to Christ than sacrifice or incense. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 11. Thy lips, O my spouse, drop as the honeycomb; honey and milk are under thy tongue, and the smell of thy garments is like the smell of Lebanon. Christ having put upon his spouse the white raiment of his own righteousness, Henry says, and perfumed it with holy joy and comfort, he is well pleased with it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 12. A garden enclosed is my sister, my spouse; a spring shut up, a fountain sealed. Christ walks in his garden unseen, Henry says, and a hedge of protection is made around it which all the powers of darkness cannot break through. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 13. Thy plants are an orchard of pomegranates, with pleasant fruits, camphire with spikenard. Saints in the church and graces in the saints are fitly compared to fruits and spices, Henry notes: they are planted and do not grow of themselves, they are precious, and grace, when ended in glory, will last for ever. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 14. Spikenard and saffron, calamus and cinnamon, with all trees of frankincense, myrrh and aloes, with all the chief spices. These spices are the graces planted in the enclosed garden, Henry says, kept to good purpose when flowers are withered. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 15. A fountain of gardens, a well of living waters, and streams from Lebanon. The souls of believers are as gardens enclosed, Henry explains, where is a well of living water, the influences of the Holy Spirit, which no opposer can corrupt; and Christ is the source which makes these gardens fruitful. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 16. Awake, O north wind, and come, thou south; blow upon my garden, that the spices thereof may flow out. Let my beloved come into his garden and eat his pleasant fruits. The church prays for the influences of the blessed Spirit to make this garden fruitful, Henry says: the north wind of conviction and the south wind of comfort stir up good affections, and the believer can have no joy of the fruits unless they redound to the glory of Christ. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
