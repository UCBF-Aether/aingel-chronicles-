The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Ecclesiastes 4 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 So I returned, and considered all the oppressions that are done under the sun: and behold the tears of such as were oppressed, and they had no comforter; and on the side of their oppressors there was power; but they had no comforter.

2 Wherefore I praised the dead which are already dead more than the living which are yet alive.

3 Yea, better is he than both they, which hath not yet been, who hath not seen the evil work that is done under the sun.

4 Again, I considered all travail, and every right work, that for this a man is envied of his neighbour. This is also vanity and vexation of spirit.

5 The fool foldeth his hands together, and eateth his own flesh.

6 Better is an handful with quietness, than both the hands full with travail and vexation of spirit.

7 Then I returned, and I saw vanity under the sun.

8 There is one alone, and there is not a second; yea, he hath neither child nor brother: yet is there no end of all his labour; neither is his eye satisfied with riches; neither saith he, For whom do I labour, and bereave my soul of good? This is also vanity, yea, it is a sore travail.

9 Two are better than one; because they have a good reward for their labour.

10 For if they fall, the one will lift up his fellow: but woe to him that is alone when he falleth; for he hath not another to help him up.

11 Again, if two lie together, then they have heat: but how can one be warm alone?

12 And if one prevail against him, two shall withstand him; and a threefold cord is not quickly broken.

13 Better is a poor and a wise child than an old and foolish king, who will no more be admonished.

14 For out of prison he cometh to reign; whereas also he that is born in his kingdom becometh poor.

15 I considered all the living which walk under the sun, with the second child that shall stand up in his stead.

16 There is no end of all the people, even of all that have been before them: they also that come after shall not rejoice in him. Surely this also is vanity and vexation of spirit.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

The Preacher turns to oppression without a comforter, to labour driven by envy, and to the loneliness of the solitary worker. Against these he sets companionship: two are better than one, a threefold cord is not quickly broken, and even a poor wise child outranks an old foolish king. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. He returned and considered all the oppressions done under the sun, and saw the tears of the oppressed with no comforter, while power stood on the side of the oppressors. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Therefore he praised the dead more than the living, not as a wish for death itself, but because the dead are past the reach of oppression that the living still endure. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Better than both is he who has not yet been, who has not seen the evil work done under the sun, because he has neither suffered oppression nor witnessed it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Again he considered all travail and every right work, and saw that for this a man is envied of his neighbour, so that even honest success breeds envy, which is also vanity and vexation of spirit. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. The fool folds his hands together and eats his own flesh, because sloth consumes a man as surely as labour wearies him, and idleness is its own ruin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Better is a handful with quietness than both hands full with travail and vexation of spirit, because a little held in peace is worth more than abundance held in turmoil. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. He returned and saw vanity under the sun in a solitary labourer, a picture of toil without fellowship or heir, which Henry makes the next instance of earthly emptiness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. There is one alone, with neither child nor brother, yet no end to his labour and his eye never satisfied with riches, and he never asks for whom he labours and bereaves his soul of good, which is also vanity and sore travail. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Two are better than one, because they have a good reward for their labour, sharing both the work and its fruit, which the solitary man cannot do. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. If one falls, the other lifts up his fellow, but woe to him that is alone when he falls, for he has not another to help him up. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. If two lie together they have heat, but how can one be warm alone, a homely proof that fellowship supplies what solitude cannot. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. If one prevails against a single man, two shall withstand him, and a threefold cord is not quickly broken, because union gives a strength that no solitary strength possesses. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Better is a poor and wise child than an old and foolish king who will no more be admonished, because wisdom, even in youth and poverty, outranks power grown deaf to counsel. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Out of prison he comes to reign, whereas also he that is born in his kingdom becomes poor, because fortune raises the low and lowers the high without regard to merit. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. He considered all the living who walk under the sun with the second child that shall stand up in the king's stead, because crowds transfer their favour to the rising heir as quickly as they gave it to the old king. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. There is no end of all the people, even of all that have been before them, yet they also that come after shall not rejoice in him, so that popularity itself is vanity and vexation of spirit. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
