The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Numbers 11 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 And when the people complained, it displeased the LORD: and the LORD heard it; and his anger was kindled; and the fire of the LORD burnt among them, and consumed them that were in the uttermost parts of the camp.

2 And the people cried unto Moses; and when Moses prayed unto the LORD, the fire was quenched.

3 And he called the name of the place Taberah: because the fire of the LORD burnt among them.

4 And the mixt multitude that was among them fell a lusting: and the children of Israel also wept again, and said, Who shall give us flesh to eat?

5 We remember the fish, which we did eat in Egypt freely; the cucumbers, and the melons, and the leeks, and the onions, and the garlick:

6 But now our soul is dried away: there is nothing at all, beside this manna, before our eyes.

7 And the manna was as coriander seed, and the colour thereof as the colour of bdellium.

8 And the people went about, and gathered it, and ground it in mills, or beat it in a mortar, and baked it in pans, and made cakes of it: and the taste of it was as the taste of fresh oil.

9 And when the dew fell upon the camp in the night, the manna fell upon it.

10 Then Moses heard the people weep throughout their families, every man in the door of his tent: and the anger of the LORD was kindled greatly; Moses also was displeased.

11 And Moses said unto the LORD, Wherefore hast thou afflicted thy servant? and wherefore have I not found favour in thy sight, that thou layest the burden of all this people upon me?

12 Have I conceived all this people? have I begotten them, that thou shouldest say unto me, Carry them in thy bosom, as a nursing father beareth the sucking child, unto the land which thou swarest unto their fathers?

13 Whence should I have flesh to give unto all this people? for they weep unto me, saying, Give us flesh, that we may eat.

14 I am not able to bear all this people alone, because it is too heavy for me.

15 And if thou deal thus with me, kill me, I pray thee, out of hand, if I have found favour in thy sight; and let me not see my wretchedness.

16 And the LORD said unto Moses, Gather unto me seventy men of the elders of Israel, whom thou knowest to be the elders of the people, and officers over them; and bring them unto the tabernacle of the congregation, that they may stand there with thee.

17 And I will come down and talk with thee there: and I will take of the spirit which is upon thee, and will put it upon them; and they shall bear the burden of the people with thee, that thou bear it not thyself alone.

18 And say thou unto the people, Sanctify yourselves against to morrow, and ye shall eat flesh: for ye have wept in the ears of the LORD, saying, Who shall give us flesh to eat? for it was well with us in Egypt: therefore the LORD will give you flesh, and ye shall eat.

19 Ye shall not eat one day, nor two days, nor five days, neither ten days, nor twenty days;

20 But even a whole month, until it come out at your nostrils, and it be loathsome unto you: because that ye have despised the LORD which is among you, and have wept before him, saying, Why came we forth out of Egypt?

21 And Moses said, The people, among whom I am, are six hundred thousand footmen; and thou hast said, I will give them flesh, that they may eat a whole month.

22 Shall the flocks and the herds be slain for them, to suffice them? or shall all the fish of the sea be gathered together for them, to suffice them?

23 And the LORD said unto Moses, Is the LORD’s hand waxed short? thou shalt see now whether my word shall come to pass unto thee or not.

24 And Moses went out, and told the people the words of the LORD, and gathered the seventy men of the elders of the people, and set them round about the tabernacle.

25 And the LORD came down in a cloud, and spake unto him, and took of the spirit that was upon him, and gave it unto the seventy elders: and it came to pass, that, when the spirit rested upon them, they prophesied, and did not cease.

26 But there remained two of the men in the camp, the name of the one was Eldad, and the name of the other Medad: and the spirit rested upon them; and they were of them that were written, but went not out unto the tabernacle: and they prophesied in the camp.

27 And there ran a young man, and told Moses, and said, Eldad and Medad do prophesy in the camp.

28 And Joshua the son of Nun, the servant of Moses, one of his young men, answered and said, My lord Moses, forbid them.

29 And Moses said unto him, Enviest thou for my sake? would God that all the LORD’s people were prophets, and that the LORD would put his spirit upon them!

30 And Moses gat him into the camp, he and the elders of Israel.

31 And there went forth a wind from the LORD, and brought quails from the sea, and let them fall by the camp, as it were a day’s journey on this side, and as it were a day’s journey on the other side, round about the camp, and as it were two cubits high upon the face of the earth.

32 And the people stood up all that day, and all that night, and all the next day, and they gathered the quails: he that gathered least gathered ten homers: and they spread them all abroad for themselves round about the camp.

33 And while the flesh was yet between their teeth, ere it was chewed, the wrath of the LORD was kindled against the people, and the LORD smote the people with a very great plague.

34 And he called the name of that place Kibrothhattaavah: because there they buried the people that lusted.

35 And the people journeyed from Kibrothhattaavah unto Hazeroth; and abode at Hazeroth.

How to read the labels

Every substantive historical claim in the editorial panels below carries one of three labels, plus a source reference:

  • Documented — directly supported by a cited source.
  • Interpreted — a reasoned explanation or proposed relationship.
  • Unresolved — evidence is insufficient to establish the claim.

A claim does NOT become Documented merely because a commentary states it. Commentary views are attributed to their authors and labeled Interpreted unless a primary textual source directly supports the claim. Where evidence runs out, the page says Unresolved and stops — it does not fill the gap.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

This chapter tells of complaint in the wilderness. Fire from the LORD burns at the edge of the camp at Taberah until Moses prays. Then the mixed multitude craves meat, the people weep for the fish, cucumbers, and leeks of Egypt, and despise the manna, which the chapter describes in detail. Moses, crushed by the burden, is given seventy elders who share the spirit that is on him, and they prophesy in the camp. Quails fall in abundance, but while the flesh is still between their teeth a plague strikes, and the place is called Kibroth-hattaavah, the graves of lust. Interpreted Editorial overview, grounded in the chapter text and its study notes

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. When the people complained, it displeased the Lord, and his fire burned among them and consumed those in the uttermost parts of the camp. JFB notes the discontent seems confined to the camp's extremities, where the mixed multitude likely stood, and Henry adds that God knows secret frettings of the heart, though Moses did not, and his judgments came gradually, that they might take warning. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. The people cried to Moses, and when Moses prayed, the fire was quenched. The judgment stopped at intercession, showing, as Henry says, that God delights not in punishing: when he begins, he is soon prevailed with to let it fall. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 3. He called the place Taberah, because the fire of the Lord burnt among them. The name remained a monument of national sin and punishment, as JFB notes, just as Kibroth-hattaavah would later name the graves of lust. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. The mixed multitude among them fell a lusting, and Israel wept again, saying, who shall give us flesh to eat? JFB identifies the multitude as Egyptians, whose appetite for animal food became a disease of imagination, and Israel, incited by their clamours, joined the complaint against the manna. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. They remembered the fish eaten freely in Egypt, the cucumbers, melons, leeks, onions and garlic. JFB details the Egyptian diet: fish from Nile lakes and canals in April and May, the smooth foot-long cucumber, juicy watermelons, sweet onions that fed the labouring classes, and garlic once abundant, all grateful food in warm countries. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Now their soul was dried away, with nothing at all beside this manna before their eyes. JFB notes daily familiarity had disgusted them with the monotonous food, and Henry answers their scorn: the manna cost no money or care and little labour, yet they talked of Egypt's cheapness as if it had cost nothing, when they paid dearly for it with hard service. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. The manna was like coriander seed, its colour like bdellium. JFB explains the resemblance was in size and figure, not colour, and bdellium, a drop of white gum or a white pearl, helps picture it, while its taste in the raw state was like fresh oil, and when ground and baked, like wafers made with honey. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. The people went about gathering it, ground it in mills or beat it in mortars, baked it in pans, and made cakes of it. JFB notes this proves it was not the natural manna of the Arabian desert, which is too gummy to be ground into meal, and the minute description shows the sinfulness of dissatisfaction with such excellent food, furnished plentifully and gratuitously. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. When the dew fell on the camp at night, the manna fell upon it. The nightly provision, arriving with the dew, was a daily, unearned gift, which makes the people's contempt for it, in Henry's words, the fault-finding of peevish minds with that which has no fault but that it is too good for them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Moses heard the people weep throughout their families, every man at his tent door, and the anger of the Lord was kindled greatly, and Moses was displeased. The weeping was universal, family by family, and it pressed on Moses as a public crisis, not a private grumble. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Moses asked the Lord why he had afflicted his servant, and why he had not found favour, that the burden of all this people was laid on him. JFB sympathises with Moses' distress, though the tone cannot be justified: he had a mighty multitude under his care with no means of satisfying their clamorous demands, and felt the undivided responsibility of his office. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Moses asked, have I conceived all this people, have I begotten them, that you should say, carry them in your bosom as a nursing father carries a sucking child? Henry notes Moses magnified his own performances and undervalued the honour God had put on him, speaking as if the people were his own burden rather than God's charge. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 13. Moses asked, whence should I have flesh to give all this people, for they weep to me, saying, give us flesh that we may eat? The question exposes his helplessness: the demand was real, the people were clamorous, and Moses had no store from which to answer them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Moses said, I am not able to bear all this people alone, because it is too heavy for me. Henry's lesson stands over the verse: had the work been much less, Moses could not have done it in his own strength, but had it been much greater, through God strengthening him he might have done it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 15. Moses prayed, if you deal thus with me, kill me out of hand, if I have found favour, and let me not see my wretchedness. Henry judges the prayer as expressed otherwise than became him, yet it reveals a leader agonised and almost overwhelmed, and teaches us to pray, lead us not into temptation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. The Lord told Moses to gather seventy men of the elders of Israel, whom he knew to be elders and officers, and bring them to the tabernacle to stand with him. JFB notes the order of seventy, whether selected or new, was empowered to assist by collective wisdom and experience, and Jewish writers call it the origin of the Sanhedrin, though it was likely only a temporary expedient. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. God would come down and talk with Moses there, and take of the spirit upon him and put it on them, that they might bear the burden with him. JFB explains the spirit means the gifts and influences of the Spirit, especially prophecy, an extraordinary penetration in discovering hidden things, and taking it did not impair Moses at all. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. Moses was to tell the people to sanctify themselves against tomorrow, for they would eat flesh. JFB notes prepare yourselves by repentance and submission, but the tenor implied a severe rebuke: the blessing promised would prove a curse. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. They would not eat one day, nor two, nor five, nor ten, nor twenty days, but a whole month. Henry's moral is the vanity of all delights of sense: they will cloy but not satisfy, while spiritual pleasures alone satisfy and last. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 20. They would eat a whole month, until it came out at their nostrils and became loathsome to them, because they despised the Lord who was among them and wept before him, saying, why came we out of Egypt? The loathing was the judgment itself: the desired flesh would sicken them, because they had despised the God who gave them manna. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 21. Moses objected that the people among whom he stood were six hundred thousand footmen, yet God said, shall flesh be given them for a whole month? JFB notes the great leader, struck by so astonishing a promise for more than two million people in a desert, betrayed a momentary incredulous spirit, uttered only to himself, sharply reproved but not punished. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Moses asked, shall the flocks and herds be slain for them to suffice them, or shall all the fish of the sea be gathered together for them? Henry notes even true and great believers sometimes find it hard to trust God under the discouragements of second causes, and against hope to believe in hope. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 23. The Lord answered, is the Lord's hand waxed short? You shall see now whether my word shall come to pass to you or not. Henry notes God brings Moses to this point: the Lord God is Almighty, and puts the proof upon the issue, for if he speaks, it is done. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 24. Moses went out and told the people the Lord's words, and gathered the seventy elders and set them round about the tabernacle. The tabernacle was chosen for the convocation because there God manifested himself, there his Spirit would be imparted, and the elders' office invested with reverence in the people's eyes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. The Lord came down in a cloud, spoke to Moses, took of the spirit on him and gave it to the seventy elders, and when it rested on them they prophesied and did not cease. JFB explains their prophesying as the performance of civil and sacred duties by extraordinary endowment, continued either uninterruptedly that first day or as permanent gifts qualifying them as magistrates. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. Two men remained in the camp, Eldad and Medad, who were written among the elders but did not go out to the tabernacle, and the spirit rested on them and they prophesied in the camp. JFB suggests modesty or ceremonial defilement kept them back, yet they received the Spirit as fully as their brethren, and Henry adds the Spirit is not confined to the tabernacle but blows where he wills. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. A young man ran and told Moses that Eldad and Medad prophesied in the camp. The report reached Moses at once, because prophesying outside the appointed place looked like a breach of order, and the camp, not the tabernacle, was where it was happening. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. Joshua, Moses' servant, answered, my lord Moses, forbid them. Henry notes Joshua did not desire punishment, only restraint for the future, out of zeal for what he thought was the unity of the church, lest they occasion schism or rival Moses. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 29. Moses answered, enviest thou for my sake? Would God that all the Lord's people were prophets, and that the Lord would put his Spirit upon them! Henry draws the church lesson: shall we reject those whom Christ has owned, or restrain any from doing good because they are not in everything of our mind? Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 30. Moses got him into the camp, he and the elders of Israel. The convocation ended with the elders returning to the camp, now endowed and authorised to share the burden Moses had carried alone. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 31. A wind from the Lord brought quails from the sea and let them fall by the camp, about a day's journey on either side, two cubits high on the face of the earth. JFB explains the migratory birds were on their journey from Egypt when an east wind forced them over the Red Sea to Israel, in countless numbers covering a vast space, flying low enough to be easily caught. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 32. The people stood up all that day, all that night, and all the next day, gathering quails, he that gathered least gathering ten homers, and they spread them abroad round the camp. JFB notes they rose in eager haste, gathering not for immediate use only but for the future, distrustful of God's goodness, and salted and dried the quails as they had been accustomed in Egypt. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 33. While the flesh was yet between their teeth, before it was chewed, the Lord's wrath was kindled and he smote them with a very great plague. JFB notes their stomachs, long inured to light manna, were unprepared for sudden heavy animal food taken intemperately, and their sin, proceeding not from necessity but wanton lust, was made to carry its own punishment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 34. He called the place Kibroth-hattaavah, because there they buried the people that lusted. The name means the graves of lust, and JFB notes it proves the mortality was confined to those who had indulged inordinately. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 35. The people journeyed from Kibroth-hattaavah to Hazeroth and abode there. JFB identifies Hazeroth as the extreme southern station of this route, a watering-place in a spacious plain, now Ain-Haderah, where the host rested after the plague. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

The report reached Moses at once, because prophesying outside the appointed place looked like a breach of order, and the camp, not the tabernacle, was where it was happening. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. Moses got him into the camp, he and the elders of Israel. The convocation ended with the elders returning to the camp, now endowed and authorised to share the burden Moses had carried alone. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 31. A wind from the Lord brought quails from the sea and let them fall by the camp, about a day's journey on either side, two cubits high on the face of the earth. JFB explains the migratory birds were on their journey from Egypt when an east wind forced them over the Red Sea to Israel, in countless numbers covering a vast space, flying low enough to be easily caught. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 32. The people stood up all that day, all that night, and all the next day, gathering quails, he that gathered least gathering ten homers, and they spread them abroad round the camp. JFB notes they rose in eager haste, gathering not for immediate use only but for the future, distrustful of God's goodness, and salted and dried the quails as they had been accustomed in Egypt. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 33. While the flesh was yet between their teeth, before it was chewed, the Lord's wrath was kindled and he smote them with a very great plague. JFB notes their stomachs, long inured to light manna, were unprepared for sudden heavy animal food taken intemperately, and their sin, proceeding not from necessity but wanton lust, was made to carry its own punishment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 34. He called the place Kibroth-hattaavah, because there they buried the people that lusted. The name means the graves of lust, and JFB notes it proves the mortality was confined to those who had indulged inordinately. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 35. The people journeyed from Kibroth-hattaavah to Hazeroth and abode there. JFB identifies Hazeroth as the extreme southern station of this route, a watering-place in a spacious plain, now Ain-Haderah, where the host rested after the plague.
