The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Jeremiah 40 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 The word that came to Jeremiah from the LORD, after that Nebuzaradan the captain of the guard had let him go from Ramah, when he had taken him being bound in chains among all that were carried away captive of Jerusalem and Judah, which were carried away captive unto Babylon.

2 And the captain of the guard took Jeremiah, and said unto him, The LORD thy God hath pronounced this evil upon this place.

3 Now the LORD hath brought it, and done according as he hath said: because ye have sinned against the LORD, and have not obeyed his voice, therefore this thing is come upon you.

4 And now, behold, I loose thee this day from the chains which were upon thine hand. If it seem good unto thee to come with me into Babylon, come; and I will look well unto thee: but if it seem ill unto thee to come with me into Babylon, forbear: behold, all the land is before thee: whither it seemeth good and convenient for thee to go, thither go.

5 Now while he was not yet gone back, he said, Go back also to Gedaliah the son of Ahikam the son of Shaphan, whom the king of Babylon hath made governor over the cities of Judah, and dwell with him among the people: or go wheresoever it seemeth convenient unto thee to go. So the captain of the guard gave him victuals and a reward, and let him go.

6 Then went Jeremiah unto Gedaliah the son of Ahikam to Mizpah; and dwelt with him among the people that were left in the land.

7 Now when all the captains of the forces which were in the fields, even they and their men, heard that the king of Babylon had made Gedaliah the son of Ahikam governor in the land, and had committed unto him men, and women, and children, and of the poor of the land, of them that were not carried away captive to Babylon;

8 Then they came to Gedaliah to Mizpah, even Ishmael the son of Nethaniah, and Johanan and Jonathan the sons of Kareah, and Seraiah the son of Tanhumeth, and the sons of Ephai the Netophathite, and Jezaniah the son of a Maachathite, they and their men.

9 And Gedaliah the son of Ahikam the son of Shaphan sware unto them and to their men, saying, Fear not to serve the Chaldeans: dwell in the land, and serve the king of Babylon, and it shall be well with you.

10 As for me, behold, I will dwell at Mizpah, to serve the Chaldeans, which will come unto us: but ye, gather ye wine, and summer fruits, and oil, and put them in your vessels, and dwell in your cities that ye have taken.

11 Likewise when all the Jews that were in Moab, and among the Ammonites, and in Edom, and that were in all the countries, heard that the king of Babylon had left a remnant of Judah, and that he had set over them Gedaliah the son of Ahikam the son of Shaphan;

12 Even all the Jews returned out of all places whither they were driven, and came to the land of Judah, to Gedaliah, unto Mizpah, and gathered wine and summer fruits very much.

13 Moreover Johanan the son of Kareah, and all the captains of the forces that were in the fields, came to Gedaliah to Mizpah,

14 And said unto him, Dost thou certainly know that Baalis the king of the Ammonites hath sent Ishmael the son of Nethaniah to slay thee? But Gedaliah the son of Ahikam believed them not.

15 Then Johanan the son of Kareah spake to Gedaliah in Mizpah secretly saying, Let me go, I pray thee, and I will slay Ishmael the son of Nethaniah, and no man shall know it: wherefore should he slay thee, that all the Jews which are gathered unto thee should be scattered, and the remnant in Judah perish?

16 But Gedaliah the son of Ahikam said unto Johanan the son of Kareah, Thou shalt not do this thing: for thou speakest falsely of Ishmael.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

After being loosed from his chains at Ramah, Jeremiah chooses to stay with Gedaliah, whom the king of Babylon has made governor at Mizpah, rather than go to Babylon. The scattered captains and the remnant gather to Gedaliah, and Johanan warns him of Ishmael's plot to kill him — a warning Gedaliah refuses to believe. What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Henry observes the heading seems misapplied, since this chapter holds no prophecy: the story only introduces the message of chapter 42 and shows its occasion, and Jeremiah, being himself concerned in the story, was the better able to give an account of it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 2. Henry notes the captain solemnly owns Jeremiah a true prophet, glorying that he had been God's instrument to fulfil what Jeremiah had been God's messenger to foretell. The heathen prince plainly sees what Israel's princes would never acknowledge, though evident as if written with a sunbeam: their sin is the cause of their miseries. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 3. Henry stresses that the people had often been told from the pulpit by their prophets that sin brought this ruin, and would not regard it; now they are told it from the bench by the conqueror, whom they dare not contradict. Sooner or later, he says, men shall be made sensible that their sin is the cause of all their miseries. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 4. Henry notes Nebuzaradan loosed Jeremiah from his chains a second time and invited him to Babylon not as a captive but as a friend and companion, promising to set his eye upon him, that is, to show him respect and see him safe and well provided for. A great change for a man lately tossed from prison to prison, now free to walk at liberty. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 5. Henry notes that while Jeremiah stood considering what to do, neither inclined to Babylon nor determined elsewhere, Nebuzaradan turned the scale by advising him to go to Gedaliah, governor under Babylon. The counsel did not bind him, Henry adds: it is friendly to give advice, but unfriendly to prescribe and be angry if advice is not taken. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 6. Henry says Jeremiah not only accepted the captain's kindness but took his advice and dwelt with Gedaliah at Mizpah among the people left in the land. Whether his prudence can be commended is doubtful, since the event did not prove to his comfort, but his pious affection to Israel may be commended: he would rather dwell with the poor in the holy land than with princes in an unholy one. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 7. Henry notes a bright sky opening upon the remnant: Gedaliah, one of themselves rather than a prince of Babylon, was made governor, so the people might be sure he would seek their peace. He was the son of Ahikam, who took Jeremiah's part in chapter 26, a man of great wisdom and mild temper under whom the few left might have been very happy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 8. Henry notes the great resort to Gedaliah from all parts: the captains who had escaped the Chaldeans by force came and quietly submitted to him for their own safety and common preservation, coming with their men and so strengthening one another. Babylon, Henry adds, was not jealous of their growing numbers but rather pleased with them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 9. Henry describes Gedaliah's oath as the model of the new government settled by an original contract: the people must own that the property of their lands was in the Chaldeans, yet fear not the sin, the reproach, or the consequences of serving Babylon, since it was what God had brought them to. If they lived peaceably, Henry says, peaceably they should live. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 10. Henry notes Gedaliah undertook to dwell at Mizpah to serve the Chaldeans in the people's name, receiving orders and paying tribute when the Chaldeans came, so that all dealings between them and Babylon would pass through his hand. The people, meanwhile, were to gather wine, summer fruits, and oil for their own use, like those who live in a land of peace and hope to eat the labour of their hands. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 11. Henry notes the dispersed Jews in Moab, Ammon, and Edom were induced by love of their own land to return as soon as they heard Gedaliah was in authority there. Canaan itself would be an unsafe, unpleasant country with no government, he says, and it was a great reviving for the dispersed to come together again under a governor of their own nation: in wrath God remembered mercy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 12. Henry notes the returnees gathered wine and summer fruits very much, the spontaneous products of a fertile soil, their corn harvest having passed before Jerusalem was taken. While Gedaliah cared for public safety he left them to enjoy the public plenty and demanded no tribute, seeking not his own profit but the profit of many. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 13. Henry marks this as a dark cloud gathering over the infant state, threatening a dreadful storm: how soon the hopeful prospect is blasted, for when God begins in judgment he will make an end. The captains' coming to Mizpah carries the warning that pride, ambition, or revenge still bearing rule will form new projects ending in ruin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 14. Henry notes Baalis king of the Ammonites had a particular spite at Gedaliah and contrived to take him off, employing Ishmael as the instrument of his malice. Johanan, having got scent of the plot, takes it for granted Gedaliah must already know it, the proofs being so plain, yet Gedaliah believed them not, being a man of sincerity himself. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 15. Henry notes Johanan gave Gedaliah private intelligence and proffered his service to prevent the murder by secretly slaying Ishmael, arguing that otherwise the gathered Jews would scatter and the remnant perish. In this, Henry says, Johanan showed more courage and zeal than sense of justice: if it were lawful to kill for prevention, who then could be safe, since malice always suspects the worst? Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 16. Henry notes Gedaliah would by no means give credit to the information of Ishmael's treachery, answering that Johanan spoke falsely of Ishmael. Herein he discovered more good humour than discretion, Henry says, more of the innocence of the dove than the wisdom of the serpent, and many have been ruined by being over-confident of the fidelity of those about them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
