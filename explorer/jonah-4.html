The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Jonah 4 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 But it displeased Jonah exceedingly, and he was very angry.

2 And he prayed unto the LORD, and said, I pray thee, O LORD, was not this my saying, when I was yet in my country? Therefore I fled before unto Tarshish: for I knew that thou art a gracious God, and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and repentest thee of the evil.

3 Therefore now, O LORD, take, I beseech thee, my life from me; for it is better for me to die than to live.

4 Then said the LORD, Doest thou well to be angry?

5 So Jonah went out of the city, and sat on the east side of the city, and there made him a booth, and sat under it in the shadow, till he might see what would become of the city.

6 And the LORD God prepared a gourd, and made it to come up over Jonah, that it might be a shadow over his head, to deliver him from his grief. So Jonah was exceeding glad of the gourd.

7 But God prepared a worm when the morning rose the next day, and it smote the gourd that it withered.

8 And it came to pass, when the sun did arise, that God prepared a vehement east wind; and the sun beat upon the head of Jonah, that he fainted, and wished in himself to die, and said, It is better for me to die than to live.

9 And God said to Jonah, Doest thou well to be angry for the gourd? And he said, I do well to be angry, even unto death.

10 Then said the LORD, Thou hast had pity on the gourd, for the which thou hast not laboured, neither madest it grow; which came up in a night, and perished in a night:

11 And should not I spare Nineveh, that great city, wherein are more than sixscore thousand persons that cannot discern between their right hand and their left hand; and also much cattle?

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

It displeases Jonah exceedingly that Nineveh is spared, and he is very angry, confessing that this is why he fled to Tarshish: he knew God is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and repents of evil. He sits east of the city under a booth to see what will become of it, God prepares a gourd for shade, then a worm that withers it, then a vehement east wind, and Jonah wishes to die. The book ends on God's question, unanswered: Jonah pitied a gourd he did not plant, and should not God spare Nineveh, with more than sixscore thousand persons who cannot discern between their right hand and their left, and also much cattle? Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. It displeased Jonah exceedingly, and he was very angry, that Nineveh had repented and found mercy. Henry notes he had no rule over his own spirit, too little reverence for God to accept what pleased God, and too little affection for men to rejoice at their conversion, grudging mercy through pride and concern for his own honour. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 2. Jonah prayed, yet awkwardly: he justified his flight, saying he had foreseen that Nineveh would repent and God would forgive them. Henry notes the strange perversity of dreading the success of his own ministry, and of making God's gracious, merciful character, his greatest glory, a matter of reproach. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 3. In passion Jonah begged God to take his life, saying it was better to die than to live. Henry calls this folly and strong corruption: Jonah was in the midst of usefulness, yet wished to die while out of temper and unfit to die, though life had just been restored to him as a mercy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 4. The Lord answered mildly, Doest thou well to be angry? Henry notes God might justly have rejected Jonah or taken him at his word, but instead reasoned with him, as the father reasoned with the elder brother, teaching us to restore the fallen with meekness and to ask ourselves whether anger at God's mercy can ever be justified. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 5. Jonah went out of the city, sat on its east side, made a booth and sat under its shadow, waiting to see what would become of the city. Henry notes he still hoped some judgment might save his credit, and that fretful spirits industriously create inconveniences so they may still have something to complain of. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 6. The Lord God prepared a gourd that grew up over Jonah to be a shadow over his head and deliver him from his grief, and Jonah was exceedingly glad of it. Henry notes God's tenderness even to a foolish and froward servant: as he had prepared a fish against the water, so he prepared a plant against the air, for a small thing coming seasonably may be a valuable blessing. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 7. God prepared a worm that smote the gourd, so that it withered. Henry notes the affliction came by divine appointment in the very thing that was his comfort: creature-comforts are withering things, and a small unseen worm at the root can destroy a large gourd. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 8. A vehement east wind drove the sun's heat upon Jonah's head, so that he fainted and wished to die. Henry notes the passion that ran to transport when the gourd flourished now ran to grief when it withered: inordinate affection lays the foundation for inordinate affliction. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 9. God asked, Doest thou well to be angry for the gourd, and Jonah answered, I do well to be angry, even unto death. Henry notes that speaking amiss in haste may be recalled, but standing to it after God's rebuke is worse: passion overruled conscience and made Jonah fly in the face of God's authority. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 10. God argued from Jonah's own pity: Jonah had pity on the gourd, for which he did not labour and which came up in a night. Henry notes the contrast: the gourd was one, not his own, of sudden growth and short life, while Nineveh's people were the work of God's own hands, grown over many years. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 11. Should not God spare Nineveh, that great city, with more than a hundred and twenty thousand who cannot discern between their right hand and their left, and also much cattle? Henry notes God's tender regard to little children and to immortal souls, and that one soul is worth more than many gourds, so God's mercy is justified against every murmurer at it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry

Unfamiliar terms

  • Tarshish. the distant port Jonah sails for from Joppa, in the opposite direction from Nineveh, fleeing from the presence of the LORD. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Nineveh. the great Assyrian city, three days' journey across, to which Jonah is sent and whose repentance spares it in Jonah 3, and whose fall Nahum announces. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Gourd. the plant God prepares to shade Jonah east of Nineveh, which a worm withers the next morning, setting up the book's closing question about pity. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry
