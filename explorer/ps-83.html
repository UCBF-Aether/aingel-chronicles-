The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Psalms 83 (King James Version (1611) for Psalms 1–150; Charles Thomson, The Septuagint (1808) for Psalm 151)

1 Keep not thou silence, O God: hold not thy peace, and be not still, O God.

2 For, lo, thine enemies make a tumult: and they that hate thee have lifted up the head.

3 They have taken crafty counsel against thy people, and consulted against thy hidden ones.

4 They have said, Come, and let us cut them off from being a nation; that the name of Israel may be no more in remembrance.

5 For they have consulted together with one consent: they are confederate against thee:

6 The tabernacles of Edom, and the Ishmaelites; of Moab, and the Hagarenes;

7 Gebal, and Ammon, and Amalek; the Philistines with the inhabitants of Tyre;

8 Assur also is joined with them: they have holpen the children of Lot. Selah.

9 Do unto them as unto the Midianites; as to Sisera, as to Jabin, at the brook of Kison:

10 Which perished at Endor: they became as dung for the earth.

11 Make their nobles like Oreb, and like Zeeb: yea, all their princes as Zebah, and as Zalmunna:

12 Who said, Let us take to ourselves the houses of God in possession.

13 O my God, make them like a wheel; as the stubble before the wind.

14 As the fire burneth a wood, and as the flame setteth the mountains on fire;

15 So persecute them with thy tempest, and make them afraid with thy storm.

16 Fill their faces with shame; that they may seek thy name, O LORD.

17 Let them be confounded and troubled for ever; yea, let them be put to shame, and perish:

18 That men may know that thou, whose name alone is JEHOVAH, art the most high over all the earth.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

A plea that God not keep silence while a confederacy of surrounding peoples — Edom, Moab, Ammon, Amalek, Philistia, Tyre and Assur among them — plots to cut Israel off from being a nation and seize the houses of God. The psalm asks God to deal with them as with Midian, Sisera and Jabin at Kishon and with Oreb, Zeeb, Zebah and Zalmunna, scattering them like stubble before the wind, so that they seek God's name and know that the LORD alone is most high over all the earth. Interpreted Psalm text above; study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Silence, peace, and stillness are three ways of saying God seems to connive: Henry reads the prayer as asking God to speak by prophets for encouragement, by providence against enemies, and by deliverance for his people, since with God saying and doing are one. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 2. Tumult and lifted head name heat, pride, and noise: enemies talk big, hoping to run God's people down with clamour, which is itself the reason to ask God to speak in wrath. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 3. Crafty counsel against hidden ones invokes the serpent's subtlety: God's people are hidden in secrecy, life hid with Christ and unknown to the world, and in safety under special protection, yet enemies consult to ruin whom God resolves to preserve. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 4. Cut off from being a nation, name no more remembered, is extirpation aimed at memory itself: Henry sees the wish that church and religion have no being in the world, Bibles and records destroyed with the people. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 5. One consent among clashing interests is Satan's kingdom undivided: Henry turns the confederacy into a rebuke, that if Herod and Pilate can be made friends to crucify, church friends should be unanimous to serve. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 6. Names listed are a roll of broken bonds: Edomites and Ishmaelites, descendants of Abraham, lead, and apostates from the church, Henry notes, have been its bitterest enemies. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 7. Gebal, and Ammon, and Amalek, the Philistines with the inhabitants of Tyre, names the nations drawn into the confederacy: the Edomites and Ishmaelites, both descendants from Abraham, lead the van — for apostates from the church have been its most bitter and spiteful enemies, witness Julian. These were allied to Israel in blood and yet in alliance against Israel. There are no bonds of nature so strong but the spirit of persecution has broken through them: the brother shall betray the brother to death. Moab and Ammon were the children of righteous Lot but, as an incestuous, so a degenerate race. The Philistines were long a thorn in Israel's side, and very vexatious. How the inhabitants of Tyre, who in David's time were Israel's firm allies, come in among their enemies, I know not — but that Assur, that is, the Assyrian, also is joined with them is not strange, or that, as the word is, they were an arm to the children of Lot. See how numerous the enemies of God's church have always been: Lord, how are those increased that trouble it! God's heritage was as a speckled bird, all the birds round about were against her — which highly magnifies the power of God in preserving to himself a church in the world, in spite of the combined force of earth and hell. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 8. Assur joined as an arm to Lot's children shows how numerous the church's enemies have always been: a speckled bird with all birds round about against her magnifies the power preserving a church in the world. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 9. Midian, Sisera, and Jabin at Kishon are precedents to plead: former defeats may be prayed back, because God is the same still to his people and against their enemies, with no variableness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 10. Which perished at Endor, they became as dung for the earth, is the precedent prayed from: he prays that their armies might be destroyed as the armies of former enemies had been. Do to them as to the Midianites — let them be routed by their own fears, for so the Midianites were, more than by Gideon's 300 men. Do to them as to the army under Sisera, general under Jabin king of Canaan, which God discomfited at the brook Kishon, near to which was Endor. They became as dung on the earth: their dead bodies were thrown like dung laid in heaps, or spread, to fatten the ground; they were trodden to dirt by Barak's small but victorious army. And this was fitly made a precedent here, because Deborah made it so to aftertimes when it was fresh: So let all thy enemies perish, O Lord! — that is, So they shall perish. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 11. Nobles like Oreb and Zeeb, princes like Zebah and Zalmunna, target leaders because princes set the people on: their malice was covetous, taking God's pleasant places for themselves, and their lot is to be made easy prey as those princes were. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 12. Who said, Let us take to ourselves the houses of God in possession, is the princes' malice: they said, Let us take to ourselves the houses of God in possession — the pleasant places of God, so the word is — by which we may understand the land of Canaan, which was a pleasant land and was Immanuel's land, or the temple, which was indeed God's pleasant place, or, as Dr. Hammond suggests, the pleasant pastures, which these Arabians, who traded in cattle, did in a particular manner seek after. The princes and nobles aimed to enrich themselves by this war, and their armies must be made as dung for the earth, to serve their covetousness and their ambition. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 13. O my God, make them like a wheel, as the stubble before the wind, is the similitude prayed: that God would make them like a wheel, that they might be in continual motion, unquiet, unsettled, and giddy in all their counsels and resolves, that they might roll down easily and speedily to their own ruin. Or, as some think, that they might be broken by the judgments of God, as the corn is broken, or beaten out, by the wheel which was then used in threshing. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 14. Fire in wood and flame on mountains are similitudes of a tempest pursued: Henry's prayer is that enemies be driven as stubble before wind, unquiet and unsettled in counsel until ruin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 15. The tempest is not mere scattering, like stubble before wind, but pursuit to utter ruin: God's storm raises terrors in sinners' own hearts, and so makes them completely miserable. Henry's point is that God can deal with the proudest, most daring defier of his justice and make him afraid as a grasshopper — it is the torment of devils that they tremble. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 16. Fill their faces with shame, that they may seek thy name, O Lord — judgment aimed at repentance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 17. Confounded, troubled forever, shamed, and perishing close the imprecation as doom, not mere anger: Henry reads the prayer as prophecy reaching all enemies of the gospel church, whoever opposes Christ's kingdom. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 18. If shame does not convert them, its perfecting will still redound to God's glory, so that other men may know and own what the enemies themselves will not. Henry spells out the three truths proved by God's triumphs: JEHOVAH is self-existent and self-sufficient, he is Most High above all gods and kings, and his kingdom rules over all the earth, even nations that do not know him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
