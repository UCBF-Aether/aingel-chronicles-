The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 4 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Then Eliphaz the Temanite answered and said,

2 If we assay to commune with thee, wilt thou be grieved? but who can withhold himself from speaking?

3 Behold, thou hast instructed many, and thou hast strengthened the weak hands.

4 Thy words have upholden him that was falling, and thou hast strengthened the feeble knees.

5 But now it is come upon thee, and thou faintest; it toucheth thee, and thou art troubled.

6 Is not this thy fear, thy confidence, thy hope, and the uprightness of thy ways?

7 Remember, I pray thee, who ever perished, being innocent? or where were the righteous cut off?

8 Even as I have seen, they that plow iniquity, and sow wickedness, reap the same.

9 By the blast of God they perish, and by the breath of his nostrils are they consumed.

10 The roaring of the lion, and the voice of the fierce lion, and the teeth of the young lions, are broken.

11 The old lion perisheth for lack of prey, and the stout lion’s whelps are scattered abroad.

12 Now a thing was secretly brought to me, and mine ear received a little thereof.

13 In thoughts from the visions of the night, when deep sleep falleth on men,

14 Fear came upon me, and trembling, which made all my bones to shake.

15 Then a spirit passed before my face; the hair of my flesh stood up:

16 It stood still, but I could not discern the form thereof: an image was before mine eyes, there was silence, and I heard a voice, saying,

17 Shall mortal man be more just than God? shall a man be more pure than his maker?

18 Behold, he put no trust in his servants; and his angels he charged with folly:

19 How much less in them that dwell in houses of clay, whose foundation is in the dust, which are crushed before the moth?

20 They are destroyed from morning to evening: they perish for ever without any regarding it.

21 Doth not their excellency which is in them go away? they die, even without wisdom.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Eliphaz answers first, and most mildly: he reminds Job that he once strengthened weak hands, then asks who ever perished being innocent, arguing that those who plow iniquity reap the same. To back his claim he reports a night vision in which a voice asked whether mortal man can be more just than God, since God charges even his angels with folly — how much less men dwelling in houses of clay, crushed before the moth. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Eliphaz the Temanite answers first. JFB calls him the mildest of Job's three accusers, driven to doubt Job's integrity by the greatness of his calamities, his complaints against God, and the belief that calamities prove guilt. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Eliphaz asks leave to attempt a word, and wonders whether Job will be grieved by it. JFB notes that pious friends often count only a touch what the sufferer feels as a wound. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Eliphaz reminds Job that he instructed many and strengthened weak hands. The past ministry is now turned into an argument: the counsellor should bear his own counsel. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Job's words upheld him who was falling, and he strengthened feeble knees. Eliphaz credits Job's former comfort of others before pressing the contrast with his present collapse. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Now trouble has come on Job himself and he faints; it touches him and he is troubled. JFB renders troubled as unhinged, having lost self-command. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Eliphaz asks whether Job's fear of God is not his confidence, and the uprightness of his ways his hope. JFB, by transposition, reads it as an appeal: if fear is hope and uprightness is confidence, then who ever perished being innocent? Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Remember, Eliphaz urges: who ever perished being innocent, or where were the righteous cut off? The question assumes suffering always traces to guilt, though JFB cautions that outward appearance alone cannot judge a life. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Those who plow iniquity and sow wickedness reap the same. JFB cites the harvest proverb running through Scripture: the sowing determines the reaping. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. By the blast of God they perish, and by the breath of his nostrils they are consumed. JFB explains the breath of his nostrils as God's anger, figured by the fiery winds of the East. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. The roaring of the lion, the voice of the fierce lion, and the teeth of young lions are broken. JFB reads the lions as wicked men: calamity falls on them despite all their resources, just as destruction falls on the lion despite its strength. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. The old lion perishes for lack of prey, and the stout lion's whelps are scattered abroad. JFB notes the five Hebrew terms for lion express phases of wickedness, and the race becomes extinct when its strength fails. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. A thing was secretly brought to Eliphaz, and his ear received a little of it. JFB explains that Eliphaz confirms his view by a divine declaration secretly imparted, a whisper conveying more than articulate words could utter. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. In thoughts from the visions of the night, when deep sleep falls on men, fear came upon Eliphaz. JFB reads this as manifold thoughts before the night visions began, so the experience is not presented as a mere delusive dream. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Fear and trembling seized Eliphaz, making all his bones shake. The terror of the vision is felt in the body before any word is spoken. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. A spirit passed before his face, and the hair of his flesh stood up. The unseen presence is sensed before it is heard. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. The apparition stood still, yet its form was indistinct, and then came a gentle voice. JFB explains that it first glides, then stands still in shadowy indistinctness, and the voice is a gentle murmur, not silence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. The voice asks whether mortal man shall be more just than God, or a man more pure than his Maker. JFB notes two Hebrew words for man — one for feebleness, one for strength — and neither, feeble or strong, is righteous before God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. God puts no trust in his servants, and charges his angels with folly. JFB explains folly as imperfection attributed even to angels in comparison with God, whose creaturely holiness at best wants moral consideration. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. How much less, then, those who dwell in houses of clay, whose foundation is dust, crushed before the moth? JFB notes Eastern houses of sun-dried clay bricks wash away easily, and man, who cannot stand before the moth physically, cannot stand before God morally. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. They are destroyed from morning to evening, perishing forever without any regarding it. JFB reads the day as one short day, and the destruction as what would follow if God withdrew his loving protection. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. Does not their excellency go away, so that they die even without wisdom? JFB notes the images of a bow unstrung and a tabernacle's cords taken down, and concludes that man's continued existence proves God's inconceivable wisdom and holiness, which alone save him from ruin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
