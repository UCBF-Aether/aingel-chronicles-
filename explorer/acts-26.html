The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Acts 26 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Then Agrippa said unto Paul, Thou art permitted to speak for thyself. Then Paul stretched forth the hand, and answered for himself:

2 I think myself happy, king Agrippa, because I shall answer for myself this day before thee touching all the things whereof I am accused of the Jews:

3 Especially because I know thee to be expert in all customs and questions which are among the Jews: wherefore I beseech thee to hear me patiently.

4 My manner of life from my youth, which was at the first among mine own nation at Jerusalem, know all the Jews;

5 Which knew me from the beginning, if they would testify, that after the most straitest sect of our religion I lived a Pharisee.

6 And now I stand and am judged for the hope of the promise made of God, unto our fathers:

7 Unto which promise our twelve tribes, instantly serving God day and night, hope to come. For which hope’s sake, king Agrippa, I am accused of the Jews.

8 Why should it be thought a thing incredible with you, that God should raise the dead?

9 I verily thought with myself, that I ought to do many things contrary to the name of Jesus of Nazareth.

10 Which thing I also did in Jerusalem: and many of the saints did I shut up in prison, having received authority from the chief priests; and when they were put to death, I gave my voice against them.

11 And I punished them oft in every synagogue, and compelled them to blaspheme; and being exceedingly mad against them, I persecuted them even unto strange cities.

12 Whereupon as I went to Damascus with authority and commission from the chief priests,

13 At midday, O king, I saw in the way a light from heaven, above the brightness of the sun, shining round about me and them which journeyed with me.

14 And when we were all fallen to the earth, I heard a voice speaking unto me, and saying in the Hebrew tongue, Saul, Saul, why persecutest thou me? it is hard for thee to kick against the pricks.

15 And I said, Who art thou, Lord? And he said, I am Jesus whom thou persecutest.

16 But rise, and stand upon thy feet: for I have appeared unto thee for this purpose, to make thee a minister and a witness both of these things which thou hast seen, and of those things in the which I will appear unto thee;

17 Delivering thee from the people, and from the Gentiles, unto whom now I send thee,

18 To open their eyes, and to turn them from darkness to light, and from the power of Satan unto God, that they may receive forgiveness of sins, and inheritance among them which are sanctified by faith that is in me.

19 Whereupon, O king Agrippa, I was not disobedient unto the heavenly vision:

20 But shewed first unto them of Damascus, and at Jerusalem, and throughout all the coasts of Judaea, and then to the Gentiles, that they should repent and turn to God, and do works meet for repentance.

21 For these causes the Jews caught me in the temple, and went about to kill me.

22 Having therefore obtained help of God, I continue unto this day, witnessing both to small and great, saying none other things than those which the prophets and Moses did say should come:

23 That Christ should suffer, and that he should be the first that should rise from the dead, and should shew light unto the people, and to the Gentiles.

24 And as he thus spake for himself, Festus said with a loud voice, Paul, thou art beside thyself; much learning doth make thee mad.

25 But he said, I am not mad, most noble Festus; but speak forth the words of truth and soberness.

26 For the king knoweth of these things, before whom also I speak freely: for I am persuaded that none of these things are hidden from him; for this thing was not done in a corner.

27 King Agrippa, believest thou the prophets? I know that thou believest.

28 Then Agrippa said unto Paul, Almost thou persuadest me to be a Christian.

29 And Paul said, I would to God, that not only thou, but also all that hear me this day, were both almost, and altogether such as I am, except these bonds.

30 And when he had thus spoken, the king rose up, and the governor, and Bernice, and they that sat with them:

31 And when they were gone aside, they talked between themselves, saying, This man doeth nothing worthy of death or of bonds.

32 Then said Agrippa unto Festus, This man might have been set at liberty, if he had not appealed unto Caesar.

  • Interpreted — a reasoned explanation or proposed relationship.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Before Agrippa, Paul retells his life as a Pharisee, his persecution of the church, his vision of the risen Jesus on the Damascus road, and his commission to open Gentile eyes, insisting he has said nothing beyond what Moses and the prophets said should come. Festus cries that much learning has made him mad, Agrippa says he is almost persuaded to be a Christian, and both agree Paul might have been freed had he not appealed to Caesar. What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Then Agrippa said unto Paul, Thou art permitted to speak for thyself. Then Paul stretched forth the hand, and answered for himself. Henry says Christianity teaches us to give a reason of the hope that is in us, and also to give honour to whom honour is due, without flattery or fear of man, and Paul's stretched-forth hand answering for himself is that reason-giving. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 2. I think myself happy, king Agrippa, because I shall answer for myself this day before thee touching all the things whereof I am accused of the Jews. Henry says surely ministers may expect, when they preach the faith of Christ, to be heard patiently, and Paul counts himself happy to answer before Agrippa, who is expert in Jewish customs and questions. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 3. Especially because I know thee to be expert in all customs and questions which are among the Jews: wherefore I beseech thee to hear me patiently. Henry says Agrippa was well versed in the Scriptures of the Old Testament, therefore could the better judge as to the controversy about Jesus being the Messiah, and Paul beseeches him to hear patiently on that ground. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 4. My manner of life from my youth, which was at the first among mine own nation at Jerusalem, know all the Jews. Henry says Paul professes that he still kept to all the good in which he was first educated and trained up, and see here what his religion was: he was a moralist, a man of virtue, and had not learned the arts of the crafty, covetous Pharisees, and he was not chargeable with any open vice and profaneness, and he was sound in the faith, with a holy regard for the ancient promise made of God unto the fathers, building his hope upon it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 5. Which knew me from the beginning, if they would testify, that after the most straitest sect of our religion I lived a Pharisee. Henry's straitest sect is Paul's Pharisee life, lived from the beginning and known to the Jews, yet Henry distinguishes his virtue from the arts of the crafty, covetous Pharisees, for Paul was a moralist, a man of virtue, not chargeable with open vice and profaneness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 6. And now I stand and am judged for the hope of the promise made of God unto our fathers. Henry says he always had a holy regard for the ancient promise made of God unto the fathers, and built his hope upon it, and now he stands and is judged for the hope of that very promise. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 7. Unto which promise our twelve tribes, instantly serving God day and night, hope to come. For which hope's sake, king Agrippa, I am accused of the Jews. Henry says the temple service, or continual course of religious duties, day and night, was kept up as the profession of faith in the promise of eternal life, and in expectation of it, and the prospect of eternal life should engage us to be diligent and stedfast in all religious exercises, and the twelve tribes instantly serving God day and night hope to come to that promise. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 8. Why should it be thought a thing incredible with you, that God should raise the dead? Henry says many things are thought to be beyond belief, only because the infinite nature and perfections of him that has revealed, performed, or promised them, are overlooked, and the resurrection is thought incredible only by overlooking the infinite God who raises the dead. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry

Henry says Paul acknowledged that while he continued a Pharisee, he was a bitter enemy to Christianity, and this was his character and manner of life in the beginning of his time, with every thing to hinder his being a Christian. Henry says the cross of Christ was a stumbling-block to the Jews, and they were in a rage at Paul's preaching the fulfilling of the Old Testament predictions, yet having obtained help of God, he continues unto this day, witnessing both to small and great, saying none other things than those which the prophets and Moses did say should come. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 23. That Christ should suffer, and that he should be the first that should rise from the dead, and should shew light unto the people, and to the Gentiles. Henry says Christ should be the first that should rise from the dead, the Head or principal One, and also, it was foretold by the prophets, that the Gentiles should be brought to the knowledge of God by the Messiah, and what in this could the Jews justly be displeased at? Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 24. As he thus spake for himself, Festus said with a loud voice, Paul, thou art beside thyself; much learning doth make thee mad. Henry says it becomes us, on all occasions, to speak the words of truth and soberness, and then we need not be troubled at the unjust censures of men, for active and laborious followers of the gospel often have been despised as dreamers or madmen, for believing such doctrines and such wonderful facts, and for attesting that the same faith and diligence, and an experience like their own, are necessary to all men, whatever their rank, in order to their salvation, but apostles and prophets, and the Son of God himself, were exposed to this charge. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 25. But he said, I am not mad, most noble Festus; but speak forth the words of truth and soberness. Henry's truth and soberness is Paul's answer: I am not mad, most noble Festus, but speak forth the words of truth and soberness, and speaking thus, we need not be troubled at the unjust censures of men. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 26. For the king knoweth of these things, before whom also I speak freely: for I am persuaded that none of these things are hidden from him; for this thing was not done in a corner. Henry's freely spoken things are known to the king: Agrippa was well versed in the Scriptures, and Paul speaks freely before him, persuaded that none of these things are hidden from him, for this thing was not done in a corner. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 27. King Agrippa, believest thou the prophets? I know that thou believest. Henry's convinced understanding is the question: King Agrippa, believest thou the prophets? I know that thou believest, and Agrippa's understanding and judgment were for the time convinced, though his heart was not changed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 28. Then Agrippa said unto Paul, Almost thou persuadest me to be a Christian. Henry says Agrippa saw a great deal of reason for Christianity, and his understanding and judgment were for the time convinced, but his heart was not changed, and his conduct and temper were widely different from the humility and spirituality of the gospel, for many are almost persuaded to be religious, who are not quite persuaded, being under strong convictions of their duty, and of the excellence of the ways of God, yet do not pursue their convictions. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 29. Paul said, I would to God, that not only thou, but also all that hear me this day, were both almost, and altogether such as I am, except these bonds. Henry says Paul urged that it was the concern of every one to become a true Christian, and that there is grace enough in Christ for all, and he expressed his full conviction of the truth of the gospel, the absolute necessity of faith in Christ in order to salvation, and such salvation from such bondage the gospel of Christ offers to the Gentiles, to a lost world, yet it is with much difficulty that any person can be persuaded he needs a work of grace on his heart, like that which was needful for the conversion of the Gentiles, so let us beware of fatal hesitation in our own conduct, and recollect how far the being almost persuaded to be a Christian is from being altogether such a one as every true believer is. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 30. When he had thus spoken, the king rose up, and the governor, and Bernice, and they that sat with them. Henry's conference rises to the verdict: the king rose up, and the governor, and Bernice, and they that sat with them, to talk between themselves of what they had heard. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 31. When they were gone aside, they talked between themselves, saying, This man doeth nothing worthy of death or of bonds. Henry says it is no small mercy to have God clear up our righteousness as the light, and our just dealing as the noon-day, to have nothing certain laid to our charge, and God makes even the enemies of his people to do them right, and the aside talk, This man doeth nothing worthy of death or of bonds, is that clearing. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 32. Then said Agrippa unto Festus, This man might have been set at liberty, if he had not appealed unto Caesar. Henry's liberty that might have been is the appeal's cost: this man might have been set at liberty, if he had not appealed unto Caesar, yet the appeal stands, and the slow providence that kept Paul waiting on God now carries him toward Rome. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry

Henry says Christ should be the first that should rise from the dead, the Head or principal One, and also, it was foretold by the prophets, that the Gentiles should be brought to the knowledge of God by the Messiah, and what in this could the Jews justly be displeased at? Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 24. As he thus spake for himself, Festus said with a loud voice, Paul, thou art beside thyself; much learning doth make thee mad. Henry says it becomes us, on all occasions, to speak the words of truth and soberness, and then we need not be troubled at the unjust censures of men, for active and laborious followers of the gospel often have been despised as dreamers or madmen, for believing such doctrines and such wonderful facts, and for attesting that the same faith and diligence, and an experience like their own, are necessary to all men, whatever their rank, in order to their salvation, but apostles and prophets, and the Son of God himself, were exposed to this charge. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 25. But he said, I am not mad, most noble Festus; but speak forth the words of truth and soberness. Henry's truth and soberness is Paul's answer: I am not mad, most noble Festus, but speak forth the words of truth and soberness, and speaking thus, we need not be troubled at the unjust censures of men. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 26. For the king knoweth of these things, before whom also I speak freely: for I am persuaded that none of these things are hidden from him; for this thing was not done in a corner. Henry's freely spoken things are known to the king: Agrippa was well versed in the Scriptures, and Paul speaks freely before him, persuaded that none of these things are hidden from him, for this thing was not done in a corner. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 27. King Agrippa, believest thou the prophets? I know that thou believest. Henry's convinced understanding is the question: King Agrippa, believest thou the prophets? I know that thou believest, and Agrippa's understanding and judgment were for the time convinced, though his heart was not changed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 28. Then Agrippa said unto Paul, Almost thou persuadest me to be a Christian. Henry says Agrippa saw a great deal of reason for Christianity, and his understanding and judgment were for the time convinced, but his heart was not changed, and his conduct and temper were widely different from the humility and spirituality of the gospel, for many are almost persuaded to be religious, who are not quite persuaded, being under strong convictions of their duty, and of the excellence of the ways of God, yet do not pursue their convictions. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 29. Paul said, I would to God, that not only thou, but also all that hear me this day, were both almost, and altogether such as I am, except these bonds. Henry says Paul urged that it was the concern of every one to become a true Christian, and that there is grace enough in Christ for all, and he expressed his full conviction of the truth of the gospel, the absolute necessity of faith in Christ in order to salvation, and such salvation from such bondage the gospel of Christ offers to the Gentiles, to a lost world, yet it is with much difficulty that any person can be persuaded he needs a work of grace on his heart, like that which was needful for the conversion of the Gentiles, so let us beware of fatal hesitation in our own conduct, and recollect how far the being almost persuaded to be a Christian is from being altogether such a one as every true believer is. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 30. When he had thus spoken, the king rose up, and the governor, and Bernice, and they that sat with them. Henry's conference rises to the verdict: the king rose up, and the governor, and Bernice, and they that sat with them, to talk between themselves of what they had heard. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 31. When they were gone aside, they talked between themselves, saying, This man doeth nothing worthy of death or of bonds. Henry says it is no small mercy to have God clear up our righteousness as the light, and our just dealing as the noon-day, to have nothing certain laid to our charge, and God makes even the enemies of his people to do them right, and the aside talk, This man doeth nothing worthy of death or of bonds, is that clearing. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 32. Then said Agrippa unto Festus, This man might have been set at liberty, if he had not appealed unto Caesar. Henry's liberty that might have been is the appeal's cost: this man might have been set at liberty, if he had not appealed unto Caesar, yet the appeal stands, and the slow providence that kept Paul waiting on God now carries him toward Rome.
