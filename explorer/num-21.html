The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Numbers 21 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 And when king Arad the Canaanite, which dwelt in the south, heard tell that Israel came by the way of the spies; then he fought against Israel, and took some of them prisoners.

2 And Israel vowed a vow unto the LORD, and said, If thou wilt indeed deliver this people into my hand, then I will utterly destroy their cities.

3 And the LORD hearkened to the voice of Israel, and delivered up the Canaanites; and they utterly destroyed them and their cities: and he called the name of the place Hormah.

4 And they journeyed from mount Hor by the way of the Red sea, to compass the land of Edom: and the soul of the people was much discouraged because of the way.

5 And the people spake against God, and against Moses, Wherefore have ye brought us up out of Egypt to die in the wilderness? for there is no bread, neither is there any water; and our soul loatheth this light bread.

6 And the LORD sent fiery serpents among the people, and they bit the people; and much people of Israel died.

7 Therefore the people came to Moses, and said, We have sinned, for we have spoken against the LORD, and against thee; pray unto the LORD, that he take away the serpents from us. And Moses prayed for the people.

8 And the LORD said unto Moses, Make thee a fiery serpent, and set it upon a pole: and it shall come to pass, that every one that is bitten, when he looketh upon it, shall live.

9 And Moses made a serpent of brass, and put it upon a pole, and it came to pass, that if a serpent had bitten any man, when he beheld the serpent of brass, he lived.

10 And the children of Israel set forward, and pitched in Oboth.

11 And they journeyed from Oboth, and pitched at Ijeabarim, in the wilderness which is before Moab, toward the sunrising.

12 From thence they removed, and pitched in the valley of Zared.

13 From thence they removed, and pitched on the other side of Arnon, which is in the wilderness that cometh out of the coasts of the Amorites: for Arnon is the border of Moab, between Moab and the Amorites.

14 Wherefore it is said in the book of the wars of the LORD, What he did in the Red sea, and in the brooks of Arnon,

15 And at the stream of the brooks that goeth down to the dwelling of Ar, and lieth upon the border of Moab.

16 And from thence they went to Beer: that is the well whereof the LORD spake unto Moses, Gather the people together, and I will give them water.

17 Then Israel sang this song, Spring up, O well; sing ye unto it:

18 The princes digged the well, the nobles of the people digged it, by the direction of the lawgiver, with their staves. And from the wilderness they went to Mattanah:

19 And from Mattanah to Nahaliel: and from Nahaliel to Bamoth:

20 And from Bamoth in the valley, that is in the country of Moab, to the top of Pisgah, which looketh toward Jeshimon.

21 And Israel sent messengers unto Sihon king of the Amorites, saying,

22 Let me pass through thy land: we will not turn into the fields, or into the vineyards; we will not drink of the waters of the well: but we will go along by the king’s high way, until we be past thy borders.

23 And Sihon would not suffer Israel to pass through his border: but Sihon gathered all his people together, and went out against Israel into the wilderness: and he came to Jahaz, and fought against Israel.

24 And Israel smote him with the edge of the sword, and possessed his land from Arnon unto Jabbok, even unto the children of Ammon: for the border of the children of Ammon was strong.

25 And Israel took all these cities: and Israel dwelt in all the cities of the Amorites, in Heshbon, and in all the villages thereof.

26 For Heshbon was the city of Sihon the king of the Amorites, who had fought against the former king of Moab, and taken all his land out of his hand, even unto Arnon.

27 Wherefore they that speak in proverbs say, Come into Heshbon, let the city of Sihon be built and prepared:

28 For there is a fire gone out of Heshbon, a flame from the city of Sihon: it hath consumed Ar of Moab, and the lords of the high places of Arnon.

29 Woe to thee, Moab! thou art undone, O people of Chemosh: he hath given his sons that escaped, and his daughters, into captivity unto Sihon king of the Amorites.

30 We have shot at them; Heshbon is perished even unto Dibon, and we have laid them waste even unto Nophah, which reacheth unto Medeba.

31 Thus Israel dwelt in the land of the Amorites.

32 And Moses sent to spy out Jaazer, and they took the villages thereof, and drove out the Amorites that were there.

33 And they turned and went up by the way of Bashan: and Og the king of Bashan went out against them, he, and all his people, to the battle at Edrei.

34 And the LORD said unto Moses, Fear him not: for I have delivered him into thy hand, and all his people, and his land; and thou shalt do to him as thou didst unto Sihon king of the Amorites, which dwelt at Heshbon.

35 So they smote him, and his sons, and all his people, until there was none left him alive: and they possessed his land.

How to read the labels

Every substantive historical claim in the editorial panels below carries one of three labels, plus a source reference:

  • Documented — directly supported by a cited source.
  • Interpreted — a reasoned explanation or proposed relationship.
  • Unresolved — evidence is insufficient to establish the claim.

A claim does NOT become Documented merely because a commentary states it. Commentary views are attributed to their authors and labeled Interpreted unless a primary textual source directly supports the claim. Where evidence runs out, the page says Unresolved and stops — it does not fill the gap.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

This chapter follows Israel's journey toward the Jordan with victories, a plague, and a song. King Arad attacks and is destroyed; the people speak against God and Moses, fiery serpents bite many, and at Moses' prayer a serpent of brass is set on a pole, so that everyone bitten who looks on it lives. Israel sings the song of the well at Beer, then defeats Sihon king of the Amorites and Og king of Bashan, and possesses their land from Arnon to Jabbok and beyond. Interpreted Editorial overview, grounded in the chapter text and its study notes

Unfamiliar terms

  • Serpent of brass. The bronze serpent Moses sets on a pole at God's command, so that everyone bitten by the fiery serpents who looks on it lives. Interpreted Chapter text in the Scripture panel above

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. When king Arad the Canaanite, who dwelt in the south, heard Israel came by the way of the spies, he fought against Israel and took some prisoners. JFB identifies Arad as an ancient town on Palestine's southernmost border near Kadesh, and notes the discomfiture was permitted to teach Israel to expect Canaan's conquest not from their own wisdom and valour, but solely from God's favour and help. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Israel vowed a vow to the Lord: if you will indeed deliver this people into my hand, then I will utterly destroy their cities. JFB notes, made to feel their weakness, they implored Heaven's aid and devoted the king's cities to future destruction, and this vow gave the place the name Hormah, slaughter and destruction, though it was not accomplished till after the Jordan. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. The Lord hearkened to Israel's voice, delivered up the Canaanites, and they utterly destroyed them and their cities, and he called the name of the place Hormah. The vow and its fulfilment are paired: Israel learned to look thoroughly to the Lord before the march round Edom even began, as Henry notes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. They journeyed from mount Hor by the way of the Red Sea, to compass the land of Edom, and the soul of the people was much discouraged because of the way. JFB traces the retrograde route through the Arabah to Elath and up through the mountains to Edom's eastern desert, and explains the discouragement: disappointment at being so near the land without entering it, vexation at Edom's refusal, and dread of a long circuit through the worst sandy desert. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. The people spoke against God and against Moses: wherefore have you brought us up out of Egypt to die in the wilderness? For there is no bread, neither is there any water, and our soul loathes this light bread. JFB notes light bread means bread without substance, a calumny refuted by forty years of toilsome journeys on its strength, while Henry asks what will please those whom manna will not please, since it is the bread of life to those who feed on it by faith. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. The Lord sent fiery serpents among the people, and they bit the people, and much people of Israel died. JFB locates the plague near the head of the Gulf of Akaba, greatly infested with venomous reptiles, and explains fiery as either their bright vivid colour or the violent inflammation their bite occasioned. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Therefore the people came to Moses, saying, we have sinned, for we have spoken against the Lord and against you: pray to the Lord that he take away the serpents from us, and Moses prayed for the people. JFB notes the severity and mortality brought them to a sense of sin, and Henry fears they would not have owned the sin if they had not felt the smart, but they relented under the rod. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. The Lord said to Moses, make a fiery serpent, and set it upon a pole, and it shall come to pass that everyone bitten, when he looks upon it, shall live. JFB explains the method was designed to show the cure was God's power and grace, not nature or art, and to be a type of faith in Christ healing all who look to him for their sins. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Moses made a serpent of brass, put it upon a pole, and it came to pass that if a serpent had bitten any man, when he beheld the serpent of brass, he lived. Henry notes the Jews themselves said it was not the sight of the serpent that cured, but in looking up to it they looked up to God as the Lord who healed them, and whosoever looked, however desperate his case, feeble his sight, or distant his place, was certainly and perfectly cured. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 10. The children of Israel set forward and pitched in Oboth. The march resumed along the eastern frontier of Edom, encamping in various stations, with the serpent's healing behind them and the land ahead. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. They journeyed from Oboth and pitched at Ije-abarim, in the wilderness before Moab, toward the sunrising. The stations trace the circuit round Edom's east side, turning the host northward toward Moab's border. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. From thence they removed and pitched in the valley of Zared. JFB describes Zared as a woody brook-valley, a torrent rising east of Moab and flowing west into the Dead Sea, with Ije-abarim supposed to be its ford. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. From thence they removed and pitched on the other side of Arnon, which is in the wilderness that comes out of the coasts of the Amorites, for Arnon is the border of Moab, between Moab and the Amorites. JFB identifies Arnon, now El-Mojib, as a deep, broad, rapid stream dividing the Moabites' and Amorites' dominions. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Wherefore it is said in the book of the wars of the Lord, what he did in the Red Sea, and in the brooks of Arnon. JFB notes a fragment is quoted here from a poem or history of Israel's wars, principally to decide the position of Arnon, and Henry adds that in every stage of life we should distinctly remember what God wrought at such a time and in such a place. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. And at the stream of the brooks that goes down to the dwelling of Ar, and lies upon the border of Moab. JFB identifies Ar as the capital of Moab, the point where the Arnon brooks marked the kingdom's edge. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. And from thence they went to Beer, that is the well whereof the Lord spoke to Moses, gather the people together, and I will give them water. JFB notes Beer means a well, its name probably given afterwards, since it is not mentioned in the itinerary of chapter 33. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. Then Israel sang this song, Spring up, O well, sing you unto it. JFB notes the little song suits the wants of travelling caravans in the East, where water is an occasion both of prayer and thanksgiving, and Henry adds they received the water with joy and thankfulness, which made the mercy doubly sweet. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. The princes dug the well, the nobles of the people dug it, by the direction of the lawgiver, with their staves. JFB notes the princes used only their official rods, not spades, suggesting a well concealed by brushwood or sand, as many Idumean wells still are, discovered by God's special interposition, and Henry adds God promised water, but they must open the ground: favours are expected in the use of means within our power, though the power is only God's. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. Israel sent messengers to Sihon king of the Amorites, saying, let me pass through your land. JFB notes the rejection of this respectful and pacific message was resented, Sihon was discomfited in battle, and Israel obtained the whole Amorite dominions by right of conquest. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Let me pass through your land: we will not turn into the fields or vineyards, neither will we drink of the waters of the well, but we will go along by the king's high way, until we are past your borders. The terms repeated Edom's offer exactly: highway only, no fields, no wells, no deviation until the border was passed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. Sihon would not suffer Israel to pass through his border, but gathered all his people together, went out against Israel into the wilderness, and fought against Israel at Jahaz. Henry notes Sihon went with his forces against Israel out of his own borders without provocation, and so ran upon his own ruin, as God's church's enemies often perish by the counsels they think most wisely taken. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 24. Israel smote him with the edge of the sword, and possessed his land from Arnon to Jabbok, even to the children of Ammon, for the border of Ammon was strong. JFB notes Arnon and Jabbok, now the Zurka, formed the southern and northern boundaries of Sihon's usurped territory, and Ammon's strong border explains why his invasion had stopped there. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. Israel took all these cities, and dwelt in all the cities of the Amorites, in Heshbon and in all its villages. JFB notes this followed the extermination of inhabitants previously doomed, so that dwelling in the cities was possession after judgment, not mere occupation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. For Heshbon was the city of Sihon king of the Amorites, who had fought against the former king of Moab and taken all his land out of his hand, even to Arnon. JFB notes Heshbon lay sixteen miles north of the Arnon, and its ruins show it was a large city, the capital from which Sihon had already conquered Moab's north. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. Wherefore they that speak in proverbs say, Come into Heshbon, let the city of Sihon be built and prepared. JFB notes this is an extract from an Amorite song exultingly anticipating an extension of their conquests to Arnon, quoted through verse 28, with the following verses as Israel's exposure of the usurpers' impotence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. For there is a fire gone out of Heshbon, a flame from the city of Sihon: it has consumed Ar of Moab, and the lords of the high places of Arnon. The Amorite boast recalled Sihon's own fire against Moab, a conquest song now turned, by quotation, into evidence against its singers. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. Woe to you, Moab! You are undone, O people of Chemosh: he has given his sons that escaped, and his daughters, into captivity to Sihon king of the Amorites. JFB identifies Chemosh as the Moabite idol, and reads the line as their god surrendering his worshippers to Sihon's victorious arms. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. We have shot at them: Heshbon is perished even to Dibon, and we have laid them waste even to Nophah, which reaches to Medeba. These verses are Israel's answer strain: the cities the Amorite song boasted of are now perished, and the usurpers' empire is laid waste by the very people they displaced. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 31. Thus Israel dwelt in the land of the Amorites. The summary closes the conquest: Israel did not merely pass through Sihon's land but dwelt in it, from Arnon to Jabbok, as its new possessors. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 32. Moses sent to spy out Jaazer, and they took its villages and drove out the Amorites that were there. The advance continued by reconnaissance: Jaazer, like Heshbon, was taken village by village before the host moved on. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 33. They turned and went up by the way of Bashan, and Og king of Bashan went out against them, he and all his people, to the battle at Edrei. JFB describes Bashan, from its rich soil, as the hilly region east of Jordan between Hermon and Gilead, and Og as a giant Amorite prince who opposed Israel's progress. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 34. The Lord said to Moses, fear him not, for I have delivered him into your hand, and you shall do to him as you did to Sihon king of the Amorites. JFB notes the encouragement was necessary, for Og's gigantic stature was calculated to inspire terror, and the promise repeated Sihon's pattern as the guarantee for Bashan. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 35. So they smote him, and his sons, and all his people, until there was none left him alive, and they possessed his land. The victory was total, as with Sihon: Og's house left no survivor, and Bashan passed to Israel entire. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

JFB notes the severity and mortality brought them to a sense of sin, and Henry fears they would not have owned the sin if they had not felt the smart, but they relented under the rod. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Wherefore it is said in the book of the wars of the Lord, what he did in the Red Sea, and in the brooks of Arnon. JFB notes a fragment is quoted here from a poem or history of Israel's wars, principally to decide the position of Arnon, and Henry adds that in every stage of life we should distinctly remember what God wrought at such a time and in such a place. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. Then Israel sang this song, Spring up, O well, sing you unto it. JFB notes the little song suits the wants of travelling caravans in the East, where water is an occasion both of prayer and thanksgiving, and Henry adds they received the water with joy and thankfulness, which made the mercy doubly sweet. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. The princes dug the well, the nobles of the people dug it, by the direction of the lawgiver, with their staves. JFB notes the princes used only their official rods, not spades, suggesting a well concealed by brushwood or sand, as many Idumean wells still are, discovered by God's special interposition, and Henry adds God promised water, but they must open the ground: favours are expected in the use of means within our power, though the power is only God's. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Matthew Henry

  • Verse 9. Moses made a serpent of brass, put it upon a pole, and it came to pass that if a serpent had bitten any man, when he beheld the serpent of brass, he lived. Henry notes the Jews themselves said it was not the sight of the serpent that cured, but in looking up to it they looked up to God as the Lord who healed them, and whosoever looked, however desperate his case, feeble his sight, or distant his place, was certainly and perfectly cured. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 23. Sihon would not suffer Israel to pass through his border, but gathered all his people together, went out against Israel into the wilderness, and fought against Israel at Jahaz. Henry notes Sihon went with his forces against Israel out of his own borders without provocation, and so ran upon his own ruin, as God's church's enemies often perish by the counsels they think most wisely taken. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
