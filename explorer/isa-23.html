The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Isaiah 23 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 The burden of Tyre. Howl, ye ships of Tarshish; for it is laid waste, so that there is no house, no entering in: from the land of Chittim it is revealed to them.

2 Be still, ye inhabitants of the isle; thou whom the merchants of Zidon, that pass over the sea, have replenished.

3 And by great waters the seed of Sihor, the harvest of the river, is her revenue; and she is a mart of nations.

4 Be thou ashamed, O Zidon: for the sea hath spoken, even the strength of the sea, saying, I travail not, nor bring forth children, neither do I nourish up young men, nor bring up virgins.

5 As at the report concerning Egypt, so shall they be sorely pained at the report of Tyre.

6 Pass ye over to Tarshish; howl, ye inhabitants of the isle.

7 Is this your joyous city, whose antiquity is of ancient days? her own feet shall carry her afar off to sojourn.

8 Who hath taken this counsel against Tyre, the crowning city, whose merchants are princes, whose traffickers are the honourable of the earth?

9 The LORD of hosts hath purposed it, to stain the pride of all glory, and to bring into contempt all the honourable of the earth.

10 Pass through thy land as a river, O daughter of Tarshish: there is no more strength.

11 He stretched out his hand over the sea, he shook the kingdoms: the LORD hath given a commandment against the merchant city, to destroy the strong holds thereof.

12 And he said, Thou shalt no more rejoice, O thou oppressed virgin, daughter of Zidon: arise, pass over to Chittim; there also shalt thou have no rest.

13 Behold the land of the Chaldeans; this people was not, till the Assyrian founded it for them that dwell in the wilderness: they set up the towers thereof, they raised up the palaces thereof; and he brought it to ruin.

14 Howl, ye ships of Tarshish: for your strength is laid waste.

15 And it shall come to pass in that day, that Tyre shall be forgotten seventy years, according to the days of one king: after the end of seventy years shall Tyre sing as an harlot.

16 Take an harp, go about the city, thou harlot that hast been forgotten; make sweet melody, sing many songs, that thou mayest be remembered.

17 And it shall come to pass after the end of seventy years, that the LORD will visit Tyre, and she shall turn to her hire, and shall commit fornication with all the kingdoms of the world upon the face of the earth.

18 And her merchandise and her hire shall be holiness to the LORD: it shall not be treasured nor laid up; for her merchandise shall be for them that dwell before the LORD, to eat sufficiently, and for durable clothing.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

The ships of Tarshish are told to howl, for Tyre, the merchant city whose traders were princes, is laid waste and there is no entering in. After seventy years Tyre returns to her trade, but the chapter ends with her merchandise consecrated to the Lord, spent on those who dwell before him rather than treasured up. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. The burden of Tyre: Howl, you ships of Tarshish, for it is laid waste, so that there is no house, no entering in, and from the land of Chittim it is revealed to them. Tyre in Hebrew is Tsur, Rock; the ships of Tarshish are Tyre's vessels returning from Tartessus in Spain, with which the Phoenicians had much commerce, and also a phrase for large distant-voyaging merchant vessels generally. No house is left, as with Old Tyre after Nebuchadnezzar's siege, and no entering means no house to enter or no possibility of entering the harbour; Chittim is Cyprus, whose Phoenician cities including Citium tell the returning ships of Tyre's downfall. The prophecy spans Shalmaneser's siege, Sargon's completion, Nebuchadnezzar's thirteen-year siege, and Alexander's destruction of New Tyre. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Be still, you inhabitants of the isle, you whom the merchants of Zidon, that pass over the sea, have replenished. Be still means struck dumb with awe, addressed to eye-witnesses of Tyre's ruin, in contrast to the busy din of commerce once heard there, now hushed and still; the isle is strictly New Tyre, and in the sense of coast, Old Tyre on the mainland. Zidon, Tyre's parent city and a fishing station at its beginning, here includes all Phoenician towns on the coast, replenished with wealth and an industrious population. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. By great waters the seed of Sihor, the harvest of the river, is her revenue, and she is a mart of nations. The great waters are the wide sea, the seed is grain, and Sihor, literally dark-coloured, is the Nile, dark and turbid with the fertilising soil of its yearly overflow; the harvest of the river is the Delta's produce, for Egypt was the ancient world's great granary, and Tyrian vessels carried Egyptian produce obtained for wine, oil, and glass into various lands for large profits, making Tyre a mart of nations favourably situated for commerce. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Be ashamed, O Zidon, for the sea has spoken, even the strength of the sea, saying: I travail not, nor bring forth children, neither do I nourish up young men, nor bring up virgins. Zidon, the parent country of Tyre and here equivalent to Phoenicia in general, is called to feel the shame, esteemed in the East, of being now as childless as if she never had any, and the strength of the sea is the stronghold, New Tyre on its rock surrounded by sea. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. As at the report concerning Egypt, so will they be sorely pained at the report of Tyre. When the report of Tyre's overthrow reaches Egypt, the Egyptians will be sorely pained, for when so powerful a neighbouring nation is destroyed, they must know their own end is near. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Pass over to Tarshish; howl, you inhabitants of the isle. Escape from Tyre to its colonies such as Tarshish, for the Tyrians fled to Carthage and elsewhere, both at Nebuchadnezzar's siege and at Alexander's. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Is this your joyous city, whose antiquity is of ancient days, whose feet carried her afar off to sojourn? Is this silent ruin all that is left of the once joyous city, ancient even in Herodotus's time when its priests boasted 2,300 years, whose feet now walk as captives to an enemy's land? Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Who has taken this counsel against Tyre, the crowning city, whose merchants are princes, whose traffickers are the honourable of the earth? The question is answered in the next verse, by the Lord of hosts; Tyre is crown-giving, the city from which dependent kingdoms arose, Tartessus in Spain, Citium in Cyprus, and Carthage in Africa, and its traffickers are literally Canaanites, famed for commerce. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. The Lord of hosts has purposed it, to stain the pride of all glory, and to bring into contempt all the honourable of the earth. Whoever the instruments in overthrowing haughty sinners, God, who has all hosts at his command, is the First Cause; to stain is rather to profane, as the Sabbath is profaned, and the pride of all glory may refer to Tyre's temple of Hercules, the oldest in the world, though it may also be a general proposition that God mars the lustre of whatever is haughty. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Pass through your land as a river, O daughter of Tarshish, for there is no more strength. The river is the Nile, and the daughter of Tarshish is Tyre and its inhabitants, about henceforth to become inhabitants of its colony Tartessus and to pour forth from Tyre as waters flow when barriers are removed; or rather, Tarshish itself and its inhabitants, kept in hard bondage working silver and lead mines by the parent city, now have the bond of restraint — the girdle — removed, since Tyre is no more. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. He stretched out his hand over the sea, he shook the kingdoms, and the Lord has given a commandment against the merchant city, to destroy its strongholds. He is Jehovah, the kingdoms are the Phoenician cities and colonies, and the merchant city is rather Canaan, meaning its north, Phoenicia, as the Phoenicians call their country Canaan on their coins. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. And he said: You will no more rejoice, O you oppressed virgin, daughter of Zidon; arise, pass over to Chittim, and there also you will have no rest. God says Tyre will no more riotously rejoice, for the oppressed virgin — deflowered, laying aside the figure, taken by storm, as Arabs compare a city never taken to an undefiled virgin — the daughter of Zidon, Tyre or the whole land of Phoenicia, will flee to Chittim, Citium in Cyprus, and there also have no rest, for colonies harshly treated by Tyre will repay her in kind. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Behold the land of the Chaldeans: this people was not, till the Assyrian founded it for them that dwell in the wilderness, and he set up its towers, and raised up its palaces, and brought it to ruin. Attention is called to the humiliation that a people of yesterday like the Chaldees should destroy the most ancient city, Tyre; the Chaldees were previously but a rude predatory people with no existence as a recognised nation, whom the Assyrian assigned the land as a permanent settlement for wilderness dwellers, and the Chaldees set up siege-towers against Tyre and laid bare the foundations of her palaces, utterly overthrowing them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Howl, you ships of Tarshish, for your strength is laid waste. The ships of Tarshish howl again, for their strength, the stronghold of Tyre, is laid waste. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. In that day Tyre will be forgotten seventy years, according to the days of one king, and after the end of seventy years Tyre will sing as a harlot. Having lost its former renown, Tyre will be in obscurity for seventy years, as in Jeremiah's seventy years, and the days of one king mean a dynasty: the Babylonian monarchy lasted properly seventy years, from Nebuchadnezzar's first year to Cyrus's taking of Babylon, and Tyre, taken in the middle of that period, is classed with the rest, all alike delivered at its end. Afterward Tyre will sing as a forgotten harlot attracting notice again by her song, for large marts of commerce are compared to harlots courting merchants of all nations for gain, and covetousness is closely akin to idolatry. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Take a harp, go about the city, you harlot that has been forgotten, make sweet melody, sing many songs, that you may be remembered. The same figure expresses that Tyre will again prosper and attract commercial intercourse of nations, becoming the same joyous, self-indulging city as before. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. After the end of seventy years the Lord will visit Tyre, and she will turn to her hire, and commit fornication with all the kingdoms of the world upon the face of the earth. The visit is not in wrath but mercy, and the hire is the image from a harlot of her gains by commerce; after the Babylonian dynasty ended Tyre was rebuilt, and again after Alexander's destruction. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. Her merchandise and her hire will be holiness to the Lord; it will not be treasured nor laid up, for her merchandise will be for them that dwell before the Lord, to eat sufficiently, and for durable clothing. Her traffic and gains will at last, long after the restoration of verse 17, be consecrated to Jehovah and freely expended in his service, not treasured; Jesus visited Tyre's neighbourhood, Paul found disciples there, it early became a Christian bishopric, but the full evangelisation of that race, like the Ethiopians, Egyptians, and Assyrians, is yet to come. Those who dwell before the Lord are the ministers of religion, or disciples sitting before Jehovah, and durable clothing means changes of raiment, which constituted much of former wealth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
