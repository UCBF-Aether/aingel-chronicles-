The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — James 2 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 My brethren, have not the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory, with respect of persons.

2 For if there come unto your assembly a man with a gold ring, in goodly apparel, and there come in also a poor man in vile raiment;

3 And ye have respect to him that weareth the gay clothing, and say unto him, Sit thou here in a good place; and say to the poor, Stand thou there, or sit here under my footstool:

4 Are ye not then partial in yourselves, and are become judges of evil thoughts?

5 Hearken, my beloved brethren, Hath not God chosen the poor of this world rich in faith, and heirs of the kingdom which he hath promised to them that love him?

6 But ye have despised the poor. Do not rich men oppress you, and draw you before the judgment seats?

7 Do not they blaspheme that worthy name by the which ye are called?

8 If ye fulfil the royal law according to the scripture, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself, ye do well:

9 But if ye have respect to persons, ye commit sin, and are convinced of the law as transgressors.

10 For whosoever shall keep the whole law, and yet offend in one point, he is guilty of all.

11 For he that said, Do not commit adultery, said also, Do not kill. Now if thou commit no adultery, yet if thou kill, thou art become a transgressor of the law.

12 So speak ye, and so do, as they that shall be judged by the law of liberty.

13 For he shall have judgment without mercy, that hath shewed no mercy; and mercy rejoiceth against judgment.

14 What doth it profit, my brethren, though a man say he hath faith, and have not works? can faith save him?

15 If a brother or sister be naked, and destitute of daily food,

16 And one of you say unto them, Depart in peace, be ye warmed and filled; notwithstanding ye give them not those things which are needful to the body; what doth it profit?

17 Even so faith, if it hath not works, is dead, being alone.

18 Yea, a man may say, Thou hast faith, and I have works: shew me thy faith without thy works, and I will shew thee my faith by my works.

19 Thou believest that there is one God; thou doest well: the devils also believe, and tremble.

20 But wilt thou know, O vain man, that faith without works is dead?

21 Was not Abraham our father justified by works, when he had offered Isaac his son upon the altar?

22 Seest thou how faith wrought with his works, and by works was faith made perfect?

23 And the scripture was fulfilled which saith, Abraham believed God, and it was imputed unto him for righteousness: and he was called the Friend of God.

24 Ye see then how that by works a man is justified, and not by faith only.

25 Likewise also was not Rahab the harlot justified by works, when she had received the messengers, and had sent them out another way?

26 For as the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without works is dead also.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Do not hold faith with favouritism, James insists: honouring a rich man in fine clothes while seating a poor man low despises the poor God has chosen, and breaks the royal law, love your neighbour as yourself, since breaking one point breaks the whole law. Faith claimed without works is dead, like words of blessing to a hungry brother with no food given, while Abraham offering Isaac and Rahab receiving the messengers show faith working with works and made complete by them. What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Believers must not hold the faith of the Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory, with respect of persons. Henry notes that professing faith in the lowly Jesus does not agree with favouring people for mere outward circumstances and appearances. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 2. If a man with a gold ring and goodly apparel comes into the assembly, and a poor man in vile raiment also comes, the two must not be treated by appearance. Henry allows civil respect, but never respect that influences church offices, censures, or any matter of religion. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 3. Saying to the rich man, sit here in a good place, while telling the poor man to stand or sit under a footstool, is partiality in action. Were all persons more spiritually-minded, Henry says, the poor would be treated with more attention in worshipping congregations. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 4. Such partiality makes believers judges of evil thoughts. Questioning ourselves is of great use in every part of the holy life, Henry urges, and we should take occasion to discourse with our souls. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 5. God has chosen the poor of this world to be rich in faith and heirs of the kingdom promised to them that love him. A lowly state, Henry notes, is most favourable for inward peace and growth in holiness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 6. Yet believers have despised the poor, while rich men oppress them and draw them before judgment seats. Consider how often riches lead to vice and mischief, Henry says, and what reproaches are thrown upon God and religion by men of wealth and worldly greatness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 7. It is the rich who blaspheme the worthy name by which believers are called. This makes the sin of partiality appear very sinful and foolish. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 8. If ye fulfil the royal law, thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself, ye do well. This law is royal because it comes from the King of kings. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 9. If ye have respect to persons, ye commit sin, and are convinced of the law as transgressors. Christians who act unjustly are convicted by the very law they profess. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 10. Whoever keeps the whole law yet offends in one point is guilty of all. According to the covenant of works, one breach of any one command brings a man under condemnation from which no obedience, past, present, or future, can deliver him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 11. He that said, do not commit adultery, also said, do not kill; so breaking one command makes a man a transgressor of the law. To think our good deeds will atone for our bad deeds, Henry notes, plainly puts us upon looking for another atonement. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 12. So speak and so do, as they that shall be judged by the law of liberty. This shows the happiness of those in Christ, Henry says: we may serve him without slavish fear, for God's restraints are not a bondage, but our own corruptions are. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 13. He shall have judgment without mercy that has shown no mercy, and mercy rejoices against judgment. God deems it his glory and joy to pardon and bless those who might justly be condemned, and his grace teaches those who partake of his mercy to copy it in their conduct. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 14. What does it profit if a man says he has faith but has not works? Can faith save him? A bare profession may gain the good opinion of pious people, Henry says, but what profit is it to gain the whole world and lose one's soul? Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 15. If a brother or sister is naked and destitute of daily food, words alone do not meet the need. All things should be accounted profitable or unprofitable as they tend to forward or hinder the salvation of our souls. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 16. Saying depart in peace, be warmed and filled, without giving the things needful to the body, profits nothing. An opinion or assent to the gospel without works is not faith, Henry insists. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 17. Even so faith, if it has not works, is dead, being alone. There is no way to show we really believe in Christ, Henry says, but by being diligent in good works from gospel motives and for gospel purposes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 18. One may say, thou hast faith and I have works; show me thy faith without thy works, and I will show thee my faith by my works. Men may boast to others and be conceited of what they really have not; true believing is not an act of the understanding only, but a work of the whole heart. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 19. Thou believest there is one God; thou doest well, but the devils also believe and tremble. Mere assent to doctrine or historical belief of facts wholly differs from saving faith, Henry notes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 20. O vain man, know that faith without works is dead. Those are wrong who put a mere notional belief of the gospel for the whole of evangelical religion. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 21. Was not Abraham our father justified by works when he offered Isaac his son upon the altar? Abraham believed God and it was reckoned to him for righteousness, and faith producing such works advanced him to peculiar favours. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 22. Faith wrought with Abraham's works, and by works was faith made perfect. Faith is the root and good works are the fruits, Henry says, and we must see to it that we have both. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 23. The scripture was fulfilled which says Abraham believed God and it was imputed to him for righteousness, and he was called the Friend of God. By works a man is justified, Henry explains, not by bare opinion or profession, but by having such faith as produces good works. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 24. Ye see then how that by works a man is justified, and not by faith only. To deny one's own reason, affections, and interests, as Abraham did, is an action fit to try a believer. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 25. Likewise Rahab the harlot was justified by works when she received the messengers and sent them out another way. Rahab's conduct proved her faith to be living and powerful, showing she believed with her heart, not merely by assent of the understanding. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 26. As the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without works is dead also. The best works without faith are dead, Henry warns, for they want root and principle; and faith without fruit is as though the root were dead. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)

Investigation (editorial — a question examined, not a conclusion)

This section investigates a question; it does not conclude it. James 2:24 says, in the chapter’s own words, that by works a man is justified, and not by faith only, while Galatians 2:16 says a man is not justified by the works of the law, but by the faith of Jesus Christ. Do the two letters contradict each other, or do they use the same words for different questions? Interpreted James 2:24 and Galatians 2:16, in the Text panels of those chapters

Parallels

Both chapters argue from Abraham: James 2:21–23 points to Abraham offering Isaac, with the Scripture fulfilled that Abraham believed God and it was counted to him for righteousness, and Galatians 3:6 quotes the same belief of Abraham. In both letters, faith is the root of the argument, and both insist that a claim of faith must show itself in a life, James by works that complete faith, Galatians by a faith that works through love. Interpreted Study notes, summarizing Matthew Henry, James 2 and Galatians 3

Differences

The targets differ. James addresses a claimed faith with no deeds: a brother or sister left naked and hungry with only words of blessing (James 2:14–17), and his examples, Abraham and Rahab, are faith shown in action. Galatians addresses works of the law, above all circumcision, offered as a requirement for standing before God (Galatians 2:16, 5:2–4). James’s note puts it as root and fruit: faith is the root and good works are the fruits, and a person is justified by having such faith as produces good works, not by bare profession. Interpreted Study notes, summarizing Matthew Henry, James 2 and Galatians 2, 5

Whether James and Paul meant the word justified in exactly the same sense in both places, and whether either letter was written as an answer to the other, is not settled by these two chapters alone, and this page does not claim direct dependence, shared authorship, or historical certainty about their relationship.
