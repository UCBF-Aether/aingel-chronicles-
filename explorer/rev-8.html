The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Revelation 8 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 And when he had opened the seventh seal, there was silence in heaven about the space of half an hour.

2 And I saw the seven angels which stood before God; and to them were given seven trumpets.

3 And another angel came and stood at the altar, having a golden censer; and there was given unto him much incense, that he should offer it with the prayers of all saints upon the golden altar which was before the throne.

4 And the smoke of the incense, which came with the prayers of the saints, ascended up before God out of the angel’s hand.

5 And the angel took the censer, and filled it with fire of the altar, and cast it into the earth: and there were voices, and thunderings, and lightnings, and an earthquake.

6 And the seven angels which had the seven trumpets prepared themselves to sound.

7 The first angel sounded, and there followed hail and fire mingled with blood, and they were cast upon the earth: and the third part of trees was burnt up, and all green grass was burnt up.

8 And the second angel sounded, and as it were a great mountain burning with fire was cast into the sea: and the third part of the sea became blood;

9 And the third part of the creatures which were in the sea, and had life, died; and the third part of the ships were destroyed.

10 And the third angel sounded, and there fell a great star from heaven, burning as it were a lamp, and it fell upon the third part of the rivers, and upon the fountains of waters;

11 And the name of the star is called Wormwood: and the third part of the waters became wormwood; and many men died of the waters, because they were made bitter.

12 And the fourth angel sounded, and the third part of the sun was smitten, and the third part of the moon, and the third part of the stars; so as the third part of them was darkened, and the day shone not for a third part of it, and the night likewise.

13 And I beheld, and heard an angel flying through the midst of heaven, saying with a loud voice, Woe, woe, woe, to the inhabiters of the earth by reason of the other voices of the trumpet of the three angels, which are yet to sound!

How to read the labels

Every substantive historical claim in the editorial panels below carries one of three labels, plus a source reference:

  • Documented — directly supported by a cited source.
  • Interpreted — a reasoned explanation or proposed relationship.
  • Unresolved — evidence is insufficient to establish the claim.

A claim does NOT become Documented merely because a commentary states it. Commentary views are attributed to their authors and labeled Interpreted unless a primary textual source directly supports the claim. Where evidence runs out, the page says Unresolved and stops — it does not fill the gap.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

At the seventh seal there is silence in heaven about half an hour, and seven angels receive seven trumpets, while another angel offers the saints' prayers with incense before the throne and then casts fire to the earth. The first four trumpets strike in thirds: hail and fire mingled with blood on the earth, a burning mountain in the sea, the star Wormwood embittering the waters, and a third of sun, moon and stars darkened. A flying angel then cries a triple woe over the three trumpet voices still to come. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, Matthew Henry

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. When the Lamb opened the seventh seal, there was silence in heaven about the space of half an hour. Henry reads this either as the silence of peace, with no complaints rising to the Lord of hosts, or as a silence of expectation, as heaven and earth stood still to see what God was doing, after the pattern of "Be silent, O all flesh, before the Lord." Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 2. John saw the seven angels who stood before God, and to them were given seven trumpets. Henry notes the angels are the wise and willing instruments of providence, furnished with materials and instructions from God, and that as the church's angels sound the gospel trumpet, heaven's angels sound the trumpet of providence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 3. Another angel, having a golden censer, came and stood at the altar, and was given much incense to offer with the prayers of all saints on the golden altar before the throne. Henry identifies this angel as the Lord Jesus, high priest of the church, whose fulness of merit is the incense that makes the saints' prayers acceptable. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 4. The smoke of the incense, with the prayers of the saints, ascended up before God out of the angel's hand. Henry notes all saints are a praying people, times of danger and expectation should be praying times, and no prayer thus recommended was ever denied audience or acceptance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 5. The angel took the censer, filled it with fire of the altar, and cast it into the earth, and there were voices, thunderings, lightnings, and an earthquake. Henry notes these commotions were God's answers to the saints' prayers and tokens of his anger, showing he would avenge himself and his people on their enemies. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 6. The seven angels who had the seven trumpets prepared themselves to sound. Henry notes all things were now prepared: the seals had introduced a new chain of providence, one part linked to another in one wise, well-connected design in God's hand. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 7. The first angel sounded, and there followed hail and fire mingled with blood, cast upon the earth, and the third part of trees was burnt up, and all green grass was burnt up. Henry notes the terrible storm, whether of heresies or of Gothic war, and its limit: even the severest calamities have bounds set by God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 8. The second angel sounded, and as it were a great mountain burning with fire was cast into the sea, and the third part of the sea became blood. Henry notes the mountain as Rome, five times sacked by Goths and Vandals, with a third part of the people destroyed, yet limited to a third, for in judgment God remembers mercy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 9. The third part of the creatures in the sea died, and the third part of the ships were destroyed. Henry ties this to the maritime and merchandising cities of the empire, on which the storm fell heavily, still within the limit of a third part. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 10. The third angel sounded, and a great star fell from heaven, burning as a lamp, and fell upon the third part of the rivers and the fountains of waters. Henry notes the falling star as either a political governor or an ecclesiastical light like Pelagius, corrupted and corrupting the churches. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 11. The star's name is Wormwood, and the third part of the waters became wormwood, and many men died of the waters because they were made bitter. Henry explains the bitterness: either civil laws, the springs of liberty and safety, were poisoned by arbitrary power, or gospel doctrines, the springs of spiritual life, were embittered by dangerous errors, so men found ruin where they sought refreshment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 12. The fourth angel sounded, and the third part of the sun, moon, and stars was smitten, so a third part was darkened, and the day shone not for a third part of it, and the night likewise. Henry reads the darkened luminaries as guides and governors of church or state, and draws the practical lesson that corruptions of doctrine and worship are themselves great judgments and tokens of others. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 13. John beheld an angel flying through the midst of heaven, saying with a loud voice, "Woe, woe, woe, to the inhabiters of the earth by reason of the other voices of the trumpet of the three angels, which are yet to sound!" Henry notes the three woes show how much the coming calamities would exceed those past, warning that if lesser judgments do not take effect, greater must be expected. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry

Unfamiliar terms

  • Wormwood. The name of the star that falls at the third trumpet and embitters a third of the waters. Interpreted Chapter text, verse 11
  • Censer and incense. The golden censer in which the angel offers incense with the prayers of the saints before the throne. Interpreted Study notes, verses 3–4, Matthew Henry
