The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Jeremiah 24 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 The LORD shewed me, and, behold, two baskets of figs were set before the temple of the LORD, after that Nebuchadrezzar king of Babylon had carried away captive Jeconiah the son of Jehoiakim king of Judah, and the princes of Judah, with the carpenters and smiths, from Jerusalem, and had brought them to Babylon.

2 One basket had very good figs, even like the figs that are first ripe: and the other basket had very naughty figs, which could not be eaten, they were so bad.

3 Then said the LORD unto me, What seest thou, Jeremiah? And I said, Figs; the good figs, very good; and the evil, very evil, that cannot be eaten, they are so evil.

4 Again the word of the LORD came unto me, saying,

5 Thus saith the LORD, the God of Israel; Like these good figs, so will I acknowledge them that are carried away captive of Judah, whom I have sent out of this place into the land of the Chaldeans for their good.

6 For I will set mine eyes upon them for good, and I will bring them again to this land: and I will build them, and not pull them down; and I will plant them, and not pluck them up.

7 And I will give them an heart to know me, that I am the LORD: and they shall be my people, and I will be their God: for they shall return unto me with their whole heart.

8 And as the evil figs, which cannot be eaten, they are so evil; surely thus saith the LORD, So will I give Zedekiah the king of Judah, and his princes, and the residue of Jerusalem, that remain in this land, and them that dwell in the land of Egypt:

9 And I will deliver them to be removed into all the kingdoms of the earth for their hurt, to be a reproach and a proverb, a taunt and a curse, in all places whither I shall drive them.

10 And I will send the sword, the famine, and the pestilence, among them, till they be consumed from off the land that I gave unto them and to their fathers.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

After Jeconiah has been carried to Babylon, Jeremiah is shown two baskets of figs set before the temple: good figs, and very evil figs that cannot be eaten. The good figs are the exiles, sent to Babylon for their good, who will be given a heart to know the Lord; the bad figs are Zedekiah, his princes, and the remnant in Egypt, given over to sword, famine and pestilence. What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. The Lord shewed me, and, behold, two baskets of figs were set before the temple of the Lord, after that Nebuchadrezzar king of Babylon had carried away captive Jeconiah the son of Jehoiakim king of Judah, and the princes of Judah, with the carpenters and smiths, from Jerusalem, and had brought them to Babylon. Henry notes the prophet saw two baskets of figs set before the temple, as offerings of first-fruits, after Jeconiah's captivity: the vision comes when captivity has already begun, to distinguish the precious from the vile. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 2. One basket had very good figs, even like the figs that are first ripe: and the other basket had very naughty figs, which could not be eaten, they were so bad. Henry asks: what creature viler than a wicked man? And what more valuable than a godly man? The very good first-ripe figs and the naughty uneatable figs are the two peoples God distinguishes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 3. Then said the Lord unto me: What seest thou, Jeremiah? And I said: Figs; the good figs, very good; and the evil, very evil, that cannot be eaten, they are so evil. Henry's answer is the vision's plain sense: good figs very good, evil figs very evil and uneatable, for God knows how to separate between the precious and the vile. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 4. Again the word of the Lord came unto me, saying. Henry's explication follows the vision: the word comes again to apply the good figs to those already sent into captivity for their good, and the bad figs to those hereafter sent for their hurt. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 5. Thus says the Lord, the God of Israel: Like these good figs, so will I acknowledge them that are carried away captive of Judah, whom I have sent out of this place into the land of the Chaldeans for their good. Henry notes the good figs represent the pious captives: we cannot determine as to God's love or hatred by what is before us, for early suffering sometimes proves for the best, and even this captivity was for their good, and God's intentions never are in vain. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 6. For I will set mine eyes upon them for good, and I will bring them again to this land: and I will build them, and not pull them down; and I will plant them, and not pluck them up. Henry notes God promises that he will own them in captivity: the Lord will own those who are his, in all conditions, assuring them of his protection in trouble, and a glorious deliverance in due time, with return, building, and planting. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 7. And I will give them an heart to know me, that I am the Lord: and they shall be my people, and I will be their God: for they shall return unto me with their whole heart. Henry notes by afflictions they were convinced of sin, humbled under the hand of God, weaned from the world, taught to pray, and turned from sins, particularly from idolatry: when our troubles are sanctified to us, we may be sure that they will end well, and they shall return to him with their whole heart, with liberty to own him for their God, to pray to him, and expect blessings from him. Let those who desire blessings from the Lord beg that he will give them a heart to know him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 8. And as the evil figs, which cannot be eaten, they are so evil; surely thus says the Lord: So will I give Zedekiah the king of Judah, and his princes, and the residue of Jerusalem, that remain in this land, and them that dwell in the land of Egypt. Henry notes the bad figs were Zedekiah and those of his party yet in the land: the evil uneatable figs are the king, his princes, Jerusalem's residue, and those in Egypt, given over as the bad figs are given. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 9. And I will deliver them to be removed into all the kingdoms of the earth for their hurt, to be a reproach and a proverb, a taunt and a curse, in all places whither I shall drive them. Henry notes these should be removed for their hurt, and forsaken of all mankind: removed into all kingdoms for hurt, a reproach, proverb, taunt, and curse wherever driven, and the Spirit of prophecy may here look forward to the dispersion of the unbelieving Jews in all the nations of the earth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 10. And I will send the sword, the famine, and the pestilence, among them, till they be consumed from off the land that I gave unto them and to their fathers. Henry warns God has many judgements, and those that escape one may expect another, till they are brought to repent: sword, famine, and pestilence until consumed from the land given to them and their fathers is the bad figs' end, meant to humble and awaken the proud and secure spirits of those yet in Jerusalem. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
