The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Wisdom of Solomon 4 (King James Version, Apocrypha; public-domain text as received)

1 Better it is to have no children, and to have virtue: for the memorial thereof is immortal: because it is known with God, and with men.

2 When it is present, men take example at it; and when it is gone, they desire it: it weareth a crown, and triumpheth for ever, having gotten the victory, striving for undefiled rewards.

3 But the multiplying brood of the ungodly shall not thrive, nor take deep rooting from bastard slips, nor lay any fast foundation.

4 For though they flourish in branches for a time; yet standing not last, they shall be shaken with the wind, and through the force of winds they shall be rooted out.

5 The imperfect branches shall be broken off, their fruit unprofitable, not ripe to eat, yea, meet for nothing.

6 For children begotten of unlawful beds are witnesses of wickedness against their parents in their trial.

7 But though the righteous be prevented with death, yet shall he be in rest.

8 For honourable age is not that which standeth in length of time, nor that is measured by number of years.

9 But wisdom is the gray hair unto men, and an unspotted life is old age.

10 He pleased God, and was beloved of him: so that living among sinners he was translated.

11 Yea speedily was he taken away, lest that wickedness should alter his understanding, or deceit beguile his soul.

12 For the bewitching of naughtiness doth obscure things that are honest; and the wandering of concupiscence doth undermine the simple mind.

13 He, being made perfect in a short time, fulfilled a long time:

14 For his soul pleased the Lord: therefore hasted he to take him away from among the wicked.

15 This the people saw, and understood it not, neither laid they up this in their minds, That his grace and mercy is with his saints, and that he hath respect unto his chosen.

16 Thus the righteous that is dead shall condemn the ungodly which are living; and youth that is soon perfected the many years and old age of the unrighteous.

17 For they shall see the end of the wise, and shall not understand what God in his counsel hath decreed of him, and to what end the Lord hath set him in safety.

18 They shall see him, and despise him; but God shall laugh them to scorn: and they shall hereafter be a vile carcase, and a reproach among the dead for evermore.

19 For he shall rend them, and cast them down headlong, that they shall be speechless; and he shall shake them from the foundation; and they shall be utterly laid waste, and be in sorrow; and their memorial shall perish.

20 And when they cast up the accounts of their sins, they shall come with fear: and their own iniquities shall convince them to their face.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Better childlessness with virtue than a multiplying brood of the ungodly, which takes no deep root. Honourable age is not length of years but wisdom and an unspotted life, and the righteous person taken early, made perfect in a short time, is at rest. The chapter closes with the living ungodly seeing the end of the wise without understanding it, and the dead righteous condemning them. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing George Leo Haydock

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Better it is to have no children and to have virtue, for virtue's memorial is immortal, known with God and men. Haydock observes that a virtuous memory outlives any lineage. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 2. When virtue is present, men take example at it, and when gone they desire it; it wears a crown and triumphs for ever, winning undefiled rewards. Haydock notes virtue's crown is not of this world only. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 3. The multiplying brood of the ungodly shall not thrive, nor take deep root from bastard slips, nor lay any fast foundation. Haydock's point is that a line rooted in sin cannot root deeply. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 4. Though branches flourish for a time, being not firm they shall be shaken by wind, and plucked up by violent winds. Haydock observes that apparent flourishing without firmness ends in uprooting. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 5. The imperfect branches shall be broken, their fruit unprofitable, sour to eat, and fit for nothing. Haydock notes fruit that never ripens is the image of such a brood. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 6. Children begotten of unlawful beds are witnesses of wickedness against their parents in their trial. Haydock reads the children themselves as standing evidence at the parents' judgment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 7. The righteous, though he die early, shall be in rest. Haydock stresses that rest, not length of days, is the righteous man's portion. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 8. Honourable age is not length of time nor number of years. Haydock's point is that age is measured by wisdom, not by years. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 9. A man's understanding is grey hairs, and an unspotted life is old age. Haydock observes that wisdom and an undefiled life are the true marks of age. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 10. The righteous pleased God and was loved, and living among sinners was taken away. Haydock notes that pleasing God, not pleasing men, is what counts, and removal may itself be mercy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 11. He was taken away speedily, lest wickedness alter his understanding or deceit beguile his soul. Haydock's reading is that early death can be protection, not punishment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 12. The bewitching of naughtiness obscures things that are honest, and the wandering of concupiscence undermines the simple mind. Haydock warns that fascination with evil darkens honest judgment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 13. He, being made perfect in a short time, fulfilled a long time. Haydock observes that perfection is not measured by duration. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 14. His soul pleased the Lord, therefore he hastened him out of the midst of wickedness, though peoples saw and understood it not. Haydock notes the peoples' failure to understand is itself part of the mystery. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 15. God's grace and mercy are with his saints, and he has respect to his chosen. Haydock stresses that the chosen are not forgotten in their removal. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 16. The righteous who dies condemns the ungodly who live, and youth soon perfected condemns the ungodly man's many years. Haydock's point is that a short righteous life is a standing judgment on long unrighteous ones. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 17. The ungodly shall see the wise man's end and not understand what God decreed for him, nor why the Lord kept him safe. Haydock notes they see the end but miss its meaning. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 18. They shall see him and despise him, but the Lord shall laugh them to scorn. Haydock observes that contempt for the righteous returns upon the despisers. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 19. They shall be a fall, dishonoured and disgraced for ever among the dead, for God shall break them and cast them headlong speechless, shaking them from the foundations. Haydock's image is total overthrow, from the foundations up. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 20. At the reckoning of sins they shall come with fear, and their iniquities shall convince them to their face. Haydock notes that at reckoning, their own deeds stand as accusers. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
