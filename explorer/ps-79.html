The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Psalms 79 (King James Version (1611) for Psalms 1–150; Charles Thomson, The Septuagint (1808) for Psalm 151)

1 O God, the heathen are come into thine inheritance; thy holy temple have they defiled; they have laid Jerusalem on heaps.

2 The dead bodies of thy servants have they given to be meat unto the fowls of the heaven, the flesh of thy saints unto the beasts of the earth.

3 Their blood have they shed like water round about Jerusalem; and there was none to bury them.

4 We are become a reproach to our neighbours, a scorn and derision to them that are round about us.

5 How long, LORD? wilt thou be angry for ever? shall thy jealousy burn like fire?

6 Pour out thy wrath upon the heathen that have not known thee, and upon the kingdoms that have not called upon thy name.

7 For they have devoured Jacob, and laid waste his dwelling place.

8 O remember not against us former iniquities: let thy tender mercies speedily prevent us: for we are brought very low.

9 Help us, O God of our salvation, for the glory of thy name: and deliver us, and purge away our sins, for thy name’s sake.

10 Wherefore should the heathen say, Where is their God? let him be known among the heathen in our sight by the revenging of the blood of thy servants which is shed.

11 Let the sighing of the prisoner come before thee; according to the greatness of thy power preserve thou those that are appointed to die;

12 And render unto our neighbours sevenfold into their bosom their reproach, wherewith they have reproached thee, O Lord.

13 So we thy people and sheep of thy pasture will give thee thanks for ever: we will shew forth thy praise to all generations.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

A lament over Jerusalem invaded and the temple defiled, with the dead left unburied and the people made a reproach to their neighbours. The prayer asks God not to hold former sins against them, to deliver and forgive for the glory of his own name, to answer the mockery of the nations, and promises that God's people will then give thanks for ever. Interpreted Psalm text above; study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Henry opens with the church's complaint as a dishonour case: invasion is not only ruin for Jerusalem but defilement of God's inheritance and temple, which is why the prayer pleads his name rather than Israel's record. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 2. The dead bodies of God's servants were left as meat for birds and beasts, unburied in dishonour. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 3. Blood shed like water with none to bury turns slaughter into public shame: the unburied dead are the visible sign of a city laid waste. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 4. Reproach, scorn, and derision from neighbours make suffering social: God's people bear mockery from round about, not only violence from invaders. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 5. How long anger and jealousy burning like fire ask whether displeasure will be endless: Henry hears a plea distinguishing God's people from impenitent sinners, for whom wrath is indeed forever. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 6. It prays God to pour out his wrath instead on heathen kingdoms that have not known him or called on his name. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 7. Henry counts this crime alone as enough: the heathen did not merely disturb Jacob but devoured him, not merely encroach on his dwelling place but lay it waste by plundering and depopulating it. That is the weight of the plea, since whoever touches God's people touches the apple of his eye, and the prayer for wrath is in effect a prophecy of judgment on ungodliness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 8. Former iniquities are pleaded against as an entail to be cut off by repentance: pardon blots out, and tender mercy must speedily prevent ruin for a people brought very low, since mercy, not merit, is the only hope left. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 9. Deliverance and purging are sought for the glory of the name, not for Israel's record: Henry insists pardon is the foundation and sweetness of deliverance, so prayer for sin's removal must outrank prayer for affliction's removal. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 10. Lest the heathen ask where their God is, the psalm prays that vengeance for shed blood be known among the heathen in their sight. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 11. Prisoners' sighs, silent because they dare not be heard, are recommended to divine pity and power: man's extremity is God's opportunity, and greatness of power must preserve those appointed to die. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 12. Sevenfold reproach returned into the bosom is prayed only where God's own name was blasphemed: Henry distinguishes reproach against us, left to forgiveness, from reproach against God, which may be prayed against as prophecy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 13. Thanksgiving forever and praise to all generations close the psalm as a vow, not a mood: a people formed to show forth praise argue that if they are cut off, the tribute of praise itself ceases. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
