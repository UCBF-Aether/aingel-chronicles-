The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 41 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Canst thou draw out leviathan with an hook? or his tongue with a cord which thou lettest down?

2 Canst thou put an hook into his nose? or bore his jaw through with a thorn?

3 Will he make many supplications unto thee? will he speak soft words unto thee?

4 Will he make a covenant with thee? wilt thou take him for a servant for ever?

5 Wilt thou play with him as with a bird? or wilt thou bind him for thy maidens?

6 Shall the companions make a banquet of him? shall they part him among the merchants?

7 Canst thou fill his skin with barbed irons? or his head with fish spears?

8 Lay thine hand upon him, remember the battle, do no more.

9 Behold, the hope of him is in vain: shall not one be cast down even at the sight of him?

10 None is so fierce that dare stir him up: who then is able to stand before me?

11 Who hath prevented me, that I should repay him? whatsoever is under the whole heaven is mine.

12 I will not conceal his parts, nor his power, nor his comely proportion.

13 Who can discover the face of his garment? or who can come to him with his double bridle?

14 Who can open the doors of his face? his teeth are terrible round about.

15 His scales are his pride, shut up together as with a close seal.

16 One is so near to another, that no air can come between them.

17 They are joined one to another, they stick together, that they cannot be sundered.

18 By his neesings a light doth shine, and his eyes are like the eyelids of the morning.

19 Out of his mouth go burning lamps, and sparks of fire leap out.

20 Out of his nostrils goeth smoke, as out of a seething pot or caldron.

21 His breath kindleth coals, and a flame goeth out of his mouth.

22 In his neck remaineth strength, and sorrow is turned into joy before him.

23 The flakes of his flesh are joined together: they are firm in themselves; they cannot be moved.

24 His heart is as firm as a stone; yea, as hard as a piece of the nether millstone.

25 When he raiseth up himself, the mighty are afraid: by reason of breakings they purify themselves.

26 The sword of him that layeth at him cannot hold: the spear, the dart, nor the habergeon.

27 He esteemeth iron as straw, and brass as rotten wood.

28 The arrow cannot make him flee: slingstones are turned with him into stubble.

29 Darts are counted as stubble: he laugheth at the shaking of a spear.

30 Sharp stones are under him: he spreadeth sharp pointed things upon the mire.

31 He maketh the deep to boil like a pot: he maketh the sea like a pot of ointment.

32 He maketh a path to shine after him; one would think the deep to be hoary.

33 Upon earth there is not his like, who is made without fear.

34 He beholdeth all high things: he is a king over all the children of pride.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

God holds up leviathan, the creature no man can draw out with a hook, tame with soft words, or take for a servant forever. His scales are shut up as with a close seal, no air passes between them; his sneezings shine, fire leaps from his mouth, and none is so fierce as to dare stir him up. The argument closes the speeches: if no one can stand before this creature, who then is able to stand before God, to whom whatsoever is under the whole heaven belongs? Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Canst thou draw out leviathan with a hook? Or his tongue with a cord which thou lettest down? JFB explains leviathan literally as the twisted animal, gathering itself in folds, a poetical generalization for all cetacean, serpentine, and saurian monsters, especially the crocodile, naturally described after the river horse, as both are found in the Nile: the crocodile has no tongue, or a very small one cleaving to the lower jaw, but as in fishing the tongue of the fish draws the baited hook to it, God asks whether Job can in like manner take leviathan. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Canst thou put a hook into his nose? Or bore his jaw through with a thorn? JFB renders hook as a rope of rushes, and thorn as a ring or hook, by which wild beasts were led about when caught, and fishes also secured and thrown into the water to keep them alive. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Will he make many supplications unto thee? Will he speak soft words unto thee? JFB renders soft words as that thou mayest spare his life: no, he is untamable. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Will he make a covenant with thee? Wilt thou take him for a servant for ever? JFB asks whether he can be tamed for domestic use, as the unicorn was asked in chapter 39. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Wilt thou play with him as with a bird? Or wilt thou bind him for thy maidens? JFB renders a bird as tamed, a creature to be played with. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Shall the companions make a banquet of him? Shall they part him among the merchants? JFB renders companions as partners in fishing, and make a banquet as partners in trade desiring to purchase him, with merchants literally as Canaanites, who were great merchants. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Canst thou fill his skin with barbed irons? Or his head with fish spears? JFB notes his hide is not penetrable, as that of fishes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Lay thine hand upon him, remember the battle, do no more. JFB explains that if thou lay thy hand upon him, thou wilt have reason ever to remember it, and wilt never try it again. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Behold, the hope of him is in vain: shall not one be cast down even at the sight of him? JFB renders hope as the hope of taking him, and cast down as with fear at the mere sight of him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. None is so fierce that he dare stir him up: who then is able to stand before God? JFB renders fierce as courageous: if a man dare attack one of God's creatures, who will dare, as Job has wished, to oppose himself to God the Creator? This is the main drift of the description of leviathan. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Who has prevented God, that he should repay him? Whatsoever is under the whole heaven is God's. JFB renders prevented as done God a favour first, anticipated him with service: none can call God to account as unjust because he has withdrawn favours, for none has laid him under a prior obligation by conferring on him something which was not already his own, and man cannot constrain the creature to be his servant, much less the Creator. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. God will not conceal his parts, nor his power, nor his comely proportion. JFB notes a resumption of the description broken off by the digression, rendering his power literally as the way, the true proportion or expression of his strength, and comely proportion as the comeliness of his structure, his apparatus or suit of apparel. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Who can discover the face of his garment? Or who can come to him with his double bridle? JFB renders discover as uncover the surface of his garment, stripping off the hard outer coat with which the inner skin is covered, and within his double jaws as the double row of teeth, with bridle implying that none dare put his hand in to insert a bridle where in other animals it is placed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Who can open the doors of his face? His teeth are terrible round about. JFB renders doors of his face as his mouth, with teeth sixty in number, larger in proportion than his body, some standing out, some serrated, fitting into each other like a comb. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. His scales are his pride, shut up together as with a close seal. JFB renders it as his furrows of shields, the rows of scales like shields covering him, seventeen such rows, shut up firmly closed together, so that a musket ball cannot penetrate him save in the eye, throat, and belly. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. One is so near to another, that no air can come between them. The rows of shields lie so tightly closed that nothing passes between them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. They are joined one to another, they stick together, that they cannot be sundered. The scales form one unbroken armour, inseparable by any force of man. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. By his sneezings a light does shine, and his eyes are like the eyelids of the morning. JFB translates his sneezing as causing a light to shine: amphibious animals emerging after long holding their breath under water respire by violently expelling the breath like one sneezing, in the effort the eyes, usually directed towards the sun, seem to flash fire, and eyelids of morning as the Egyptian hieroglyphic painting the eyes of the crocodile as the symbol for morning, because the eyes appear first before the whole body emerges from the deep. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. Out of his mouth go burning lamps, and sparks of fire leap out. JFB renders burning lamps as torches, namely in respiring, seeming to go out of his mouth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Out of his nostrils goes smoke, as out of a seething pot or caldron. JFB renders seething as boiling, literally blown under, under which a fire is blown. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. His breath kindles coals, and a flame goes out of his mouth. JFB notes this as poetical imagery. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. In his neck remains strength, and sorrow is turned into joy before him. JFB renders remaineth as abiding permanently, his chief strength being in the neck, and sorrow as anxiety or dismay personified, turned into joy as dancing or exulting: wherever he goes he spreads terror before him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. The flakes of his flesh are joined together: they are firm in themselves, they cannot be moved. JFB renders flakes as dewlaps, that which falls down, joined fast and firm together, not hanging loose as in the ox, and firm in themselves as upon him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. His heart is as firm as a stone, yea, as hard as a piece of the nether millstone. JFB notes that in large beasts less acute in feeling there is great firmness of the heart and slower motion, and the nether millstone on which the upper turns is especially hard. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. When he raises up himself, the mighty are afraid: by reason of breakings they purify themselves. JFB explains he as the crocodile, a type of the awe which the Creator inspires when he rises in wrath, with breakings as terror of mind, and purify themselves as wandering from the way, fleeing away bewildered. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. The sword of him that lays at him cannot hold: the spear, the dart, nor the habergeon. JFB notes cannot hold as on his hard skin, and habergeon as coat of mail, with avail taken by zeugma out of hold. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. He esteems iron as straw, and brass as rotten wood. JFB renders iron and brass as weapons, powerless against him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. The arrow cannot make him flee: slingstones are turned with him into stubble. JFB renders arrow literally as son of the bow, and stubble as the effect of arrows, no more than throwing stubble at him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. Darts are counted as stubble: he laughs at the shaking of a spear. JFB renders darts as clubs, darts having been already mentioned. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. Sharp stones are under him: he spreads sharp pointed things upon the mire. JFB renders stones as potsherds, the sharp and pointed scales on the belly like broken pieces of pottery, and sharp-pointed things as a threshing instrument, but on the mire: when he lies on the mire he leaves the marks of his scales so imprinted that one might fancy a threshing instrument with sharp teeth had been drawn over it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 31. He makes the deep to boil like a pot: he makes the sea like a pot of ointment. JFB notes whenever he moves, the sea as the Nile, and pot of ointment as the vessel in which it is mixed, appropriate to the crocodile, which emits a musky smell. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 32. He makes a path to shine after him; one would think the deep to be hoary. JFB renders path as the foam on his track, and hoary as hair of the aged. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 33. Upon earth there is not his like, who is made without fear. JFB renders who as being one who is made without fear, unmatched among creatures. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 34. He beholds all high things: he is a king over all the children of pride. JFB renders beholdeth as as their superior, and children of pride as the proud and fierce beasts: to humble the pride of man and teach implicit submission is the aim of Jehovah's speech and of the book, therefore with leviathan, the type of God in his lordship over creation, he closes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Unfamiliar terms

  • Leviathan (verse 1). Literally, in Jamieson-Fausset-Brown’s rendering, the twisted animal: untamable, armoured in rows of scales shut up as with a close seal, breathing seeming fire, and too fierce for any man to stir up. The study note calls leviathan a poetical generalization for cetacean, serpentine, and saurian monsters, especially the crocodile, so no single known species is settled.
