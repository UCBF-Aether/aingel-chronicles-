The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Genesis 4 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 And Adam knew Eve his wife; and she conceived, and bare Cain, and said, I have gotten a man from the LORD.

2 And she again bare his brother Abel. And Abel was a keeper of sheep, but Cain was a tiller of the ground.

3 And in process of time it came to pass, that Cain brought of the fruit of the ground an offering unto the LORD.

4 And Abel, he also brought of the firstlings of his flock and of the fat thereof. And the LORD had respect unto Abel and to his offering:

5 But unto Cain and to his offering he had not respect. And Cain was very wroth, and his countenance fell.

6 And the LORD said unto Cain, Why art thou wroth? and why is thy countenance fallen?

7 If thou doest well, shalt thou not be accepted? and if thou doest not well, sin lieth at the door. And unto thee shall be his desire, and thou shalt rule over him.

8 And Cain talked with Abel his brother: and it came to pass, when they were in the field, that Cain rose up against Abel his brother, and slew him.

9 And the LORD said unto Cain, Where is Abel thy brother? And he said, I know not: Am I my brother’s keeper?

10 And he said, What hast thou done? the voice of thy brother’s blood crieth unto me from the ground.

11 And now art thou cursed from the earth, which hath opened her mouth to receive thy brother’s blood from thy hand;

12 When thou tillest the ground, it shall not henceforth yield unto thee her strength; a fugitive and a vagabond shalt thou be in the earth.

13 And Cain said unto the LORD, My punishment is greater than I can bear.

14 Behold, thou hast driven me out this day from the face of the earth; and from thy face shall I be hid; and I shall be a fugitive and a vagabond in the earth; and it shall come to pass, that every one that findeth me shall slay me.

15 And the LORD said unto him, Therefore whosoever slayeth Cain, vengeance shall be taken on him sevenfold. And the LORD set a mark upon Cain, lest any finding him should kill him.

16 And Cain went out from the presence of the LORD, and dwelt in the land of Nod, on the east of Eden.

17 And Cain knew his wife; and she conceived, and bare Enoch: and he builded a city, and called the name of the city, after the name of his son, Enoch.

18 And unto Enoch was born Irad: and Irad begat Mehujael: and Mehujael begat Methusael: and Methusael begat Lamech.

19 And Lamech took unto him two wives: the name of the one was Adah, and the name of the other Zillah.

20 And Adah bare Jabal: he was the father of such as dwell in tents, and of such as have cattle.

21 And his brother’s name was Jubal: he was the father of all such as handle the harp and organ.

22 And Zillah, she also bare Tubalcain, an instructer of every artificer in brass and iron: and the sister of Tubalcain was Naamah.

23 And Lamech said unto his wives, Adah and Zillah, Hear my voice; ye wives of Lamech, hearken unto my speech: for I have slain a man to my wounding, and a young man to my hurt.

24 If Cain shall be avenged sevenfold, truly Lamech seventy and sevenfold.

25 And Adam knew his wife again; and she bare a son, and called his name Seth: For God, said she, hath appointed me another seed instead of Abel, whom Cain slew.

26 And to Seth, to him also there was born a son; and he called his name Enos: then began men to call upon the name of the LORD.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Cain and Abel bring offerings; Cain's anger at Abel's acceptance turns to murder, and Cain is marked and sent out as a wanderer. His line builds a city and takes up herding, music and metalwork, while Lamech boasts of vengeance; the chapter closes with Seth and Enos, when men began to call on the name of the LORD. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Eve bore Cain and said she had gotten a man from the LORD. Henry suggests she hoped this first son might be the promised seed, a hope that would be disappointed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. She bore his brother Abel, who became a keeper of sheep while Cain tilled the ground. Each son had a calling, which Henry takes as God's will that everyone have useful work. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. In time Cain brought an offering of the fruit of the ground to the LORD. Worship itself was no new invention: from the beginning, people brought offerings to God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Abel brought the firstlings of his flock and their fat, and the LORD had respect to Abel and his offering. Henry stresses Abel came in faith, as a sinner trusting God's appointed way, while Cain did not. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. But to Cain and his offering God had no respect, so Cain was very angry and his face fell. Rejection exposed a proud, unbelieving heart, not merely a faulty gift. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. God asked Cain why he was angry and why his face had fallen. The question gives Cain a chance to examine his own heart before anger hardens into action. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. God promised that if Cain did well he would be accepted, but warned that sin lay crouching at the door and must be ruled. Henry notes God notices every angry and envious look, and calls Cain to master the passion before it masters him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Cain talked with Abel in the field and rose up and killed him. Henry calls this the progress of cherished envy: malice in the heart ends in murder by the hands. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. God asked where Abel was, and Cain answered he did not know, asking if he was his brother's keeper. He covers deliberate murder with a deliberate lie, denying responsibility for the brother he should have protected. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. God said Abel's blood cried to him from the ground. Henry notes the Hebrew word is plural, bloods, as if every life Abel might have fathered also cries out, and murder cannot be buried deep enough to silence it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Cain was now cursed from the earth, which had opened its mouth to receive his brother's blood. Unlike Adam's curse, which fell on the ground, this curse falls directly on Cain himself. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. The ground would no longer yield its strength to him, and he would be a fugitive and wanderer. His punishment fits his chosen portion: the earth he loved becomes a curse to him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Cain complained his punishment was greater than he could bear. Henry notes he complains of his suffering, not of his sin, showing hardness of heart. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Cain feared that being driven from God's presence, anyone who found him would kill him. Banishment from God's face is the deepest part of the sentence, worse than wandering itself. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. God set a mark on Cain so that no one finding him would kill him, and promised sevenfold vengeance on his killer. Even in judgment, God restrains vengeance and preserves Cain's life for his own wise ends. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Cain went out from God's presence and dwelt in the land of Nod, east of Eden. To go out from God's presence is to leave the place of worship and fellowship, not merely to travel east. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. Cain knew his wife, she bore Enoch, and Cain built a city named after his son. The city-building shows Cain settling into the world he chose, seeking security apart from God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. Enoch fathered Irad, and the line runs through Mehujael and Methusael to Lamech. This brief genealogy traces Cain's line forward to the next figure of note, Lamech. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. Lamech took two wives, Adah and Zillah. This is the first polygamy recorded, a departure from the one-man-one-woman pattern of Eden, noted without approval. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Adah bore Jabal, father of those who dwell in tents and keep cattle. Cain's line is credited with the arts of nomadic herding life, showing skill can flourish apart from godliness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. His brother Jubal became father of all who handle harp and organ. Music also arises in Cain's line, a good gift used in a family that has left God's presence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Zillah bore Tubal-cain, instructor of workers in brass and iron, and his sister was Naamah. Metalwork completes the picture: Cain's descendants pioneer crafts and technology. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. Lamech boasted to his wives that he had killed a man for wounding him, and a young man for hurting him. His song celebrates revenge without remorse, the opposite of repentance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. If Cain was avenged sevenfold, Lamech claimed seventy-sevenfold for himself. He twists God's protection of Cain into a licence for his own vengeance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. Adam knew his wife again, and she bore Seth, saying God had appointed another seed instead of Abel, whom Cain slew. Seth's birth renews the godly line after Abel's death. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. Seth fathered Enos, and then men began to call upon the name of the LORD. Henry and JFB read this as the renewal of public worship: after Abel, the godly line again openly invokes God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
