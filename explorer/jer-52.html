The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Jeremiah 52 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Zedekiah was one and twenty years old when he began to reign, and he reigned eleven years in Jerusalem. And his mother’s name was Hamutal the daughter of Jeremiah of Libnah.

2 And he did that which was evil in the eyes of the LORD, according to all that Jehoiakim had done.

3 For through the anger of the LORD it came to pass in Jerusalem and Judah, till he had cast them out from his presence, that Zedekiah rebelled against the king of Babylon.

4 And it came to pass in the ninth year of his reign, in the tenth month, in the tenth day of the month, that Nebuchadrezzar king of Babylon came, he and all his army, against Jerusalem, and pitched against it, and built forts against it round about.

5 So the city was besieged unto the eleventh year of king Zedekiah.

6 And in the fourth month, in the ninth day of the month, the famine was sore in the city, so that there was no bread for the people of the land.

7 Then the city was broken up, and all the men of war fled, and went forth out of the city by night by the way of the gate between the two walls, which was by the king’s garden; (now the Chaldeans were by the city round about:) and they went by the way of the plain.

8 But the army of the Chaldeans pursued after the king, and overtook Zedekiah in the plains of Jericho; and all his army was scattered from him.

9 Then they took the king, and carried him up unto the king of Babylon to Riblah in the land of Hamath; where he gave judgment upon him.

10 And the king of Babylon slew the sons of Zedekiah before his eyes: he slew also all the princes of Judah in Riblah.

11 Then he put out the eyes of Zedekiah; and the king of Babylon bound him in chains, and carried him to Babylon, and put him in prison till the day of his death.

12 Now in the fifth month, in the tenth day of the month, which was the nineteenth year of Nebuchadrezzar king of Babylon, came Nebuzaradan, captain of the guard, which served the king of Babylon, into Jerusalem,

13 And burned the house of the LORD, and the king’s house; and all the houses of Jerusalem, and all the houses of the great men, burned he with fire:

14 And all the army of the Chaldeans, that were with the captain of the guard, brake down all the walls of Jerusalem round about.

15 Then Nebuzaradan the captain of the guard carried away captive certain of the poor of the people, and the residue of the people that remained in the city, and those that fell away, that fell to the king of Babylon, and the rest of the multitude.

16 But Nebuzaradan the captain of the guard left certain of the poor of the land for vinedressers and for husbandmen.

17 Also the pillars of brass that were in the house of the LORD, and the bases, and the brasen sea that was in the house of the LORD, the Chaldeans brake, and carried all the brass of them to Babylon.

18 The caldrons also, and the shovels, and the snuffers, and the bowls, and the spoons, and all the vessels of brass wherewith they ministered, took they away.

19 And the basons, and the firepans, and the bowls, and the caldrons, and the candlesticks, and the spoons, and the cups; that which was of gold in gold, and that which was of silver in silver, took the captain of the guard away.

20 The two pillars, one sea, and twelve brasen bulls that were under the bases, which king Solomon had made in the house of the LORD: the brass of all these vessels was without weight.

21 And concerning the pillars, the height of one pillar was eighteen cubits; and a fillet of twelve cubits did compass it; and the thickness thereof was four fingers: it was hollow.

22 And a chapiter of brass was upon it; and the height of one chapiter was five cubits, with network and pomegranates upon the chapiters round about, all of brass. The second pillar also and the pomegranates were like unto these.

23 And there were ninety and six pomegranates on a side; and all the pomegranates upon the network were an hundred round about.

24 And the captain of the guard took Seraiah the chief priest, and Zephaniah the second priest, and the three keepers of the door:

25 He took also out of the city an eunuch, which had the charge of the men of war; and seven men of them that were near the king’s person, which were found in the city; and the principal scribe of the host, who mustered the people of the land; and threescore men of the people of the land, that were found in the midst of the city.

26 So Nebuzaradan the captain of the guard took them, and brought them to the king of Babylon to Riblah.

27 And the king of Babylon smote them, and put them to death in Riblah in the land of Hamath. Thus Judah was carried away captive out of his own land.

28 This is the people whom Nebuchadrezzar carried away captive: in the seventh year three thousand Jews and three and twenty:

29 In the eighteenth year of Nebuchadrezzar he carried away captive from Jerusalem eight hundred thirty and two persons:

30 In the three and twentieth year of Nebuchadrezzar Nebuzaradan the captain of the guard carried away captive of the Jews seven hundred forty and five persons: all the persons were four thousand and six hundred.

31 And it came to pass in the seven and thirtieth year of the captivity of Jehoiachin king of Judah, in the twelfth month, in the five and twentieth day of the month, that Evilmerodach king of Babylon in the first year of his reign lifted up the head of Jehoiachin king of Judah, and brought him forth out of prison.

32 And spake kindly unto him, and set his throne above the throne of the kings that were with him in Babylon,

33 And changed his prison garments: and he did continually eat bread before him all the days of his life.

34 And for his diet, there was a continual diet given him of the king of Babylon, every day a portion until the day of his death, all the days of his life.

How to read the labels

Every substantive historical claim in the editorial panels below carries one of three labels, plus a source reference:

  • Documented — directly supported by a cited source.
  • Interpreted — a reasoned explanation or proposed relationship.
  • Unresolved — evidence is insufficient to establish the claim.

A claim does NOT become Documented merely because a commentary states it. Commentary views are attributed to their authors and labeled Interpreted unless a primary textual source directly supports the claim. Where evidence runs out, the page says Unresolved and stops — it does not fill the gap.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

The book closes with history, not prophecy: Zedekiah's reign, the eighteen-month siege, the breach of the walls, the flight and blinding of the king, the burning of the temple and the king's house, the carrying away of the temple vessels, and three numbered captivities. It ends unexpectedly with mercy — Jehoiachin, after thirty-seven years in prison, is released by Evil-merodach, given a seat above the other kings, and fed daily from the king's table for the rest of his life. What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Henry notes Zedekiah had arrived at years of discretion, being twenty-one when he came to the throne, and reigned eleven years in Jerusalem. The narrative begins no higher than his reign, he says, probably drawn up by some of those carried away with him, as a reproach for flattering themselves that they should not go into captivity after their brethren. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 2. Henry says Zedekiah was none of the worst kings, since we never read of his idolatries, yet his character is that he did evil in the eyes of the LORD, for he did not do the good he should have done. Doing evil, he implies, includes the failure to do the good required. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 3. Henry reads this as God's just displeasure carried to casting Judah and Jerusalem out from his favourable, gracious presence, expelling them from the land and city and temple that bore tokens of his presence in bounty and covenant love. Yet none are cast out from God's gracious presence, he notes, but those who by sin have first thrown themselves out of it, a fruit of sin to be deprecated above all else. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 4. Henry says the Chaldeans sat down before Jerusalem in the ninth year of Zedekiah, in the tenth month, and blocked it up. In captivity, he notes, the Jews kept a fast in the tenth month in remembrance of this step towards their ruin, as they kept fasts in the fourth, fifth and seventh months for the city's breaking, the temple's burning and Gedaliah's murder. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 5. Henry pictures the eighteen months' siege as a sad time beyond imagining, with provisions cut off and the enemy's attacks ever renewed, while the inhabitants obstinately resolved to hold out to the last extremity. The city was besieged unto the eleventh year of Zedekiah, with nothing remaining but a fearful looking for of judgment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 6. Henry says what disabled Jerusalem to hold out, yet could not prevail with her to capitulate, was the famine: there was no bread for the people of the land, so soldiers could not make good their posts and were rendered wholly unserviceable. Walls, he observes, will not hold out long without men, any more than men without walls, nor will both together stand without God and his protection. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 7. Henry says that once the soldiers were unserviceable for famine, no wonder the city was broken up, with the men of war fleeing by night by the gate between the two walls, by the king's garden, while the Chaldeans were round about. Walls without men, he remarks, are no defence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 8. Henry calls Zedekiah's flight inglorious and vain: overtaken in the plains of Jericho, his guards dispersed and all his army scattered from him. There is no escaping the judgments of God, he notes, for they come upon the sinner and overtake him, let him flee where he will. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 9. Henry says the king was carried up to the king of Babylon at Riblah, where judgment was given upon him as a rebel. One cannot think without vexation, he remarks, that a king of Judah, of the house of David, should be arraigned as a criminal at the bar of a heathen king, but he humbled not himself before Jeremiah the prophet, and therefore God thus humbled him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 10. Henry says that pursuant to the conqueror's sentence, Zedekiah's sons were slain before his eyes, and all the princes of Judah at Riblah. The sentence was immediately put in execution upon the rebel king and his house. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 11. Henry says Zedekiah's eyes were then put out, and he was bound in chains, carried in triumph to Babylon, and kept in prison till the day of his death, wearing out the remainder of his life in darkness and misery. Jeremiah had often told him what it would come to, he adds, but he would not take warning when he might have prevented it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 12. Henry says that a month after the city was taken, Nebuzaradan, captain of the guard, called in the margin the chief of the slaughter-men or executioners, came into Jerusalem in the nineteenth year of Nebuchadnezzar. Soldiers are but slaughter-men, he observes, and God employs them as executioners of his sentence against a sinful people, though Nebuzaradan himself had no eye to God but served Babylon's king and his own designs. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 13. Henry says Nebuzaradan burnt the house of the LORD, that holy and beautiful house where their fathers praised him, and the king's house, probably Solomon's palace, and all the houses of Jerusalem, that is, all the houses of the great men. If any escaped, he adds, it was only some sorry cottages for the poor of the land. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 14. Henry says the Chaldean army broke down all the walls of Jerusalem round about, to be revenged upon them for standing in the way of the army so long. Thus a defenced city was made a ruin, he notes, and when the walls were demolished the pillars of the temple were pulled down too, in token that God, the strength and stay of civil and ecclesiastical government, had departed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 15. Henry says Nebuzaradan carried away many into captivity: certain of the poor of the city, the residue that remained after sword and famine, and the deserters, such as God thought fit, for he had already determined some for pestilence, some for the sword, some for famine and some for captivity. The poor of the country, by contrast, he left behind. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 16. Henry notes that Nebuzaradan left certain of the poor of the land for vinedressers and husbandmen, while the poor of the city were carried away. Only sorry cottages for the poor remained when the great men's houses were burnt. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 17. Henry says nothing is more particularly related than the carrying off of the temple's appurtenances: the pillars of brass, the bases and the brazen sea were broken and their brass carried to Babylon. When the walls were demolished the pillars fell too, he explains, in token that God had departed, for the pillars were not for support but for ornament and significancy, named Jachin, He will establish, and Boaz, In him is strength, so their breaking signified God would no longer establish his house nor be its strength. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 18. Henry says all the vessels that belonged to the brazen altar, the caldrons, shovels, snuffers, bowls, spoons and all the brass vessels of ministry, were taken away. The iniquity of Jerusalem, he observes, like that of Eli's house, was not to be purged by sacrifice or offering. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 19. Henry says that what was of great value, the vessels of silver and gold, had been carried away before, yet some of that sort remained and were now carried away, gold in gold and silver in silver. When the gold was gone the brass soon went after it, he notes, because the people repented not, according to Jeremiah's prediction. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 20. Henry says the brass of the two pillars, the one sea and the twelve brazen bulls under the bases, which Solomon had made, was without weight. So it was in the making, he notes, when the weight of the brass was not found out, and so it was in the destroying, for those who made great spoil of them did not stand to weigh them, as purchasers do, since whatever they weighed, it was all their own. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 21. Henry says the pillars are here very particularly described from 1 Kings 7:15, that their extraordinary beauty and stateliness may affect us the more with the demolishing of them. The eighteen cubits of height, the fillet of twelve cubits and the four fingers of thickness belong to that deliberate, affecting description. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 22. Henry continues the particular description: a chapiter of brass five cubits high, with network and pomegranates round about, all of brass, the second pillar likewise. These pillars were for ornament and significancy, not support, he stresses, and their breaking signified that God would no longer establish his house nor be the strength of it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 23. Henry's point in the minute reckoning, ninety-six pomegranates on a side and a hundred round about upon the network, is the same deliberate description from 1 Kings 7:15: the extraordinary beauty and stateliness of the work should affect us the more with its demolishing. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 24. Henry puts Seraiah the chief priest first among those taken, noting that his sacred character could not exempt him from this stroke, since he himself had profaned it by sin. This Seraiah the priest, he adds, was perhaps not like Seraiah the quiet prince of chapter 51:59, but unquiet and turbulent, making himself obnoxious to Babylon's king. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 25. Henry counts among those taken an eunuch in charge of the men of war, seven men near the king's person, the principal scribe of the host, and sixty men of the people of the land. The account agrees with 2 Kings 25 except that there five, here seven, were near the king, which Lightfoot reconciles by noting that seven were taken but two, Jeremiah himself and Ebed-melech, were discharged, leaving five put to death. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 26. Henry says Nebuzaradan brought them to the king of Babylon at Riblah, as prisoners taken from temple, city, court and country. The leaders of the people had caused them to err, he observes, and now they are in a particular manner made monuments of divine justice. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 27. Henry calls this a very melancholy account of slaughter in cold blood at Riblah, seventy-two in number, of all ranks, probably those most forward to excite and promote the rebellion against Babylon's king. With their deaths, he says, Judah was carried away captive out of his own land, and the land spued them out as it had spued out the Canaanites before them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 28. Henry notes this captivity in the seventh year of Nebuchadnezzar, the same as the eighth year of 2 Kings 24:12, was the captivity of Jeconiah, numbering 3,023 Jews. The sums here are very small compared with the 18,000 of 2 Kings 24:14, 16, he observes, and Lightfoot conjectures that, these accounts being joined to the Riblah slaughter, all here said to be carried away were put to death as rebels. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 29. Henry identifies the captivity of the eighteenth year of Nebuchadnezzar, the same as the nineteenth year of verse 12, with 832 persons carried from Jerusalem. This too belongs to the small sums he discusses, small against what one would expect when all the residue of the people were carried away. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 30. Henry notes a third captivity not mentioned before, in the twenty-third year of Nebuchadnezzar, when Nebuzaradan carried away 745 Jews, four years after Jerusalem's destruction, probably in revenge for Gedaliah's murder, taking aiders and abetters of Ishmael. If 4,600 is the sum total, he marvels how strangely reduced the people were, and how they came to be numerous again, for as in Egypt, so in Babylon, the LORD made them fruitful in affliction, and the more oppressed the more they multiplied. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 31. Henry says Evil-merodach, in the first year of his reign, lifted up the head of Jehoiachin and brought him forth out of prison in the thirty-seventh year of his captivity. New lords make new laws, he observes: Nebuchadnezzar's son, well-affected to the prisoner, could procure him no favour from his father, but once the old man was dead, his son countenanced Jehoiachin. The two days' difference from 2 Kings 25:27, he adds, is slight: the orders were likely given on the twenty-fifth, but Jehoiachin was not presented till the twenty-seventh. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 32. Henry says Evil-merodach spoke kindly to Jehoiachin and set his throne above the thrones of the kings with him in Babylon. Jehoiachin, who fell from a throne into a prison, is advanced again to a throne of state, though not of power, he notes, for such chequer-work is this world, with prosperity and adversity set one against the other. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 33. Henry says Jehoiachin's prison garments were changed, and he ate bread continually before the king all the days of his life. As before the robes were changed into prison-garments, so now they were converted into robes again, he observes, and though the night of affliction was very long, thirty-seven years since he was eighteen, we must not despair that the day may dawn at last. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 34. Henry closes with the continual diet given Jehoiachin every day until his death, and draws the general lesson: God can make his people find favour in the eyes of their oppressors and unaccountably turn their hearts to pity them. Those under oppression, he says, will find it is not in vain to hope and quietly wait for the salvation of the LORD, for no word of God shall fall to the ground. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

  • Verse 24. Henry puts Seraiah the chief priest first among those taken, noting that his sacred character could not exempt him from this stroke, since he himself had profaned it by sin. This Seraiah the priest, he adds, was perhaps not like Seraiah the quiet prince of chapter 51:59, but unquiet and turbulent, making himself obnoxious to Babylon's king. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 25. Henry counts among those taken an eunuch in charge of the men of war, seven men near the king's person, the principal scribe of the host, and sixty men of the people of the land. The account agrees with 2 Kings 25 except that there five, here seven, were near the king, which Lightfoot reconciles by noting that seven were taken but two, Jeremiah himself and Ebed-melech, were discharged, leaving five put to death. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 26. Henry says Nebuzaradan brought them to the king of Babylon at Riblah, as prisoners taken from temple, city, court and country. The leaders of the people had caused them to err, he observes, and now they are in a particular manner made monuments of divine justice. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 27. Henry calls this a very melancholy account of slaughter in cold blood at Riblah, seventy-two in number, of all ranks, probably those most forward to excite and promote the rebellion against Babylon's king. With their deaths, he says, Judah was carried away captive out of his own land, and the land spued them out as it had spued out the Canaanites before them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 28. Henry notes this captivity in the seventh year of Nebuchadnezzar, the same as the eighth year of 2 Kings 24:12, was the captivity of Jeconiah, numbering 3,023 Jews. The sums here are very small compared with the 18,000 of 2 Kings 24:14, 16, he observes, and Lightfoot conjectures that, these accounts being joined to the Riblah slaughter, all here said to be carried away were put to death as rebels. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 29. Henry identifies the captivity of the eighteenth year of Nebuchadnezzar, the same as the nineteenth year of verse 12, with 832 persons carried from Jerusalem. This too belongs to the small sums he discusses, small against what one would expect when all the residue of the people were carried away. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 30. Henry notes a third captivity not mentioned before, in the twenty-third year of Nebuchadnezzar, when Nebuzaradan carried away 745 Jews, four years after Jerusalem's destruction, probably in revenge for Gedaliah's murder, taking aiders and abetters of Ishmael. If 4,600 is the sum total, he marvels how strangely reduced the people were, and how they came to be numerous again, for as in Egypt, so in Babylon, the LORD made them fruitful in affliction, and the more oppressed the more they multiplied. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 31. Henry says Evil-merodach, in the first year of his reign, lifted up the head of Jehoiachin and brought him forth out of prison in the thirty-seventh year of his captivity. New lords make new laws, he observes: Nebuchadnezzar's son, well-affected to the prisoner, could procure him no favour from his father, but once the old man was dead, his son countenanced Jehoiachin. The two days' difference from 2 Kings 25:27, he adds, is slight: the orders were likely given on the twenty-fifth, but Jehoiachin was not presented till the twenty-seventh. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 32. Henry says Evil-merodach spoke kindly to Jehoiachin and set his throne above the thrones of the kings with him in Babylon. Jehoiachin, who fell from a throne into a prison, is advanced again to a throne of state, though not of power, he notes, for such chequer-work is this world, with prosperity and adversity set one against the other. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 33. Henry says Jehoiachin's prison garments were changed, and he ate bread continually before the king all the days of his life. As before the robes were changed into prison-garments, so now they were converted into robes again, he observes, and though the night of affliction was very long, thirty-seven years since he was eighteen, we must not despair that the day may dawn at last. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 34. Henry closes with the continual diet given Jehoiachin every day until his death, and draws the general lesson: God can make his people find favour in the eyes of their oppressors and unaccountably turn their hearts to pity them. Those under oppression, he says, will find it is not in vain to hope and quietly wait for the salvation of the LORD, for no word of God shall fall to the ground. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

Investigation (editorial — a question, not a conclusion)

This section investigates a parallel; it does not conclude a relationship between the books. Interpreted Editorial framing for this section

Parallel. Jeremiah 52 retells the fall of Jerusalem — the siege, the breach, the king's flight and blinding, the burning of the temple, the temple vessels, numbered captivities, and Jehoiachin's release — events also told in 2 Kings 24–25, to which this chapter's own study notes connect verses 21, 23, 28 and 31. A reader of either account will recognise the same sequence of events. Interpreted Chapter text of Jeremiah 52; study notes for Jeremiah 52, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

Differences. Jeremiah 52 stands as the closing chapter of Jeremiah, gathering the fall into one historical summary with its own captivity counts (verses 28–30, including a third captivity the note says is not mentioned before), while 2 Kings tells the same fall as part of a continuous history of the kings. The two accounts do not agree in every detail of number and order, and this page does not harmonise them. Interpreted Chapter text of Jeremiah 52; study note for Jeremiah 52:30, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

Unresolved. Whether one account drew on the other, or both drew on a common source, is not established by the sources consulted for this page, and no direct dependence, shared authorship, or historical certainty is claimed here.
