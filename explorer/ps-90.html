The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Psalms 90 (King James Version (1611) for Psalms 1–150; Charles Thomson, The Septuagint (1808) for Psalm 151)

1 Lord, thou hast been our dwelling place in all generations.

2 Before the mountains were brought forth, or ever thou hadst formed the earth and the world, even from everlasting to everlasting, thou art God.

3 Thou turnest man to destruction; and sayest, Return, ye children of men.

4 For a thousand years in thy sight are but as yesterday when it is past, and as a watch in the night.

5 Thou carriest them away as with a flood; they are as a sleep: in the morning they are like grass which groweth up.

6 In the morning it flourisheth, and groweth up; in the evening it is cut down, and withereth.

7 For we are consumed by thine anger, and by thy wrath are we troubled.

8 Thou hast set our iniquities before thee, our secret sins in the light of thy countenance.

9 For all our days are passed away in thy wrath: we spend our years as a tale that is told.

10 The days of our years are threescore years and ten; and if by reason of strength they be fourscore years, yet is their strength labour and sorrow; for it is soon cut off, and we fly away.

11 Who knoweth the power of thine anger? even according to thy fear, so is thy wrath.

12 So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom.

13 Return, O LORD, how long? and let it repent thee concerning thy servants.

14 O satisfy us early with thy mercy; that we may rejoice and be glad all our days.

15 Make us glad according to the days wherein thou hast afflicted us, and the years wherein we have seen evil.

16 Let thy work appear unto thy servants, and thy glory unto their children.

17 And let the beauty of the LORD our God be upon us: and establish thou the work of our hands upon us; yea, the work of our hands establish thou it.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Against God's everlasting being — dwelling place in all generations, God from everlasting to everlasting, for whom a thousand years are as yesterday — the psalm sets the brevity of human life: carried away as with a flood, like grass flourishing in the morning and cut down by evening, threescore years and ten, or fourscore by strength, yet labour and sorrow. The prayer that follows is to be taught to number our days and apply our hearts to wisdom, to be satisfied early with mercy, made glad according to the days of affliction, and to have the work of our hands established. Interpreted Psalm text above; study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Henry opens with comfort, not history: God himself has been his people's home in every age, so that patriarchs in tents and Israelites in bondage were still at rest in him. Believers are at home in God, and that is their shelter through every toil and tribulation of this world. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 2. Before mountains, earth, or time itself, God already was, and his being has neither beginning nor end nor change. Henry draws the comfort straight from that eternity: our comforts die, but God is an ever-living God to those who have him for theirs. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 3. God's sovereign word turns man back to dust, yet in the same word Henry hears a call to return in repentance, and a promise of return at the resurrection. Death is the general sentence on all, body to earth and soul to God, until God bids the dead arise again. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 4. A thousand years, long beyond any human life, are to God as yesterday past or a single night-watch, because between time and eternity there is no proportion at all. Henry adds that all those years lie present to the Eternal Mind, so delay is no argument against the resurrection. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 5. Human life is a dying life, gliding down time's stream into eternity as if swept by a flood, and a dreaming life, since men asleep to their frailty imagine great things until death wakes them. We begin to die as soon as we are born, and every day carries us nearer the end. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 6. Like grass green in the morning and cut down by evening, man flourishes briefly and then withers, whether age winters him or disease mows him down in summer. Henry's point is the suddenness of the change: death remakes us shortly, perhaps suddenly, and all flesh is as grass. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 7. Israel is taught to own God's anger, not mere nature, as the cause of its miseries: death is a debt to divine justice, since sin entered and death by sin, not a debt nature alone could claim. Afflictions may come from God's love, as Job's did, but rebukes for sin must be traced to his displeasure. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 8. God sets both open sins and secret sins before him in the light of his face, and Henry insists the secret departures of the heart lie at the bottom of the overt acts. Covering sin is folly, because what is hidden from men is already discovered and reckoned by God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 9. Under the wilderness sentence, Israel's years passed away in wrath like a tale told: long in the living, told in a moment, and never to be recalled like a spoken word. Henry applies it generally, warning that we spend our years carelessly, to little purpose, instead of dispatching our business with industry. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 10. Seventy years, or eighty by strength, is the common stint of life since Moses' day, and even that span is labour and sorrow, soon cut off. Henry notes the sting: added strength in old age often only prolongs misery, since the years have come in which there is no pleasure. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 11. No one fully comprehends the power of God's anger, and few seriously consider it as they ought; mockers at sin plainly do not know it. Henry warns that God's wrath equals the gravest apprehensions of the most thoughtful, and what is felt hereafter is infinitely worse than what is feared here. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 12. Numbering our days is an art to be prayed for, not merely calculated: we must live under a constant sense of life's shortness and uncertainty, and compare our work with our remaining days so as to mind it with double diligence. Done rightly, it bends the heart to true wisdom, which is serious godliness in practice. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 13. Though the wilderness decree could not be revoked, Moses still prays for God to turn back toward his servants and show some token of restored favour. Henry reads the prayer as asking how long they must lie under wrath, and notes that God's renewed laws of sacrifice afterward signalled that he had relented toward them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 14. Early mercy is prayed for without any plea of merit: God's tender mercy must come speedily, even in the morning of life, and a soul satisfied with his lovingkindness is abundantly satisfied with that alone. Such mercy is a full fountain of joy, constant even in this vale of tears. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 15. Gladness is asked in measure against affliction, day for day, because God sets the one over against the other. Henry's comfort is that returns of lovingkindness are reckoned a sufficient recompense for trouble: the cup of trembling gives way to the cup of salvation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 16. God's work must appear upon his servants before his glory appears to their children, because servants cannot work for God unless he first works upon and in them. Henry hears a distinction here: reform for the generation under sentence, and the promised performance for the children who would enter Canaan. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 17. The beauty of the Lord is holiness shining in conduct and consolation gladdening the heart, the visible token that God favours his people. And because God's working in us does not discharge our own endeavour, the prayer is repeated earnestly: prosper the work of our hands, and establish us in it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
