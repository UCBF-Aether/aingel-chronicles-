The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — 1 Corinthians 4 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Let a man so account of us, as of the ministers of Christ, and stewards of the mysteries of God.

2 Moreover it is required in stewards, that a man be found faithful.

3 But with me it is a very small thing that I should be judged of you, or of man’s judgment: yea, I judge not mine own self.

4 For I know nothing by myself; yet am I not hereby justified: but he that judgeth me is the Lord.

5 Therefore judge nothing before the time, until the Lord come, who both will bring to light the hidden things of darkness, and will make manifest the counsels of the hearts: and then shall every man have praise of God.

6 And these things, brethren, I have in a figure transferred to myself and to Apollos for your sakes; that ye might learn in us not to think of men above that which is written, that no one of you be puffed up for one against another.

7 For who maketh thee to differ from another? and what hast thou that thou didst not receive? now if thou didst receive it, why dost thou glory, as if thou hadst not received it?

8 Now ye are full, now ye are rich, ye have reigned as kings without us: and I would to God ye did reign, that we also might reign with you.

9 For I think that God hath set forth us the apostles last, as it were appointed to death: for we are made a spectacle unto the world, and to angels, and to men.

10 We are fools for Christ’s sake, but ye are wise in Christ; we are weak, but ye are strong; ye are honourable, but we are despised.

11 Even unto this present hour we both hunger, and thirst, and are naked, and are buffeted, and have no certain dwellingplace;

12 And labour, working with our own hands: being reviled, we bless; being persecuted, we suffer it:

13 Being defamed, we intreat: we are made as the filth of the world, and are the offscouring of all things unto this day.

14 I write not these things to shame you, but as my beloved sons I warn you.

15 For though ye have ten thousand instructers in Christ, yet have ye not many fathers: for in Christ Jesus I have begotten you through the gospel.

16 Wherefore I beseech you, be ye followers of me.

17 For this cause have I sent unto you Timotheus, who is my beloved son, and faithful in the Lord, who shall bring you into remembrance of my ways which be in Christ, as I teach every where in every church.

18 Now some are puffed up, as though I would not come to you.

19 But I will come to you shortly, if the Lord will, and will know, not the speech of them which are puffed up, but the power.

20 For the kingdom of God is not in word, but in power.

21 What will ye? shall I come unto you with a rod, or in love, and in the spirit of meekness?

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Paul asks to be regarded as a minister of Christ and steward of God's mysteries, warns against judging before the Lord comes, and contrasts the Corinthians' self-satisfaction with the apostles' hunger, thirst, nakedness, and reviling. He writes as their father in the gospel, sending Timothy to remind them of his ways, and asks whether he should come with a rod or in love and the spirit of meekness. What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Paul and Apollos are to be regarded as ministers of Christ, not as heads of the church in whom believers glory — headship belongs to Christ alone. They are stewards, not depositories of grace but dispensers of it, proclaiming as heralds the deep truths of God, the mysteries known only by revelation, so far as hearers are disposed to receive them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. What is required in stewards, here on earth as in earthly service, is that a man be found faithful. The difference is that God's stewards do not await man's judgment to test them, but the testing of the day of the Lord — an argument against partial preferences for teachers based on their gifts. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Being judged by man's judgment amounts to a very small matter compared with God's, not because Paul despises their judgment but because man's day is nothing beside the Lord's day. He does not even assume the right of approving himself; the Lord alone is the Decider of his case. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Paul is conscious of no ministerial unfaithfulness, yet that does not justify him, for conscience is not an infallible guide. He does not consider his own conscience decisive; only the Lord's judgment can do that. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Therefore believers must not forestall God's prerogative by judging before the time, until the Lord comes. He will bring hidden things of darkness to light and manifest the counsels of hearts, since present judgments see only outward acts and cannot see motives; then each will receive his due praise, estimated by motives rather than exaggerated praise heaped on favorite teachers. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Paul has transferred to himself and Apollos, as a figure or type, what holds good of all teachers, naming the two whose names were used as party cries while meaning others he spares naming. The lesson is not to go beyond what is written — to revere the silence of Scripture as much as its declarations, and so dogmatize less on what is not expressly revealed — and not to be puffed up for one minister against another. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Who distinguishes one believer above another? Not himself, but God. To glory as if a gift had not been received is to forget that everything is owed to God, not to oneself. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. With irony Paul says they are already filled full, already rich, already seated as kings without him — as if they no longer needed to hunger and thirst after righteousness, and had already reached the kingdom for which Christians must strive and suffer. He truly wishes it were so, that he might reign with them, for their prosperity would redound to him as their father in Christ. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. God has set forth the apostles last, as criminals appointed to death and exhibited as a theatrical spectacle to the world, to angels and to men. Like condemned men in the amphitheater, set forth last to fight wild beasts, they are a gazing-stock — yet to angels they are a spectacle worthy of intense regard, though lowest in the world's judgment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Ironically, the apostles are fools for Christ's sake while the Corinthians are wise in Christ, the apostles weak while they are strong, the apostles despised while they are honorable. The contrast turns on worldly philosophy and rhetoric, which the Corinthians prized and the apostles did not use. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Even to the present hour the apostles hunger and thirst, are insufficiently clad, and are buffeted as slaves — the reverse of the Corinthians reigning as kings. Paul's Master before him was likewise buffeted as a slave before dying a slave's death. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. The apostles labor with their own hands, a fact confirmed by Paul's later words to the Ephesian elders that these hands ministered to his necessities. The undesigned agreement between letter and history is incompatible with forgery. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Being defamed, the apostles entreat — praying for their defamers as Christ enjoined, and replying gently. They have become as the refuse and sweepings of the world, the rubbish thrown out after a cleaning, and not of the world only but of all things. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Paul writes not to shame them but to admonish them as beloved sons, as a father admonishes without provoking wrath. They might well be ashamed at the disparity between their father and themselves, his spiritual children. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Though they have ten thousand instructors — more than was desirable — they have not many fathers, for Paul begot them spiritually in Christ Jesus. The instructors were evangelical teachers, but tutors only, without a father's rights and peculiar affection; and since Paul spiritually regenerated them while baptizing almost none of them, regeneration cannot be inseparably in and by baptism. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Paul urges them to be imitators of him in his ways which are in Christ, not in his crosses and sufferings. Imitation means following his walk and teaching, not seeking his afflictions. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. For this reason Paul sent Timothy, his son in the faith, beloved and faithful in the Lord, best suited from his spiritual connection with Paul to remind them of the apostle's walk and teaching, which they had in some respects forgotten. What Paul taught everywhere in every church must be necessary at Corinth also. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. Some were puffed up as though Paul would not come himself, misconstruing his sending of Timothy. A puffed-up spirit was the besetting sin of Corinth, and Paul guards against the misreading in advance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. Paul emphatically says he will come shortly, after Pentecost, if the Lord will — a wise proviso. He cares nothing for the high-sounding speech of the puffed up, but will take cognizance of their power, whether they are really powerful in the Spirit or not. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. The kingdom of God is not in word but in power. Not empty speeches but the manifest power of the Spirit attests the reign of the gospel, in a church or in an individual. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. Paul leaves the manner of his coming to them: shall he come with a rod of displeasure, or in love and the spirit of meekness? The choice is theirs, by their repentance or refusal. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Unfamiliar terms

Plain-language glosses for words and phrases in this chapter that a modern reader may find unfamiliar. They are editorial aids, not Scripture.

  • stewards of the mysteries. Household managers entrusted with God's revealed secrets; Paul's description of apostles. Interpreted Chapter text and study notes for this chapter
  • spectacle. A public show; Paul says the apostles are made a spectacle to the world, angels, and men. Interpreted Chapter text and study notes for this chapter
  • rod. The discipline Paul says he could bring on his visit, contrasted with coming in love and meekness. Interpreted Chapter text and study notes for this chapter
