The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Psalms 120 (King James Version (1611) for Psalms 1–150; Charles Thomson, The Septuagint (1808) for Psalm 151)

1 In my distress I cried unto the LORD, and he heard me.

2 Deliver my soul, O LORD, from lying lips, and from a deceitful tongue.

3 What shall be given unto thee? or what shall be done unto thee, thou false tongue?

4 Sharp arrows of the mighty, with coals of juniper.

5 Woe is me, that I sojourn in Mesech, that I dwell in the tents of Kedar!

6 My soul hath long dwelt with him that hateth peace.

7 I am for peace: but when I speak, they are for war.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

A short cry in distress: the LORD heard when the speaker cried, and the prayer is for deliverance from lying lips and a deceitful tongue — sharp arrows of the mighty with coals of juniper are what the false tongue deserves. The speaker laments sojourning in Meshech and dwelling in the tents of Kedar, long dwelling with one who hates peace: he is for peace, but when he speaks, they are for war. Interpreted Psalm text above; study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Henry records deliverance by prayer from lying lips: distressed by flatterers smiling and kissing while aiming to smite under the fifth rib, and by forged accusations blackening names and endangering lives — the lot of innocent and excellent, David typing Christ — he cried to God who holds hearts and can bridle tongues, and was heard, enemies baffled at last. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 2. Henry prays deliverance of soul from lying lips and deceitful tongues: having prayed earnestly to be kept from lying himself and hating it heartily, he may with more confidence pray to be kept from being belied by others and from its ill consequences, appealing to him with power over bad men's consciences. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 3. Henry addresses the false tongue itself for conscience-awakening: consider what shall be given thee by the righteous Judge — surely sinners durst not act so if they knew the end thereof, for God will preserve his people from this mischievous generation and reckon with enemies. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 4. Henry threatens sharp arrows of the Almighty and juniper coals: wrath flying swiftly like arrows, striking ere aware, piercing strongest armour and hardest heart, with vehement heat keeping fire very long even when seeming out — the portion of the false tongue, for all loving and making a lie share the lake burning eternally. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 5. Henry laments ill neighbourhood in Mesech and Kedar figures: not literally dwelling so far off, but among rude, barbarous, litigious people like those shepherds — forced from God's ordinances, sojourning prolonged from his resting-place, a good man never at home banished from means of grace and communion. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 6. Henry counts long dwelling with peace-haters a grievous burden: cast and kept among those he hopes to be for ever separated from, like Lot in Sodom, vexatious and quarrelsome neighbours making the closet psalm's complaint — yet he will not love peace less for seeking it in vain. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 7. Henry ends with the climax ascent, I am for peace: though he speaks peace, they are for war, and for his love they are adversaries, as Christ's enemies stoned him for good words and works — so we must not think it strange, nor be overcome of evil, but still try to overcome evil with good. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
