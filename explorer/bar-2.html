The Text — Baruch 2 (King James Version, Apocrypha; public-domain text as received)

1 Therefore the Lord hath made good his word, which he pronounced against us, and against our judges that judged Israel, and against our kings, and against our princes, and against the men of Israel and Juda,

2 To bring upon us great plagues, such as never happened under the whole heaven, as it came to pass in Jerusalem, according to the things that were written in the law of Moses;

3 That a man should eat the flesh of his own son, and the flesh of his own daughter.

4 Moreover he hath delivered them to be in subjection to all the kingdoms that are round about us, to be as a reproach and desolation among all the people round about, where the Lord hath scattered them.

5 Thus we were cast down, and not exalted, because we have sinned against the Lord our God, and have not been obedient unto his voice.

6 To the Lord our God appertaineth righteousness: but unto us and to our fathers open shame, as appeareth this day.

7 For all these plagues are come upon us, which the Lord hath pronounced against us

8 Yet have we not prayed before the Lord, that we might turn every one from the imaginations of his wicked heart.

9 Wherefore the Lord watched over us for evil, and the Lord hath brought it upon us: for the Lord is righteous in all his works which he hath commanded us.

10 Yet we have not hearkened unto his voice, to walk in the commandments of the Lord, that he hath set before us.

11 And now, O Lord God of Israel, that hast brought thy people out of the land of Egypt with a mighty hand, and high arm, and with signs, and with wonders, and with great power, and hast gotten thyself a name, as appeareth this day:

12 O Lord our God, we have sinned, we have done ungodly, we have dealt unrighteously in all thine ordinances.

13 Let thy wrath turn from us: for we are but a few left among the heathen, where thou hast scattered us.

14 Hear our prayers, O Lord, and our petitions, and deliver us for thine own sake, and give us favour in the sight of them which have led us away:

15 That all the earth may know that thou art the Lord our God, because Israel and his posterity is called by thy name.

16 O Lord, look down from thine holy house, and consider us: bow down thine ear, O Lord, to hear us.

17 Open thine eyes, and behold; for the dead that are in the graves, whose souls are taken from their bodies, will give unto the Lord neither praise nor righteousness:

18 But the soul that is greatly vexed, which goeth stooping and feeble, and the eyes that fail, and the hungry soul, will give thee praise and righteousness, O Lord.

19 Therefore we do not make our humble supplication before thee, O Lord our God, for the righteousness of our fathers, and of our kings.

20 For thou hast sent out thy wrath and indignation upon us, as thou hast spoken by thy servants the prophets, saying,

21 Thus saith the Lord, Bow down your shoulders to serve the king of Babylon: so shall ye remain in the land that I gave unto your fathers.

22 But if ye will not hear the voice of the Lord, to serve the king of Babylon,

23 I will cause to cease out of the cites of Judah, and from without Jerusalem, the voice of mirth, and the voice of joy, the voice of the bridegroom, and the voice of the bride: and the whole land shall be desolate of inhabitants.

24 But we would not hearken unto thy voice, to serve the king of Babylon: therefore hast thou made good the words that thou spakest by thy servants the prophets, namely, that the bones of our kings, and the bones of our fathers, should be taken out of their place.

25 And, lo, they are cast out to the heat of the day, and to the frost of the night, and they died in great miseries by famine, by sword, and by pestilence.

26 And the house which is called by thy name hast thou laid waste, as it is to be seen this day, for the wickedness of the house of Israel and the house of Juda.

27 O Lord our God, thou hast dealt with us after all thy goodness, and according to all that great mercy of thine,

28 As thou spakest by thy servant Moses in the day when thou didst command him to write the law before the children of Israel, saying,

29 If ye will not hear my voice, surely this very great multitude shall be turned into a small number among the nations, where I will scatter them.

30 For I knew that they would not hear me, because it is a stiffnecked people: but in the land of their captivities they shall remember themselves.

31 And shall know that I am the Lord their God: for I will give them an heart, and ears to hear:

32 And they shall praise me in the land of their captivity, and think upon my name,

33 And return from their stiff neck, and from their wicked deeds: for they shall remember the way of their fathers, which sinned before the Lord.

34 And I will bring them again into the land which I promised with an oath unto their fathers, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and they shall be lords of it: and I will increase them, and they shall not be diminished.

35 And I will make an everlasting covenant with them to be their God, and they shall be my people: and I will no more drive my people of Israel out of the land that I have given them.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

The confession continues and widens: the Lord's word against judges, kings, princes and fathers has been made good, the people have been scattered and brought low, and the prayer turns to God's mercies and to the promise that if they return, an everlasting covenant will be made with them to be their God and they his people. Interpreted Chapter text and study notes for this chapter

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 2. Heaven is invoked because after the death of Josias the nation was exposed to the greatest misery, its kings imprisoned first by the Egyptians and then by the Chaldeans, with surprising changes in eight or nine years. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 3. The daughter eaten is not mentioned elsewhere in sacred history for this time, except in the siege of Samaria, though captives of Israel now at Babylon under the same king may have suffered it, and all distinction of the kingdoms is now at an end, while the prayer greatly resembles that of Daniel. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 4. The kings are those of Egypt and Babylon, while the Ammonites and their neighbours derided the scattered people, so the reproach comes from every side. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 5. Brought under, and not uppermost, is a proverbial expression from Deuteronomy, describing a people made the tail and not the head because they sinned by not obeying God's voice. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 9. The Lord watched over us for evil, that is, watched to punish, for when he spares he seems to slumber, so the watching here is a watching for judgment, not for protection. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 12. God's justices are so called because God's law is frequently called justice, since its observance makes men just, so the confession of sin against his justices is a confession against the law that justifies. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 16. The holy house is the temple, or the prayer is heard from heaven, so looking down from the holy house means either the temple on earth or the dwelling above. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 17. The dead in hell shall not give glory and justice to the Lord, that is, they shall not acknowledge and glorify his justice as penitent sinners do upon earth, and all in the grave are incapable of making known God's perfections, which is why the prayer appeals to his glory. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 18. The phrase of evil is not in the Greek, and a soul which bears grandeur with pain, like Esther, and humbles itself before God, is most graciously received, while failing by fasting marks the dispositions required for prayer. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 19. The fathers are joined in the Greek by kings, and prayers are rendered mercy, for the exiles boast not of good works, trusting not in the just works of their fathers or of themselves to receive pity, since mercy here denotes prayer or humiliation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 21. To serve the king of Babylon is as Jeremias repeatedly admonished, so the command to bow the shoulder and serve is the prophet's own counsel, not a new doctrine. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 23. Without any footstep of inhabitants means the land shall become a desert, as Isaias and Lamentations describe, with no voice of mirth or of bridegroom or bride left in it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 24. The place is explained by the soldiers ransacking the tombs in hopes of plunder, and Joakim was left unburied, which would be very afflicting to the Jews, since the bones of kings and fathers were removed from their resting places. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 25. Banishment is rendered captivity in the Syriac, or pestilence inflicted by God, so the deaths by famine, sword and banishment cover every form of the judgment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 26. The day refers to the temple whose vessels were taken away, with few left to attend, and the prophet foresees the event, so the desolation of the temple is spoken of as already present. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 30. The heart means to be seriously converted, as the prophets had foretold, so turning to the heart in captivity is conversion itself, not merely a change of place. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 33. The neck is literally the back, insensible to stripes, so the stiff neck is a back that no longer feels the blows that should have corrected it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 34. Thereof is literally of them, though the other reading is more conformable to the Greek, a textual point that does not change the promise of return to the land sworn to the fathers. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 35. Another covenant is in the Greek an everlasting covenant, by Christ, prefigured by the covenant Nehemias renewed, for the law of Moses was in force till Christ came, and his law continues unto the end. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock

Investigation (editorial — a question, not a conclusion)

This section investigates a parallel; it does not conclude a relationship between the books. Interpreted Editorial framing for this section

Parallel. The confession in Baruch 1–2 — a people scattered for sin, acknowledging that the Lord's word against them has been made good, and pleading God's mercies — greatly resembles, as Haydock's note for Baruch 2:3 itself says, the prayer of Daniel. Both are communal confessions spoken in exile, owning guilt rather than excusing it. Interpreted Study note for Baruch 2:3, summarizing George Leo Haydock; chapter text of Baruch 2

Differences. Baruch's confession is sent from Babylon with money for offerings in Jerusalem and is framed as a book to be read there, and Haydock's note claims resemblance, not identity: the wording and the petitions that follow are Baruch's own, and this page does not treat the resemblance as quotation. Interpreted Chapter text of Baruch 1–2; study note for Baruch 2:3, summarizing George Leo Haydock

Unresolved. Whether Baruch's prayer drew directly on Daniel's, or both drew on a shared penitential tradition, is not established by the sources consulted for this page, and no direct dependence, shared authorship, or historical certainty is claimed here.
