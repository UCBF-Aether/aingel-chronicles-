The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 42 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Then Job answered the LORD, and said,

2 I know that thou canst do every thing, and that no thought can be withholden from thee.

3 Who is he that hideth counsel without knowledge? therefore have I uttered that I understood not; things too wonderful for me, which I knew not.

4 Hear, I beseech thee, and I will speak: I will demand of thee, and declare thou unto me.

5 I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee.

6 Wherefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes.

7 And it was so, that after the LORD had spoken these words unto Job, the LORD said to Eliphaz the Temanite, My wrath is kindled against thee, and against thy two friends: for ye have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job hath.

8 Therefore take unto you now seven bullocks and seven rams, and go to my servant Job, and offer up for yourselves a burnt offering; and my servant Job shall pray for you: for him will I accept: lest I deal with you after your folly, in that ye have not spoken of me the thing which is right, like my servant Job.

9 So Eliphaz the Temanite and Bildad the Shuhite and Zophar the Naamathite went, and did according as the LORD commanded them: the LORD also accepted Job.

10 And the LORD turned the captivity of Job, when he prayed for his friends: also the LORD gave Job twice as much as he had before.

11 Then came there unto him all his brethren, and all his sisters, and all they that had been of his acquaintance before, and did eat bread with him in his house: and they bemoaned him, and comforted him over all the evil that the LORD had brought upon him: every man also gave him a piece of money, and every one an earring of gold.

12 So the LORD blessed the latter end of Job more than his beginning: for he had fourteen thousand sheep, and six thousand camels, and a thousand yoke of oxen, and a thousand she asses.

13 He had also seven sons and three daughters.

14 And he called the name of the first, Jemima; and the name of the second, Kezia; and the name of the third, Kerenhappuch.

15 And in all the land were no women found so fair as the daughters of Job: and their father gave them inheritance among their brethren.

16 After this lived Job an hundred and forty years, and saw his sons, and his sons’ sons, even four generations.

17 So Job died, being old and full of days.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Job answers the LORD at last: I know that thou canst do every thing, and that no thought can be withheld from thee; I have uttered that I understood not, things too wonderful for me — wherefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes. God then rebukes Eliphaz and the two friends, for they have not spoken of him the thing that is right as his servant Job has, and accepts Job’s prayer for them. The LORD gives Job twice as much as he had before, with seven sons and three daughters, and Job dies old and full of days. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Then Job answered the LORD, and said. JFB heads this chapter as Job's penitent reply, the answer to God's second address. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. I know that thou canst do every thing, and that no thought can be withheld from thee. JFB explains that in the first clause Job owns God to be omnipotent over nature, as contrasted with his own feebleness, and in the second that God is supremely just in all his dealings, with thought as purpose: while to Job's finite view God's plans seem bad, to the All-wise One they continue unhindered and will at last be seen to be as good as they are infinitely wise. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Who is he that hides counsel without knowledge? Therefore have I uttered that I understood not, things too wonderful for me, which I knew not. JFB notes Job in God's own words expresses his deep and humble penitence: God's word concerning our guilt should form the groundwork of confession, and Job in omitting by words goes even further than God's accusation, confessing not merely his words but his whole thoughts and ways were without knowledge, with too wonderful as his rash denial that God has any fixed plan in governing human affairs merely because that plan was too wonderful for his comprehension. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Hear, I beseech thee, and I will speak: I will demand of thee, and declare thou unto me. JFB notes that when Job said hear, his demand convicted him of being without knowledge: God alone could speak thus to Job, not Job to God, so Job quotes again God's words as the groundwork of retracting his own foolish words. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear, but now mine eye sees thee. JFB explains hearing and seeing as often in antithesis, and seeth not as God's face but his presence in the veil of a dark cloud: Job implies that besides this literal seeing he now saw spiritually what he had indistinctly taken on hearsay before, concerning God's infinite wisdom. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Wherefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes. JFB renders myself as I abhor, and retract the rash speeches made against God, marking the end of the poetical dialogue and the beginning of the prose epilogue. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. And it was so, that after the LORD had spoken these words unto Job, the LORD said to Eliphaz the Temanite, my wrath is kindled against thee, and against thy two friends: for ye have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job has. JFB explains to Eliphaz because he was the foremost of the three friends, their speeches being but the echo of his, and right literally as well-grounded, sure and true: their spirit towards Job was unkindly, and to justify themselves they used false arguments, namely that calamities always prove peculiar guilt, so though it was for God they spoke falsely, God reproves them; Job had spoken rightly in relation to them, denying their theory and the alleged fact of peculiar guilt and hypocrisy, but wrongly in relation to God, and that extreme he has now repented of, so God speaks of him as now altogether right. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Therefore take unto you now seven bullocks and seven rams, and go to my servant Job, and offer up for yourselves a burnt offering, and my servant Job shall pray for you: for him will I accept, lest I deal with you after your folly, in that ye have not spoken of me the thing which is right, like my servant Job. JFB notes seven as the number offered by the Gentile prophet, and that Job plainly lived before the legal priesthood, with patriarchs acting as priests for their families and sometimes as praying mediators, foreshadowing the true Mediator, though sacrifice accompanies and is the groundwork on which mediation rests, with him as his person only, which must first be accepted before God can accept his offering, and folly as impiety. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. So Eliphaz the Temanite and Bildad the Shuhite and Zophar the Naamathite went, and did according as the LORD commanded them: the LORD also accepted Job. JFB notes the forgiving spirit of Job foreshadows the love of Christ and of Christians to enemies. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. And the LORD turned the captivity of Job, when he prayed for his friends: also the LORD gave Job twice as much as he had before. JFB renders turned captivity as proverbial for restored, or amply indemnified for all he had lost, so that the future vindication of man against Satan at the resurrection has its earnest in the temporal vindication of Job by Jehovah in person, and twice as the glorious recompense following intercession for enemies. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Then came there unto him all his brethren, and all his sisters, and all they that had been of his acquaintance before, and did eat bread with him in his house, and they bemoaned him, and comforted him over all the evil that the LORD had brought upon him: every man also gave him a piece of money, and every one an earring of gold. JFB notes it was Job's complaint in his misery that his brethren were estranged from him, and these now return with the return of his prosperity, with swallow friends leaving in winter and returning with spring, eat bread in token of friendship, piece of money as presents usual in visiting a man of rank after a calamity, the Hebrew kesita, weighed out not coined, likely equal to four shekels, and earring whether for nose or ear, much gold in the East being in the shape of ornaments in the absence of banks. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. So the LORD blessed the latter end of Job more than his beginning: for he had fourteen thousand sheep, and six thousand camels, and a thousand yoke of oxen, and a thousand she asses. JFB notes the blessing came probably by degrees, not all at once. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. He had also seven sons and three daughters. JFB notes the same number as before, perhaps by a second wife, his first wife being last mentioned in chapter 19. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. And he called the name of the first, Jemima, and the name of the second, Kezia, and the name of the third, Keren-happuch. JFB explains the names as significant of restored prosperity: Jemima as daylight, after his night of calamity, or a dove, Kezia as cassia, an aromatic herb, instead of his offensive breath and ulcers, and Keren-happuch as horn of stibium, a paint with which females dyed their eyelids, in contrast to his horn defiled in the dust, the names also implying the beauty of his daughters. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. And in all the land were no women found so fair as the daughters of Job: and their father gave them inheritance among their brethren. JFB notes inheritance among brethren as an unusual favour in the East to daughters, who in the Jewish law only inherited if there were no sons, a proof of wealth and unanimity. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. After this lived Job a hundred and forty years, and saw his sons, and his sons' sons, even four generations. JFB notes the Septuagint makes Job live a hundred seventy years after his calamity and two hundred forty in all, which would make him seventy at the time of his calamity, and that sons' sons are a proof of divine favour. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. So Job died, being old and full of days. JFB renders full of days as fully sated and contented with all the happiness that life could give him, realizing what Eliphaz had painted as the lot of the godly, and notes the Septuagint adds that he will rise again with those whom the Lord will raise up, perhaps derived spuriously from Matthew 27. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Where the speakers disagree

The book’s own verdict resolves the long dispute: the LORD tells Eliphaz that his wrath is kindled against him and the two friends, because they have not spoken of him the thing that is right, as his servant Job has. Job, who demanded a hearing, ends by repenting in dust and ashes for uttering what he did not understand — and it is Job, not his accusers, who prays for his friends and is accepted. The friends’ rule is thus rejected by God himself, while Job’s demand is answered by a vision of God rather than by an acquittal in court.
