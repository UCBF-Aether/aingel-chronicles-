The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Wisdom of Solomon 13 (King James Version, Apocrypha; public-domain text as received)

1 Surely vain are all men by nature, who are ignorant of God, and could not out of the good things that are seen know him that is: neither by considering the works did they acknowledge the workmaster;

2 But deemed either fire, or wind, or the swift air, or the circle of the stars, or the violent water, or the lights of heaven, to be the gods which govern the world.

3 With whose beauty if they being delighted took them to be gods; let them know how much better the Lord of them is: for the first author of beauty hath created them.

4 But if they were astonished at their power and virtue, let them understand by them, how much mightier he is that made them.

5 For by the greatness and beauty of the creatures proportionably the maker of them is seen.

6 But yet for this they are the less to be blamed: for they peradventure err, seeking God, and desirous to find him.

7 For being conversant in his works they search him diligently, and believe their sight: because the things are beautiful that are seen.

8 Howbeit neither are they to be pardoned.

9 For if they were able to know so much, that they could aim at the world; how did they not sooner find out the Lord thereof?

10 But miserable are they, and in dead things is their hope, who call them gods, which are the works of men's hands, gold and silver, to shew art in, and resemblances of beasts, or a stone good for nothing, the work of an ancient hand.

11 Now a carpenter that felleth timber, after he hath sawn down a tree meet for the purpose, and taken off all the bark skilfully round about, and hath wrought it handsomely, and made a vessel thereof fit for the service of man's life;

12 And after spending the refuse of his work to dress his meat, hath filled himself;

13 And taking the very refuse among those which served to no use, being a crooked piece of wood, and full of knots, hath carved it diligently, when he had nothing else to do, and formed it by the skill of his understanding, and fashioned it to the image of a man;

14 Or made it like some vile beast, laying it over with vermilion, and with paint colouring it red, and covering every spot therein;

15 And when he had made a convenient room for it, set it in a wall, and made it fast with iron:

16 For he provided for it that it might not fall, knowing that it was unable to help itself; for it is an image, and hath need of help:

17 Then maketh he prayer for his goods, for his wife and children, and is not ashamed to speak to that which hath no life.

18 For health he calleth upon that which is weak: for life prayeth to that which is dead; for aid humbly beseecheth that which hath least means to help: and for a good journey he asketh of that which cannot set a foot forward:

19 And for gaining and getting, and for good success of his hands, asketh ability to do of him, that is most unable to do any thing.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

People ignorant of God are vain when they take fire, wind, air, stars, water or crafted things for gods, though those who seek God through the beauty of his works are less to be blamed, yet not pardoned. The folly sharpens with the carpenter who uses a tree's refuse for cooking and then carves the leftover crooked piece into a god, fastens it lest it fall, and prays to what is weak for health and to what is dead for life. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing George Leo Haydock

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Surely vain are all men by nature who are ignorant of God, and could not out of the good things seen know him who is, nor by considering the works acknowledge the workmaster. Haydock's opening is that nature's good things should lead to the Maker, and failure to follow them is vanity. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 2. But they deemed fire, or wind, or swift air, or the circle of stars, or violent water, or lights of heaven, to be the gods governing the world. Haydock lists the elements mistaken for gods. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 3. With whose beauty, if being delighted they took them for gods, let them know how much better the Lord of them is, for the first author of beauty created them. Haydock's argument is from beauty: the author of beauty exceeds the beautiful thing. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 4. If they were astonished at their power and virtue, let them understand by them how much mightier he is who made them. Haydock argues from power likewise: the Maker's power exceeds the made thing's. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 5. For by the greatness and beauty of creatures, proportionably, the Maker of them is seen. Haydock states the proportion: greatness and beauty in creatures reveal the Maker proportionably. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 6. But yet for this they are the less to be blamed, for they perhaps err seeking God and desiring to find him. Haydock notes a mitigation: those who err while seeking God are less blameworthy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 7. For being conversant in his works, they search him diligently, and believe their sight, because the things seen are beautiful. Haydock observes they search diligently among works, and are misled by beauty itself. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 8. Howbeit neither are they to be pardoned. Haydock's qualification is sharp: mitigation is not pardon. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 9. For if they were able to know so much that they could aim at the world, how did they not sooner find out the Lord thereof? Haydock's question is that knowledge enough to aim at the world should have found its Lord sooner. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 10. But miserable are they, and in dead things is their hope, who called them gods which are the works of men's hands, gold and silver, to show art in, and resemblances of beasts, or a stone good for nothing, the work of an ancient hand. Haydock contrasts the seekers with idol-makers, whose hope is in dead things made by hands. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 11. Now a carpenter that fells timber, saws out a tree, and with skill planes it, uses all its offal for preparation of food, and then takes a crooked piece of wood good for nothing, carves it diligently, and shapes it by skill. Haydock begins the carpenter's story: the same tree feeds him and furnishes his god. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 12. The carpenter's offal he uses for food, and a useless crooked piece he carves into an image. Haydock notes the division: one part for use, one part for worship, from the same wood. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 13. He takes pains with carving, shapes it with skill, and makes it like the image of a man, or a vile beast. Haydock observes the skill spent on making a man-like or beast-like image. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 14. He colours it with vermilion and paints its face, covering every spot in it. Haydock notes the painting: vermilion and cosmetics hide the wood's spots. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 15. He makes a convenient room for it, sets it in a wall, and fastens it with iron, that it cannot fall. Haydock pictures the god fastened with iron, lest it fall. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 16. He provides for it lest it fall, knowing it cannot help itself, for it is an image needing help. Haydock's irony is that the god needs provision against falling. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 17. Then he prays to it for his goods, his wife, and his children, and is not ashamed to speak to that which has no life. Haydock notes the shame: prayer for family goods addressed to a lifeless thing. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 18. For health he calls upon what is weak, for life prays to what is dead, for aid humbly beseeches what has no experience, and for a good journey asks what cannot use its feet. Haydock's catalogue of absurd prayers is the chapter's centre: each request is addressed to a powerless thing. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 19. For gaining and getting, and for good success of his hands, he asks ability of him who is most unable to do anything. Haydock closes the catalogue with the same irony: success of hands is asked of what has no hands to help. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
