The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 9 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Then Job answered and said,

2 I know it is so of a truth: but how should man be just with God?

3 If he will contend with him, he cannot answer him one of a thousand.

4 He is wise in heart, and mighty in strength: who hath hardened himself against him, and hath prospered?

5 Which removeth the mountains, and they know not: which overturneth them in his anger.

6 Which shaketh the earth out of her place, and the pillars thereof tremble.

7 Which commandeth the sun, and it riseth not; and sealeth up the stars.

8 Which alone spreadeth out the heavens, and treadeth upon the waves of the sea.

9 Which maketh Arcturus, Orion, and Pleiades, and the chambers of the south.

10 Which doeth great things past finding out; yea, and wonders without number.

11 Lo, he goeth by me, and I see him not: he passeth on also, but I perceive him not.

12 Behold, he taketh away, who can hinder him? who will say unto him, What doest thou?

13 If God will not withdraw his anger, the proud helpers do stoop under him.

14 How much less shall I answer him, and choose out my words to reason with him?

15 Whom, though I were righteous, yet would I not answer, but I would make supplication to my judge.

16 If I had called, and he had answered me; yet would I not believe that he had hearkened unto my voice.

17 For he breaketh me with a tempest, and multiplieth my wounds without cause.

18 He will not suffer me to take my breath, but filleth me with bitterness.

19 If I speak of strength, lo, he is strong: and if of judgment, who shall set me a time to plead?

20 If I justify myself, mine own mouth shall condemn me: if I say, I am perfect, it shall also prove me perverse.

21 Though I were perfect, yet would I not know my soul: I would despise my life.

22 This is one thing, therefore I said it, He destroyeth the perfect and the wicked.

23 If the scourge slay suddenly, he will laugh at the trial of the innocent.

24 The earth is given into the hand of the wicked: he covereth the faces of the judges thereof; if not, where, and who is he?

25 Now my days are swifter than a post: they flee away, they see no good.

26 They are passed away as the swift ships: as the eagle that hasteth to the prey.

27 If I say, I will forget my complaint, I will leave off my heaviness, and comfort myself:

28 I am afraid of all my sorrows, I know that thou wilt not hold me innocent.

29 If I be wicked, why then labour I in vain?

30 If I wash myself with snow water, and make my hands never so clean;

31 Yet shalt thou plunge me in the ditch, and mine own clothes shall abhor me.

32 For he is not a man, as I am, that I should answer him, and we should come together in judgment.

33 Neither is there any daysman betwixt us, that might lay his hand upon us both.

34 Let him take his rod away from me, and let not his fear terrify me:

35 Then would I speak, and not fear him; but it is not so with me.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Job concedes Bildad’s doctrine — God does not pervert justice — and presses the question it leaves: how can a man be just with God? God removes mountains, shakes the earth, spreads the heavens, and makes the stars, and no man can answer him one of a thousand. His central charge is that God destroys the perfect and the wicked alike, and that there is no daysman, no mediator, to lay a hand on them both and make a fair trial possible. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Job answers Bildad, conceding Bildad's doctrine yet pressing his own question: how can a man be just with God? The reply accepts that God does not pervert justice, and asks how a mere man could assert his right before him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Job knows it is true that God does not pervert justice, but asks how man should be just with God. JFB notes the Gospel answers the question Job can only pose. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. If God will contend with man, man cannot answer him one of a thousand. JFB explains contend as deigning to enter into judgment, and that man would not dare utter one of a thousand ready answers, from awe of God's majesty. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. God is wise in heart and mighty in strength; who has hardened himself against him and prospered? JFB notes God confounds the ablest arguer by wisdom and the mightiest by power, and that to prosper one must fall in with God's arrangements. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. God removes mountains and they know it not, overturning them in his anger. JFB reads know not as suddenly, unexpectedly, before they are aware. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. God shakes the earth out of its place, and its pillars tremble. JFB gives the poetic image of the earth resting on pillars that tremble in an earthquake. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. God commands the sun and it rises not, and seals up the stars. JFB explains this by eclipse and earthquake darkness, and sealing as totally covering a room so its contents cannot be seen. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. God alone spreads out the heavens and treads on the waves of the sea. JFB stresses God's governing power over nature: he bows the heavens alone and walks majestically over the mountain waves as a conqueror taming their violence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. God makes Arcturus, Orion, and the Pleiades, and the chambers of the south. JFB explains Arcturus as the great bear that never sets, Orion as the chained fool-hero, the Pleiades as the heap or knot of stars, and the chambers as the unseen southern stars. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. God does great things past finding out, and wonders without number. JFB notes the line is repeated from Eliphaz, now turned to Job's argument about God's unsearchable power. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. God goes by Job and Job sees him not; he passes on and Job perceives him not. JFB gives the image of a howling wind bursting invisibly: God is felt in the awful effects of his wrath but is not seen. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. If God takes away, who can hinder him, or who will say what are you doing? JFB notes God only takes his own, as an absolute king whom a mortal cannot call to account. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. If God will not withdraw his anger, the proud helpers stoop under him. JFB explains the proud helpers as arrogant would-be allies of one contending with the Almighty, who avail nothing against him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. How much less shall Job answer God and choose out words to reason with him? JFB notes the weak must stoop where the mighty stoop, and choosing words means using a well-chosen speech to reason. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Though Job were righteous, he would not answer, but would make supplication to his judge. JFB notes that even conscious of no sin, he would not dare say so, but would leave justification to God's judgment and mercy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. If Job called and God answered, he would not believe God had hearkened to his voice. The next verse explains why: God breaks him with a tempest, like a tree stripped of its leaves. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. God breaks Job with a tempest and multiplies his wounds without cause. JFB links this to the preceding verse: the tempest-breaking explains Job's unbelief that God had heard him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. God will not suffer Job to take his breath, but fills him with bitterness. The complaint is of unrelieved affliction, breath itself denied between blows. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. If Job speaks of strength, God is strong; if of judgment, who shall set a time to plead? JFB notes the sense is the same whether the words are God's or Job's: lo expresses God's swift readiness for battle when challenged. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. If Job justifies himself, his own mouth shall condemn him; if he says he is perfect, it shall prove him perverse. JFB notes the condemning it may be his mouth or God himself. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. Though Job were perfect, he would not know his soul and would despise his life. JFB renders it literally: I perfect, I should not know my soul, I would disown my life, compelled in contending with infinite God to ignore his own innocence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. This is one thing: God destroys the perfect and the wicked alike. JFB calls this Job's central point against his friends: the righteous and wicked are alike afflicted, and great suffering here does not prove great guilt. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. If the scourge slays suddenly, God laughs at the trial of the innocent. JFB clarifies: while the scourge slays the wicked suddenly, God disregards, not derides, the gradual pining away of the innocent. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. The earth is given into the hand of the wicked, and he covers the faces of its judges. JFB contrasts the wicked oppressor who holds the earth with righteous judges led to execution, their faces covered as culprits were. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. Job's days are swifter than a post, fleeing away and seeing no good. JFB explains the post as a fleet courier in the Persian empire, carrying royal commands on dromedaries or on foot, not a slow caravan. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. Job's days pass as swift ships, as the eagle hastening to its prey. JFB explains the swift ships as light reed canoes or papyrus skiffs on the Nile, swift from their lightness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. If Job says he will forget his complaint, leave off his heaviness, and comfort himself, still he fears all his sorrows will return. JFB gives the apodosis: he is afraid God will not hold him innocent even if his sufferings are removed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. Job is afraid of all his sorrows, knowing God will not hold him innocent. The fear is not of pain alone but of a verdict never cleared. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. If Job is wicked, why does he labour in vain? JFB notes Job submits not so much because convinced God is right as because God is powerful and he is weak. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. If Job washes himself with snow water and cleanses his hands, God would still plunge him in the ditch and his own clothes would abhor him. JFB notes snow water was thought more cleansing for its whiteness, and the cleansing agent is lye, alkali used as soap. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 31. Yet God would plunge Job in the ditch, and his own clothes would abhor him. Even the whitest washing cannot make him presentable before a God who treats him as guilty. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 32. God is not a man as Job is, that they should come together in judgment. The inequality of the parties makes a fair trial impossible on Job's terms. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 33. Neither is there any daysman between them to lay his hand on them both. JFB explains daysman as mediator or umpire, whose imposed hand expresses power to adjudicate, and notes Christians know such a Mediator on a level with both, the God-man Christ Jesus. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 34. Let God take his rod away from Job, and let not his fear terrify him. JFB explains the rod here not as punishment but as power: Job cannot meet God fairly while God deals with him on the footing of almighty power. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 35. Then Job would speak and not fear him, but it is not so with him. JFB notes that as things stand, with the rod not taken away, Job is not on a footing of equality to vindicate himself. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Unfamiliar terms

  • Daysman (verse 33). A mediator or umpire, whose imposed hand expresses power to adjudicate between two parties. Job says there is no such daysman between him and God. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Arcturus and Orion (verse 9). Star groups named as God’s making: Arcturus as the great bear that never sets, Orion as the chained fool-hero of the old sky stories. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Where the speakers disagree

Bildad holds that God does not pervert justice, so guilt must explain suffering; Job concedes God’s justice and power, yet insists God destroys the perfect and the wicked alike, and that without a daysman — a mediator between God and man — no fair trial is possible. Job is disputing the friends’ inference from justice to guilt, not justice itself. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
