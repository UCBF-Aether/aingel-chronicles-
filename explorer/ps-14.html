The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Psalms 14 (King James Version (1611) for Psalms 1–150; Charles Thomson, The Septuagint (1808) for Psalm 151)

1 The fool hath said in his heart, There is no God. They are corrupt, they have done abominable works, there is none that doeth good.

2 The LORD looked down from heaven upon the children of men, to see if there were any that did understand, and seek God.

3 They are all gone aside, they are all together become filthy: there is none that doeth good, no, not one.

4 Have all the workers of iniquity no knowledge? who eat up my people as they eat bread, and call not upon the LORD.

5 There were they in great fear: for God is in the generation of the righteous.

6 Ye have shamed the counsel of the poor, because the LORD is his refuge.

7 Oh that the salvation of Israel were come out of Zion! when the LORD bringeth back the captivity of his people, Jacob shall rejoice, and Israel shall be glad.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

The fool's practical denial of God issues in universal corruption. God searches from heaven and finds none doing good, oppressors eat God's people and are seized by fear where God stands with the righteous, and the psalm ends longing for salvation from Zion. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown; chapter text

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. The fool says in his heart, There is no God: this is not reasoned atheism but a practical denial in the heart, and it issues in corrupt, abominable works, with none doing good. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. The Lord looks down from heaven on the children of men, to see if any understand and seek God: God searches, and the search exposes the universal failure. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. All have gone aside and together become filthy; none does good, no, not one: Paul cites this passage in Romans to prove the common guilt of mankind. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Have workers of iniquity no knowledge, who eat up God's people as bread and do not call on the Lord? Oppression of the godly and neglect of prayer go together, and both betray ignorance of God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. There they were in great fear, for God is in the generation of the righteous: persecutors who feared no man are seized by fear when they perceive God with his people. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. You have shamed the counsel of the poor, because the Lord is his refuge: mocking the poor's trust is futile, for their refuge is God himself. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Oh that salvation for Israel were come out of Zion! When the Lord brings back his people's captivity, Jacob shall rejoice and Israel be glad: the psalm ends longing for the deliverance that God alone brings from Zion. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Psalms 14 and 53: the same psalm twice?

This section investigates a parallel between two texts. It does not conclude how the parallel came to be, and it claims no direct dependence, shared authorship, or historical certainty about either text's origin. Interpreted Standard corpus, ps.json (Psalms 14 and 53)

Parallels. The two printed texts track each other closely: the fool saying in his heart there is no God; God looking down from heaven to see if any understand and seek God; all gone aside and together become filthy, none doing good, no, not one; workers of iniquity eating God's people as bread and not calling on the Lord; and the same closing longing, that salvation for Israel were come out of Zion, captivity brought back, Jacob rejoicing and Israel glad.

Differences. Psalm 53 is one verse shorter (6 verses against 14's 7): it has no counterpart to Psalm 14 verse 6, shaming the counsel of the poor because the Lord is his refuge. The divine name is mostly 'God' in Psalm 53 where Psalm 14 has 'the LORD.' And verse 5 differs most: Psalm 14 has God in the generation of the righteous, while Psalm 53 has fear where no fear was, God scattering the bones of him that encamps against his people and putting him to shame.

Whether one text was adapted from the other, both were drawn from a shared source, or the likeness arose another way, is not established by the sources consulted for this page. Unresolved No source consulted for this page establishes the relation between the two texts
