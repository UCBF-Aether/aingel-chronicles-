The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Psalms 102 (King James Version (1611) for Psalms 1–150; Charles Thomson, The Septuagint (1808) for Psalm 151)

1 Hear my prayer, O LORD, and let my cry come unto thee.

2 Hide not thy face from me in the day when I am in trouble; incline thine ear unto me: in the day when I call answer me speedily.

3 For my days are consumed like smoke, and my bones are burned as an hearth.

4 My heart is smitten, and withered like grass; so that I forget to eat my bread.

5 By reason of the voice of my groaning my bones cleave to my skin.

6 I am like a pelican of the wilderness: I am like an owl of the desert.

7 I watch, and am as a sparrow alone upon the house top.

8 Mine enemies reproach me all the day; and they that are mad against me are sworn against me.

9 For I have eaten ashes like bread, and mingled my drink with weeping.

10 Because of thine indignation and thy wrath: for thou hast lifted me up, and cast me down.

11 My days are like a shadow that declineth; and I am withered like grass.

12 But thou, O LORD, shall endure for ever; and thy remembrance unto all generations.

13 Thou shalt arise, and have mercy upon Zion: for the time to favour her, yea, the set time, is come.

14 For thy servants take pleasure in her stones, and favour the dust thereof.

15 So the heathen shall fear the name of the LORD, and all the kings of the earth thy glory.

16 When the LORD shall build up Zion, he shall appear in his glory.

17 He will regard the prayer of the destitute, and not despise their prayer.

18 This shall be written for the generation to come: and the people which shall be created shall praise the LORD.

19 For he hath looked down from the height of his sanctuary; from heaven did the LORD behold the earth;

20 To hear the groaning of the prisoner; to loose those that are appointed to death;

21 To declare the name of the LORD in Zion, and his praise in Jerusalem;

22 When the people are gathered together, and the kingdoms, to serve the LORD.

23 He weakened my strength in the way; he shortened my days.

24 I said, O my God, take me not away in the midst of my days: thy years are throughout all generations.

25 Of old hast thou laid the foundation of the earth: and the heavens are the work of thy hands.

26 They shall perish, but thou shalt endure: yea, all of them shall wax old like a garment; as a vesture shalt thou change them, and they shall be changed:

27 But thou art the same, and thy years shall have no end.

28 The children of thy servants shall continue, and their seed shall be established before thee.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

A prayer of an afflicted speaker whose days are consumed like smoke, bones burned, heart withered like grass, eating ashes like bread and mingling drink with weeping, reproached all day by enemies, lifted up and cast down by God's indignation. The turn comes at verse 12: "But thou, O LORD, shalt endure for ever." From that enduring God the psalm hopes for Zion's set time of favour, the building up of Zion, and regard for the prayer of the destitute and the groaning of prisoners. The speaker's own shortened days are set against a God whose years have no end, who laid the earth's foundation and will outlast the heavens like a changed garment, so that the children of his servants shall continue and their seed be established before him. Interpreted Psalm text above; study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Hear my prayer, and let my cry come unto thee, is the prayer of the afflicted, composed by one himself afflicted, afflicted with the church and for it — and on those of a public spirit afflictions of that kind lie heavier than any other. It is calculated for an afflicted state and intended for the use of others in like distress, for whatsoever was written aforetime was written designedly for our use, and here the Holy Ghost has drawn up our petition and put words into our mouths. It is often the lot of the best saints to be sorely affected, and even good men may be almost overwhelmed and ready to faint — and when our state is afflicted and our spirits overwhelmed, it is our duty and interest to pray, pouring out our complaints before the Lord, which shows both the leave God gives us to be free with him and the liberty of speech and access we have, and what an ease it is to an afflicted spirit to unburden itself by a humble representation of its grievances. Our care must be that God would graciously hear us, for if our prayers are not pleasing to God they will be to no purpose to ourselves, and our hope is that he has appointed us to seek him and promised we shall not seek him in vain. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 2. Hide not the face, incline the ear, answer speedily: Henry reads the plea as asking favour's smile even before deliverance's hand, manifest thyself to me, then manifest thyself for me, with pressing prayer allowed in humility and patience. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 3. For my days are consumed like smoke, and my bones are burned as an hearth, is the low condition to which the psalmist was reduced by his afflictions: his body was macerated and emaciated, and he had become a perfect skeleton, nothing but skin and bones. As prosperity and joy are represented by making fat the bones, and the bones flourishing like a herb, so great trouble and grief are here represented by the contrary: my bones are burnt as a hearth; they cleave to my skin; nay, my heart is smitten, and withered like grass; it touches the vitals, and there is a sensible decay there. If we be thus brought low by bodily distempers, let us not think it strange; the body is like grass, weak and of the earth, no wonder then that it withers. And he looked upon himself as a dying man: my days are consumed like smoke, which vanishes away quickly. Or, they are consumed in smoke, of which nothing remains. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 4. The heart smitten and withered like grass, so that he forgets to eat his bread, belongs to Henry's picture of a body macerated and emaciated into a perfect skeleton, nothing but skin and bones: as prosperity and joy are represented by fat bones flourishing like a herb, so great trouble and grief are represented by the contrary — bones burnt as a hearth, cleaving to the skin, the heart smitten and withered like grass, touching the vitals with a sensible decay, scorched with the burning heat of troubles. If we are thus brought low by bodily distempers, let us not think it strange, for the body is like grass, weak and of the earth, and no wonder it withers. And he was very melancholy and of a sorrowful spirit, so taken up with thoughts of his troubles that he forgot to eat his bread, having no appetite for necessary food nor relish of it — for when God hides his face from a soul, the delights of sense will be sapless things. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 5. Groaning cleaving bones to skin makes grief visible in the body: continual sighing exhausts spirits, appetite fails, and the frame withers, trouble touching vitals, as prosperity once made bones flourish. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 6. I am like a pelican of the wilderness: I am like an owl of the desert, is the psalmist's melancholy solitude: he affected solitude, as melancholy people do. His friends deserted him and were shy of him, and he cared as little for their company: I am like a pelican of the wilderness, or a bittern, so some, that make a doleful noise; I am like an owl, that affects to lodge in deserted ruined buildings. Those who do thus, when they are in sorrow, humour themselves indeed; but they prejudice themselves, and know not what they do, nor what advantage they hereby give to the tempter. In affliction we should sit alone to consider our ways, but not sit alone to indulge an inordinate grief. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 7. I watch, and am as a sparrow alone upon the house top, is the same melancholy solitude continued: I watch, and am as a sparrow upon the house-top. I live in a garret, and there spend my hours in poring on my troubles and bemoaning myself. Those who do thus, when they are in sorrow, humour themselves indeed; but they prejudice themselves, and know not what they do, nor what advantage they hereby give to the tempter. In affliction we should sit alone to consider our ways, but not sit alone to indulge an inordinate grief. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 8. Enemies reproaching all the day, mad against him and sworn against him, is the third part of the complaint: when his friends went off from him, his foes set themselves against him, designing both to create vexation — for an ingenuous mind regrets reproach — and to bring an odium upon him before men. When they could not otherwise reach him they shot bitter words as arrows at him, unwearied, all the day, a continual dropping. They were very outrageous, mad, obstinate and implacable, sworn against him as the Jews who bound themselves with an oath to kill Paul, or sworn against him as accusers to take away his life. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 9. Eating ashes like bread and mingling drink with weeping is fasting and weeping under the tokens of God's displeasure: instead of eating his bread he has lain down in dust and ashes, and when he should have refreshed himself with drinking he has only eased himself with weeping. And what is the matter? Because of God's indignation and wrath — it was not so much the trouble itself that troubled him as the wrath of God which he was under apprehensions of as the cause of the trouble. This was the wormwood and gall in the affliction: thou hast lifted me up and cast me down, as that which we cast to the ground with a design to dash it to pieces, lifting first that we may throw down with more violence — or, thou hast formerly lifted me up in honour, joy and uncommon prosperity, but the remembrance of that aggravates the present grief. We must eye the hand of God both in lifting us up and casting us down, and say, blessed be the name of the Lord, who both gives and takes away. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 10. Because of thine indignation and thy wrath: for thou hast lifted me up, and cast me down, is the psalmist fasting and weeping under the tokens of God's displeasure: I have eaten ashes like bread; instead of eating my bread, I have lain down in dust and ashes, and I have mingled my drink with weeping; when I should have refreshed myself with drinking I have only eased myself with weeping. And what is the matter? He tells us: because of thy wrath. It was not so much the trouble itself that troubled him as the wrath of God which he was under the apprehensions of as the cause of the trouble. This, this was the wormwood and the gall in the affliction and the misery: thou hast lifted me up and cast me down, as that which we cast to the ground with a design to dash it to pieces; we lift up first, that we may throw it down with the more violence; or, thou hast formerly lifted me up in honour, and joy, and uncommon prosperity; but the remembrance of that aggravates the present grief and makes it the more grievous. We must eye the hand of God both in lifting us up and casting us down, and say, Blessed be the name of the Lord, who both gives and takes away. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 11. My days are like a shadow that declineth; and I am withered like grass, is the psalmist looking upon himself as a dying man: my days are like a shadow that declines, like the evening-shadow, or a forerunner of approaching night. And, I am withered like grass, scorched with the burning heat of my troubles. If we be thus brought low by bodily distempers, let us not think it strange; the body is like grass, weak and of the earth, no wonder then that it withers. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 12. But thou, O LORD, shalt endure for ever; and thy remembrance unto all generations, is the seasonable comfort fetched in against these grievances, from the eternity of God: we are dying creatures, and our interests and comforts are dying, but God is an everliving everlasting God: my days are like a shadow; there is no remedy; night is coming upon me; but, thou, O Lord! shalt endure for ever. Our life is transient, but thine is permanent; our friends die, but thou our God diest not; what threatened us cannot touch thee; our names will be written in the dust and buried in oblivion, but thy remembrance shall be unto all generations; to the end of time, nay, to eternity, thou shalt be known and honoured. A good man loves God better than himself, and therefore can balance his own sorrow and death with the pleasing thought of the unchangeable blessedness of the Eternal Mind. God endures for ever, his church's faithful patron and protector; and, his honour and perpetual remembrance being very much bound up in her interests, we may be confident that they shall not be neglected. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 13. Arising to have mercy upon Zion, because the time to favour her is come, builds the hope of deliverance on God's goodness — she has become an object of his pity — and on his power, stirring himself up to do it in contempt of all opposition from the church's enemies, for the zeal of the Lord of hosts shall do this. Most encouraging is that there is a time set for the church's deliverance, which will come not only some time but at the appointed time, the time Infinite Wisdom appointed and therefore the best time, and Eternal Truth fixed and therefore a certain time, not to be forgotten nor further adjourned. At the end of seventy years the time to favour Zion by delivering her from the daughter of Babylon did come. Zion in ruins is the temple built in the city of David, and favouring Zion is building the temple up again — the greatest favour to any people, for no blessing is more desirable to a ruined state than restoring and re-establishing their church-privileges. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 14. Servants taking pleasure in Zion's stones and favouring her dust is why the favour is wished and longed for: it would be a great rejoicing to Zion's friends, who take pleasure even in the temple's stones though thrown down and scattered, and favour the very rubbish and ruins of it. When the temple was ruined, yet its stones were to be had for a new building, and there were those who encouraged themselves with that, having a favour even for its dust. Those who truly love the church love it in affliction as well as in prosperity, and it is good ground to hope God will favour Zion's ruins when he puts it into his people's hearts to favour them and show it by prayers and endeavours — and a good plea with God for mercy that there are those so affectionately concerned for her, waiting for the Lord's salvation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 15. The heathen fearing the name of the Lord and kings of the earth his glory is the good influence Zion's restoration would have on her neighbours, a happy means perhaps of their conversion, at least of their conviction: the heathen shall have high thoughts of him and his people, and kings shall be affected with his glory, having better thoughts of the church than they had when God by providence thus puts honour upon it, afraid of doing anything against it when they see God taking its part — nay, saying, we will go with you, for we have seen that God is with you, as many of the people of the land became Jews when the fear of the Jews fell upon them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 16. When the Lord shall build up Zion, he shall appear in his glory, and that redounds to the honour of Zion's God: they take it for granted it will be done, for God himself has undertaken it, and for that reason all who have made his glory their highest end desire and pray for it. The edifying of the church will be the glorifying of God, and therefore we may be assured it will be done in the set time — and those who pray in faith, Father, glorify thy name, may receive the same answer given to Christ himself by a voice from heaven, I have both glorified it and I will glorify it yet again, though now for a time it may be eclipsed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 17. Regarding the prayer of the destitute and not despising it answers the fear that God's people's prayers are slighted and no notice taken of them: they will be reviewed and greatly encouraged. When great men appear in their glory they are apt to look with disdain on the poor who apply to them, but the great God will not do so. The petitioners are destitute — an elegant word signifying the heath in the wilderness, a low shrub like the hyssop of the wall, in a low and broken state, enriched with spiritual blessings but destitute of temporal good things, the poor, weak, desolate and stripped — or it may signify that low and broken spirit which God looks for in all who draw nigh to him. He will regard their prayer, look at it, peruse their petition, and will not despise it; more is implied than expressed, for he will value it, be well pleased with it, and return an answer of peace, the greatest honour that can be put upon it. When we consider our meanness, vileness, darkness, deadness and the manifold defects in our prayers, we have cause to suspect they will be received with disdain in heaven — but we are assured of the contrary, for we have an advocate with the Father and are under grace, not under the law. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 18. This being written for the generation to come, that the people which shall be created shall praise the Lord, makes God's favour to destitute praying people a lasting encouragement to prayer: none may despair though destitute, nor think their prayers forgotten because they have not an answer immediately. The experiences of others should be our encouragements to seek God and trust in him, and if we have the comfort of others' experiences it is fit we give God the glory of them. Many now unborn shall, by reading the church's history, be wrought upon to turn proselytes, and the people created anew by divine grace, a kind of first-fruits of his creatures, shall praise the Lord for his answers to their prayers when they were more destitute. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 19. God's looking down from the height of his sanctuary to behold the earth is care for prisoners under condemnation unjustly, as sheep appointed for slaughter, whose discharge shall be taken care of: from heaven, where he has prepared his throne, that high and holy place of prospect, nothing on earth is or can be hidden from his all-seeing eye. He looks down not to take a view of the kingdoms of the world and their glory, but to do acts of grace, to hear the groaning of the prisoners — which we desire to be out of the hearing of — and not only to hear them but to help them, to loose those appointed to death when there is but a step between them and it. Some understand it of the Jews' release from Babylon, God hearing their groaning there as he did in Egypt and coming down to deliver them. God takes notice not only of the prayers of his afflicted people, the language of grace, but even of their groans, the language of nature — divine pity in hearing the prisoner's groans, and divine power in loosing the prisoner's bonds, even when appointed to death and pinioned and double-shackled, as in Peter's case. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 20. Groaning heard and appointed-to-death loosed show pity and power together: God looks down not to survey kingdoms but to hear groans, language of nature as well as prayer, and looses bonds even double-shackled, as with Peter. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 21. Declaring the name of the Lord in Zion and his praise in Jerusalem is the end of such deliverances: instances of divine condescension and compassion will help to declare his name in Zion, making it appear that he answers to the name he himself proclaimed, The Lord God, gracious and merciful, and this declaration of his name in Zion shall be the matter of his praise in Jerusalem. If God by his providences declares his name, we must by our acknowledgments declare his praise, which ought to be the echo of his name. God discharges his people who were prisoners and captives in Babylon that they may declare his name in Zion, the place he chose to put his name there, and his praise in Jerusalem at their return — for in captivity they could not sing Zion's songs, and God brought them again to Jerusalem in order that they might sing them there. For this end God gives liberty from bondage and life from the dead. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 22. When the people are gathered together, and the kingdoms, to serve the Lord, such deliverances draw others to God's worship: when God's people were gathered at Jerusalem after their return from Babylon, many out of the kingdoms joined with them to serve the Lord, as in Ezra, where not only the children of Israel come out of captivity but many who had separated themselves from the heathen kept the feast of unleavened bread with joy. But it may look further, at the conversion of the Gentiles to the faith of Christ in the latter days, for Christ has proclaimed liberty to the captives and the opening of the prison to those bound, that they may declare the name of the Lord in the gospel-church, in which Jews and Gentiles shall unite. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 23. Weakening his strength in the way and shortening his days is the imminent danger that the Jewish church was in of being quite extirpated by the captivity in Babylon: for many ages they were in the way to the performance of the great promise concerning the Messiah, longing for it as a traveller longs to be at his journey's end, the legal institutions leading them in the way — but when the ten tribes were lost in Assyria and the two almost lost in Babylon, the nation's strength was weakened and its day shortened, for they said, our hope is lost, we are cut off for our parts. And then what becomes of the promise that Shiloh should arise out of Judah, the star out of Jacob, the Messiah out of David's family? If these fail, the promise fails. It applies also to two common afflictions of our time: being sickly, for bodily distempers soon weaken our strength in the way, making the keepers of the house tremble and strong men bow themselves; and being short-lived, for when in the midst of our days our strength is weakened, what can we expect but that our months should be cut off in the midst? We must own God's hand in it, for in his hand our strength and time are, and reconcile it to his love, for it has often been the lot of those who used their strength well to have it weakened, and of those who could ill be spared to have their days shortened. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 24. 'O my God, take me not away in the midst of my days' is a prayer for the church's continuance: let not this poor church be cut off in the midst of the days assigned it by the promise, let it not be cut off till the Messiah shall come — destroy it not, for that blessing is in it. She is a criminal, but for the sake of that blessing in her she pleads for a reprieve. It is a prayer for the afflicted which, with submission to God's will, we may in faith put up, that God would not take us away in the midst of our days, but, if it be his will, spare us to do him further service and to be made riper for heaven. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 25. Of old laying the foundation of the earth, and the heavens being the work of his hands, is the plea from the eternity of the promised Messiah, which the apostle quotes and tells us he says to the Son: it is comfortable, in reference to all changes passing over the church and all dangers it is in, that Jesus Christ is the same yesterday, today and for ever, his years throughout all generations and not to be shortened — and comfortable in reference to the decay and death of our own bodies and the removal of friends, that God is an everliving God and therefore, if he be ours, in him we may have everlasting consolation. To illustrate the Creator's eternity, the psalm compares it with the creature's mutability, for it is God's sole prerogative to be unchangeable: God made the world and therefore had a being before it from eternity, the Son of God, the eternal Word, by whom all things were made, in the beginning from eternity with God and God himself. Earth and heaven, with all their hosts, derive their being from God by his Son — the earth founded on the seas and floods and yet abiding, much more shall the church, built upon a rock, abide; the heavens are the work of his hands, and by him all their motions and influences are directed, so God is the fountain not only of all being but of all power and dominion, and the great Redeemer is fit to be entrusted with all power in heaven and earth, since as Creator of both he perfectly knows both and is entitled to both. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 26. Perishing heavens waxing old like a garment, changed as a vesture, set against an unchanging God: a portion in the creature fades, a portion in God is perpetual, and the change is alteration toward new heavens, not annihilation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 27. Thou art the same, and thy years shall have no end, follows from God unmaking the world again by the same almighty power that made it: they shall perish, for thou shalt change them, and therefore, no doubt, thou shalt endure. God and the world, Christ and the creature, are rivals for the innermost and uppermost place in the soul of man, the immortal soul — and what is said here should decide the controversy at once for God and Christ. A portion in the creature is fading and dying: they shall perish, they will not last so long as we shall last, and the day is coming when earth and all works therein shall be burnt up — then what will become of those who laid up treasure in it? Heaven and earth shall wax old as a garment, not by gradual decay but, when the set time comes, laid aside like an old garment we have no more occasion for, changed as a vesture, not annihilated but altered, so that they shall not be at all the same, but new heavens and a new earth. God can change them as he pleases and when he pleases, and their constant changes in day and night, summer and winter, are earnests of their last and final change, when heavens and time, measured by them, shall be no more. A portion in God is perpetual and everlasting: thou art the same, subject to no change, and thy years have no end — Christ will be the same in performance that he was in promise, the same to his church in captivity that he was to his church at liberty. Let not the church fear the weakening of her strength or the shortening of her days while Christ himself is both her strength and her life, for he is the same and has said, because I live ye shall live also. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 28. The children of thy servants shall continue, and their seed shall be established before thee, is the comfortable assurance of an answer to the prayer: since Christ is the same, the church shall continue from one generation to another, for from the eternity of the head we may infer the perpetuity of the body, though often weak and distempered and even at death's door. Those who hope to wear out the saints of the Most High will be mistaken. Christ's servants shall have children, those children shall have a seed, a succession of professing people — the church, as well as the world, is under the blessing, be fruitful and multiply — and these children shall continue, not in their own persons, by reason of death, but in their seed, established before God in his service and by his grace, for the entail of religion shall not be cut off while the world stands, but as one generation of good people passes away another shall come, and thus the throne of Christ shall endure. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

  • Verse 4. The heart smitten and withered like grass, so that he forgets to eat his bread, belongs to Henry's picture of a body macerated and emaciated into a perfect skeleton, nothing but skin and bones: as prosperity and joy are represented by fat bones flourishing like a herb, so great trouble and grief are represented by the contrary — bones burnt as a hearth, cleaving to the skin, the heart smitten and withered like grass, touching the vitals with a sensible decay, scorched with the burning heat of troubles. If we are thus brought low by bodily distempers, let us not think it strange, for the body is like grass, weak and of the earth, and no wonder it withers. And he was very melancholy and of a sorrowful spirit, so taken up with thoughts of his troubles that he forgot to eat his bread, having no appetite for necessary food nor relish of it — for when God hides his face from a soul, the delights of sense will be sapless things. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 5. Groaning cleaving bones to skin makes grief visible in the body: continual sighing exhausts spirits, appetite fails, and the frame withers, trouble touching vitals, as prosperity once made bones flourish. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 6. I am like a pelican of the wilderness: I am like an owl of the desert, is the psalmist's melancholy solitude: he affected solitude, as melancholy people do. His friends deserted him and were shy of him, and he cared as little for their company: I am like a pelican of the wilderness, or a bittern, so some, that make a doleful noise; I am like an owl, that affects to lodge in deserted ruined buildings. Those who do thus, when they are in sorrow, humour themselves indeed; but they prejudice themselves, and know not what they do, nor what advantage they hereby give to the tempter. In affliction we should sit alone to consider our ways, but not sit alone to indulge an inordinate grief. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 7. I watch, and am as a sparrow alone upon the house top, is the same melancholy solitude continued: I watch, and am as a sparrow upon the house-top. I live in a garret, and there spend my hours in poring on my troubles and bemoaning myself. Those who do thus, when they are in sorrow, humour themselves indeed; but they prejudice themselves, and know not what they do, nor what advantage they hereby give to the tempter. In affliction we should sit alone to consider our ways, but not sit alone to indulge an inordinate grief. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 8. Enemies reproaching all the day, mad against him and sworn against him, is the third part of the complaint: when his friends went off from him, his foes set themselves against him, designing both to create vexation — for an ingenuous mind regrets reproach — and to bring an odium upon him before men. When they could not otherwise reach him they shot bitter words as arrows at him, unwearied, all the day, a continual dropping. They were very outrageous, mad, obstinate and implacable, sworn against him as the Jews who bound themselves with an oath to kill Paul, or sworn against him as accusers to take away his life. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 9. Eating ashes like bread and mingling drink with weeping is fasting and weeping under the tokens of God's displeasure: instead of eating his bread he has lain down in dust and ashes, and when he should have refreshed himself with drinking he has only eased himself with weeping. And what is the matter? Because of God's indignation and wrath — it was not so much the trouble itself that troubled him as the wrath of God which he was under apprehensions of as the cause of the trouble. This was the wormwood and gall in the affliction: thou hast lifted me up and cast me down, as that which we cast to the ground with a design to dash it to pieces, lifting first that we may throw down with more violence — or, thou hast formerly lifted me up in honour, joy and uncommon prosperity, but the remembrance of that aggravates the present grief. We must eye the hand of God both in lifting us up and casting us down, and say, blessed be the name of the Lord, who both gives and takes away. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 10. Because of thine indignation and thy wrath: for thou hast lifted me up, and cast me down, is the psalmist fasting and weeping under the tokens of God's displeasure: I have eaten ashes like bread; instead of eating my bread, I have lain down in dust and ashes, and I have mingled my drink with weeping; when I should have refreshed myself with drinking I have only eased myself with weeping. And what is the matter? He tells us: because of thy wrath. It was not so much the trouble itself that troubled him as the wrath of God which he was under the apprehensions of as the cause of the trouble. This, this was the wormwood and the gall in the affliction and the misery: thou hast lifted me up and cast me down, as that which we cast to the ground with a design to dash it to pieces; we lift up first, that we may throw it down with the more violence; or, thou hast formerly lifted me up in honour, and joy, and uncommon prosperity; but the remembrance of that aggravates the present grief and makes it the more grievous. We must eye the hand of God both in lifting us up and casting us down, and say, Blessed be the name of the Lord, who both gives and takes away. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 11. My days are like a shadow that declineth; and I am withered like grass, is the psalmist looking upon himself as a dying man: my days are like a shadow that declines, like the evening-shadow, or a forerunner of approaching night. And, I am withered like grass, scorched with the burning heat of my troubles. If we be thus brought low by bodily distempers, let us not think it strange; the body is like grass, weak and of the earth, no wonder then that it withers. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 12. But thou, O LORD, shalt endure for ever; and thy remembrance unto all generations, is the seasonable comfort fetched in against these grievances, from the eternity of God: we are dying creatures, and our interests and comforts are dying, but God is an everliving everlasting God: my days are like a shadow; there is no remedy; night is coming upon me; but, thou, O Lord! shalt endure for ever. Our life is transient, but thine is permanent; our friends die, but thou our God diest not; what threatened us cannot touch thee; our names will be written in the dust and buried in oblivion, but thy remembrance shall be unto all generations; to the end of time, nay, to eternity, thou shalt be known and honoured. A good man loves God better than himself, and therefore can balance his own sorrow and death with the pleasing thought of the unchangeable blessedness of the Eternal Mind. God endures for ever, his church's faithful patron and protector; and, his honour and perpetual remembrance being very much bound up in her interests, we may be confident that they shall not be neglected. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 13. Arising to have mercy upon Zion, because the time to favour her is come, builds the hope of deliverance on God's goodness — she has become an object of his pity — and on his power, stirring himself up to do it in contempt of all opposition from the church's enemies, for the zeal of the Lord of hosts shall do this. Most encouraging is that there is a time set for the church's deliverance, which will come not only some time but at the appointed time, the time Infinite Wisdom appointed and therefore the best time, and Eternal Truth fixed and therefore a certain time, not to be forgotten nor further adjourned. At the end of seventy years the time to favour Zion by delivering her from the daughter of Babylon did come. Zion in ruins is the temple built in the city of David, and favouring Zion is building the temple up again — the greatest favour to any people, for no blessing is more desirable to a ruined state than restoring and re-establishing their church-privileges. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 14. Servants taking pleasure in Zion's stones and favouring her dust is why the favour is wished and longed for: it would be a great rejoicing to Zion's friends, who take pleasure even in the temple's stones though thrown down and scattered, and favour the very rubbish and ruins of it. When the temple was ruined, yet its stones were to be had for a new building, and there were those who encouraged themselves with that, having a favour even for its dust. Those who truly love the church love it in affliction as well as in prosperity, and it is good ground to hope God will favour Zion's ruins when he puts it into his people's hearts to favour them and show it by prayers and endeavours — and a good plea with God for mercy that there are those so affectionately concerned for her, waiting for the Lord's salvation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 15. The heathen fearing the name of the Lord and kings of the earth his glory is the good influence Zion's restoration would have on her neighbours, a happy means perhaps of their conversion, at least of their conviction: the heathen shall have high thoughts of him and his people, and kings shall be affected with his glory, having better thoughts of the church than they had when God by providence thus puts honour upon it, afraid of doing anything against it when they see God taking its part — nay, saying, we will go with you, for we have seen that God is with you, as many of the people of the land became Jews when the fear of the Jews fell upon them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 16. When the Lord shall build up Zion, he shall appear in his glory, and that redounds to the honour of Zion's God: they take it for granted it will be done, for God himself has undertaken it, and for that reason all who have made his glory their highest end desire and pray for it. The edifying of the church will be the glorifying of God, and therefore we may be assured it will be done in the set time — and those who pray in faith, Father, glorify thy name, may receive the same answer given to Christ himself by a voice from heaven, I have both glorified it and I will glorify it yet again, though now for a time it may be eclipsed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 17. Regarding the prayer of the destitute and not despising it answers the fear that God's people's prayers are slighted and no notice taken of them: they will be reviewed and greatly encouraged. When great men appear in their glory they are apt to look with disdain on the poor who apply to them, but the great God will not do so. The petitioners are destitute — an elegant word signifying the heath in the wilderness, a low shrub like the hyssop of the wall, in a low and broken state, enriched with spiritual blessings but destitute of temporal good things, the poor, weak, desolate and stripped — or it may signify that low and broken spirit which God looks for in all who draw nigh to him. He will regard their prayer, look at it, peruse their petition, and will not despise it; more is implied than expressed, for he will value it, be well pleased with it, and return an answer of peace, the greatest honour that can be put upon it. When we consider our meanness, vileness, darkness, deadness and the manifold defects in our prayers, we have cause to suspect they will be received with disdain in heaven — but we are assured of the contrary, for we have an advocate with the Father and are under grace, not under the law. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 18. This being written for the generation to come, that the people which shall be created shall praise the Lord, makes God's favour to destitute praying people a lasting encouragement to prayer: none may despair though destitute, nor think their prayers forgotten because they have not an answer immediately. The experiences of others should be our encouragements to seek God and trust in him, and if we have the comfort of others' experiences it is fit we give God the glory of them. Many now unborn shall, by reading the church's history, be wrought upon to turn proselytes, and the people created anew by divine grace, a kind of first-fruits of his creatures, shall praise the Lord for his answers to their prayers when they were more destitute. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 19. God's looking down from the height of his sanctuary to behold the earth is care for prisoners under condemnation unjustly, as sheep appointed for slaughter, whose discharge shall be taken care of: from heaven, where he has prepared his throne, that high and holy place of prospect, nothing on earth is or can be hidden from his all-seeing eye. He looks down not to take a view of the kingdoms of the world and their glory, but to do acts of grace, to hear the groaning of the prisoners — which we desire to be out of the hearing of — and not only to hear them but to help them, to loose those appointed to death when there is but a step between them and it. Some understand it of the Jews' release from Babylon, God hearing their groaning there as he did in Egypt and coming down to deliver them. God takes notice not only of the prayers of his afflicted people, the language of grace, but even of their groans, the language of nature — divine pity in hearing the prisoner's groans, and divine power in loosing the prisoner's bonds, even when appointed to death and pinioned and double-shackled, as in Peter's case. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 20. Groaning heard and appointed-to-death loosed show pity and power together: God looks down not to survey kingdoms but to hear groans, language of nature as well as prayer, and looses bonds even double-shackled, as with Peter. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 21. Declaring the name of the Lord in Zion and his praise in Jerusalem is the end of such deliverances: instances of divine condescension and compassion will help to declare his name in Zion, making it appear that he answers to the name he himself proclaimed, The Lord God, gracious and merciful, and this declaration of his name in Zion shall be the matter of his praise in Jerusalem. If God by his providences declares his name, we must by our acknowledgments declare his praise, which ought to be the echo of his name. God discharges his people who were prisoners and captives in Babylon that they may declare his name in Zion, the place he chose to put his name there, and his praise in Jerusalem at their return — for in captivity they could not sing Zion's songs, and God brought them again to Jerusalem in order that they might sing them there. For this end God gives liberty from bondage and life from the dead. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 22. When the people are gathered together, and the kingdoms, to serve the Lord, such deliverances draw others to God's worship: when God's people were gathered at Jerusalem after their return from Babylon, many out of the kingdoms joined with them to serve the Lord, as in Ezra, where not only the children of Israel come out of captivity but many who had separated themselves from the heathen kept the feast of unleavened bread with joy. But it may look further, at the conversion of the Gentiles to the faith of Christ in the latter days, for Christ has proclaimed liberty to the captives and the opening of the prison to those bound, that they may declare the name of the Lord in the gospel-church, in which Jews and Gentiles shall unite. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 23. Weakening his strength in the way and shortening his days is the imminent danger that the Jewish church was in of being quite extirpated by the captivity in Babylon: for many ages they were in the way to the performance of the great promise concerning the Messiah, longing for it as a traveller longs to be at his journey's end, the legal institutions leading them in the way — but when the ten tribes were lost in Assyria and the two almost lost in Babylon, the nation's strength was weakened and its day shortened, for they said, our hope is lost, we are cut off for our parts. And then what becomes of the promise that Shiloh should arise out of Judah, the star out of Jacob, the Messiah out of David's family? If these fail, the promise fails. It applies also to two common afflictions of our time: being sickly, for bodily distempers soon weaken our strength in the way, making the keepers of the house tremble and strong men bow themselves; and being short-lived, for when in the midst of our days our strength is weakened, what can we expect but that our months should be cut off in the midst? We must own God's hand in it, for in his hand our strength and time are, and reconcile it to his love, for it has often been the lot of those who used their strength well to have it weakened, and of those who could ill be spared to have their days shortened. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 24. 'O my God, take me not away in the midst of my days' is a prayer for the church's continuance: let not this poor church be cut off in the midst of the days assigned it by the promise, let it not be cut off till the Messiah shall come — destroy it not, for that blessing is in it. She is a criminal, but for the sake of that blessing in her she pleads for a reprieve. It is a prayer for the afflicted which, with submission to God's will, we may in faith put up, that God would not take us away in the midst of our days, but, if it be his will, spare us to do him further service and to be made riper for heaven. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 25. Of old laying the foundation of the earth, and the heavens being the work of his hands, is the plea from the eternity of the promised Messiah, which the apostle quotes and tells us he says to the Son: it is comfortable, in reference to all changes passing over the church and all dangers it is in, that Jesus Christ is the same yesterday, today and for ever, his years throughout all generations and not to be shortened — and comfortable in reference to the decay and death of our own bodies and the removal of friends, that God is an everliving God and therefore, if he be ours, in him we may have everlasting consolation. To illustrate the Creator's eternity, the psalm compares it with the creature's mutability, for it is God's sole prerogative to be unchangeable: God made the world and therefore had a being before it from eternity, the Son of God, the eternal Word, by whom all things were made, in the beginning from eternity with God and God himself. Earth and heaven, with all their hosts, derive their being from God by his Son — the earth founded on the seas and floods and yet abiding, much more shall the church, built upon a rock, abide; the heavens are the work of his hands, and by him all their motions and influences are directed, so God is the fountain not only of all being but of all power and dominion, and the great Redeemer is fit to be entrusted with all power in heaven and earth, since as Creator of both he perfectly knows both and is entitled to both. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 26. Perishing heavens waxing old like a garment, changed as a vesture, set against an unchanging God: a portion in the creature fades, a portion in God is perpetual, and the change is alteration toward new heavens, not annihilation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 27. Thou art the same, and thy years shall have no end, follows from God unmaking the world again by the same almighty power that made it: they shall perish, for thou shalt change them, and therefore, no doubt, thou shalt endure. God and the world, Christ and the creature, are rivals for the innermost and uppermost place in the soul of man, the immortal soul — and what is said here should decide the controversy at once for God and Christ. A portion in the creature is fading and dying: they shall perish, they will not last so long as we shall last, and the day is coming when earth and all works therein shall be burnt up — then what will become of those who laid up treasure in it? Heaven and earth shall wax old as a garment, not by gradual decay but, when the set time comes, laid aside like an old garment we have no more occasion for, changed as a vesture, not annihilated but altered, so that they shall not be at all the same, but new heavens and a new earth. God can change them as he pleases and when he pleases, and their constant changes in day and night, summer and winter, are earnests of their last and final change, when heavens and time, measured by them, shall be no more. A portion in God is perpetual and everlasting: thou art the same, subject to no change, and thy years have no end — Christ will be the same in performance that he was in promise, the same to his church in captivity that he was to his church at liberty. Let not the church fear the weakening of her strength or the shortening of her days while Christ himself is both her strength and her life, for he is the same and has said, because I live ye shall live also. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 28. The children of thy servants shall continue, and their seed shall be established before thee, is the comfortable assurance of an answer to the prayer: since Christ is the same, the church shall continue from one generation to another, for from the eternity of the head we may infer the perpetuity of the body, though often weak and distempered and even at death's door. Those who hope to wear out the saints of the Most High will be mistaken. Christ's servants shall have children, those children shall have a seed, a succession of professing people — the church, as well as the world, is under the blessing, be fruitful and multiply — and these children shall continue, not in their own persons, by reason of death, but in their seed, established before God in his service and by his grace, for the entail of religion shall not be cut off while the world stands, but as one generation of good people passes away another shall come, and thus the throne of Christ shall endure. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
