The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Matthew 3 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 In those days came John the Baptist, preaching in the wilderness of Judaea,

2 And saying, Repent ye: for the kingdom of heaven is at hand.

3 For this is he that was spoken of by the prophet Esaias, saying, The voice of one crying in the wilderness, Prepare ye the way of the Lord, make his paths straight.

4 And the same John had his raiment of camel’s hair, and a leathern girdle about his loins; and his meat was locusts and wild honey.

5 Then went out to him Jerusalem, and all Judaea, and all the region round about Jordan,

6 And were baptized of him in Jordan, confessing their sins.

7 But when he saw many of the Pharisees and Sadducees come to his baptism, he said unto them, O generation of vipers, who hath warned you to flee from the wrath to come?

8 Bring forth therefore fruits meet for repentance:

9 And think not to say within yourselves, We have Abraham to our father: for I say unto you, that God is able of these stones to raise up children unto Abraham.

10 And now also the axe is laid unto the root of the trees: therefore every tree which bringeth not forth good fruit is hewn down, and cast into the fire.

11 I indeed baptize you with water unto repentance. but he that cometh after me is mightier than I, whose shoes I am not worthy to bear: he shall baptize you with the Holy Ghost, and with fire:

12 Whose fan is in his hand, and he will throughly purge his floor, and gather his wheat into the garner; but he will burn up the chaff with unquenchable fire.

13 Then cometh Jesus from Galilee to Jordan unto John, to be baptized of him.

14 But John forbad him, saying, I have need to be baptized of thee, and comest thou to me?

15 And Jesus answering said unto him, Suffer it to be so now: for thus it becometh us to fulfil all righteousness. Then he suffered him.

16 And Jesus, when he was baptized, went up straightway out of the water: and, lo, the heavens were opened unto him, and he saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove, and lighting upon him:

17 And lo a voice from heaven, saying, This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

John the Baptist preaches repentance in the wilderness of Judea, baptizing in Jordan those who confess their sins, and rebukes Pharisees and Sadducees who come to his baptism as a generation of vipers, warning that the axe is laid to the root and the coming one will baptize with the Holy Ghost and with fire. Jesus then comes to be baptized, John at first hinders him, the heavens open, the Spirit descends like a dove, and a voice says, “This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased.” Interpreted Reading of this chapter's text as printed above and its study notes

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. After Malachi there was no prophet until John the Baptist came, about six months before his Master, preaching in the wilderness of Judea — the thinly peopled Jordan valley, not an uninhabited desert. No place is so remote as to shut us out from the visits of divine grace. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. “Repent ye” denotes a total alteration of mind — judgment, disposition and affections given another and better bias — issuing in a changed way. The kingdom of heaven, a phrase used nearly thirty times in this peculiarly Jewish Gospel and drawn from Daniel 7, is at hand, and repentance is its proper preparation because it is essentially spiritual. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. This is he spoken of by Isaiah: the voice of one crying in the wilderness, “Prepare ye the way of the Lord, make his paths straight.” Quoted in all four Gospels, it makes the forerunner the connecting link between the old and new economies, calling for every obstruction, public or personal, to be removed so all flesh may see God's salvation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. John's raiment of camel's hair with a leathern girdle was the prophetic dress of Elijah, and his meat of locusts and wild honey the food of the poor. Dress, diet and shrill wilderness cry recalled the stern days of Elijah, and those who call others to mourn for sin ought themselves to live a serious, self-denying life. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Then went out to him Jerusalem and all Judea and all the region round about Jordan, from the metropolitan centre to the extremities of the province. Many came to John's baptism, Henry warns, but few kept the profession they made; curiosity may bring forward hearers who are never subject to the power of the word. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. They were baptized in Jordan, confessing their sins, probably aloud — a public seal of felt need of deliverance, of expectation of the coming Deliverer, and of readiness to welcome him. Jews were familiar with baptizing proselytes from heathenism, but this baptism of Jews themselves was quite new and strange. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Seeing many Pharisees and Sadducees come to his baptism, John cried, “O generation of vipers” — viper brood, charging both mutually antagonistic sects alike with poisoning the nation's religious principles. He suspected popularity rather than spiritual anxiety had drawn them, and asked who had warned them to flee from the wrath to come, God's righteous displeasure against sin, already merited yet wholly to come at judgment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Bring forth therefore fruit meet for repentance — fruit befitting a true penitent, not mere profession. Penitents, Henry adds, are humble in their own eyes, thankful for the least mercy, patient under affliction, watchful against sin, abounding in duty and charitable in judging others. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Think not to say, “We have Abraham to our father” — that pillow on which the nation fatally reposed, the rock on which it split. God is able of these stones to raise up children to Abraham: he stands in no need of them to make good his promise, and the saying points to Gentiles, stone-dead in sin, called into the room of unbelieving Israel. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Now also the axe lieth at the root of the trees, ready to strike — an expressive figure of impending judgment. Every tree not bringing forth good fruit is hewn down and cast into the fire; the fire, called unquenchable elsewhere, is the future torment of the impenitent, everlasting punishment, and “cast” carries a strength of just indignation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. John baptized with water unto repentance, but he that cometh after him is mightier, whose sandals — tied and borne by the meanest servants — John is not worthy to bear. He alone shall baptize with the Holy Ghost and with fire: not a separate baptism of hell-fire, but the fiery, searching, refining character of the Spirit's own purifying work on the soul. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. His fan is in his hand, ready for use — the preaching of the Gospel itself, separating solid wheat from worthless chaff. He will throughly purge his floor, the visible church, gather his wheat, his true-hearted saints, into the garner, and burn the chaff, empty professors void of solid principle, with unquenchable fire. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Then cometh Jesus from Galilee to Jordan to be baptized of John, after nearly thirty years spent in privacy at Nazareth, calmly awaiting the Father's appointed time. Unlike Moses, who rashly anticipated his call, he waited until all other applicants that day had been disposed of, that he might not seem merely one of the crowd — separate from sinners even in baptism. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. John was in the act of hindering him: “I have need to be baptized of thee, and comest thou to me?” The emphasis lies in the pronouns — shall the Master come to the servant, the sinless Saviour to a sinner? John regarded Jesus as needing no purification but qualified to impart it, yet bore his testimony in a beautiful spirit, like Peter's “Lord, dost thou wash my feet?” Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. “Suffer it to be so now” — let it pass for the present, startling as the seeming incongruity is. “Thus it becometh us to fulfil all righteousness”: Christ owns every divine institution and, as JFB weighs it, symbolically pledges himself to the whole righteousness of the law, entering on and engaging to fulfil it all. Then John suffered him, yielding to higher authority than his own impressions. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Jesus went up straightway from the water, Luke adding that he was praying — for the work before him, the Spirit to rest upon him, and the Father's glory. The heavens were opened, Mark says cleaving, and he saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove in bodily shape and lighting, indeed abiding, upon him: chaste purity, harmlessness, meekness and beauteousness marking the holy, harmless, undefiled One. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. A voice from heaven said, “This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased” — Mark and Luke give it directly, “Thou art.” The aorist expresses absolute complacency once and for ever felt, best rendered “I delight,” echoing Isaiah 42:1, “Mine Elect, in whom my soul delighteth,” with the Spirit put upon him to bring forth judgment to the Gentiles. At this baptism the three Persons are manifested: the Father confirming the Son as Mediator, the Son entering his work, the Spirit descending to be communicated through him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Investigation (editorial — a parallel question, not a conclusion)

Parallel. The baptism is told in Matthew and also, in the notes' own terms, in Mark and Luke: the heavens were opened, Mark says cleaving, the Spirit descended like a dove in bodily shape, and the voice spoke. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Difference. Matthew gives the voice in the third person, “This is my beloved Son”, while Mark and Luke give it directly, “Thou art”. Luke adds that Jesus was praying. The notes record these wording differences; they do not explain why the wordings differ. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
