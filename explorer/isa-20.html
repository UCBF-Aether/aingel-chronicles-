The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Isaiah 20 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 In the year that Tartan came unto Ashdod, (when Sargon the king of Assyria sent him,) and fought against Ashdod, and took it;

2 At the same time spake the LORD by Isaiah the son of Amoz, saying, Go and loose the sackcloth from off thy loins, and put off thy shoe from thy foot. And he did so, walking naked and barefoot.

3 And the LORD said, Like as my servant Isaiah hath walked naked and barefoot three years for a sign and wonder upon Egypt and upon Ethiopia;

4 So shall the king of Assyria lead away the Egyptians prisoners, and the Ethiopians captives, young and old, naked and barefoot, even with their buttocks uncovered, to the shame of Egypt.

5 And they shall be afraid and ashamed of Ethiopia their expectation, and of Egypt their glory.

6 And the inhabitant of this isle shall say in that day, Behold, such is our expectation, whither we flee for help to be delivered from the king of Assyria: and how shall we escape?

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

As an acted sign, Isaiah walks barefoot and stripped for three years, picturing how Assyria will lead Egypt and Ethiopia away captive. The warning is aimed at Judah's party that trusted Egypt for help against Assyria: if Egypt itself falls, the coastal peoples ask, how will we escape? Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. In the year that Tartan came to Ashdod, when Sargon king of Assyria sent him, and he fought against Ashdod and took it. In Sargon's reign, 722–715 B.C., an Assyrian invasion of Egypt took place, and its success is here foretold to warn the party among the Jews who expected aid from Egypt or Ethiopia; Tartan was probably the general later sent by Sennacherib against Hezekiah, and Ashdod, Greek Azotus on the Mediterranean and one of the five Philistine cities, was the key to Egypt in that quarter, so its taking was a necessary preliminary to invading Egypt, while Sargon himself remained with the Phoenician cities or led the main force into Egypt. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. At the same time the Lord spoke by Isaiah son of Amoz, saying: Go and loose the sackcloth from off your loins and put off your shoe from your foot, and he did so, walking naked and barefoot. The sackcloth is the loose outer garment of coarse dark hair-cloth worn by mourners and prophets, fastened at the waist by a girdle, and naked here means uncovered: Isaiah merely put off the outer sackcloth, retaining the tunic or inner vest, as an emblem that Egypt would be stripped of its possessions, while his very dress was a silent exhortation to repentance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. The Lord said: Like as my servant Isaiah has walked naked and barefoot three years for a sign and wonder upon Egypt and upon Ethiopia. The symbolical action did not continue all this time but at intervals, to keep it before the people's minds during that period — or rather, a three years' sign, a sign that a three years' calamity would come on Egypt and Ethiopia — and this is the only instance of a strictly symbolical act performed by Isaiah, whereas with later prophets such acts were common. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. So will the king of Assyria lead away the Egyptians prisoners and the Ethiopians captives, young and old, naked and barefoot, even with their buttocks uncovered, to the shame of Egypt. Captives are represented thus on Egyptian monuments, where Egypt and Ethiopia appear in alliance as here, and the shameful exposure matches the prophet's own uncovered state. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. They will be afraid and ashamed of Ethiopia their expectation, and of Egypt their glory. They are the Philistine allies of Egypt who trusted in it against Assyria, and a warning to the party among the Jews who, though Judah was then Assyria's subordinate ally, looked to Egypt as preferable: Ethiopia was their expectation, an alliance hoped for but not yet obtained, and Egypt their glory and boast, since that alliance was completed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. The inhabitant of this isle — that is, coast on the Mediterranean, Philistia and perhaps Phoenicia — will say in that day: Behold, such is our expectation, whither we flee for help to be delivered from the king of Assyria, and how will we escape? If Egypt, in which they trusted, was overcome, how will they, a small weak state, escape? The emphatic "we" drives the warning home to Judah's own party of Egyptian trust. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
