The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Matthew 7 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Judge not, that ye be not judged.

2 For with what judgment ye judge, ye shall be judged: and with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again.

3 And why beholdest thou the mote that is in thy brother’s eye, but considerest not the beam that is in thine own eye?

4 Or how wilt thou say to thy brother, Let me pull out the mote out of thine eye; and, behold, a beam is in thine own eye?

5 Thou hypocrite, first cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother’s eye.

6 Give not that which is holy unto the dogs, neither cast ye your pearls before swine, lest they trample them under their feet, and turn again and rend you.

7 Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you:

8 For every one that asketh receiveth; and he that seeketh findeth; and to him that knocketh it shall be opened.

9 Or what man is there of you, whom if his son ask bread, will he give him a stone?

10 Or if he ask a fish, will he give him a serpent?

11 If ye then, being evil, know how to give good gifts unto your children, how much more shall your Father which is in heaven give good things to them that ask him?

12 Therefore all things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them: for this is the law and the prophets.

13 Enter ye in at the strait gate: for wide is the gate, and broad is the way, that leadeth to destruction, and many there be which go in thereat:

14 Because strait is the gate, and narrow is the way, which leadeth unto life, and few there be that find it.

15 Beware of false prophets, which come to you in sheep’s clothing, but inwardly they are ravening wolves.

16 Ye shall know them by their fruits. Do men gather grapes of thorns, or figs of thistles?

17 Even so every good tree bringeth forth good fruit; but a corrupt tree bringeth forth evil fruit.

18 A good tree cannot bring forth evil fruit, neither can a corrupt tree bring forth good fruit.

19 Every tree that bringeth not forth good fruit is hewn down, and cast into the fire.

20 Wherefore by their fruits ye shall know them.

21 Not every one that saith unto me, Lord, Lord, shall enter into the kingdom of heaven; but he that doeth the will of my Father which is in heaven.

22 Many will say to me in that day, Lord, Lord, have we not prophesied in thy name? and in thy name have cast out devils? and in thy name done many wonderful works?

23 And then will I profess unto them, I never knew you: depart from me, ye that work iniquity.

24 Therefore whosoever heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them, I will liken him unto a wise man, which built his house upon a rock:

25 And the rain descended, and the floods came, and the winds blew, and beat upon that house; and it fell not: for it was founded upon a rock.

26 And every one that heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them not, shall be likened unto a foolish man, which built his house upon the sand:

27 And the rain descended, and the floods came, and the winds blew, and beat upon that house; and it fell: and great was the fall of it.

28 And it came to pass, when Jesus had ended these sayings, the people were astonished at his doctrine:

29 For he taught them as one having authority, and not as the scribes.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

The sermon continues with judgment: judge not, take the beam from your own eye before the mote in your brother's, do not give holy things to dogs or cast pearls before swine, and ask, seek and knock, for the Father gives good things to those who ask. It then sets the strait gate against the broad way, warns against false prophets known by their fruits, insists that doing the Father's will, not saying “Lord, Lord”, is what counts, and ends with the wise builder on rock and the foolish on sand, to the amazement of the multitudes at Jesus' authority. What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Judge not, that ye be not judged: what is condemned is not simple judging, nor necessary discrimination for our own guidance, but the disposition to look unfavourably on others' character and actions, springing into rash, unjust, unlovely judgments. The argument confirms it: that your own character be not pronounced upon with like severity at the great day. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. With what judgment ye judge, ye shall be judged, and with what measure ye mete — whatever standard ye apply to others — it shall be measured to you again. This proverbial maxim is a great principle in the divine administration, and harsh judgment is self-punished even here, as people shrink from contact with habitual censurers, fearing they may be next. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Why beholdest thou the mote — splinter, any small fault — in thy brother's eye, but considerest not the beam, the much greater fault, in thine own eye? Some sins are as motes, others as beams; not that any sin is little, for a mote in the eye and a gnat in the throat are both painful and dangerous. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. How wilt thou say, Let me pull out the mote out of thine eye, while a beam is in thine own? Here is a just reproof to those who quarrel with brethren for small faults while allowing themselves greater ones; charity calls our brother's fault a splinter, while true repentance teaches us to call our own a beam. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Thou hypocrite, first cast out the beam out of thine own eye, and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother's eye. The hypocrisy lies in the pretence of zealous, compassionate charity impossible in one who leaves worse faults uncorrected in himself; only he who jealously judges himself is fit to reprove others, and then with reluctance, moderation and love. First reform thyself — the good rule for reprovers. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Give not that which is holy unto the dogs — savage, snarling haters of truth and righteousness — neither cast ye your pearls before swine, the impure or coarse, incapable of appreciating Christianity's priceless jewels, lest they trample them under foot as swine do and turn again and rend you as dogs do. This condemns the opposite extreme to censoriousness, want of discrimination; yet guard against too readily setting neighbours down as dogs and swine to excuse doing them good. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you — a climax of growing importunity, each term presenting desire differently: we ask for what we wish, seek for what we miss, knock for that from which we feel shut out. The difficulty of the foregoing duties of tender, holy, discriminating love recalls prayer with a new turn: “How shall we ever carry out such precepts?” — “Go to God with it.” Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. For every one that asketh receiveth, and he that seeketh findeth, and to him that knocketh it shall be opened — presumed, that he asks aright, in faith, with honest purpose to use what he receives. Whatever you pray for according to the promise shall be given if God see it fit; every one that asketh aright is welcome to the throne of grace, Jew or Gentile, young or old, rich or poor, learned or unlearned. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. What man is there of you, whom if his son ask bread, a loaf, will he give him a stone — round and smooth like such a loaf, but only to mock him? It is explained by a comparison from earthly parents and their readiness to give their children what they ask. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Or if he ask a fish, will he give him a serpent — like it indeed, but only to sting him? Parents are often foolishly fond, but God is all-wise; he knows what we need, what we desire, and what is fit for us, and will never bid us pray and then give what would be hurtful. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. If ye then, being evil, know how to give good gifts unto your children, how much more shall your Father in heaven give good things to them that ask him! Bad as fallen nature is, the father in us is not extinguished; what a heart, then, must the Father of all fathers have towards his pleading children! Luke's “Holy Spirit” instead of “good things” reflects a riper stage of teaching. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Therefore all things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them, for this is the law and the prophets — to say all in one word, the substance of all relative duty, all Scripture in a nutshell, the royal law. It means not our wayward, capricious wishes, but what, in impartial judgment putting ourselves in their place, we consider it reasonable they should do to us. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Enter ye in at the strait gate — as if hardly wide enough to admit one at all, expressing the difficulty of the first right step, a triumph over all natural inclinations; Luke strengthens it, “Strive to enter in.” For wide is the gate, easily entered, and broad is the way, easily trodden, that leadeth to destruction, and many there be which go in thereat, lured by ease and company. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Because strait is the gate and narrow is the way which leadeth unto life, and few there be that find it. The whole course is as difficult as the first step: self denied, body kept under, corruptions mortified, temptations resisted, duties done, much tribulation gone through. Yet this way should invite us all, for it leads to life — present comfort in God's favour and eternal bliss at the end. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Beware of false prophets — teachers coming as authorized expounders of God's mind and guides to heaven — which come in sheep's clothing, a bland, gentle, plausible exterior persuading you the gate is not strait nor the way narrow, but inwardly are ravening wolves, bent on devouring the flock for their own ends. Nothing so prevents entering the strait gate as carnal, soothing, flattering doctrines opposing the truth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Ye shall know them by their fruits — not their doctrines, which correspond to the tree itself, but the practical effect of their teaching, the proper fruit of the tree. Do men gather grapes of thorns, any prickly plant, or figs of thistles? Every tree bears its own fruit, and they may be known by the drift and effects of their doctrines. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. Even so every good tree bringeth forth good fruit, but a corrupt tree bringeth forth evil fruit. Those opinions come not from God that lead to sin; some part of the false teachers' temper and conduct is contrary to the mind of Christ. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. A good tree cannot bring forth evil fruit, neither can a corrupt tree bring forth good fruit — the heart determines and is the only proper interpreter of the actions of our life. Obvious as this is, no teacher of divine truth can insist on it too much against making a merit of actions apart from the motives prompting them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. Every tree that bringeth not forth good fruit is hewn down and cast into the fire, as at 3:10. If not fit for fruit, trees are fit for fuel; the same warning given to the nation now guards the church against fruitless teachers. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Wherefore by their fruits ye shall know them. The point now pressed is not so much the end of such teachers as the means of detecting them — their fruits — and this leads to the solemn warning against religious hypocrisy in general. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. Not every one that saith unto me, Lord, Lord — the reduplication denoting zeal in according the title, which our Lord claims and expects — shall enter into the kingdom of heaven, but he that doeth the will of my Father in heaven, that will this discourse has set forth. It will not be enough to own him as Master in word and tongue; we must believe, repent, live holily and love one another, for this is his will, even our sanctification. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Many will say to me in that day — the day, emphatically unnamed, of entering or not entering the kingdom — Lord, Lord, have we not prophesied, publicly taught with inspired authority, in thy name, and in thy name cast out devils and done many wonderful works? These three highest services, done through his name's power, are pleaded in astonishment at being disowned, each question repeated in the same form. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. Then will I profess unto them, openly proclaiming and tearing off the mask: I never knew you — what they claimed as intimacy he repudiates with scornful dignity, for the acquaintance was never broken off, there never was any. Depart from me, ye that work iniquity — fresh from its scenes and acts as they stand before the Judge. Let every one that nameth Christ's name depart from all sin, lest we perish with a lie in our right hand. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. Therefore whosoever heareth these sayings of mine and doeth them, I will liken him unto a wise man — shrewd, prudent, provident — which built his house upon a rock, the rock of true discipleship, genuine subjection to Christ. This parable teaches hearing and doing: some sayings may seem hard to flesh and blood, but they must be done, for Christ is laid for a foundation and everything besides Christ is sand. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. The rain descended from above, the floods came from below, the winds blew sweeping across, and beat upon that house from every direction, and it fell not, for it was founded upon a rock. There is a storm coming that will try every man's work, but nothing shall separate the wise builder from the love of Christ Jesus. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. Every one that heareth these sayings of mine and doeth them not shall be likened unto a foolish man which built his house upon the sand — a loose foundation of empty profession and mere external services, worldly prosperity, or outward religious profession, all too weak to bear such a fabric as our hopes of heaven. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. The rain descended, the floods came, the winds blew and beat upon that house, and it fell, and great was the fall of it — terrible the ruin, lively imagery to an audience accustomed to the fierceness of an Eastern tempest sweeping everything unsteady before it. The house fell when the builder most needed shelter and expected it, and when it was too late to build another. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. When Jesus had ended these sayings, the people were astonished at his doctrine — his teaching, the reference being to the manner quite as much as the matter. This sermon, ever so often read over, is always new, and every word proves its Author divine. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. For he taught them as one having authority — the inserted “one” only weakens the statement — and not as the scribes. The consciousness of divine authority, as Lawgiver, Expounder and Judge, so beamed through his teaching that the scribes' teaching could not but appear drivelling in such a light. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Investigation (editorial — a parallel question, not a conclusion)

Parallel. The strait gate saying appears in Matthew and, in the note's terms, in Luke as well. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Difference. Luke strengthens the saying to “Strive to enter in”, while Matthew has “Enter ye in at the strait gate”. The note records the stronger Lukan wording without adjudicating which form is earlier. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
