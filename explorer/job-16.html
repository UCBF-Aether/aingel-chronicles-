The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 16 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Then Job answered and said,

2 I have heard many such things: miserable comforters are ye all.

3 Shall vain words have an end? or what emboldeneth thee that thou answerest?

4 I also could speak as ye do: if your soul were in my soul’s stead, I could heap up words against you, and shake mine head at you.

5 But I would strengthen you with my mouth, and the moving of my lips should asswage your grief.

6 Though I speak, my grief is not asswaged: and though I forbear, what am I eased?

7 But now he hath made me weary: thou hast made desolate all my company.

8 And thou hast filled me with wrinkles, which is a witness against me: and my leanness rising up in me beareth witness to my face.

9 He teareth me in his wrath, who hateth me: he gnasheth upon me with his teeth; mine enemy sharpeneth his eyes upon me.

10 They have gaped upon me with their mouth; they have smitten me upon the cheek reproachfully; they have gathered themselves together against me.

11 God hath delivered me to the ungodly, and turned me over into the hands of the wicked.

12 I was at ease, but he hath broken me asunder: he hath also taken me by my neck, and shaken me to pieces, and set me up for his mark.

13 His archers compass me round about, he cleaveth my reins asunder, and doth not spare; he poureth out my gall upon the ground.

14 He breaketh me with breach upon breach, he runneth upon me like a giant.

15 I have sewed sackcloth upon my skin, and defiled my horn in the dust.

16 My face is foul with weeping, and on my eyelids is the shadow of death;

17 Not for any injustice in mine hands: also my prayer is pure.

18 O earth, cover not thou my blood, and let my cry have no place.

19 Also now, behold, my witness is in heaven, and my record is on high.

20 My friends scorn me: but mine eye poureth out tears unto God.

21 O that one might plead for a man with God, as a man pleadeth for his neighbour!

22 When a few years are come, then I shall go the way whence I shall not return.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Job answers Eliphaz’s second speech with its famous verdict: miserable comforters are you all. If their places were reversed, he says, he would strengthen them with his mouth and move his head in sympathy — instead they shake their heads and gape at him. God, he says, has delivered him to the ungodly, yet he appeals past his mocking friends to God himself as his witness on high. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Job answers Eliphaz, rejecting his second speech as he rejected the first. The reply names the friends' comfort for what it is. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Job has heard many such things: miserable comforters are you all. JFB leaves the verdict to stand on its own, the friends' speeches being the miserable comfort in question. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Shall vain words have an end, or what emboldens you that you answer? JFB notes Job retorts Eliphaz's reproach of words of wind, and emboldeneth as what wearies you so that you contradict, or what provoked you. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Job also could speak as the friends do, heap up words against them, and shake his head at them. JFB renders heap up as marshalling an army of words, and shaking the head as nodding in Eastern scorn. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. But Job would strengthen them with his mouth, and the moving of his lips would assuage their grief. JFB calls this bitter irony: Job could offer the same heartless mouth-strengthening, opposed to strengthening with the heart, real consolation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Though Job speaks, his grief is not assuaged, and though he forbears, what goes from him? JFB renders eased literally as what portion of his sufferings goes from him — nothing, either way. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. But now God has made Job weary, and has desolated all his company. JFB renders but now as ah, and company as a band of witnesses who could attest his innocence, his children and servants, now cut off. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. God has filled Job with wrinkles, which rise up against him as a witness, and his leanness testifies to his face. JFB explains the Hebrew as fettered: God has disabled him by pains from attesting his innocence, and his leanness, also meaning lie, is a false witness construed as proof of guilt. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. He tears Job in his wrath and hates him, gnashing upon him with his teeth, while his enemy sharpens his eyes upon him. JFB gives the wild beast image, and renders hateth as pursues hard, with God sharpening his eyes as an enemy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Men have gaped upon Job with their mouth, smitten him upon the cheek reproachfully, and gathered themselves against him. JFB notes gaping is to mock, not devour, smiting the cheek is contemptuous abuse, and gathering is conspiring unanimously. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. God has delivered Job to the ungodly and turned him over into the hands of the wicked. JFB identifies the ungodly as his professed friends persecuting him with unkind speeches, and turned over as cast headlong into the wicked's hands. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Job was at ease, but God broke him asunder, took him by the neck, shook him to pieces, and set him up for his mark. JFB notes the animal seizing prey by the neck, and the mark as God letting Job recover strength only to torment him ceaselessly. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. God's archers compass Job round about, cleaving his reins asunder without sparing, and pouring out his gall upon the ground. JFB continues the mark image: God is accompanied by the three friends, whose words wound like sharp arrows, and gall stands for a vital part. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. God breaks Job with breach upon breach, running upon him like a giant. JFB gives the fortress image: storming walls by making breaches, with the giant as a mighty warrior. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Job has sewed sackcloth upon his skin and defiled his horn in the dust. JFB explains the sack as closely sewed to the body, and the horn, emblem of power, thrown in the dust as humiliation from former greatness, joined with the fury of a horned beast tearing earth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Job's face is foul with weeping, and on his eyelids is the shadow of death. JFB renders foul as red, flushed and heated, and the shadow of death as darkening through many tears. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. Not for any injustice in Job's hands, and his prayer is pure. The claim stands against the friends' charge: his hands hold no injustice, his prayer is pure. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. O earth, cover not Job's blood, and let his cry have no place. JFB explains his blood as his undeserved suffering, compared to a murdered man whose blood the earth refuses to drink until avenged, and no place as no resting-place for his cry. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. Also now, behold, Job's witness is in heaven, and his record is on high. JFB notes that even now, greatly misunderstood on earth, God in heaven is sensible of his innocence, and the record is in the high places — his witness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Job's friends scorn him, but his eye pours out tears unto God. JFB renders the friends as his mockers, a heart-cutting paradox, leaving God alone to attest his innocence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. Oh that one might plead for a man with God, as a man pleads for his neighbour! JFB renders one as He, God himself: God must support Job against God, since he makes him suffer yet alone knows his innocence, prefiguring the advocacy of Christ. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. When a few years are come, Job shall go the way whence he shall not return. JFB notes few as years of number, opposed to numberless: his remaining years are countable and short. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Where the speakers disagree

Eliphaz, in his second speech, treats Job’s words as wind that condemns him. Job answers that such comfort is miserable: if the friends were in his place, he would strengthen them with his mouth, not shake his head at them. He appeals past them to God as his witness — the friends offer doctrine, Job asks for an advocate. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
