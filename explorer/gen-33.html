The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Genesis 33 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 And Jacob lifted up his eyes, and looked, and, behold, Esau came, and with him four hundred men. And he divided the children unto Leah, and unto Rachel, and unto the two handmaids.

2 And he put the handmaids and their children foremost, and Leah and her children after, and Rachel and Joseph hindermost.

3 And he passed over before them, and bowed himself to the ground seven times, until he came near to his brother.

4 And Esau ran to meet him, and embraced him, and fell on his neck, and kissed him: and they wept.

5 And he lifted up his eyes, and saw the women and the children; and said, Who are those with thee? And he said, The children which God hath graciously given thy servant.

6 Then the handmaidens came near, they and their children, and they bowed themselves.

7 And Leah also with her children came near, and bowed themselves: and after came Joseph near and Rachel, and they bowed themselves.

8 And he said, What meanest thou by all this drove which I met? And he said, These are to find grace in the sight of my lord.

9 And Esau said, I have enough, my brother; keep that thou hast unto thyself.

10 And Jacob said, Nay, I pray thee, if now I have found grace in thy sight, then receive my present at my hand: for therefore I have seen thy face, as though I had seen the face of God, and thou wast pleased with me.

11 Take, I pray thee, my blessing that is brought to thee; because God hath dealt graciously with me, and because I have enough. And he urged him, and he took it.

12 And he said, Let us take our journey, and let us go, and I will go before thee.

13 And he said unto him, My lord knoweth that the children are tender, and the flocks and herds with young are with me: and if men should overdrive them one day, all the flock will die.

14 Let my lord, I pray thee, pass over before his servant: and I will lead on softly, according as the cattle that goeth before me and the children be able to endure, until I come unto my lord unto Seir.

15 And Esau said, Let me now leave with thee some of the folk that are with me. And he said, What needeth it? let me find grace in the sight of my lord.

16 So Esau returned that day on his way unto Seir.

17 And Jacob journeyed to Succoth, and built him an house, and made booths for his cattle: therefore the name of the place is called Succoth.

18 And Jacob came to Shalem, a city of Shechem, which is in the land of Canaan, when he came from Padanaram; and pitched his tent before the city.

19 And he bought a parcel of a field, where he had spread his tent, at the hand of the children of Hamor, Shechem’s father, for an hundred pieces of money.

20 And he erected there an altar, and called it EleloheIsrael.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Jacob meets Esau, bowing seven times, and Esau runs to embrace him and weep; Jacob's gifts are at last accepted and the brothers part in peace. Jacob settles at Shechem, buys ground, and raises an altar he calls El-elohe-Israel, God, the God of Israel. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Jacob looked up and saw Esau coming with four hundred men, and arranged his children with Leah, Rachel, and the maidservants. JFB notes he placed the dearest last, so they would be least exposed, and his fears were now gone after prevailing with God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. The maidservants and their children went foremost, Leah and hers next, and Rachel and Joseph last. The order was a father's protective care in the face of an uncertain meeting. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Jacob himself went ahead of them all and bowed to the ground seven times as he approached his brother. JFB describes the Eastern obeisance: bowing with the body parallel to the ground, advancing, and bowing again until standing before the superior. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Esau ran to meet Jacob, embraced him, fell on his neck, kissed him, and they wept. Henry's point is that God holds all hearts in his hands and can turn an enemy to peace when a man's ways please the Lord. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Esau asked who the women and children were, and Jacob answered that they were the children God had graciously given his servant. JFB notes Jacob could not give even a common answer without the language of piety. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. The maidservants came near with their children and bowed. The whole household now shared in the humble, respectful greeting. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Leah with her children came near and bowed, and then Joseph and Rachel bowed as well. Each group in turn acknowledged Esau as the elder brother. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Esau asked what Jacob meant by all the droves he had met, and Jacob said they were to find grace in his lord's sight. The present was presented as a plea for favour, not as payment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Esau replied that he had enough and told Jacob to keep what he had for himself. The refusal at first showed a brother content, not a creditor demanding settlement. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Jacob urged Esau to receive the present, saying that seeing Esau's face in favour was like seeing the face of God. The comparison expresses relief: a dreaded meeting had turned gracious. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Jacob pressed his blessing on Esau, because God had dealt graciously with him and he had enough, and Esau took it. JFB notes that in the East a superior's acceptance of a gift is proof of friendship and, from an enemy, of reconciliation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Esau proposed that they travel together, with himself going before Jacob, as escort and companion. JFB reads this as both friendship and protection, which Jacob prudently declined. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Jacob pleaded that the children were tender and the flocks and herds with young could not be overdriven for a day without dying. Henry uses this tender care as a picture of the good Shepherd who gently leads those with young. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Jacob asked Esau to go on ahead, while he followed softly at the pace of cattle and children until he came to Esau in Seir. JFB notes Jacob may have meant to visit Seir by a roundabout route, but whether he did so is not recorded. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Esau offered to leave some of his men with Jacob, but Jacob asked what need there was, seeking only grace in his lord's sight. Henry warns against too close an intimacy with ungodly relations that could become a snare or an offence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. So Esau returned that day on his way to Seir, and the brothers parted in peace. The parting closed twenty years of estrangement without a quarrel. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. Jacob journeyed to Succoth, built a house, and made booths for his cattle, which gave the place its name. JFB notes Succoth means booths, the first station where Jacob halted on arriving in Canaan. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. Jacob came safely to Shalem, a city of Shechem in Canaan, and pitched his tent before the city. JFB notes Shalem may mean peace, a tribute to the God who had promised a safe return, or it may be a place-name near Shechem. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. He bought the parcel of ground where he had spread his tent from Hamor's sons for a hundred pieces of money. JFB notes he was thus the first patriarch to become a landowner in Canaan, and the price was literally in lambs, probably coin bearing a lamb's figure. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. There Jacob built an altar and called it El-elohe-Israel, God, the God of Israel. Henry and JFB agree this was real thanks, not words only: where Jacob pitched a tent, God had an altar. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
