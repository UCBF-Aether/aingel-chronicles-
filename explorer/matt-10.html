The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Matthew 10 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 And when he had called unto him his twelve disciples, he gave them power against unclean spirits, to cast them out, and to heal all manner of sickness and all manner of disease.

2 Now the names of the twelve apostles are these; The first, Simon, who is called Peter, and Andrew his brother; James the son of Zebedee, and John his brother;

3 Philip, and Bartholomew; Thomas, and Matthew the publican; James the son of Alphaeus, and Lebbaeus, whose surname was Thaddaeus;

4 Simon the Canaanite, and Judas Iscariot, who also betrayed him.

5 These twelve Jesus sent forth, and commanded them, saying, Go not into the way of the Gentiles, and into any city of the Samaritans enter ye not:

6 But go rather to the lost sheep of the house of Israel.

7 And as ye go, preach, saying, The kingdom of heaven is at hand.

8 Heal the sick, cleanse the lepers, raise the dead, cast out devils: freely ye have received, freely give.

9 Provide neither gold, nor silver, nor brass in your purses,

10 Nor scrip for your journey, neither two coats, neither shoes, nor yet staves: for the workman is worthy of his meat.

11 And into whatsoever city or town ye shall enter, enquire who in it is worthy; and there abide till ye go thence.

12 And when ye come into an house, salute it.

13 And if the house be worthy, let your peace come upon it: but if it be not worthy, let your peace return to you.

14 And whosoever shall not receive you, nor hear your words, when ye depart out of that house or city, shake off the dust of your feet.

15 Verily I say unto you, It shall be more tolerable for the land of Sodom and Gomorrha in the day of judgment, than for that city.

16 Behold, I send you forth as sheep in the midst of wolves: be ye therefore wise as serpents, and harmless as doves.

17 But beware of men: for they will deliver you up to the councils, and they will scourge you in their synagogues;

18 And ye shall be brought before governors and kings for my sake, for a testimony against them and the Gentiles.

19 But when they deliver you up, take no thought how or what ye shall speak: for it shall be given you in that same hour what ye shall speak.

20 For it is not ye that speak, but the Spirit of your Father which speaketh in you.

21 And the brother shall deliver up the brother to death, and the father the child: and the children shall rise up against their parents, and cause them to be put to death.

22 And ye shall be hated of all men for my name’s sake: but he that endureth to the end shall be saved.

23 But when they persecute you in this city, flee ye into another: for verily I say unto you, Ye shall not have gone over the cities of Israel, till the Son of man be come.

24 The disciple is not above his master, nor the servant above his lord.

25 It is enough for the disciple that he be as his master, and the servant as his lord. If they have called the master of the house Beelzebub, how much more shall they call them of his household?

26 Fear them not therefore: for there is nothing covered, that shall not be revealed; and hid, that shall not be known.

27 What I tell you in darkness, that speak ye in light: and what ye hear in the ear, that preach ye upon the housetops.

28 And fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell.

29 Are not two sparrows sold for a farthing? and one of them shall not fall on the ground without your Father.

30 But the very hairs of your head are all numbered.

31 Fear ye not therefore, ye are of more value than many sparrows.

32 Whosoever therefore shall confess me before men, him will I confess also before my Father which is in heaven.

33 But whosoever shall deny me before men, him will I also deny before my Father which is in heaven.

34 Think not that I am come to send peace on earth: I came not to send peace, but a sword.

35 For I am come to set a man at variance against his father, and the daughter against her mother, and the daughter in law against her mother in law.

36 And a man’s foes shall be they of his own household.

37 He that loveth father or mother more than me is not worthy of me: and he that loveth son or daughter more than me is not worthy of me.

38 And he that taketh not his cross, and followeth after me, is not worthy of me.

39 He that findeth his life shall lose it: and he that loseth his life for my sake shall find it.

40 He that receiveth you receiveth me, and he that receiveth me receiveth him that sent me.

41 He that receiveth a prophet in the name of a prophet shall receive a prophet’s reward; and he that receiveth a righteous man in the name of a righteous man shall receive a righteous man’s reward.

42 And whosoever shall give to drink unto one of these little ones a cup of cold water only in the name of a disciple, verily I say unto you, he shall in no wise lose his reward.

Every substantive historical claim in the editorial panels below carries one of three labels, plus a source reference:

  • Documented — directly supported by a cited source.
  • Interpreted — a reasoned explanation or proposed relationship.
  • Unresolved — evidence is insufficient to establish the claim.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Jesus sends out the twelve apostles by name, first to the lost sheep of the house of Israel, with power to heal, cleanse lepers, raise the dead and cast out devils, freely given and freely to give, taking no money, bag or extra coat, and shaking off the dust where they are not received. He warns them of persecution before councils and kings, of betrayal within households, and of the sword and cross that following him brings, and promises that even a cup of cold water given to a little one shall not lose its reward. What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. When he had called unto him his twelve disciples, he gave them power — both power and authority, or right: he both qualified and authorized them — against unclean spirits, to cast them out, and to heal all manner of sickness and disease. “Apostle” signifies messenger, sent to proclaim his kingdom, and in the grace of the gospel there is a salve for every sore. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Now the names of the twelve apostles are these: the first, Simon, who is called Peter, and Andrew his brother; James the son of Zebedee, and John his brother. Matthew gives the names here in connection with the Mission, not the appointment, and their names are recorded to their honour — yet they had more reason to rejoice their names were written in heaven. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Philip, and Bartholomew — Bartholomew being a family surname, the same as Nathanael of Cana, who follows Philip in every list, Philip having brought him to Jesus; Thomas, and Matthew the publican — in none of the four lists is he so branded but in his own, as if he would have all know how deep a debtor he had been; James son of Alphaeus, apparently Cleopas, husband of Mary sister to the Virgin, so James the Less was the Lord's cousin; and Lebbaeus, surnamed Thaddaeus, the same as Judas brother of James, “Judas, not Iscariot.” Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Simon the Canaanite — better, the Zealot, the Aramaic term for one of the sect who bound themselves as a voluntary ecclesiastical police to see the law not broken with impunity; and Judas Iscariot, Judas of Kerioth in Judah, who also betrayed him, a note of infamy attached to his name in all the catalogues. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. These twelve Jesus sent forth, and commanded them: Go not into the way of the Gentiles, and into any city of the Samaritans enter ye not. The Samaritans, Gentiles by blood descended from Assyria's transports, had adopted the Jewish religion with admixtures, standing intermediate between Gentiles and Jews; the restraint was only for this first mission, until Pentecost's order — Jerusalem, Judea, Samaria, uttermost earth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. But go rather to the lost sheep of the house of Israel. Until Christ's death broke down the middle wall of partition, the commission was to the Jews only, who, though the visible people of God, were lost sheep not merely as all sinners are, but as abandoned to wander by faithless shepherds. The Gentiles must not have the gospel brought them till the Jews have refused it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. As ye go, preach, saying, The kingdom of heaven is at hand. They preached to establish the faith; the kingdom, to animate the hope; of heaven, to inspire love of heavenly things and contempt of earthly; at hand, that men may prepare without delay. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Heal the sick, cleanse the lepers, raise the dead, cast out devils — the first communication of supernatural power by Christ himself to his followers, anticipating Pentecost, and right royally dispensed. Freely ye have received, freely give: an apple of gold in a setting of silver; in proclaiming free grace we must above all avoid the appearance of a hireling spirit. Miracles confirmed doctrine, showing its intent was to heal sick souls and raise the dead in sin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Provide neither gold, nor silver, nor brass in your purses — literally your belts, in which money was kept. They were to go without provision for the journey, trusting the mission's Sender rather than their own stores. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Nor scrip for your journey, the traveller's bag for provisions, neither two coats, tunics worn next the skin — take no change of dress — neither shoes, change of them, nor yet staves: the true reading is “a staff,” meaning they were not to procure even that expressly for the journey, but go with what they had. For the workman is worthy of his meat, his maintenance, a principle universally recognized and authoritatively applied to the Lord's workmen, cited by Paul as Scripture. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Into whatsoever city or town ye shall enter, enquire carefully who in it is worthy — meet to entertain such messengers, not in rank but in congenial disposition — and there abide till ye go thence, not shifting about as if discontented, but returning the welcome with a courteous, contented disposition. The servant of Christ is the ambassador of peace to whatever place he is sent, and it behoves him to find out the best persons in every place. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. When ye come into an house — not the worthy house, but the house ye first enter, to try if it be worthy — salute it, showing the usual civilities. His message is even to the vilest sinners, yet he must conduct himself courteously to all. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. If the house be worthy, showing this by giving you welcome, let your peace come upon it — the ancient Eastern salutation, “Peace be to this house,” meaning from Christ's messengers something far higher, both in gift and giving, than the current salutation. But if it be not worthy, let your peace return to you: if peace finds a shut door, take it back to yourselves who know how to value it, and it will taste sweeter for having been offered though rejected. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Whosoever shall not receive you, nor hear your words, when ye depart out of that house or city, shake off the dust of your feet — for a testimony against them, as Mark and Luke add. By this symbolical action they vividly shook themselves from all connection with such and all responsibility for the guilt of rejecting them and their message. The whole counsel of God must be declared, and those who will not attend must be shown their state is dangerous, lest their privileges only increase their condemnation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Verily, it shall be more tolerable for the land of Sodom and Gomorrha in the day of judgment, than for that city. Those Cities of the Plain, given to flames for loathsome impurities, shall be treated as less criminal than places which, though morally respectable, reject the gospel message and affront its bearers. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Behold, I send you forth as sheep in the midst of wolves — defenceless among those ready to make a prey of you; that sheep should be sent among wolves sounds strange indeed, hence “Behold.” Be ye therefore wise as serpents, and harmless as doves: alone, serpent wisdom is mere cunning and dove harmlessness little better than weakness, but combined, wisdom saves from unnecessary exposure and harmlessness from sinful expedients to escape it. Disciples hated as serpents need the serpent's wisdom, yet must do nobody hurt and bear nobody ill-will. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. Beware of men: for they will deliver you up to the councils, the local courts and civil magistrates in general, and they will scourge you in their synagogues — persecution at the hands of the ecclesiastics. They were to avoid all things giving advantage to enemies, all meddling with worldly or political concerns, all appearance of evil or selfishness, and all underhand measures. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. Ye shall be brought before governors, provincial rulers, and kings, the highest tribunals, for my sake, for a testimony against them — rather, to them, to bear testimony to the truth and its glorious effects — and the Gentiles, a hint their message would not long be confined to Israel. The Acts of the Apostles are the best commentary on these warnings. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. When they deliver you up, take no thought — be not solicitous or anxious — how or what ye shall speak, either in what manner to make your defence or of what matter it shall consist, for it shall be given you in that same hour what ye shall speak. When God calls us to speak for him, we may depend on him to teach us what to say; disciples must think more how to do well than how to speak well. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. For it is not ye that speak, but the Spirit of your Father which speaketh in you. How remarkably this has been verified, the whole history of persecution thrillingly proclaims, from Acts to the latest martyrology. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. The brother shall deliver up the brother to death, and the father the child: and the children shall rise up against their parents, and cause them to be put to death, for example by lodging information with the authorities. The deepest hostility of the old nature to the new was to issue in awful wrenches of the dearest ties; persecutors are worse than beasts, preying upon their own kind, and sufferings from friends and relations cut most deeply. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Ye shall be hated of all men for my name's sake: but he that endureth to the end shall be saved. The universality of this hatred shows it owing to no temporary excitement or personal prejudice, and no discretion consistent with fidelity would stifle it, though it might soften its violence. Enduring to the end is the proper evidence of a profession's reality, as drawing back unto perdition evidences want of root from the first; they may be borne to the end because sufferers shall be borne up under them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. When they persecute you in this city, flee ye into another. In great peril disciples may go out of the way of danger, though never out of the way of duty, and no sinful means may be used to escape, for then it is not a door of God's opening. For verily, ye shall not have gone over the cities of Israel, till the Son of man be come: “the coming of the Son of man” here refers immediately to the crisis of Israel's history as the visible kingdom, when Christ would come and judge it and on Jerusalem's ruins establish his own kingdom, a lively anticipation of his second coming. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. The disciple is not above his master, nor the servant above his lord — a maxim our Lord repeats in various connections. Christ has dealt fairly with us in telling us the worst we can meet with in his service, and would have us deal so with ourselves in sitting down and counting the cost. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. It is enough for the disciple that he be as his master, and the servant as his lord. If they have called the master of the house Beelzebub — rightly Beelzebul, a name become a synonym of Satan, since all idolatry was regarded as devil worship — how much more shall they call them of his household, the inmates. Teacher and disciples, Lord and servants, Master and household are so bound up together that they cannot look to fare better than he, and should think it enough if they fare no worse. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. Fear them not therefore: for there is nothing covered, that shall not be revealed; and hid, that shall not be known. There is no use or need of concealing anything; right and wrong, truth and error are about to come into open and deadly collision, and the day is coming when all hidden things shall be disclosed and every one have his due. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. What I tell you in darkness, in the privacy of teaching for which men are not yet ripe, that speak ye in the light; and what ye hear in the ear, that preach ye upon the housetops. Give free and fearless utterance to all I have taught you: they must deliver their message publicly, for all are deeply concerned in the gospel, and the whole counsel of God must be made known. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. Fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul — Luke adds, “and after that have no more that they can do” — but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell. This is decisive proof of a hell for body as well as soul in the eternal world, torment with elements adapted to the material as well as spiritual part of our nature, both existing for ever. The fear of man brings a snare that disturbs peace and draws into sin, and must be striven and prayed against; tribulation cannot take away God's love to them or theirs to him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. Are not two sparrows sold for a farthing? and one of them shall not fall on the ground — exhausted or killed — without your Father; Luke says not one is forgotten before God. See how Providence extends to all creatures, even sparrows; this should silence all fears of God's people. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. But the very hairs of your head are all numbered. This denotes the account God takes and keeps of his people, as in the language of 1 Samuel 14:45 and Acts 27:34. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 31. Fear ye not therefore, ye are of more value than many sparrows. Was ever language of such simplicity felt to carry such weight? Here lies much of the charm and power of our Lord's teaching; ye are of more value than many sparrows, and the strength shall be according to the day. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 32. Whosoever therefore shall confess me before men, despising the shame, him will I confess also before my Father which is in heaven — I will not be ashamed of him, but own him before the most august of all assemblies. It is our duty not only to believe in Christ but to profess that faith, in suffering for him when called to it as well as in serving him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 33. But whosoever shall deny me before men, him will I also deny before my Father which is in heaven — he shall have from me his own treatment of me on earth. That denial only is meant which is persisted in, and that confession only can have the blessed recompense promised which is the real and constant language of faith and love. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 34. Think not that I am come to send peace on earth: I came not to send peace, but a sword — strife, discord, conflict, deadly opposition between eternally hostile principles, penetrating and rending asunder the dearest ties. Religion is worth everything; all who believe its truth will come up to the price and make everything else yield to it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 35. For I am come to set a man at variance against his father, and the daughter against her mother, and the daughter in law against her mother in law. The strongest bonds of love and duty have often been broken through from enmity against Christ, and all who will live godly in Christ Jesus must suffer persecution. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 36. And a man's foes shall be they of his own household — quoted from Micah 7:6, an extension of the Psalmist's complaint, most affectingly illustrated in Judas's treason against our Lord. Hence arises the necessity of a choice between Christ and the nearest relations, putting them to the severest test. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 37. He that loveth father or mother more than me is not worthy of me: and he that loveth son or daughter more than me is not worthy of me. As preference of the one would necessitate abandonment of the other, our Lord with sublime yet awful self-respect asserts his own claims to supreme affection; those best prepared for the life to come sit most loose to this present life. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 38. He that taketh not his cross, and followeth after me, is not worthy of me. We are so accustomed to “taking up one's cross” as preparedness for trials in general that we lose its primary sense here: preparedness to go forth even to crucifixion, as our Lord bore his own cross to Calvary — remarkable, since he had not yet hinted he would die this death, nor was crucifixion a Jewish punishment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 39. He that findeth his life shall lose it: and he that loseth his life for my sake shall find it. The paradox turns on “life” in a double sense, lower and higher, natural and spiritual, temporal and eternal: entire sacrifice of the lower, or willingness to make it, is indispensable to preserving the higher, and he who cannot surrender the one for the other shall eventually lose both. Christ will lead us through sufferings to glory with him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 40. He that receiveth you receiveth me, and he that receiveth me receiveth him that sent me. As an ambassador's treatment expresses the light in which his sender is viewed, so your authority is mine, as mine is my Father's. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 41. He that receiveth a prophet in the name of a prophet shall receive a prophet's reward. A prophet is one divinely commissioned to deliver a message from heaven, predicting future events being no necessary part of the office; receiving him for his office's sake and love to his master is an encouragement to those who are not prophets. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 42. And whosoever shall give to drink unto one of these little ones a cup of cold water only in the name of a disciple, verily he shall in no wise lose his reward. “Little ones” denotes their lowliness in spirit and littleness in an undiscerning world's eyes, while high in Heaven's esteem; here is a descending climax — prophet, righteous man, little one — signifying that however low we come down in services to those that are Christ's, all done for his sake and bearing love to his name shall be divinely appreciated and rewarded. Though kindness ever so small, if there be occasion and ability to do no more, it shall be accepted, not as deserved, but from God's free gift. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

For the workman is worthy of his meat, his maintenance, a principle universally recognized and authoritatively applied to the Lord's workmen, cited by Paul as Scripture. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Into whatsoever city or town ye shall enter, enquire carefully who in it is worthy — meet to entertain such messengers, not in rank but in congenial disposition — and there abide till ye go thence, not shifting about as if discontented, but returning the welcome with a courteous, contented disposition. The servant of Christ is the ambassador of peace to whatever place he is sent, and it behoves him to find out the best persons in every place. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. When ye come into an house — not the worthy house, but the house ye first enter, to try if it be worthy — salute it, showing the usual civilities. His message is even to the vilest sinners, yet he must conduct himself courteously to all. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. If the house be worthy, showing this by giving you welcome, let your peace come upon it — the ancient Eastern salutation, “Peace be to this house,” meaning from Christ's messengers something far higher, both in gift and giving, than the current salutation. But if it be not worthy, let your peace return to you: if peace finds a shut door, take it back to yourselves who know how to value it, and it will taste sweeter for having been offered though rejected. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Whosoever shall not receive you, nor hear your words, when ye depart out of that house or city, shake off the dust of your feet — for a testimony against them, as Mark and Luke add. By this symbolical action they vividly shook themselves from all connection with such and all responsibility for the guilt of rejecting them and their message. The whole counsel of God must be declared, and those who will not attend must be shown their state is dangerous, lest their privileges only increase their condemnation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Verily, it shall be more tolerable for the land of Sodom and Gomorrha in the day of judgment, than for that city. Those Cities of the Plain, given to flames for loathsome impurities, shall be treated as less criminal than places which, though morally respectable, reject the gospel message and affront its bearers. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Behold, I send you forth as sheep in the midst of wolves — defenceless among those ready to make a prey of you; that sheep should be sent among wolves sounds strange indeed, hence “Behold.” Be ye therefore wise as serpents, and harmless as doves: alone, serpent wisdom is mere cunning and dove harmlessness little better than weakness, but combined, wisdom saves from unnecessary exposure and harmlessness from sinful expedients to escape it. Disciples hated as serpents need the serpent's wisdom, yet must do nobody hurt and bear nobody ill-will. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. Beware of men: for they will deliver you up to the councils, the local courts and civil magistrates in general, and they will scourge you in their synagogues — persecution at the hands of the ecclesiastics. They were to avoid all things giving advantage to enemies, all meddling with worldly or political concerns, all appearance of evil or selfishness, and all underhand measures. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. Ye shall be brought before governors, provincial rulers, and kings, the highest tribunals, for my sake, for a testimony against them — rather, to them, to bear testimony to the truth and its glorious effects — and the Gentiles, a hint their message would not long be confined to Israel. The Acts of the Apostles are the best commentary on these warnings. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. When they deliver you up, take no thought — be not solicitous or anxious — how or what ye shall speak, either in what manner to make your defence or of what matter it shall consist, for it shall be given you in that same hour what ye shall speak. When God calls us to speak for him, we may depend on him to teach us what to say; disciples must think more how to do well than how to speak well. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. For it is not ye that speak, but the Spirit of your Father which speaketh in you. How remarkably this has been verified, the whole history of persecution thrillingly proclaims, from Acts to the latest martyrology. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. The brother shall deliver up the brother to death, and the father the child: and the children shall rise up against their parents, and cause them to be put to death, for example by lodging information with the authorities. The deepest hostility of the old nature to the new was to issue in awful wrenches of the dearest ties; persecutors are worse than beasts, preying upon their own kind, and sufferings from friends and relations cut most deeply. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Ye shall be hated of all men for my name's sake: but he that endureth to the end shall be saved. The universality of this hatred shows it owing to no temporary excitement or personal prejudice, and no discretion consistent with fidelity would stifle it, though it might soften its violence. Enduring to the end is the proper evidence of a profession's reality, as drawing back unto perdition evidences want of root from the first; they may be borne to the end because sufferers shall be borne up under them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. When they persecute you in this city, flee ye into another. In great peril disciples may go out of the way of danger, though never out of the way of duty, and no sinful means may be used to escape, for then it is not a door of God's opening. For verily, ye shall not have gone over the cities of Israel, till the Son of man be come: “the coming of the Son of man” here refers immediately to the crisis of Israel's history as the visible kingdom, when Christ would come and judge it and on Jerusalem's ruins establish his own kingdom, a lively anticipation of his second coming. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. The disciple is not above his master, nor the servant above his lord — a maxim our Lord repeats in various connections. Christ has dealt fairly with us in telling us the worst we can meet with in his service, and would have us deal so with ourselves in sitting down and counting the cost. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. It is enough for the disciple that he be as his master, and the servant as his lord. If they have called the master of the house Beelzebub — rightly Beelzebul, a name become a synonym of Satan, since all idolatry was regarded as devil worship — how much more shall they call them of his household, the inmates. Teacher and disciples, Lord and servants, Master and household are so bound up together that they cannot look to fare better than he, and should think it enough if they fare no worse. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. Fear them not therefore: for there is nothing covered, that shall not be revealed; and hid, that shall not be known. There is no use or need of concealing anything; right and wrong, truth and error are about to come into open and deadly collision, and the day is coming when all hidden things shall be disclosed and every one have his due. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. What I tell you in darkness, in the privacy of teaching for which men are not yet ripe, that speak ye in the light; and what ye hear in the ear, that preach ye upon the housetops. Give free and fearless utterance to all I have taught you: they must deliver their message publicly, for all are deeply concerned in the gospel, and the whole counsel of God must be made known. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. Fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul — Luke adds, “and after that have no more that they can do” — but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell. This is decisive proof of a hell for body as well as soul in the eternal world, torment with elements adapted to the material as well as spiritual part of our nature, both existing for ever. The fear of man brings a snare that disturbs peace and draws into sin, and must be striven and prayed against; tribulation cannot take away God's love to them or theirs to him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. Are not two sparrows sold for a farthing? and one of them shall not fall on the ground — exhausted or killed — without your Father; Luke says not one is forgotten before God. See how Providence extends to all creatures, even sparrows; this should silence all fears of God's people. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. But the very hairs of your head are all numbered. This denotes the account God takes and keeps of his people, as in the language of 1 Samuel 14:45 and Acts 27:34. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 31. Fear ye not therefore, ye are of more value than many sparrows. Was ever language of such simplicity felt to carry such weight? Here lies much of the charm and power of our Lord's teaching; ye are of more value than many sparrows, and the strength shall be according to the day. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 32. Whosoever therefore shall confess me before men, despising the shame, him will I confess also before my Father which is in heaven — I will not be ashamed of him, but own him before the most august of all assemblies. It is our duty not only to believe in Christ but to profess that faith, in suffering for him when called to it as well as in serving him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 33. But whosoever shall deny me before men, him will I also deny before my Father which is in heaven — he shall have from me his own treatment of me on earth. That denial only is meant which is persisted in, and that confession only can have the blessed recompense promised which is the real and constant language of faith and love. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 34. Think not that I am come to send peace on earth: I came not to send peace, but a sword — strife, discord, conflict, deadly opposition between eternally hostile principles, penetrating and rending asunder the dearest ties. Religion is worth everything; all who believe its truth will come up to the price and make everything else yield to it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 35. For I am come to set a man at variance against his father, and the daughter against her mother, and the daughter in law against her mother in law. The strongest bonds of love and duty have often been broken through from enmity against Christ, and all who will live godly in Christ Jesus must suffer persecution. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 36. And a man's foes shall be they of his own household — quoted from Micah 7:6, an extension of the Psalmist's complaint, most affectingly illustrated in Judas's treason against our Lord. Hence arises the necessity of a choice between Christ and the nearest relations, putting them to the severest test. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 37. He that loveth father or mother more than me is not worthy of me: and he that loveth son or daughter more than me is not worthy of me. As preference of the one would necessitate abandonment of the other, our Lord with sublime yet awful self-respect asserts his own claims to supreme affection; those best prepared for the life to come sit most loose to this present life. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 38. He that taketh not his cross, and followeth after me, is not worthy of me. We are so accustomed to “taking up one's cross” as preparedness for trials in general that we lose its primary sense here: preparedness to go forth even to crucifixion, as our Lord bore his own cross to Calvary — remarkable, since he had not yet hinted he would die this death, nor was crucifixion a Jewish punishment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 39. He that findeth his life shall lose it: and he that loseth his life for my sake shall find it. The paradox turns on “life” in a double sense, lower and higher, natural and spiritual, temporal and eternal: entire sacrifice of the lower, or willingness to make it, is indispensable to preserving the higher, and he who cannot surrender the one for the other shall eventually lose both. Christ will lead us through sufferings to glory with him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 40. He that receiveth you receiveth me, and he that receiveth me receiveth him that sent me. As an ambassador's treatment expresses the light in which his sender is viewed, so your authority is mine, as mine is my Father's. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 41. He that receiveth a prophet in the name of a prophet shall receive a prophet's reward. A prophet is one divinely commissioned to deliver a message from heaven, predicting future events being no necessary part of the office; receiving him for his office's sake and love to his master is an encouragement to those who are not prophets. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 42. And whosoever shall give to drink unto one of these little ones a cup of cold water only in the name of a disciple, verily he shall in no wise lose his reward. “Little ones” denotes their lowliness in spirit and littleness in an undiscerning world's eyes, while high in Heaven's esteem; here is a descending climax — prophet, righteous man, little one — signifying that however low we come down in services to those that are Christ's, all done for his sake and bearing love to his name shall be divinely appreciated and rewarded. Though kindness ever so small, if there be occasion and ability to do no more, it shall be accepted, not as deserved, but from God's free gift. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Investigation (editorial — a parallel question, not a conclusion)

Parallel. The mission charge, including shaking off the dust where messengers are not received, is told in Matthew and, in the note's terms, in Mark and Luke as well. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Difference. Mark and Luke add that the dust-shaking is for a testimony against the place, a point Matthew's verse states by the act itself without that gloss in the note. The note records the addition without further comment. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
