The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Psalms 98 (King James Version (1611) for Psalms 1–150; Charles Thomson, The Septuagint (1808) for Psalm 151)

1 O sing unto the LORD a new song; for he hath done marvellous things: his right hand, and his holy arm, hath gotten him the victory.

2 The LORD hath made known his salvation: his righteousness hath he openly shewed in the sight of the heathen.

3 He hath remembered his mercy and his truth toward the house of Israel: all the ends of the earth have seen the salvation of our God.

4 Make a joyful noise unto the LORD, all the earth: make a loud noise, and rejoice, and sing praise.

5 Sing unto the LORD with the harp; with the harp, and the voice of a psalm.

6 With trumpets and sound of cornet make a joyful noise before the LORD, the King.

7 Let the sea roar, and the fulness thereof; the world, and they that dwell therein.

8 Let the floods clap their hands: let the hills be joyful together

9 Before the LORD; for he cometh to judge the earth: with righteousness shall he judge the world, and the people with equity.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Another new song, this time grounded in victory already won: the LORD's right hand and holy arm have gotten him the victory, his salvation is made known, and his righteousness openly shown in the sight of the heathen, because he remembered his mercy and truth toward Israel, so that all the ends of the earth have seen the salvation of God. All the earth is called to joyful noise with harp, trumpets and cornet before the LORD the King, and sea, floods and hills join in, before the LORD who comes to judge the earth with righteousness and the people with equity. Interpreted Psalm text above; study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. O sing unto the LORD a new song; for he hath done marvellous things: his right hand, and his holy arm, hath gotten him the victory, is the wonders he has wrought and the conquests he has won: first, the wonders he has wrought — he has done marvellous things. Note, the work of our salvation by Christ is a work of wonder. If we take a view of all the steps of it from the contrivance of it, and the counsels of God concerning it before all time, to the consummation of it, and its everlasting consequences when time shall be no more, we shall say, God has in it done marvellous things; it is all his doing and it is marvellous in our eyes. The more it is known the more it will be admired. Second, the conquests he has won: his right hand and his holy arm have gotten him the victory. Our Redeemer has surmounted all the difficulties that lay in the way of our redemption, has broken through them all, and was not discouraged by the services or sufferings appointed him. He has subdued all the enemies that opposed it, has gotten the victory over Satan, disarmed him, and cast him out of his strong-holds, has spoiled principalities and powers, has taken the prey from the mighty, and given death his death's wound. He has gotten a clear and complete victory, not only for himself, but for us also, for we through him are more than conquerors. He got this victory by his own power: there was none to help, none to uphold, none that durst venture into the service but his right hand and his holy arm, which are always stretched out with good success, because they are never stretched out but in a good cause, these have gotten him the victory, have brought him relief or deliverance. God's power and faithfulness, called here his right hand and his holy arm, brought relief to the Lord Jesus, in raising him from the dead, and exalting him personally to the right hand of God; so Dr. Hammond. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 2. The LORD hath made known his salvation: his righteousness hath he openly shewed in the sight of the heathen, is the discoveries he has made to the world of the work of redemption: what he has wrought for us he has revealed to us, and both by his Son — the gospel-revelation is that on which the gospel-kingdom is founded — the word which God sent. The opening of the sealed book is that which is to be celebrated with songs of praise, because by it was brought to light the mystery which had long been hid in God. Observe, first, the subject of this discovery — his salvation and his righteousness. Righteousness and salvation are often put together. Salvation denotes the redemption itself, and righteousness the way in which it was wrought, by the righteousness of Christ. Or the salvation includes all our gospel-privileges and the righteousness all our gospel-duties: both are made known, for God has joined them together, and we must not separate them. Or righteousness is here put for the way of our justification by Christ, which is revealed in the gospel to be by faith. Second, the plainness of this discovery. He has openly shown it, not in types and figures as under the law, but it is written as with a sunbeam, that he that runs may read it. Ministers are appointed to preach it with all plainness of speech. Third, the extent of this discovery. It is made in the sight of the heathen, and not of the Jews only: all the ends of the earth have seen the salvation of our God; for to the Gentiles was the word of salvation sent. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 3. He hath remembered his mercy and his truth toward the house of Israel: all the ends of the earth have seen the salvation of our God, is the accomplishment of the prophecies and promises of the Old Testament: he has remembered his mercy and his truth towards the house of Israel. God had mercy in store for the seed of Abraham, and had given them many and great assurances of the kindness he designed them in the latter days; and it was in pursuance of all those that he raised up his Son Jesus to be not only a light to lighten the Gentiles, but the glory of his people Israel; for he sent him, in the first place, to bless them. God is said, in sending Christ, to perform the mercy promised to our fathers, and to remember the holy covenant. It was in consideration of that, and not of their merit. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 4. Making a joyful noise, all the earth, is the call to all the children of men to rejoice in the setting up of Christ's kingdom, for they all have, or may have, benefit by it. Again and again we are called by all ways and means possible to express our joy and give God praise: a loud noise, as those affected with glad tidings and desirous to affect others with them, rejoicing and singing Hosannas and Hallelujahs, welcoming him to the throne as new kings are welcomed, with acclamations and loud shouts till the earth rings again, as when Solomon was proclaimed, the shouts of the crowd accompanied with singers and players on instruments as is usual in such solemnities. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 5. Singing with the harp and the voice of a psalm means letting sacred songs attend the new King: express your joy thus, proclaim it, excite it yet more, and propagate it among others. The songs are assisted with sacred music, with the soft and gentle melody of the harp — the quieter half of the accompaniment Henry pairs with the martial instruments of the next verse. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 6. Trumpets and cornet proclaim a victorious King whose glory is to be celebrated, who goes forth conquering and to conquer, so he is proclaimed with martial sound before the Lord, the King. All this joy must be directed to God and expressed in a solemn religious manner — make a joyful noise to the Lord, sing to the Lord, do it before the Lord the King — for carnal mirth is an enemy to this holy joy. When David danced before the ark he pleaded that it was before the Lord, and the piety and devotion of the intention not only vindicated what he did but commended it; so we must rejoice before the Lord whenever we draw near to him, before the Lord Jesus, and before him not only as Saviour but as King, the King of kings, the church's King and our King. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 7. Letting the sea roar is to the same purport as Psalm 96: let that roaring be called not, as it used to be, a dreadful noise, but a joyful noise, for the coming of Christ and the salvation wrought out by him have quite altered the property of the troubles and terrors of this world. When the floods lift up their voice and lift up their waves, we must not construe that to be the sea roaring against us, but rather rejoicing with us. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 8. The floods clapping their hands and the hills joyful together mean the inferior creatures expressing joy as men do when they clap their hands, and the hills that trembled for fear before God when he came down to give the law at Mount Sinai now dancing for joy before him when his gospel is preached and the word of the Lord goes forth from Zion in a still small voice. This intimates that Christ's kingdom would be a blessing to the whole creation, and that as inferior creatures declare the glory of the Creator, so they declare the glory of the Redeemer, for by him all things not only subsist in their being but consist in their order — and that, if the children of men were wanting in paying their due respects to the Redeemer, he must look for his honour from the sea and the floods, which would shame the stupidity and ingratitude of mankind. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 9. Before the Lord, for he cometh to judge the earth, may principally refer to his second coming, this second mention of his coming after Psalm 96: the new heavens and new earth we look for, according to his promise, when all these things shall be so dissolved as to be refined, and then he shall come to judge the world with righteousness. In the prospect of that day all that are sanctified do rejoice, and even the sea, the floods and the hills would rejoice if they could — and if all rejoice, why should not we? Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
