The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 3 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 After this opened Job his mouth, and cursed his day.

2 And Job spake, and said,

3 Let the day perish wherein I was born, and the night in which it was said, There is a man child conceived.

4 Let that day be darkness; let not God regard it from above, neither let the light shine upon it.

5 Let darkness and the shadow of death stain it; let a cloud dwell upon it; let the blackness of the day terrify it.

6 As for that night, let darkness seize upon it; let it not be joined unto the days of the year, let it not come into the number of the months.

7 Lo, let that night be solitary, let no joyful voice come therein.

8 Let them curse it that curse the day, who are ready to raise up their mourning.

9 Let the stars of the twilight thereof be dark; let it look for light, but have none; neither let it see the dawning of the day:

10 Because it shut not up the doors of my mother’s womb, nor hid sorrow from mine eyes.

11 Why died I not from the womb? why did I not give up the ghost when I came out of the belly?

12 Why did the knees prevent me? or why the breasts that I should suck?

13 For now should I have lain still and been quiet, I should have slept: then had I been at rest,

14 With kings and counsellors of the earth, which build desolate places for themselves;

15 Or with princes that had gold, who filled their houses with silver:

16 Or as an hidden untimely birth I had not been; as infants which never saw light.

17 There the wicked cease from troubling; and there the weary be at rest.

18 There the prisoners rest together; they hear not the voice of the oppressor.

19 The small and great are there; and the servant is free from his master.

20 Wherefore is light given to him that is in misery, and life unto the bitter in soul;

21 Which long for death, but it cometh not; and dig for it more than for hid treasures;

22 Which rejoice exceedingly, and are glad, when they can find the grave?

23 Why is light given to a man whose way is hid, and whom God hath hedged in?

24 For my sighing cometh before I eat, and my roarings are poured out like the waters.

25 For the thing which I greatly feared is come upon me, and that which I was afraid of is come unto me.

26 I was not in safety, neither had I rest, neither was I quiet; yet trouble came.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

After seven days of silence Job breaks it by cursing his birthday, not God: he wishes the day of his birth had never dawned and that he had died at birth. Death, he says, would have been rest — the place where the wicked cease from troubling, prisoners hear no oppressor, and the small and great lie together. The chapter sets the question the whole debate will circle: why was he ever born into such suffering? Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. After seven days of silence Job opens his mouth and curses his day. JFB notes the formula marks deliberation and gravity, and that Job curses his birthday, not his God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Job speaks, answering not an actual question but the question his whole case virtually poses. JFB warns that desiring death to escape troubles is a mark of corruption, unlike desiring it to be free from sin, yet notes Job's trials were greater and his light less than ours. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Job wishes the day of his birth and the night that announced a man-child would perish. JFB notes night is personified as speaking, and that a male birth was a matter of joy in the East. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Let that day be darkness, unregarded by God and unlit by light. JFB renders it poetically: let God not stoop from his bright throne to seek it out of its dark hiding-place. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Let darkness and the shadow of death claim the day, with a gathered mass of dark clouds over it. JFB reads stain as the ancient chaotic night resuming its rights over light and claiming that day as its own. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Let darkness seize the night and dissolve it utterly, so it has no rejoicing place in the circle of days and months that form the year. JFB stresses the poetic personification: the night is not to rejoice among the days. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Let that night be solitary, with no joyful voice in it. JFB renders solitary as unfruitful: would that it had not given birth to me. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Let those who curse days, even magicians believed able by charms to make a day evil and to rouse leviathan, curse that day. JFB explains the cursers of the day as enchanters claiming power over wild beasts and evil omens. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Let the stars of that night's twilight be dark, let it look for light and have none, and not see the dawning. JFB notes the dawning is literally the eyelashes of morning, the sun's first rays breaking forth like opening eyelids. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Job curses the night because it did not shut the doors of his mother's womb or hide sorrow from his eyes. The complaint turns from the day itself to the birth it allowed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Why did I not die from the womb, or give up the ghost as I came out of the belly? Job wishes his life had ended at its very beginning, before suffering could reach him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Why did the knees receive me, and why did the breasts nurse me? JFB explains the knees as the father's solemn recognition, placing the newborn on his knees as his own child whom he was bound to rear. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Had he died at birth, Job says, he would have lain quiet and slept. JFB traces the gradation — lain, then quiet, then slept — and notes death in Scripture is called sleep. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. There he would rest with kings and counsellors who built desolate places for themselves. JFB reads the irony: their grand buildings proved ruins, and the great ones, wearied with heaping perishable treasures, find rest only in death. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Or with princes who had gold and filled their houses with silver. Their wealth, like the kings' buildings, ends in the same quiet grave. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Or as a hidden untimely birth that never saw the light, like infants who never lived. JFB judges even that lot preferable to the restless miser's life Job now endures. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. There the wicked cease from troubling, and the weary are at rest. JFB explains the wicked as the ever-restless, full of desires, and the weary as those whose strength is worn out. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. There the prisoners rest together and do not hear the oppressor's voice. Death frees them from their chains. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. The small and great are there, and the servant is free from his master. JFB notes the slave is manumitted from slavery in death. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Why is light given to him in misery, and life to the bitter in soul? JFB notes God is reverentially omitted, and light means life, which ill suits mourners whose feelings accord with the grave. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. They long for death but it does not come, and dig for it more than for hidden treasure. The image turns death into a prize men mine for in vain. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. They rejoice exceedingly and are glad when they can find the grave. What others dread, the sufferer counts as discovery and joy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. Why is light given to a man whose way is hidden, whom God has hedged in? JFB pictures a wanderer who has lost his way and is hedged in with no exit of escape. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. Job's sighing comes before he eats, and his roarings pour out like waters. JFB explains the sighing as preventing his eating, and the roarings as the rushing sound of streaming water. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. The thing Job greatly feared has come upon him, and what he was afraid of has overtaken him. JFB traces it through his trials: hearing of one loss he feared another, and now he fears his friends' ill opinion of him as a hypocrite. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. Job was not in safety, had no rest, was not quiet, yet trouble came. JFB reads this not of his former state but of the beginning of his troubles, from which there was no intermission — and now a fresh trouble, his friends' suspicion, gives the starting-point of the whole controversy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Unfamiliar terms

  • Curse his day. Job curses his birthday, the day of his birth — the study note stresses he curses his day, not his God. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Leviathan (verse 8). Named among the cursers of days: enchanters believed able by charms to make a day evil and to rouse leviathan, a great sea creature of the old stories. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
