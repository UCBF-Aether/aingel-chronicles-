The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Ecclesiastes 3 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 To every thing there is a season, and a time to every purpose under the heaven:

2 A time to be born, and a time to die; a time to plant, and a time to pluck up that which is planted;

3 A time to kill, and a time to heal; a time to break down, and a time to build up;

4 A time to weep, and a time to laugh; a time to mourn, and a time to dance;

5 A time to cast away stones, and a time to gather stones together; a time to embrace, and a time to refrain from embracing;

6 A time to get, and a time to lose; a time to keep, and a time to cast away;

7 A time to rend, and a time to sew; a time to keep silence, and a time to speak;

8 A time to love, and a time to hate; a time of war, and a time of peace.

9 What profit hath he that worketh in that wherein he laboureth?

10 I have seen the travail, which God hath given to the sons of men to be exercised in it.

11 He hath made every thing beautiful in his time: also he hath set the world in their heart, so that no man can find out the work that God maketh from the beginning to the end.

12 I know that there is no good in them, but for a man to rejoice, and to do good in his life.

13 And also that every man should eat and drink, and enjoy the good of all his labour, it is the gift of God.

14 I know that, whatsoever God doeth, it shall be for ever: nothing can be put to it, nor any thing taken from it: and God doeth it, that men should fear before him.

15 That which hath been is now; and that which is to be hath already been; and God requireth that which is past.

16 And moreover I saw under the sun the place of judgment, that wickedness was there; and the place of righteousness, that iniquity was there.

17 I said in mine heart, God shall judge the righteous and the wicked: for there is a time there for every purpose and for every work.

18 I said in mine heart concerning the estate of the sons of men, that God might manifest them, and that they might see that they themselves are beasts.

19 For that which befalleth the sons of men befalleth beasts; even one thing befalleth them: as the one dieth, so dieth the other; yea, they have all one breath; so that a man hath no preeminence above a beast: for all is vanity.

20 All go unto one place; all are of the dust, and all turn to dust again.

21 Who knoweth the spirit of man that goeth upward, and the spirit of the beast that goeth downward to the earth?

22 Wherefore I perceive that there is nothing better, than that a man should rejoice in his own works; for that is his portion: for who shall bring him to see what shall be after him?

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

To everything there is a season, the famous poem of paired times, and God has made everything beautiful in its time while setting eternity in human hearts. Judgment, the shared dust of humans and beasts, and the counsel to rejoice in one's own work close the chapter. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. To everything there is a season and a time to every purpose under heaven, because human life is not governed by man's will but by appointments he does not control. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. There is a time to be born and to die, to plant and to pluck up, pairing the beginnings and endings of life and labour, neither of which man can fix at his pleasure. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. There is a time to kill and to heal, to break down and to build up, because destruction and repair each have their appointed hour in a world God orders. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. There is a time to weep and to laugh, to mourn and to dance, because sorrow and joy succeed one another in seasons, and neither lasts unbroken. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. There is a time to cast away stones and to gather them, to embrace and to refrain from embracing, because even gathering and affection must wait their season. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. There is a time to get and to lose, to keep and to cast away, because possession itself is seasonal, and what is kept in one season is thrown away in another. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. There is a time to rend and to sew, to keep silence and to speak, because mourning and repair, restraint and utterance, each belong to their own hour. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. There is a time to love and to hate, a time of war and a time of peace, because the affections and the affairs of nations alike turn in seasons appointed over them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. What profit has he that works in that wherein he labours, if every work is bounded by its season and no season can be held beyond its time. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. He has seen the travail God has given to the sons of men to be exercised in it, a labour that humbles man by showing him how little of life he commands. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. God has made everything beautiful in his time, and has set the world in man's heart, so that man longs for eternity yet cannot find out the work of God from beginning to end. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. He knows there is no good in earthly things but for a man to rejoice and do good in his life, receiving present comfort as the good that is actually within his reach. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Every man should eat and drink and enjoy the good of all his labour, because this enjoyment is the gift of God, not a right earned by toil. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Whatever God does shall be for ever, nothing can be put to it or taken from it, and God does it that men should fear before him, since his work alone is unchangeable. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. That which has been is now, and that which is to be has already been, and God requires that which is past, because history repeats itself under his unchanging government. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. He saw under the sun, in the place of judgment, wickedness, and in the place of righteousness, iniquity, because even the seats meant for justice are corrupted by men. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. He said in his heart that God shall judge the righteous and the wicked, for there is a time for every purpose and for every work, and corrupt judgment on earth does not cancel judgment in heaven. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. Concerning the sons of men, God manifests them that they may see they themselves are beasts, because death, which they share with beasts, strips away the pride of life. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. As the one dies so dies the other, both have one breath, and man has no preeminence above a beast in this, for all is vanity when life is measured only by its breath. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. All go unto one place, all are of the dust and all turn to dust again, because the body, whether of man or beast, returns to the earth from which it came. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. Who knows whether the spirit of man goes upward and the spirit of the beast goes downward to the earth, a question Henry reads as man's ignorance, not as denial, since the difference of souls is known to God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Therefore he perceives there is nothing better than that a man should rejoice in his own works, for that is his portion, and who shall bring him to see what shall be after him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
