The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 13 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Lo, mine eye hath seen all this, mine ear hath heard and understood it.

2 What ye know, the same do I know also: I am not inferior unto you.

3 Surely I would speak to the Almighty, and I desire to reason with God.

4 But ye are forgers of lies, ye are all physicians of no value.

5 O that ye would altogether hold your peace! and it should be your wisdom.

6 Hear now my reasoning, and hearken to the pleadings of my lips.

7 Will ye speak wickedly for God? and talk deceitfully for him?

8 Will ye accept his person? will ye contend for God?

9 Is it good that he should search you out? or as one man mocketh another, do ye so mock him?

10 He will surely reprove you, if ye do secretly accept persons.

11 Shall not his excellency make you afraid? and his dread fall upon you?

12 Your remembrances are like unto ashes, your bodies to bodies of clay.

13 Hold your peace, let me alone, that I may speak, and let come on me what will.

14 Wherefore do I take my flesh in my teeth, and put my life in mine hand?

15 Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him: but I will maintain mine own ways before him.

16 He also shall be my salvation: for an hypocrite shall not come before him.

17 Hear diligently my speech, and my declaration with your ears.

18 Behold now, I have ordered my cause; I know that I shall be justified.

19 Who is he that will plead with me? for now, if I hold my tongue, I shall give up the ghost.

20 Only do not two things unto me: then will I not hide myself from thee.

21 Withdraw thine hand far from me: and let not thy dread make me afraid.

22 Then call thou, and I will answer: or let me speak, and answer thou me.

23 How many are mine iniquities and sins? make me to know my transgression and my sin.

24 Wherefore hidest thou thy face, and holdest me for thine enemy?

25 Wilt thou break a leaf driven to and fro? and wilt thou pursue the dry stubble?

26 For thou writest bitter things against me, and makest me to possess the iniquities of my youth.

27 Thou puttest my feet also in the stocks, and lookest narrowly unto all my paths; thou settest a print upon the heels of my feet.

28 And he, as a rotten thing, consumeth, as a garment that is moth eaten.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Job presses on: what the friends know, he knows also, and he will speak to the Almighty and reason with God rather than accept their verdict. The friends, he says, are forgers of lies and physicians of no value, speaking deceitfully for God as if partiality could please him. Though God slay him, yet will he trust in him — but he will maintain his own ways before him, and demands only two things for a fair hearing: that God withdraw his hand, and let not his dread terrify him. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Job's eye has seen all this, his ear has heard and understood it. JFB notes all this refers to the dealings of Providence discussed in chapter 12. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. What the friends know, Job knows also; he is not inferior to them. The claim of equality, made before, is now the ground for appealing past them to God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Surely Job would speak to the Almighty and desire to reason with God. JFB notes Job wishes to plead his cause before God, increasingly convinced his would-be physicians are valueless. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. But the friends are forgers of lies and physicians of no value. JFB renders forgers as artful twisters of vain speeches. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Oh that the friends would altogether hold their peace, and it should be their wisdom! JFB cites the Arab saying that the wise are dumb, and silence is wisdom. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Hear now Job's reasoning, and hearken to the pleadings of his lips. He demands a hearing for his own defence, not their verdict. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Will the friends speak wickedly for God and talk deceitfully for him? JFB explains deceitfully as using fallacies to vindicate God, as if the end justified the means, by asserting Job is a sinner because he is a sufferer. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Will they accept God's person and contend for God? JFB explains accepting his person as a judge favouring one party for personal reasons, and contends that partiality can never please the impartial God, nor a good cause excuse unfair arguments. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Is it good that God should search them out, or as one man mocks another, do they mock him? JFB asks whether the issue will be good when God searches their arguments, and renders mock as deceiving God as one man deceives another. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. God will surely reprove the friends if they secretly accept persons. JFB notes God can vindicate his own acts successfully and needs no fallacious argument from man. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Shall not God's excellency make them afraid, and his dread fall upon them? JFB asks whether God's majesty will not frighten them from employing sophisms in his name. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. The friends' remembrances are like unto ashes, and their bodies to bodies of clay. JFB explains remembrances as well-remembered proverbial maxims, parables of ashes in their lightness, and bodies as clay entrenchments easily destroyed, which will not shelter them when God reproves their injustice to Job. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Hold their peace, let Job alone, that he may speak, and let come on him what will. JFB notes Job wishes to be spared their speeches so he can speak out all his mind about his wretchedness, happen what will. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Why does Job take his flesh in his teeth and put his life in his hand? JFB calls it a proverb for anxiously desiring to save one's life, imaged by a wild beast carrying prey in its teeth and a man holding in his hand what he would keep secure. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Though God slay Job, yet will he trust in him, but he will maintain his own ways before him. JFB notes the textual reading is not, agreeing with Job's hopeless passages, yet he will vindicate himself before God as not a hypocrite. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. He also shall be Job's salvation, for a hypocrite shall not come before him. JFB explains that this very desire to come before God speaks for his acquittal, since a hypocrite would not wish to come before him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. Hear diligently Job's speech and his declaration with their ears. JFB notes the declaration is his wish to justify himself immediately before God, heard attentively. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. Behold, Job has ordered his cause, and knows he shall be justified. JFB explains ordered as constant preparation for defence in confidence of innocence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. Who is he that will plead with Job? If any can contend and prove him false, he will hold his tongue and give up the ghost. JFB renders it as staking his life on it: silence and death if disproved. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Only do not two things unto Job, and then he will not hide himself from God. JFB notes the address turns to God, and not hide means standing forth boldly to maintain his cause. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. Withdraw God's hand far from Job, and let not his dread make him afraid. The two conditions for a fair hearing are removal of the crushing hand and of terror. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Then call, and Job will answer, or let Job speak and God answer him. JFB explains the trial language: call is a challenge to the defendant, answer the defence, speak the plaintiff's plea, answer the reply to the plea. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. How many are Job's iniquities and sins? Make them known to him. JFB notes the catalogue ought to be great to judge by the severity crushing one already bowed down, and that Job claims unconsciousness of a single particular sin, much less many. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. Why does God hide his face and hold Job for his enemy? JFB explains hiding the face by the gloomy impression of the sun suddenly clouded over, and enemy as one robbed of power by ceaseless sufferings. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. Will God break a leaf driven to and fro, and pursue the dry stubble? JFB compares Job to a fallen leaf the storm still chases, and break literally to shaking with terrors. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. God writes bitter things against Job and makes him possess the iniquities of his youth. JFB explains writest as a judicial phrase for noting down determined punishment, and possessing as inheriting in old age the punishments of sins acquired in youth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. God puts Job's feet in stocks, looks narrowly to all his paths, and sets a print upon his heels. JFB explains the stocks as a prisoner's fastening until execution, the narrow look as an overseer's watch, and the print as a line drawn round his soles beyond which he must not move. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. And he, as a rotten thing, consumes, as a garment moth-eaten. JFB notes Job now speaks of himself in the third person, forming the transition to the general lot of man in chapter 14. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Where the speakers disagree

The friends defend God by arguing Job must be guilty; Job calls their defence forgery — speaking deceitfully for God, accepting his person as a partial judge would. He will not take their verdict: he demands to reason with God himself, to have his iniquities named, and to be heard without terror. The chapter stages a dispute about method as much as outcome: may God be defended with fallacies? Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
