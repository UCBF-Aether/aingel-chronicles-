The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Psalms 92 (King James Version (1611) for Psalms 1–150; Charles Thomson, The Septuagint (1808) for Psalm 151)

1 It is a good thing to give thanks unto the Lord, and to sing praises unto thy name, O most High:

2 To shew forth thy lovingkindness in the morning, and thy faithfulness every night,

3 Upon an instrument of ten strings, and upon the psaltery; upon the harp with a solemn sound.

4 For thou, LORD, hast made me glad through thy work: I will triumph in the works of thy hands.

5 O LORD, how great are thy works! and thy thoughts are very deep.

6 A brutish man knoweth not; neither doth a fool understand this.

7 When the wicked spring as the grass, and when all the workers of iniquity do flourish; it is that they shall be destroyed for ever:

8 But thou, LORD, art most high for evermore.

9 For, lo, thine enemies, O LORD, for, lo, thine enemies shall perish; all the workers of iniquity shall be scattered.

10 But my horn shalt thou exalt like the horn of an unicorn: I shall be anointed with fresh oil.

11 Mine eye also shall see my desire on mine enemies, and mine ears shall hear my desire of the wicked that rise up against me.

12 The righteous shall flourish like the palm tree: he shall grow like a cedar in Lebanon.

13 Those that be planted in the house of the LORD shall flourish in the courts of our God.

14 They shall still bring forth fruit in old age; they shall be fat and flourishing;

15 To shew that the LORD is upright: he is my rock, and there is no unrighteousness in him.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

A song of thanks: it is good to show forth God's lovingkindness in the morning and his faithfulness every night, with ten-stringed instruments and harp, rejoicing in the works of his hands. The wicked may spring up like grass and flourish, but only to be destroyed for ever, while the LORD is most high for evermore and his enemies perish. The righteous, by contrast, flourish like the palm tree and grow like a cedar in Lebanon, planted in the house of the LORD and still bearing fruit in old age, to show that the LORD is upright, his rock, with no unrighteousness in him. Interpreted Psalm text above; study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Praising God is good work — good in itself and good for us, our duty as the rent and tribute owed to our great Lord, which we are unjust to withhold, and our privilege, to be admitted and hope to be accepted in it. It is pleasant and profitable, work that is its own wages, the work of angels and of heaven, and giving thanks for mercies received is the way of fetching in further mercy, sung to the name of him who is Most High, exalted above all blessing and praise. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 2. To shew forth thy lovingkindness in the morning, and thy faithfulness every night, is how and when we must praise God: we must do it by showing forth his lovingkindness and his faithfulness. Being convinced of his glorious attributes and perfections, we must show them forth, as those that are greatly affected with them ourselves and desire to affect others with them likewise. We must show forth, not only his greatness and majesty, his holiness and justice, which magnify him and strike an awe upon us, but his lovingkindness and his faithfulness; for his goodness is his glory, and by these he proclaims his name. His mercy and truth are the great supports of our faith and hope, and the great encouragements of our love and obedience; these therefore we must show forth as our pleas in prayer and the matter of our joy. And we must praise God in the morning and every night, not only on sabbath days, but every day; it is that which the duty of every day requires. We must praise God, not only in public assemblies, but in secret, and in our families, showing forth, to ourselves and those about us, his lovingkindness and faithfulness. We must begin and end every day with praising God, must give him thanks every morning, when we are fresh and before the business of the day comes in upon us, and every night, when we are again composed and retired, and are recollecting ourselves; we must give him thanks every morning for the mercies of the night and every night for the mercies of the day; going out and coming in we must bless God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 3. Praise is shown forth on instruments with a solemn sound — grave, apt to fix the spirits, not gay and apt to dissipate them. What is shown forth is not only God's greatness, majesty, holiness and justice, which magnify him and strike awe, but his lovingkindness and faithfulness, for his goodness is his glory, and mercy and truth are the great supports of faith and hope and the great encouragements of love and obedience, our pleas in prayer and the matter of our joy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 4. Those can best recommend praise who have themselves experienced its pleasantness: God's works have many a time rejoiced the psalmist's heart, so whatever others think of them he must think and speak well of them. If God has made our hearts glad through providence for us, grace in us, and the great work of redemption, we should give him the honour — fetching encouragement for faith and hope, a joyful prospect from joyful remembrance, and triumph over all opposition. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 5. God's works are great beyond conception and beyond expression, products of great power and wisdom, of great consequence and importance, while men's works are nothing to them. We cannot comprehend their greatness and must reverently wonder and stand amazed — and their greatness leads to the depth of his thoughts, which as much exceed our wisdom's contrivances as his works exceed our power's efforts, fetching a compass and reaching a length we cannot fathom. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 6. The brutish and foolish are those who do not know or understand how great God's works are, who will not acquaint themselves with them nor give him their glory, regarding not the work of the Lord nor considering the operation of his hands. In particular they misunderstand their own prosperity, taking it as a pledge of happiness when it is a preparative for ruin — and those who through grace are acquainted with Providence's designs, and love to be so, have the more reason to be thankful. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 7. The wicked spring as grass in spring — numerous, thickly sown, green and growing fast — and workers of iniquity flourish in pomp, power and every instance of outward prosperity, easy and succeeding, so that one would think it evidence of God's favour and an earnest of something better in reserve. It is quite otherwise: it is that they shall be destroyed for ever, for the prosperity of fools shall slay them, as sheep designed for slaughter are put into the fattest pasture. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 8. Against workers of iniquity who fight high for a time and think to carry all before them with a high hand, Henry sets the contrast: thou art high, most high, for evermore. Their height will be humbled and brought down, but his is everlasting — so we need not fear the pride and power of evil men nor be discouraged by their impotent menaces, for the moth shall eat them up as a garment, but God's righteousness shall be for ever. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 9. God's enemies perish because they impudently avow themselves his enemies, contrary to him and fighting against him, in rebellion against his crown and dignity — who ever hardened his heart against God and prospered? All impenitent workers of iniquity are deemed God's enemies, and Christ reckons his enemies those who will not have him reign over them, to be brought forth and slain before him. Now closely linked in combination against God, they shall be scattered, disabled to help one another, and in the world to come separated from the congregation of the righteous. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 10. The horn exalted like a unicorn's is honour from its fountain: the great God, high for evermore, will himself exalt his people for ever, for he is the praise of all his saints. The wicked are forbidden to lift up the horn, but those who commit their honour and power to God to keep, raise, use and dispose of as he pleases may hope for the greatest height, in this world or the other, shining as the sun when enemies perish. Fresh oil is fresh confirmation in office, abundance of plenty, renewed comforts when spirits droop — grace, the anointing of the Spirit, received as there is occasion from the fulness in Christ — and in old age, rich mercy refreshing hoary heads found in the way of righteousness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 11. The eye seeing its desire on enemies is triumph in foresight of their destruction, not as the misery of fellow-creatures but as redounding to the honour of God's justice and holiness: seeing them not only disabled from further mischief but reckoned with for mischief done, brought either to repentance or to ruin. In the Hebrew it is no more than that eye shall look and ear shall hear — not saying what, but that in what is seen and heard God will be glorified and the psalmist therefore satisfied, a pattern Henry applies to Christ's victory over Satan, death and hell and the final ruin of the impenitent. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 12. The righteous flourish like the palm and grow like the cedar because where God gives true grace he will give more grace: they grow higher like Lebanon's tall cedars, nearer heaven with holy ambition toward the upper world, and stronger and fitter for use, as clean hands grow stronger and stronger. Their flourishing is both in the credit of their profession and in the comfort and joy of their souls — cheerful themselves and respected by all about them — and unlike grass soon withered, the palm is long-lived and evergreen, growing the more it is pressed down, so the righteous flourish under burdens and multiply under affliction. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 13. Planted in the house of the Lord, the trees of righteousness do not grow of themselves: they are planted not in common soil but in paradise, in God's house, receiving all sap and virtue that keep them alive and fruitful from his grace, by his word and Spirit. They fix themselves to holy ordinances, take root in them, abide by them, put themselves under divine protection, and bring forth all their fruits to God's honour — their root in his house, their branches spreading in his courts, their life hid with Christ in God while their light shines before men, adorning the doctrine of God our Saviour as flourishing trees adorn a house's courts. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 14. Fruit in old age is the promise that distinguishes God's trees from others: other trees leave off bearing when old, but in God's trees the strength of grace does not fail with the strength of nature, and the saints' last days are sometimes their best days and their last work their best work. The fruits are the products of sanctification — lively devotion, useful conversation, good works by which God is glorified and others edified — and perseverance in them is the surest evidence of sincerity, showing the Lord upright, true to every promise and constant to the work he began. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 15. To shew that the LORD is upright: he is my rock, and there is no unrighteousness in him, is the fruitfulness of the righteous showing God's faithfulness: it is here said to show that the Lord is upright, that he is true to his promises and faithful to every word that he has spoken, and that he is constant to the work which he has begun. As it is by the promises that believers first partake of a divine nature, so it is by the promises that that divine nature is preserved and kept up; and therefore the power it exerts is an evidence that the Lord is upright, and so he will show himself with an upright man. This the psalmist triumphs in: He is my rock and there is no unrighteousness in him. I have chosen him for my rock on which to build, in the clefts of which to take shelter, on the top of which to set my feet. I have found him a rock, strong and stedfast, and his word as firm as a rock. I have found — and let every one speak as he finds — that there is no unrighteousness in him. He is as able, and will be as kind, as his word makes him to be. All that ever trusted in God found him faithful and all-sufficient, and none were ever made ashamed of their hope in him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
