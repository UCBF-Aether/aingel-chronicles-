The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — 1 Corinthians 11 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Be ye followers of me, even as I also am of Christ.

2 Now I praise you, brethren, that ye remember me in all things, and keep the ordinances, as I delivered them to you.

3 But I would have you know, that the head of every man is Christ; and the head of the woman is the man; and the head of Christ is God.

4 Every man praying or prophesying, having his head covered, dishonoureth his head.

5 But every woman that prayeth or prophesieth with her head uncovered dishonoureth her head: for that is even all one as if she were shaven.

6 For if the woman be not covered, let her also be shorn: but if it be a shame for a woman to be shorn or shaven, let her be covered.

7 For a man indeed ought not to cover his head, forasmuch as he is the image and glory of God: but the woman is the glory of the man.

8 For the man is not of the woman: but the woman of the man.

9 Neither was the man created for the woman; but the woman for the man.

10 For this cause ought the woman to have power on her head because of the angels.

11 Nevertheless neither is the man without the woman, neither the woman without the man, in the Lord.

12 For as the woman is of the man, even so is the man also by the woman; but all things of God.

13 Judge in yourselves: is it comely that a woman pray unto God uncovered?

14 Doth not even nature itself teach you, that, if a man have long hair, it is a shame unto him?

15 But if a woman have long hair, it is a glory to her: for her hair is given her for a covering.

16 But if any man seem to be contentious, we have no such custom, neither the churches of God.

17 Now in this that I declare unto you I praise you not, that ye come together not for the better, but for the worse.

18 For first of all, when ye come together in the church, I hear that there be divisions among you; and I partly believe it.

19 For there must be also heresies among you, that they which are approved may be made manifest among you.

20 When ye come together therefore into one place, this is not to eat the Lord’s supper.

21 For in eating every one taketh before other his own supper: and one is hungry, and another is drunken.

22 What? have ye not houses to eat and to drink in? or despise ye the church of God, and shame them that have not? What shall I say to you? shall I praise you in this? I praise you not.

23 For I have received of the Lord that which also I delivered unto you, That the Lord Jesus the same night in which he was betrayed took bread:

24 And when he had given thanks, he brake it, and said, Take, eat: this is my body, which is broken for you: this do in remembrance of me.

25 After the same manner also he took the cup, when he had supped, saying, This cup is the new testament in my blood: this do ye, as oft as ye drink it, in remembrance of me.

26 For as often as ye eat this bread, and drink this cup, ye do shew the Lord’s death till he come.

27 Wherefore whosoever shall eat this bread, and drink this cup of the Lord, unworthily, shall be guilty of the body and blood of the Lord.

28 But let a man examine himself, and so let him eat of that bread, and drink of that cup.

29 For he that eateth and drinketh unworthily, eateth and drinketh damnation to himself, not discerning the Lord’s body.

30 For this cause many are weak and sickly among you, and many sleep.

31 For if we would judge ourselves, we should not be judged.

32 But when we are judged, we are chastened of the Lord, that we should not be condemned with the world.

33 Wherefore, my brethren, when ye come together to eat, tarry one for another.

34 And if any man hunger, let him eat at home; that ye come not together unto condemnation. And the rest will I set in order when I come.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Paul praises the Corinthians for keeping the ordinances, then addresses head coverings in worship and the shameful divisions at the Lord's table, where some feast while others hunger. He repeats the Lord's institution of the Supper on the night of betrayal, warns that eating unworthily eats and drinks judgment, urges self-examination, and orders that those who hunger should eat at home so the assembly does not meet for condemnation. What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Be imitators of Paul, even as he is of Christ — who did not please himself but gave himself, laying aside divine glory and dying as man for us. Earthly teachers are to be followed only so far as they follow Christ, and this verse belongs with the end of chapter 10 on pleasing others rather than oneself. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Paul praises the Corinthians for remembering him in their general practice and keeping the ordinances — the apostolic traditions, directions given by word of mouth or in writing, here mainly ceremonies. Only traditions proved to be apostolic, which are those preserved in the written word, ought to be observed; the Lord's Supper, by contrast, Paul says he received from the Lord himself, not merely delivered. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. The head of every man is Christ, the head of the woman is the man, and the head of Christ is God. The Corinthian women, on the ground of abolished distinctions in Christ, claimed equality and came forward to pray and prophesy without the customary head-covering. The gospel raised women from degradation, and they stand on a level with men in grace, yet subjection in order, modesty, and seemliness is to be maintained, grounded in the order of creation. That God is head of the Son implies the Son is of the same essence as the Father, as the woman is of the essence of the man. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Every man praying or prophesying with his head covered dishonors his head. It was the Greek custom at Corinth for men in worship to be uncovered, while Jews wore a veil to show reverence and unworthiness to look on God. A covering is a mark of subjection that makes a man look downward instead of upward to his spiritual Head, Christ, to whom alone he owes subjection; the covering of one under Christ is not seen because Christ is not seen, while the covering of one under man is seen. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Every woman praying or prophesying with her head uncovered dishonors her head, acting against divine ordinance and modest propriety. In putting away the veil she puts away the badge of her subjection to man, which is her true honor, since through him it connects her with Christ; the head-covering was the emblem of maiden modesty and conjugal chastity, and uncovering the head signaled withdrawal from her husband's power. Her public speaking here is an extraordinary case justified only by miraculous gifts, as with Anna and Priscilla, while the ordinary rule is silence in public; the passage records what took place at Corinth, reserving censure for chapter 14. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. If a woman will not be covered, let her also be shorn — if she chooses to be uncovered in front, let her be so behind as well, shorn or shaven. Since being shorn or shaven is admittedly a shame, an unbecoming thing, she should be covered. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. A man ought not to cover his head, since he is the image and glory of God — created in God's image first and directly, and representing God's glory, an ideal realized most fully in the Son of man. But the woman is the glory of man: she is created in God's image too, yet as the moon shines with light derived from the sun, so in the order of creation she shines with light derived from man, though in grace she comes individually into direct communion with God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. For the man is not of the woman, but the woman of the man, taken out of man at her original creation. The woman was made by God mediately through the man, who was as it were a veil between her and God, so she should wear the veil in public worship in acknowledgment of this subordination in creation; the man, made immediately by God as his glory, has no veil between himself and God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Neither was the man created for the woman, but the woman for the man, the immediate object of her creation. As the church, the bride, is made for Christ, so the woman made for the man attains her true glory by fulfilling that end, and brings shame on herself by departing from it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. For this reason the woman ought to have power on her head — the head-covering, the emblem of being under man's power and exercising delegated authority under him. She should wear it because of the angels, who are present at Christian assemblies and delight in the orderly subordination of worshippers in their places; as angels in God's presence are veiled, so the woman in man's presence is to be veiled, lest indecorousness offend them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Nevertheless, neither is the man without the woman nor the woman without the man in the Lord. Neither sex is insulated or independent of the other in the Christian life; the one needs the other, and together man and woman realize the ideal of redeemed humanity represented by the bride, the church. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. As the woman was formed out of the man, even so is man born by means of woman; but all things, including both man and woman, are from God as their source. They depend mutually on each other, and both on him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Judge in yourselves: is it comely for a woman to pray to God uncovered? Paul appeals to their own sense of decorum — by rejecting the emblem of subjection she passes at one leap beyond both man and angels. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Does not nature itself teach that if a man has long hair it is a shame to him? Nature has provided woman, not man, with long hair, proving man was designed to be uncovered and woman covered; the Nazarite's long hair was lawful as part of a God-sanctioned vow, not a counterexample. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. If a woman has long hair, it is a glory to her, for her hair is given her for a covering. This does not mean she needs no additional covering; rather, her long hair shows she ought to cover her head as much as possible, and the will ought to accord with nature. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. If anyone seems contentious about this, the apostles have no such custom — no custom of women praying uncovered — nor do the churches of God. The usage of the true churches, a number of independent witnesses, is a valid argument in external rites, especially negatively: what was not received among them ought not to be admitted. Catholic usage is a general test of decency, not an infallible test of truth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. In what follows Paul enjoins rather than praises: their coming together is not for the better but for the worse, retrograding rather than progressing, so that the result must be condemnation. He qualifies his earlier praise for keeping the ordinances with an injunction on a matter in which he praises them not — the Lord's Supper. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. First of all, when they come together in the assembly, there are divisions — schisms not merely of opinion but in outward acts at the love-feasts. Paul partly believes the report, excepting the innocent: unwilling to believe all he hears, but unable to disbelieve some. The very ordinance instituted to unite believers in one body had become an occasion of division. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. There must also be heresies — not merely recent schisms of opinion but inveterate, matured separations and established parties in secession. The 'must be' arises from sin in professors necessarily bearing its natural fruits, which God overrules to probation of character for godly and ungodly alike and to discipline for glory, so that those who are approved may be made manifest through the disapproved becoming manifest. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. When they come together in one place, there is no such thing as eating the Lord's Supper, for each is greedily intent only on devouring his own supper, some excluded altogether because not waited for, some drunken while others are hungry. The love-feast usually preceded the Supper, and the bread and wine were taken from it; the excesses there made a true celebration with discernment of its solemnity impossible. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. For in eating, each one takes before the other his own supper — the rich before the poor, who had no supper of their own, instead of tarrying for one another. One's own belly becomes his god, and the Lord's Supper, the spiritual feast, never enters his thoughts; one has more than is good for him, another less. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Have you not houses to eat and drink in? Home is the place to satiate appetite, not the assembly of the brethren. By their conduct they despise the church of God, the congregation mostly composed of the poor whom God has chosen, and shame those who have no houses to eat and drink in and ought to have received their portion from wealthier brethren at the love-feasts. In this Paul praises them not. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. What Paul delivered is not his own invention but the Lord's institution, received from the Lord by immediate revelation. The renewal of the Supper's institution by special revelation to Paul enhances its solemnity, and the similarity between Luke's and Paul's accounts suggests Luke drew his information from the apostle. On the night he was betrayed, with the traitor at the table and death before his eyes, Christ left this ordinance as his last gift to commemorate his death — an amazing pledge of love in return for injury from man. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. The Lord Jesus took bread, gave thanks, and broke it — the breaking involving distribution, which reproves the Corinthian mode of each taking his own supper first. 'This is my body, broken for you' cannot mean the literal body, since Christ was sensibly present when he spoke; the disciples could only have understood him symbolically, as bread is to bodily health so his body is to the spiritual health of the believing communicant. 'Do this in remembrance of me' is the purpose of the ordinance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. After supper he took the cup, the new covenant in his blood — the cup as the parchment-deed on which his new covenant, his last will, is written and sealed, making over all blessings here and hereafter, ratified by his blood, not by the blood of goats and calves. As often as they drink it, they are to do so in remembrance of him; the old sacrifices brought sins continually to remembrance, but the Supper brings to remembrance Christ and his sacrifice once for all for the full remission of sins. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. As often as they eat this bread and drink this cup, they publicly show — announce — the Lord's death till he comes. The word means publicly professing that the Lord has died for me, a vivid realization by faith of Christ as a living person, not a mere abstract dogma. That the Supper is in remembrance of him implies he is bodily absent though spiritually present, and eating and drinking the pledges implies personal appropriation of the benefits of his death, as Jews understood feasts after propitiatory sacrifices. Till he comes, when symbols give way to his manifested body, the death is shown afresh at his table. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. Whoever eats the bread or drinks the cup of the Lord unworthily is guilty of the body and blood of the Lord. Unworthiness in the act, not in the person, is meant: impropriety in either element vitiates true communion in both, and partaking unworthily shares in the guilt of that death. Yet however unworthy we are, if we examine ourselves and find penitent belief in Christ's gospel, we may worthily communicate. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. Let a man examine — prove and test — his own state of mind toward Christ's death and his capability of discerning the Lord's body. Not auricular confession to a priest, but self-examination is necessary, and its purpose is not that he stay away but that, after due self-examination, he may eat. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. He who eats and drinks without discerning the body eats and drinks judgment to himself — 'damnation' is a mistranslation that has stumbled many, since the judgment described is temporal. Not duly judging and distinguishing from common food the sacramental pledges of the Lord's body brings that judgment; the Lord's body is his literal body appreciated and discerned by the soul in faithful receiving, not present in the elements themselves. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. For this cause many among them are weak and sickly, and many sleep — lulled in death, not violently but by sickness sent as the Lord's chastening for the individual's salvation, the mind being brought to a right state on the sick bed. The weak has naturally no strength; the sickly has lost strength by disease. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 31. If we would duly judge ourselves, we should not be judged — we would escape these present judgments. Duly judging ourselves is the way to duly discern the Lord's body, and this warns against making priestly absolution after full confession a necessary preliminary to the Supper. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 32. When we are judged, we are chastened by the Lord, that we should not be condemned with the world — who, being bastards, are without chastening. The Lord's chastening is corrective, distinguishing his children from the world. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 33. When you come together to eat, tarry one for another, in contrast to each taking his own supper first. All the viands brought to the feast were common property, so they should wait till all were met to partake together of the common feast of fellowship. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 34. If anyone hungers so as not to be able to tarry for others, let him eat at home, taking off the edge of his hunger there. The rest — the other questions they asked about the due celebration of the Supper — Paul will set in order when he comes, lest their coming together be for judgment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Unfamiliar terms

Plain-language glosses for words and phrases in this chapter that a modern reader may find unfamiliar. They are editorial aids, not Scripture.

  • ordinances. The traditions delivered by Paul, kept by the Corinthians in their general practice. Interpreted Chapter text and study notes for this chapter
  • unworthily. In an unworthy manner; Paul warns that eating and drinking the Supper so brings judgment on oneself. Interpreted Chapter text and study notes for this chapter

'This is my body, broken for you' cannot mean the literal body, since Christ was sensibly present when he spoke; the disciples could only have understood him symbolically, as bread is to bodily health so his body is to the spiritual health of the believing communicant. 'Do this in remembrance of me' is the purpose of the ordinance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. After supper he took the cup, the new covenant in his blood — the cup as the parchment-deed on which his new covenant, his last will, is written and sealed, making over all blessings here and hereafter, ratified by his blood, not by the blood of goats and calves. As often as they drink it, they are to do so in remembrance of him; the old sacrifices brought sins continually to remembrance, but the Supper brings to remembrance Christ and his sacrifice once for all for the full remission of sins. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. As often as they eat this bread and drink this cup, they publicly show — announce — the Lord's death till he comes. The word means publicly professing that the Lord has died for me, a vivid realization by faith of Christ as a living person, not a mere abstract dogma. That the Supper is in remembrance of him implies he is bodily absent though spiritually present, and eating and drinking the pledges implies personal appropriation of the benefits of his death, as Jews understood feasts after propitiatory sacrifices. Till he comes, when symbols give way to his manifested body, the death is shown afresh at his table. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. Whoever eats the bread or drinks the cup of the Lord unworthily is guilty of the body and blood of the Lord. Unworthiness in the act, not in the person, is meant: impropriety in either element vitiates true communion in both, and partaking unworthily shares in the guilt of that death. Yet however unworthy we are, if we examine ourselves and find penitent belief in Christ's gospel, we may worthily communicate. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. Let a man examine — prove and test — his own state of mind toward Christ's death and his capability of discerning the Lord's body. Not auricular confession to a priest, but self-examination is necessary, and its purpose is not that he stay away but that, after due self-examination, he may eat. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. He who eats and drinks without discerning the body eats and drinks judgment to himself — 'damnation' is a mistranslation that has stumbled many, since the judgment described is temporal. Not duly judging and distinguishing from common food the sacramental pledges of the Lord's body brings that judgment; the Lord's body is his literal body appreciated and discerned by the soul in faithful receiving, not present in the elements themselves. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. For this cause many among them are weak and sickly, and many sleep — lulled in death, not violently but by sickness sent as the Lord's chastening for the individual's salvation, the mind being brought to a right state on the sick bed. The weak has naturally no strength; the sickly has lost strength by disease. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 31. If we would duly judge ourselves, we should not be judged — we would escape these present judgments. Duly judging ourselves is the way to duly discern the Lord's body, and this warns against making priestly absolution after full confession a necessary preliminary to the Supper. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 32. When we are judged, we are chastened by the Lord, that we should not be condemned with the world — who, being bastards, are without chastening. The Lord's chastening is corrective, distinguishing his children from the world. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 33. When you come together to eat, tarry one for another, in contrast to each taking his own supper first. All the viands brought to the feast were common property, so they should wait till all were met to partake together of the common feast of fellowship. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 34. If anyone hungers so as not to be able to tarry for others, let him eat at home, taking off the edge of his hunger there. The rest — the other questions they asked about the due celebration of the Supper — Paul will set in order when he comes, lest their coming together be for judgment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
