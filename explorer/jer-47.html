The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Jeremiah 47 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 The word of the LORD that came to Jeremiah the prophet against the Philistines, before that Pharaoh smote Gaza.

2 Thus saith the LORD; Behold, waters rise up out of the north, and shall be an overflowing flood, and shall overflow the land, and all that is therein; the city, and them that dwell therein: then the men shall cry, and all the inhabitants of the land shall howl.

3 At the noise of the stamping of the hoofs of his strong horses, at the rushing of his chariots, and at the rumbling of his wheels, the fathers shall not look back to their children for feebleness of hands;

4 Because of the day that cometh to spoil all the Philistines, and to cut off from Tyrus and Zidon every helper that remaineth: for the LORD will spoil the Philistines, the remnant of the country of Caphtor.

5 Baldness is come upon Gaza; Ashkelon is cut off with the remnant of their valley: how long wilt thou cut thyself?

6 O thou sword of the LORD, how long will it be ere thou be quiet? put up thyself into thy scabbard, rest, and be still.

7 How can it be quiet, seeing the LORD hath given it a charge against Ashkelon, and against the sea shore? there hath he appointed it.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

This brief oracle against the Philistines pictures waters rising out of the north, overflowing the land, with fathers too feeble to look back for their children. The sword of the Lord is given its charge against Ashkelon and the sea shore, and the chapter ends by asking how it can be quiet when the Lord has commanded it. What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Henry notes the date is observable: this word came against the Philistines before Pharaoh smote Gaza, when they were in full strength and lustre, their cities in good condition with no peril foreseen by human probabilities, so Pharaoh's blow would be but an earnest and beginning of sorrows, and Henry recalls the Philistines had always been sworn enemies and dangerous near neighbours to Israel, humbled in David's time but grown considerable again until Nebuchadnezzar cut them off. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 2. Henry explains the waters rising out of the north as both multitudes of people and great threatening calamities: the Chaldean army shall overflow the small Philistine country like a deluge, a terrible storm out of the cold climate whence fair weather was said to come, so that all the inhabitants of the land shall howl and nothing but lamentation shall be heard. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 3. Henry notes that before killing begins, the very stamping of horses' hoofs and rattling of chariots and rumbling of wheels shall strike such terror that fathers shall not look back to their children for feebleness of hands, seeming void of natural affection, despairing of carrying them off and forgetting even those pieces of themselves. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 4. Henry stresses it is a day to spoil the Philistines because the LORD will spoil them, and those whom God will spoil must needs be spoiled, while Tyre and Zidon, strong wealthy cities that used to help the Philistines in a strait, shall themselves be involved in the common ruin with every helper that remains cut off, and the remnant of Caphtor, kin to the Philistines by Genesis 10, shall be spoiled with them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 5. Henry explains baldness upon Gaza and Ashkelon cut off with the remnant of their valley either as invaders stripping them of all ornaments or as themselves made bald in token of extreme grief, with the fruitful valley's products spoiled and made prey, and notes the prophet's tender question, how long wilt thou cut thyself, marking a calamity not only cutting but long cutting. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 6. Henry reads the prophet's plea to the sword of the LORD to put itself into its scabbard, rest, and be still as expressing earnest desire to see an end of war, looking with compassion even upon the Philistines, and notes war is the sword of the LORD, with which he punishes his enemies' crimes and pleads his people's cause, yet once drawn it does not quickly find the scabbard again. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 7. Henry gives the answer stopping his own complaint: how can the sword be quiet when the LORD has given it a charge against Ashkelon and the sea shore and there appointed it, since every bullet has its charge from the LORD of hosts, commander-in-chief, and the sword cannot be sheathed until it has fulfilled its charge, as God's word and rod accomplish that for which he sends them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
