The Text — Psalms 6 (King James Version (1611) for Psalms 1–150; Charles Thomson, The Septuagint (1808) for Psalm 151)

1 O LORD, rebuke me not in thine anger, neither chasten me in thy hot displeasure.

2 Have mercy upon me, O LORD; for I am weak: O LORD, heal me; for my bones are vexed.

3 My soul is also sore vexed: but thou, O LORD, how long?

4 Return, O LORD, deliver my soul: oh save me for thy mercies’ sake.

5 For in death there is no remembrance of thee: in the grave who shall give thee thanks?

6 I am weary with my groaning; all the night make I my bed to swim; I water my couch with my tears.

7 Mine eye is consumed because of grief; it waxeth old because of all mine enemies.

8 Depart from me, all ye workers of iniquity; for the LORD hath heard the voice of my weeping.

9 The LORD hath heard my supplication; the LORD will receive my prayer.

10 Let all mine enemies be ashamed and sore vexed: let them return and be ashamed suddenly.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

A plea from weakness and grief: the speaker begs correction in mercy rather than anger, weeps through the night under enemies' pressure, and then turns, because the Lord has heard his weeping, so that shame falls back on his enemies. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown; chapter text

The name David in the verse notes comes from the psalm's traditional heading as reported by the commentators, not from a verse printed above. Authorship and date remain Unresolved (see Trace). Interpreted Study notes for this chapter

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. David prays not to be rebuked in anger nor chastened in hot displeasure: he accepts correction but begs that it come in mercy, not wrath. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Weakness is his plea: he is feeble, his bones vexed, and he asks for mercy and healing from the Lord who wounds and heals. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. His soul is sore vexed, and he cries, how long — the question of a sufferer who trusts God yet feels the delay. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. He asks the Lord to return, deliver his soul, and save him for mercies' sake: the ground of the plea is God's mercy, not David's merit. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. In death there is no remembrance and no thanks in the grave, in the sense of this life's public praise; David pleads that a life spared may still praise God on earth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Weary with groaning, he waters his bed with tears all night: grief is unhidden before God, and night makes it heavier. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. His eye is consumed with grief and aged by his enemies: sorrow shows in the body, and enemies aggravate it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Workers of iniquity are told to depart, for the Lord has heard his weeping: the turn of the psalm is from lament to assurance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. The Lord has heard his supplication and will receive his prayer: hearing is repeated because certainty has replaced despair. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. His enemies shall be ashamed and sore vexed, turned back suddenly: the shame they planned for him falls on them, and swiftly. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
