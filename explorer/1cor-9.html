The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — 1 Corinthians 9 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Am I not an apostle? am I not free? have I not seen Jesus Christ our Lord? are not ye my work in the Lord?

2 If I be not an apostle unto others, yet doubtless I am to you: for the seal of mine apostleship are ye in the Lord.

3 Mine answer to them that do examine me is this,

4 Have we not power to eat and to drink?

5 Have we not power to lead about a sister, a wife, as well as other apostles, and as the brethren of the Lord, and Cephas?

6 Or I only and Barnabas, have not we power to forbear working?

7 Who goeth a warfare any time at his own charges? who planteth a vineyard, and eateth not of the fruit thereof? or who feedeth a flock, and eateth not of the milk of the flock?

8 Say I these things as a man? or saith not the law the same also?

9 For it is written in the law of Moses, Thou shalt not muzzle the mouth of the ox that treadeth out the corn. Doth God take care for oxen?

10 Or saith he it altogether for our sakes? For our sakes, no doubt, this is written: that he that ploweth should plow in hope; and that he that thresheth in hope should be partaker of his hope.

11 If we have sown unto you spiritual things, is it a great thing if we shall reap your carnal things?

12 If others be partakers of this power over you, are not we rather? Nevertheless we have not used this power; but suffer all things, lest we should hinder the gospel of Christ.

13 Do ye not know that they which minister about holy things live of the things of the temple? and they which wait at the altar are partakers with the altar?

14 Even so hath the Lord ordained that they which preach the gospel should live of the gospel.

15 But I have used none of these things: neither have I written these things, that it should be so done unto me: for it were better for me to die, than that any man should make my glorying void.

16 For though I preach the gospel, I have nothing to glory of: for necessity is laid upon me; yea, woe is unto me, if I preach not the gospel!

17 For if I do this thing willingly, I have a reward: but if against my will, a dispensation of the gospel is committed unto me.

18 What is my reward then? Verily that, when I preach the gospel, I may make the gospel of Christ without charge, that I abuse not my power in the gospel.

19 For though I be free from all men, yet have I made myself servant unto all, that I might gain the more.

20 And unto the Jews I became as a Jew, that I might gain the Jews; to them that are under the law, as under the law, that I might gain them that are under the law;

21 To them that are without law, as without law, (being not without law to God, but under the law to Christ,) that I might gain them that are without law.

22 To the weak became I as weak, that I might gain the weak: I am made all things to all men, that I might by all means save some.

23 And this I do for the gospel’s sake, that I might be partaker thereof with you.

24 Know ye not that they which run in a race run all, but one receiveth the prize? So run, that ye may obtain.

25 And every man that striveth for the mastery is temperate in all things. Now they do it to obtain a corruptible crown; but we an incorruptible.

26 I therefore so run, not as uncertainly; so fight I, not as one that beateth the air:

27 But I keep under my body, and bring it into subjection: lest that by any means, when I have preached to others, I myself should be a castaway.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Paul defends his apostleship, asks whether he has no right to food, drink, and a believing wife, and cites the law, the temple, and the Lord's ordinance that preachers should live from the gospel, yet says he has used none of these rights. He has become all things to all people to save some, and disciplines his body like an athlete in training, lest after preaching to others he himself should be a castaway. Interpreted Overview of this chapter's text and study notes

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Paul confirms his teaching on not stumbling a brother by his own example of forgoing undoubted apostolic rights. Am I not free? Am I not an apostle? He has seen Jesus bodily, not in a mere vision — a leading function of an apostle as witness of the resurrection — and the Corinthians themselves are his work in the Lord, the seal of his apostleship. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. If Paul is not an apostle to others, he is doubtless one to the Corinthians. Their conversion by his preaching, accompanied by miracles and gifts, vouches for his apostleship as a seal attests a document's genuineness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. To those who call his apostleship in question, his answer is this: the Corinthians themselves are the seal of his apostleship. His defense rests on their own existence as a church. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Have we not the right to eat and drink without laboring with our hands? The question expects the answer, surely you will not say we have not. Paul's not exercising this right was twisted by opponents into an insinuation that he knew himself no true apostle. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Paul had the undoubted right to lead about a sister as a wife, as other apostles did — Cephas was a married man — and as the Lord's brethren did. He did not exercise it, both to save the church the expense of maintaining a wife on his wide circuits and to give himself undistractedly to building up the church, in contrast to the Corinthians' self-seeking liberty at the cost of destroying the church. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Barnabas, long Paul's associate, likewise forbore to claim the maintenance that is a minister's right, and Paul supported himself by tent-making. Only he and Barnabas among the apostles are singled out as working with their hands. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Who serves as a soldier at his own expense, plants a vineyard without eating its fruit, or feeds a flock without drinking its milk? The minister is spiritually soldier, vine-dresser, and shepherd alike, and each by common right lives from his work. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Paul speaks not merely according to human judgment but with the sanction of divine law. The rights he claims rest on Scripture, not on custom alone. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. The law says not to muzzle the ox treading out the corn, when oxen threshed in the open air by treading the sheaves. God cares for the lower animal, but the animal is not the ultimate object of the law; in the humane consideration shown to the ox we learn that still more is due to man, the head of creation, and that the human laborer, spiritual as well as temporal, is worthy of his hire. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. The lawgiver by all means says this for our sakes as the ultimate object in the lower world. The plowman ought to plow in hope and the thresher in hope of partaking — the obligation rests on the people not to let their minister labor without remuneration. The spiritual plowman first plants a church, and the thresher tends one already planted. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. If the apostles sowed spiritual treasures to the Corinthians, may they not at least reap the only thing the Corinthians have to give, the goods that nourish the flesh? The exchange is vastly unequal in the Corinthians' favor. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. If others partake of this right over the Corinthians, whether true or false apostles, how much more might Paul and Barnabas, considering their greater labors? Yet they suffered all things without complaint, concealing their straitened circumstances, lest they hinder the gospel by giving any handle for imputing self-seeking if they received support. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Those who minister about holy things — the Jewish priests and Levites — live from the temple, partakers with the altar, since part of the victims went to the altar's service and the rest was shared by the priests. The same principle of support applies to Christian ministers. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Even so the Lord has ordained that those who preach the gospel should live from the gospel. The only inference is the parallel of support, not that Christian ministry is sacrificial like the Jewish priesthood; the Christian minister's plain duty is preaching the gospel, in contrast to ministering about sacrifices and waiting at the altar. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Paul used none of these rights, and he has not written to have them now applied to his case, as with soldier, planter, shepherd, plowman, and priest. Rather than hinder the gospel by any pretext for a charge of interested motives, he would die of hunger than have his glorying — preaching without remuneration — made void. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. If Paul preaches the gospel, even without charge, he has nothing to glory of in the preaching itself, for necessity is laid on him, as on Jeremiah and Jonah. His sole ground for glorying is preaching without charge, since no necessity is laid on him as to that; it is his voluntary act for the gospel's sake. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. If Paul preached of his own accord he would have a reward, but as it is he preaches involuntarily, by the constraining grace of God, and has had a dispensation entrusted to him. He can claim no reward for doing only his duty, but would incur woe if he failed in it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. Paul's reward, then, is that by making the gospel without charge, where he might rightfully have claimed maintenance, he might win the more. He does not use his power to the full, and that self-denial is his glorying, while the ultimate reward is the gaining of souls. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. Though free from all men, Paul made himself servant to all, that he might gain the more — as many of them as possible. Gain is a fitting word for a reward, and he repeats it through the passage. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. To the Jews Paul became as a Jew in matters of Jewish usage, not Judaizing in essentials, and to those under the law as under the law in ceremonies not then repugnant to Christianity, that he might gain those under the law. He himself belonged nationally to the Jews but not in creed to those under the law. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. To those without revealed law, the heathen, Paul became as without law, not urging ceremonies and works of the law but the hearing of faith, and discoursing in their own manner, as at Athens from their own poets. Yet he was not without law to God, but responsible to law in relation to Christ — no longer subject to the literal law from without, but to the inward law of faith in Christ, the law of love. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. To the weak Paul became weak, that he might gain the weak — establishing inexperienced Christians instead of being a stumbling-block to them, and keeping them from falling from faith. He conformed himself to the feelings of each class, that out of them all he might gain some, for the gain of even some is worth the expenditure of all means. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. Paul does all this for the gospel's sake, that he may be a fellow partaker of its blessings promised at Christ's coming, together with those he has gained to the gospel. His self-denial aims at sharing the prize with his converts. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. The Isthmian games, well known and a patriotic pride to the Corinthians who lived nearby, picture Christian earnestness. In a race course all run but one receives the prize — yet even if only one could be saved it would be worth running — so run in such a way that you may fully obtain, persevering to the end, as candidates swore to ten months' training and lived on strict self-denying diet. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. Every man who strives in wrestling, a severer contest than the race, is temperate in all things, as Paul abstained from claiming sustenance for the sake of gaining the more. The athletes strove for a corruptible crown, a chaplet of soon-withering pine leaves; believers strive for an incorruptible crown, a wreath that does not fade. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. Paul runs not uncertainly, as a runner unsure of the goal, and fights not as one beating the air in sham-fight. In all his acts — becoming all things to all men, receiving no sustenance — he has a definite end, to gain the more, looking straight to the goal, casting away encumbrance, and indifferent to bystanders. The real adversary is Satan acting through the flesh. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. Paul keeps his body under and brings it into subjection, bruising it as in boxing and leading it captive as a slave — not ascetic maceration, but keeping down natural self-seeking so as to lay himself out entirely for the work. Unless the soul keeps the body under, the body will get above the soul; the body makes a good servant but a bad master. He does this lest, after heralding the gospel to others as the herald summoned racers and crowned conquerors, he himself should be a castaway, rejected by God the Judge of the race. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Unfamiliar terms

Plain-language glosses for words and phrases in this chapter that a modern reader may find unfamiliar. They are editorial aids, not Scripture.

  • castaway. Disqualified or rejected after the contest; Paul's fear for himself if he fails to discipline his body. Interpreted Chapter text and study notes for this chapter
  • all things to all men. Paul's principle of adapting to Jew, Gentile, weak, and strong, without surrendering the gospel, to win some. Interpreted Chapter text and study notes for this chapter
  • muzzle the ox. The law's provision Paul cites: the labouring ox must not be muzzled while treading out the grain. Interpreted Chapter text and study notes for this chapter
