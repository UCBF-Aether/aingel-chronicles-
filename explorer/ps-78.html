The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Psalms 78 (King James Version (1611) for Psalms 1–150; Charles Thomson, The Septuagint (1808) for Psalm 151)

1 Give ear, O my people, to my law: incline your ears to the words of my mouth.

2 I will open my mouth in a parable: I will utter dark sayings of old:

3 Which we have heard and known, and our fathers have told us.

4 We will not hide them from their children, shewing to the generation to come the praises of the LORD, and his strength, and his wonderful works that he hath done.

5 For he established a testimony in Jacob, and appointed a law in Israel, which he commanded our fathers, that they should make them known to their children:

6 That the generation to come might know them, even the children which should be born; who should arise and declare them to their children:

7 That they might set their hope in God, and not forget the works of God, but keep his commandments:

8 And might not be as their fathers, a stubborn and rebellious generation; a generation that set not their heart aright, and whose spirit was not stedfast with God.

9 The children of Ephraim, being armed, and carrying bows, turned back in the day of battle.

10 They kept not the covenant of God, and refused to walk in his law;

11 And forgat his works, and his wonders that he had shewed them.

12 Marvellous things did he in the sight of their fathers, in the land of Egypt, in the field of Zoan.

13 He divided the sea, and caused them to pass through; and he made the waters to stand as an heap.

14 In the daytime also he led them with a cloud, and all the night with a light of fire.

15 He clave the rocks in the wilderness, and gave them drink as out of the great depths.

16 He brought streams also out of the rock, and caused waters to run down like rivers.

17 And they sinned yet more against him by provoking the most High in the wilderness.

18 And they tempted God in their heart by asking meat for their lust.

19 Yea, they spake against God; they said, Can God furnish a table in the wilderness?

20 Behold, he smote the rock, that the waters gushed out, and the streams overflowed; can he give bread also? can he provide flesh for his people?

21 Therefore the LORD heard this, and was wroth: so a fire was kindled against Jacob, and anger also came up against Israel;

22 Because they believed not in God, and trusted not in his salvation:

23 Though he had commanded the clouds from above, and opened the doors of heaven,

24 And had rained down manna upon them to eat, and had given them of the corn of heaven.

25 Man did eat angels’ food: he sent them meat to the full.

26 He caused an east wind to blow in the heaven: and by his power he brought in the south wind.

27 He rained flesh also upon them as dust, and feathered fowls like as the sand of the sea:

28 And he let it fall in the midst of their camp, round about their habitations.

29 So they did eat, and were well filled: for he gave them their own desire;

30 They were not estranged from their lust. But while their meat was yet in their mouths,

31 The wrath of God came upon them, and slew the fattest of them, and smote down the chosen men of Israel.

32 For all this they sinned still, and believed not for his wondrous works.

33 Therefore their days did he consume in vanity, and their years in trouble.

34 When he slew them, then they sought him: and they returned and enquired early after God.

35 And they remembered that God was their rock, and the high God their redeemer.

36 Nevertheless they did flatter him with their mouth, and they lied unto him with their tongues.

37 For their heart was not right with him, neither were they stedfast in his covenant.

38 But he, being full of compassion, forgave their iniquity, and destroyed them not: yea, many a time turned he his anger away, and did not stir up all his wrath.

39 For he remembered that they were but flesh; a wind that passeth away, and cometh not again.

40 How oft did they provoke him in the wilderness, and grieve him in the desert!

41 Yea, they turned back and tempted God, and limited the Holy One of Israel.

42 They remembered not his hand, nor the day when he delivered them from the enemy.

43 How he had wrought his signs in Egypt, and his wonders in the field of Zoan.

44 And had turned their rivers into blood; and their floods, that they could not drink.

45 He sent divers sorts of flies among them, which devoured them; and frogs, which destroyed them.

46 He gave also their increase unto the caterpiller, and their labour unto the locust.

47 He destroyed their vines with hail, and their sycomore trees with frost.

48 He gave up their cattle also to the hail, and their flocks to hot thunderbolts.

49 He cast upon them the fierceness of his anger, wrath, and indignation, and trouble, by sending evil angels among them.

50 He made a way to his anger; he spared not their soul from death, but gave their life over to the pestilence;

51 And smote all the firstborn in Egypt; the chief of their strength in the tabernacles of Ham:

52 But made his own people to go forth like sheep, and guided them in the wilderness like a flock.

53 And he led them on safely, so that they feared not: but the sea overwhelmed their enemies.

54 And he brought them to the border of his sanctuary, even to this mountain, which his right hand had purchased.

55 He cast out the heathen also before them, and divided them an inheritance by line, and made the tribes of Israel to dwell in their tents.

56 Yet they tempted and provoked the most high God, and kept not his testimonies:

57 But turned back, and dealt unfaithfully like their fathers: they were turned aside like a deceitful bow.

58 For they provoked him to anger with their high places, and moved him to jealousy with their graven images.

59 When God heard this, he was wroth, and greatly abhorred Israel:

60 So that he forsook the tabernacle of Shiloh, the tent which he placed among men;

61 And delivered his strength into captivity, and his glory into the enemy’s hand.

62 He gave his people over also unto the sword; and was wroth with his inheritance.

63 The fire consumed their young men; and their maidens were not given to marriage.

64 Their priests fell by the sword; and their widows made no lamentation.

65 Then the LORD awaked as one out of sleep, and like a mighty man that shouteth by reason of wine.

66 And he smote his enemies in the hinder parts: he put them to a perpetual reproach.

67 Moreover he refused the tabernacle of Joseph, and chose not the tribe of Ephraim:

68 But chose the tribe of Judah, the mount Zion which he loved.

69 And he built his sanctuary like high palaces, like the earth which he hath established for ever.

70 He chose David also his servant, and took him from the sheepfolds:

71 From following the ewes great with young he brought him to feed Jacob his people, and Israel his inheritance.

72 So he fed them according to the integrity of his heart; and guided them by the skilfulness of his hands.

How to read the labels

Every substantive historical claim in the editorial panels below carries one of three labels, plus a source reference:

  • Documented — directly supported by a cited source.
  • Interpreted — a reasoned explanation or proposed relationship.
  • Unresolved — evidence is insufficient to establish the claim.

A claim does NOT become Documented merely because a commentary states it. Commentary views are attributed to their authors and labeled Interpreted unless a primary textual source directly supports the claim. Where evidence runs out, the page says Unresolved and stops — it does not fill the gap.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

A long teaching psalm — "give ear, O my people, to my law" — that retells Israel's history so the coming generation will set its hope in God and keep his commandments, unlike fathers who were stubborn and rebellious. It moves through the plagues in Egypt and the sea passage, manna and water in the wilderness, repeated provocation and unbelief despite wonders, God's compassion turning away anger, the settlement in Canaan and its slide into idolatry at high places, the forsaking of Shiloh and the ark's captivity, and the reversal in which Ephraim is refused, Judah and Mount Zion are chosen, and David is taken from the sheepfolds to feed Jacob with integrity of heart and skilfulness of hands. Interpreted Psalm text above; study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. The psalmist calls his people to hear God's law with inclined ears, as pupils before a teacher. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 2. He will open his mouth in a parable and utter dark sayings — weighty, excellent things that deserve serious attention, not riddles meant to hide their meaning. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 3. These are things heard and known, told by the fathers, not new inventions. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 4. Henry makes transmission a debt repaid to parents and owed to posterity: the deposit to hand down is praise, power in deed, and wonders done, before language, liberty, or property. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 5. The law arrives with a charge attached: testimony and statute in Jacob exist so parents train children, and grown children in turn declare them, keeping the church from becoming a thing of one age. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 6. The teaching passes down so that children yet unborn may arise and declare it to their own children. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 7. Henry names the twofold fruit of remembering: encouragement to conform, with hope set on God and commands kept, since works duly considered strengthen both trust and obedience. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 8. The warning is against an inherited pattern: a generation that professed covenant yet never set its heart aright, whose spirit flew off at every occasion, shows hypocrisy as the high road to apostasy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 9. Ephraim, armed with bows, turned back in the day of battle — equipped yet faithless when tested. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 10. Ephraim's armed retreat becomes the psalm's exhibit: covenant-breaking here is not ignorance but refusal to walk in a law already given. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 11. Forgetfulness here is not a lapse of memory but a moral act: wonders shown are dropped, and with them the obligation they imposed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 12. Zoan stands for wonders done in full sight of the fathers, with Egypt as the stage where mercy and judgment were both displayed, aggravating later unbelief. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 13. The sea passage is remembered as a road, not a marvel alone: waters heaped while a people walked through, so that deliverance itself became instruction. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 14. Guidance by day and by night marks a people never left to find their own road, in light or in darkness, cloud and fire together. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 15. Rock water in a dry place answers the charge of a God who cannot provide: depths are opened where no depth was expected. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 16. Streams running like rivers turn a single miracle into abundance: provision is not measured out drop by drop. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 17. Sin after mercy is the psalm's aggravation: provocation continues in the very wilderness where provision had just arrived. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 18. Appetite becomes a trial of God: the question is not whether food will come, but whether the Provider can be trusted to furnish a table where none is seen. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 19. Speech against God follows appetite: doubt is voiced as a challenge to divine power in a place with no visible supply. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 20. They reasoned from the rock's water to doubt bread and flesh, as if one mercy proved nothing about the next. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 21. Wrath kindled is presented as judicial, not temperamental: fire against Jacob answers a settled refusal to believe, and anger rises for that refusal. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 22. The root of the anger was unbelief: they believed not in God and trusted not in his salvation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 23. Even after refusal, heaven's doors open: command over clouds shows a Provider who gives before the people deserve, making the gift itself a rebuke to distrust. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 24. He rained down manna, the corn of heaven, for them to eat. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 25. Manna as angels' food lifts provision above mere subsistence: heaven's corn is sent to the full, so that want cannot excuse the murmur. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 26. Winds are instruments, not accidents: east and south winds at God's power bring the promised flesh, showing providence commanding nature's movements. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 27. Flesh rained as dust and birds as sand turn desire into excess: the answer to lust is abundance, and abundance itself becomes the setting for judgment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 28. And he let it fall in the midst of their camp, round about their habitations, is God making the winds his caterers: though the winds seem to blow where they list, yet when he pleased he could make them fetch in provisions. He caused an east wind to blow and a south wind — either a south-east wind, or an east wind first to bring in the quails from that quarter and then a south wind to bring in more — so that he rained flesh upon them, and that of the most delicate sort, not butchers' meat, but wild-fowl, and abundance of it, as dust, as the sand of the sea, so that the meanest Israelite might have sufficient, and it cost them nothing, no, not the pains of fetching it from the mountains, for he let it fall in the midst of their camp, round about their habitation. See how good God is even to the evil and unthankful, and wonder that his goodness does not overcome their badness. See what little reason we have to judge of God's love by such gifts of his bounty: these dainty bits are no tokens of his peculiar favour. Christ gave dry bread to the disciples that he loved, but a sop dipped in the sauce to Judas that betrayed him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 29. They eat to fullness on their own desire, and Henry's point is that God grants the craving, so the granting exposes what the craving was worth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 30. They were not estranged from their lust, but while their meat was yet in their mouths, is lust's insatiable nature: though he gave them their own desire, they were not estranged from their lust — their appetite was insatiable, they were well filled and yet they were not satisfied, for they knew not what they would have. Such is the nature of lust: it is content with nothing, and the more it is humoured the more humoursome it grows. Those that indulge their lust will never be estranged from it. Or it intimates that God's liberality did not make them ashamed of their ungrateful lustings, as it would have done if they had had any sense of honour. But what came of it? While the meat was yet in their mouth, rolled under the tongue as a sweet morsel, the wrath of God came upon them and slew the fattest of them, those that were most luxurious and most daring. They were fed as sheep for the slaughter: the butcher takes the fattest first. We may suppose there were some pious and contented Israelites, that did eat moderately of the quails and were never the worse — for it was not the meat that poisoned them, but their own lust. Let epicures and sensualists here read their doom: the end of those who make a god of their belly is destruction; the prosperity of fools shall destroy them, and their ruin will be the greater. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 31. Judgment falls where strength was proudest: the fattest slain and chosen men struck down, so that the very vigour Israel trusted in is cut off. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 32. For all this they sinned still, and believed not for his wondrous works, is judgments failing where mercies failed: the judgments of God upon them did not reform them, nor attain the end, any more than his mercies. For all this, they sinned still — they murmured and quarrelled with God and Moses as much as ever. Though God was wroth and smote them, yet they went on frowardly in the way of their heart. They believed not for his wondrous works: though his works of justice were as wondrous and as great proofs of his power as his works of mercy, yet they were not wrought upon by them to fear God, nor convinced how much it was their interest to make him their friend. Those hearts are hard indeed that will neither be melted by the mercies of God nor broken by his judgments. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 33. Days consumed in vanity and years in trouble make sin its own punishment: a life spent chasing its own desire ends empty, not merely punished from outside. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 34. When he slew them, then they sought him and enquired early after God — trouble drove them back to prayer. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 35. And they remembered that God was their rock, and the high God their redeemer, is a plausible but counterfeit repentance: when he slew them, or condemned them to be slain, then they sought him — they confessed their fault, and begged his pardon. When some were slain others in a fright cried to God for mercy, and promised they would reform and be very good; then they returned to God, and enquired early after him. So one would have taken them to be such as desired to find him. And they pretended to do this because, however they had forgotten it formerly, now they remembered that God was their rock — and therefore now that they needed him they would fly to him and take shelter in him — and that the high God was their Redeemer, who brought them out of Egypt and to whom therefore they might come with boldness. Afflictions are sent to put us in mind of God as our rock and our redeemer — for, in prosperity, we are apt to forget him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 36. Flattery with the mouth and lies with the tongue expose counterfeit repentance: words of submission without a heart set right are the psalm's recurring fraud. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 37. A heart not right and a covenant not kept go together: outward engagement without inward steadfastness collapses at the first pressure. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 38. Compassion interrupts deserved destruction: anger turned away many times, not stirred to its full, is Henry's evidence that pardon, not mere delay, governs the history. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 39. God remembers flesh: a wind passing and not returning is the ground of forbearance toward a people too frail to bear full wrath. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 40. Repeated provocation in wilderness and desert grieves as well as angers: Henry stresses a God sinned against in the place of his own provision. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 41. They turned back, tempted God, and limited the Holy One of Israel by their unbelief. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 42. They remembered not his hand, nor the day when he delivered them from the enemy, is the occasion of their limiting God: that which occasioned their limiting God for the future was their forgetting his former favours. They remembered not his hand, how strong it is and how it had been stretched out for them, nor the day when he delivered them from the enemy, Pharaoh, that great enemy who sought their ruin. There are some days made remarkable by signal deliverances, which ought never to be forgotten — for the remembrance of them would encourage us in our greatest straits. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 43. Signs in Egypt and wonders at Zoan are recalled as forgotten evidence: the plagues were instruction, meant to cure Egyptian idolatry as well as to force an exit. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 44. Rivers turned to blood strike at Egypt's lifeblood: floods undrinkable show a judgment aimed at the source of a nation's confidence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 45. Flies devouring and frogs destroying are not random pests but a mixed plague: the land's own creatures turn against its people. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 46. Increase given to caterpillar and labour to locust reverses harvest: what hands worked for is eaten by what hands cannot control. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 47. He destroyed their vines with hail, and their sycamore trees with frost, is one of Egypt's specified plagues: the hail, which destroyed their trees, especially their vines, the weakest of trees, and their cattle, especially their flocks of sheep, the weakest of their cattle, which were killed with hot thunderbolts — and the frost, or congealed rain, as the word signifies, was so violent that it destroyed even the sycamore-trees. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 48. He gave up their cattle also to the hail, and their flocks to hot thunderbolts, continues that plague: the hail destroyed their trees, especially their vines, the weakest of trees, and their cattle, especially their flocks of sheep, the weakest of their cattle, which were killed with hot thunderbolts. God can make the weakest and most despicable animals instruments of his wrath when he pleases; what they want in strength may be made up in number — and here the very hail is made his instrument against Egypt's weakest wealth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 49. Fierceness, wrath, indignation, and trouble arrive together, by evil angels as agents: Henry reads the final plague as anger given a way, not anger losing control. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 50. A way made to anger spares no soul from death and gives life to pestilence: restraint is lifted, and the plague runs its appointed course. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 51. And smote all the first-born in Egypt, the chief of their strength in the tabernacles of Ham, is the last and sorest of Egypt's plagues, and that which perfected Israel's deliverance: it was first in intention, but last in execution — for, if gentler methods would have done the work, this would have been prevented. The anger of God was the cause of it: wrath had now come upon the Egyptians to the uttermost, Pharaoh's heart having been often hardened after less judgments had softened it, so God now stirred up all his wrath — he cast upon them the fierceness of his anger, anger in the highest degree, wrath and indignation the cause, and trouble, tribulation and anguish, the effect. The angels of God were the instruments: he sent evil angels among them — not evil in their own nature, but in respect to the errand upon which they were sent; they were destroying angels, or angels of punishment, which passed through all the land of Egypt, with orders, according to the weighed paths of God's anger, not to kill all, but the first-born only. Good angels become evil angels to sinners. The execution itself was very severe: he spared not their soul from death, but suffered death to ride in triumph among them and gave their life over to the pestilence, which cut the thread of life off immediately — for he smote all the first-born in Egypt, the chief of their strength, the hopes of their respective families. Children are the parents' strength, and the first-born the chief of their strength. Thus, because Israel was precious in God's sight, he gave men for them and people for their life. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 52. Sheep led like a flock contrasts with Egypt's ruin: the same God who plagued oppressors guides his own through wilderness as a shepherd guides, not drives. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 53. Safety without fear while the sea covers enemies inverts the Exodus terror: those who feared drowning watch drowning fall on their pursuers. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 54. The border of the sanctuary and the mountain purchased by God's right hand mark arrival as possession, not accident: a people brought to a place already claimed for them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 55. Heathen cast out, inheritance divided by line, tribes settled in tents: conquest becomes order, with each tribe given a measured place. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 56. After settlement in Canaan the children proved like their fathers, bringing old corruptions into new homes: they tempted and provoked God, failed to keep his testimonies, and turned back like a deceitful bow that promises the mark but breaks at the draw. Henry stresses there was no hold in their professions, and their relapses into idolatry were spiritual adultery that moved a jealous God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 57. Henry sets this relapse against the whole history: after settlement they turned back like their fathers, unfaithful in the very land where God had settled them, so the bow image marks a people failing at the moment of use. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 58. High places and graven images provoke jealousy after settlement: in Canaan itself, the old wilderness sin takes a settled, idolatrous form. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 59. When God heard this, he was wroth, and greatly abhorred Israel, is God's displeasure at Canaan idolatry: when God heard this, when he heard the cry of their iniquity, which came up before him, he was wroth, he took it very heinously, as well he might, and he greatly abhorred Israel, whom he had greatly loved and delighted in. Those that had been the people of his choice became the generation of his wrath. Presumptuous sins, idolatries especially, render even Israelites odious to God's holiness and obnoxious to his justice. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 60. Shiloh forsaken is God withdrawing his tent from among men: the tabernacle placed there is left, because the people for whom it stood have left him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 61. And delivered his strength into captivity, and his glory into the enemy's hand, is God giving up all into the enemy's hands: God permits the Philistines to take the ark prisoner, and carry it off as a trophy of their victory, to show that he had not only forsaken the tabernacle, but even the ark itself, which shall now be no longer a token of his presence. He delivered his strength into captivity, as if it had been weakened and overcome, and his glory fell under the disgrace of being abandoned into the enemy's hand. When the ark has become as a stranger among Israelites, no marvel if it soon be made a prisoner among Philistines. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 62. A people given to the sword and an inheritance under wrath show judgment turning inward: the nation becomes the object of the anger once aimed at Egypt. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 63. The fire consumed their young men, and their maidens were not given to marriage, is Israel's armies routed: he gave his people over unto the sword, to the sword of his own justice and of the enemy's rage, for he was wroth with his inheritance — and that wrath of his was the fire which consumed their young men, in the prime of their time, by the sword or sickness, and made such a devastation of them that their maidens were not praised, that is, were not given in marriage, which is honourable in all, because there were no young men for them to be given to, and because the distresses and calamities of Israel were so many and great that the joys of marriage-solemnities were judged unseasonable, and it was said, Blessed is the womb that beareth not. General destructions produce a scarcity of men. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 64. Henry reads the priests' fall as just, not accidental: Hophni and Phinehas had made themselves vile, and priesthood aggravated rather than protected them, since they went into battle without call or warrant. Their widows made no lamentation because ceremony was swallowed by substantial grief — Phinehas's widow died herself after naming her son Ichabod. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 65. God awaking as from sleep is Henry's image of seeming delay ending: after the ark's capture, divine action resumes with the force of a mighty man roused. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 66. Enemies smitten in the hinder parts and put to perpetual reproach reverse the shame: Philistine triumph becomes lasting disgrace. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 67. God never sent the ark back to Shiloh in Ephraim, and Henry says its ruins stand as monuments of divine justice. Yet moving the ark was not removing it: Shiloh lost it, Israel did not, and the rejection of Shiloh was the election of Zion, with Judah chosen because Jesus, greater than Joshua, would come from that tribe. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 68. Judah chosen and Zion loved close the long reversal: after Ephraim's failure and Shiloh's loss, the establishment moves to the tribe and mountain God delights in. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 69. He built his sanctuary like high palaces — stately above princes' palaces and firm as the earth while it was to stand, a house God himself is said to build. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 70. He chose David also his servant, and took him from the sheepfolds, is God setting a good government over them: he set a monarchy, and a monarch after his own heart — he chose David his servant out of all the thousands of Israel, and put the sceptre into his hand, out of whose loins Christ was to come, and who was to be a type of him. The meanness of his beginning: his extraction indeed was great, for he descended from the prince of the tribe of Judah, but his education was poor. He was bred not a scholar, not a soldier, but a shepherd. He was taken from the sheep-folds, as Moses was — for God delights to put honour upon the humble and diligent, to raise the poor out of the dust and to set them among princes, and sometimes he finds those most fit for public action that have spent the beginning of their time in solitude and contemplation. The Son of David was upbraided with the obscurity of his original: Is not this the carpenter? David was taken, he does not say from leading the rams, but from following the ewes, especially those great with young, which intimated that of all the good properties of a shepherd he was most remarkable for his tenderness and compassion to those of his flock that most needed his care. This temper of mind fitted him for government, and made him a type of Christ, who, when he feeds his flock like a shepherd, does with a particular care gently lead those that are with young. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 71. From following the ewes great with young he brought him to feed Jacob his people, and Israel his inheritance, is the greatness of David's advancement: God preferred him to feed Jacob his people. It was a great honour that God put upon him, in advancing him to be a king, especially to be king over Jacob and Israel, God's peculiar people, near and dear to him — but withal it was a great trust reposed in him when he was charged with the government of those that were God's own inheritance. God advanced him to the throne that he might feed them, not that he might feed himself, that he might do good, not that he might make his family great. It is the charge given to all the under-shepherds, both magistrates and ministers, that they feed the flock of God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 72. David fed them with integrity of heart and guided them by skilfulness of hands — the shepherd-king as the psalm's closing picture of God's care. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

Matthew Henry (Complete)

  • Verse 1. The psalmist calls his people to hear God's law with inclined ears, as pupils before a teacher. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 2. He will open his mouth in a parable and utter dark sayings — weighty, excellent things that deserve serious attention, not riddles meant to hide their meaning. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 3. These are things heard and known, told by the fathers, not new inventions. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 4. Henry makes transmission a debt repaid to parents and owed to posterity: the deposit to hand down is praise, power in deed, and wonders done, before language, liberty, or property. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 5. The law arrives with a charge attached: testimony and statute in Jacob exist so parents train children, and grown children in turn declare them, keeping the church from becoming a thing of one age. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 6. The teaching passes down so that children yet unborn may arise and declare it to their own children. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 7. Henry names the twofold fruit of remembering: encouragement to conform, with hope set on God and commands kept, since works duly considered strengthen both trust and obedience. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 8. The warning is against an inherited pattern: a generation that professed covenant yet never set its heart aright, whose spirit flew off at every occasion, shows hypocrisy as the high road to apostasy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 9. Ephraim, armed with bows, turned back in the day of battle — equipped yet faithless when tested. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 10. Ephraim's armed retreat becomes the psalm's exhibit: covenant-breaking here is not ignorance but refusal to walk in a law already given. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 11. Forgetfulness here is not a lapse of memory but a moral act: wonders shown are dropped, and with them the obligation they imposed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 12. Zoan stands for wonders done in full sight of the fathers, with Egypt as the stage where mercy and judgment were both displayed, aggravating later unbelief. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 13. The sea passage is remembered as a road, not a marvel alone: waters heaped while a people walked through, so that deliverance itself became instruction. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 14. Guidance by day and by night marks a people never left to find their own road, in light or in darkness, cloud and fire together. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 15. Rock water in a dry place answers the charge of a God who cannot provide: depths are opened where no depth was expected. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 16. Streams running like rivers turn a single miracle into abundance: provision is not measured out drop by drop. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 17. Sin after mercy is the psalm's aggravation: provocation continues in the very wilderness where provision had just arrived. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 18. Appetite becomes a trial of God: the question is not whether food will come, but whether the Provider can be trusted to furnish a table where none is seen. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 19. Speech against God follows appetite: doubt is voiced as a challenge to divine power in a place with no visible supply. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 20. They reasoned from the rock's water to doubt bread and flesh, as if one mercy proved nothing about the next. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 21. Wrath kindled is presented as judicial, not temperamental: fire against Jacob answers a settled refusal to believe, and anger rises for that refusal. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 22. The root of the anger was unbelief: they believed not in God and trusted not in his salvation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 23. Even after refusal, heaven's doors open: command over clouds shows a Provider who gives before the people deserve, making the gift itself a rebuke to distrust. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 24. He rained down manna, the corn of heaven, for them to eat. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 25. Manna as angels' food lifts provision above mere subsistence: heaven's corn is sent to the full, so that want cannot excuse the murmur. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 26. Winds are instruments, not accidents: east and south winds at God's power bring the promised flesh, showing providence commanding nature's movements. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 27. Flesh rained as dust and birds as sand turn desire into excess: the answer to lust is abundance, and abundance itself becomes the setting for judgment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 28. And he let it fall in the midst of their camp, round about their habitations, is God making the winds his caterers: though the winds seem to blow where they list, yet when he pleased he could make them fetch in provisions. He caused an east wind to blow and a south wind — either a south-east wind, or an east wind first to bring in the quails from that quarter and then a south wind to bring in more — so that he rained flesh upon them, and that of the most delicate sort, not butchers' meat, but wild-fowl, and abundance of it, as dust, as the sand of the sea, so that the meanest Israelite might have sufficient, and it cost them nothing, no, not the pains of fetching it from the mountains, for he let it fall in the midst of their camp, round about their habitation. See how good God is even to the evil and unthankful, and wonder that his goodness does not overcome their badness. See what little reason we have to judge of God's love by such gifts of his bounty: these dainty bits are no tokens of his peculiar favour. Christ gave dry bread to the disciples that he loved, but a sop dipped in the sauce to Judas that betrayed him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 29. They eat to fullness on their own desire, and Henry's point is that God grants the craving, so the granting exposes what the craving was worth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 30. They were not estranged from their lust, but while their meat was yet in their mouths, is lust's insatiable nature: though he gave them their own desire, they were not estranged from their lust — their appetite was insatiable, they were well filled and yet they were not satisfied, for they knew not what they would have. Such is the nature of lust: it is content with nothing, and the more it is humoured the more humoursome it grows. Those that indulge their lust will never be estranged from it. Or it intimates that God's liberality did not make them ashamed of their ungrateful lustings, as it would have done if they had had any sense of honour. But what came of it? While the meat was yet in their mouth, rolled under the tongue as a sweet morsel, the wrath of God came upon them and slew the fattest of them, those that were most luxurious and most daring. They were fed as sheep for the slaughter: the butcher takes the fattest first. We may suppose there were some pious and contented Israelites, that did eat moderately of the quails and were never the worse — for it was not the meat that poisoned them, but their own lust. Let epicures and sensualists here read their doom: the end of those who make a god of their belly is destruction; the prosperity of fools shall destroy them, and their ruin will be the greater. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 31. Judgment falls where strength was proudest: the fattest slain and chosen men struck down, so that the very vigour Israel trusted in is cut off. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 32. For all this they sinned still, and believed not for his wondrous works, is judgments failing where mercies failed: the judgments of God upon them did not reform them, nor attain the end, any more than his mercies. For all this, they sinned still — they murmured and quarrelled with God and Moses as much as ever. Though God was wroth and smote them, yet they went on frowardly in the way of their heart. They believed not for his wondrous works: though his works of justice were as wondrous and as great proofs of his power as his works of mercy, yet they were not wrought upon by them to fear God, nor convinced how much it was their interest to make him their friend. Those hearts are hard indeed that will neither be melted by the mercies of God nor broken by his judgments. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 33. Days consumed in vanity and years in trouble make sin its own punishment: a life spent chasing its own desire ends empty, not merely punished from outside. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 34. When he slew them, then they sought him and enquired early after God — trouble drove them back to prayer. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 35. And they remembered that God was their rock, and the high God their redeemer, is a plausible but counterfeit repentance: when he slew them, or condemned them to be slain, then they sought him — they confessed their fault, and begged his pardon. When some were slain others in a fright cried to God for mercy, and promised they would reform and be very good; then they returned to God, and enquired early after him. So one would have taken them to be such as desired to find him. And they pretended to do this because, however they had forgotten it formerly, now they remembered that God was their rock — and therefore now that they needed him they would fly to him and take shelter in him — and that the high God was their Redeemer, who brought them out of Egypt and to whom therefore they might come with boldness. Afflictions are sent to put us in mind of God as our rock and our redeemer — for, in prosperity, we are apt to forget him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 36. Flattery with the mouth and lies with the tongue expose counterfeit repentance: words of submission without a heart set right are the psalm's recurring fraud. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 37. A heart not right and a covenant not kept go together: outward engagement without inward steadfastness collapses at the first pressure. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 38. Compassion interrupts deserved destruction: anger turned away many times, not stirred to its full, is Henry's evidence that pardon, not mere delay, governs the history. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 39. God remembers flesh: a wind passing and not returning is the ground of forbearance toward a people too frail to bear full wrath. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 40. Repeated provocation in wilderness and desert grieves as well as angers: Henry stresses a God sinned against in the place of his own provision. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 41. They turned back, tempted God, and limited the Holy One of Israel by their unbelief. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 42. They remembered not his hand, nor the day when he delivered them from the enemy, is the occasion of their limiting God: that which occasioned their limiting God for the future was their forgetting his former favours. They remembered not his hand, how strong it is and how it had been stretched out for them, nor the day when he delivered them from the enemy, Pharaoh, that great enemy who sought their ruin. There are some days made remarkable by signal deliverances, which ought never to be forgotten — for the remembrance of them would encourage us in our greatest straits. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 43. Signs in Egypt and wonders at Zoan are recalled as forgotten evidence: the plagues were instruction, meant to cure Egyptian idolatry as well as to force an exit. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 44. Rivers turned to blood strike at Egypt's lifeblood: floods undrinkable show a judgment aimed at the source of a nation's confidence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 45. Flies devouring and frogs destroying are not random pests but a mixed plague: the land's own creatures turn against its people. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 46. Increase given to caterpillar and labour to locust reverses harvest: what hands worked for is eaten by what hands cannot control. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 47. He destroyed their vines with hail, and their sycamore trees with frost, is one of Egypt's specified plagues: the hail, which destroyed their trees, especially their vines, the weakest of trees, and their cattle, especially their flocks of sheep, the weakest of their cattle, which were killed with hot thunderbolts — and the frost, or congealed rain, as the word signifies, was so violent that it destroyed even the sycamore-trees. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 48. He gave up their cattle also to the hail, and their flocks to hot thunderbolts, continues that plague: the hail destroyed their trees, especially their vines, the weakest of trees, and their cattle, especially their flocks of sheep, the weakest of their cattle, which were killed with hot thunderbolts. God can make the weakest and most despicable animals instruments of his wrath when he pleases; what they want in strength may be made up in number — and here the very hail is made his instrument against Egypt's weakest wealth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 49. Fierceness, wrath, indignation, and trouble arrive together, by evil angels as agents: Henry reads the final plague as anger given a way, not anger losing control. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 50. A way made to anger spares no soul from death and gives life to pestilence: restraint is lifted, and the plague runs its appointed course. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 51. And smote all the first-born in Egypt, the chief of their strength in the tabernacles of Ham, is the last and sorest of Egypt's plagues, and that which perfected Israel's deliverance: it was first in intention, but last in execution — for, if gentler methods would have done the work, this would have been prevented. The anger of God was the cause of it: wrath had now come upon the Egyptians to the uttermost, Pharaoh's heart having been often hardened after less judgments had softened it, so God now stirred up all his wrath — he cast upon them the fierceness of his anger, anger in the highest degree, wrath and indignation the cause, and trouble, tribulation and anguish, the effect. The angels of God were the instruments: he sent evil angels among them — not evil in their own nature, but in respect to the errand upon which they were sent; they were destroying angels, or angels of punishment, which passed through all the land of Egypt, with orders, according to the weighed paths of God's anger, not to kill all, but the first-born only. Good angels become evil angels to sinners. The execution itself was very severe: he spared not their soul from death, but suffered death to ride in triumph among them and gave their life over to the pestilence, which cut the thread of life off immediately — for he smote all the first-born in Egypt, the chief of their strength, the hopes of their respective families. Children are the parents' strength, and the first-born the chief of their strength. Thus, because Israel was precious in God's sight, he gave men for them and people for their life. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 52. Sheep led like a flock contrasts with Egypt's ruin: the same God who plagued oppressors guides his own through wilderness as a shepherd guides, not drives. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 53. Safety without fear while the sea covers enemies inverts the Exodus terror: those who feared drowning watch drowning fall on their pursuers. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 54. The border of the sanctuary and the mountain purchased by God's right hand mark arrival as possession, not accident: a people brought to a place already claimed for them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 55. Heathen cast out, inheritance divided by line, tribes settled in tents: conquest becomes order, with each tribe given a measured place. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 56. After settlement in Canaan the children proved like their fathers, bringing old corruptions into new homes: they tempted and provoked God, failed to keep his testimonies, and turned back like a deceitful bow that promises the mark but breaks at the draw. Henry stresses there was no hold in their professions, and their relapses into idolatry were spiritual adultery that moved a jealous God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 57. Henry sets this relapse against the whole history: after settlement they turned back like their fathers, unfaithful in the very land where God had settled them, so the bow image marks a people failing at the moment of use. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 58. High places and graven images provoke jealousy after settlement: in Canaan itself, the old wilderness sin takes a settled, idolatrous form. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 59. When God heard this, he was wroth, and greatly abhorred Israel, is God's displeasure at Canaan idolatry: when God heard this, when he heard the cry of their iniquity, which came up before him, he was wroth, he took it very heinously, as well he might, and he greatly abhorred Israel, whom he had greatly loved and delighted in. Those that had been the people of his choice became the generation of his wrath. Presumptuous sins, idolatries especially, render even Israelites odious to God's holiness and obnoxious to his justice. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 60. Shiloh forsaken is God withdrawing his tent from among men: the tabernacle placed there is left, because the people for whom it stood have left him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 61. And delivered his strength into captivity, and his glory into the enemy's hand, is God giving up all into the enemy's hands: God permits the Philistines to take the ark prisoner, and carry it off as a trophy of their victory, to show that he had not only forsaken the tabernacle, but even the ark itself, which shall now be no longer a token of his presence. He delivered his strength into captivity, as if it had been weakened and overcome, and his glory fell under the disgrace of being abandoned into the enemy's hand. When the ark has become as a stranger among Israelites, no marvel if it soon be made a prisoner among Philistines. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 62. A people given to the sword and an inheritance under wrath show judgment turning inward: the nation becomes the object of the anger once aimed at Egypt. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 63. The fire consumed their young men, and their maidens were not given to marriage, is Israel's armies routed: he gave his people over unto the sword, to the sword of his own justice and of the enemy's rage, for he was wroth with his inheritance — and that wrath of his was the fire which consumed their young men, in the prime of their time, by the sword or sickness, and made such a devastation of them that their maidens were not praised, that is, were not given in marriage, which is honourable in all, because there were no young men for them to be given to, and because the distresses and calamities of Israel were so many and great that the joys of marriage-solemnities were judged unseasonable, and it was said, Blessed is the womb that beareth not. General destructions produce a scarcity of men. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 64. Henry reads the priests' fall as just, not accidental: Hophni and Phinehas had made themselves vile, and priesthood aggravated rather than protected them, since they went into battle without call or warrant. Their widows made no lamentation because ceremony was swallowed by substantial grief — Phinehas's widow died herself after naming her son Ichabod. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 65. God awaking as from sleep is Henry's image of seeming delay ending: after the ark's capture, divine action resumes with the force of a mighty man roused. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 66. Enemies smitten in the hinder parts and put to perpetual reproach reverse the shame: Philistine triumph becomes lasting disgrace. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 67. God never sent the ark back to Shiloh in Ephraim, and Henry says its ruins stand as monuments of divine justice. Yet moving the ark was not removing it: Shiloh lost it, Israel did not, and the rejection of Shiloh was the election of Zion, with Judah chosen because Jesus, greater than Joshua, would come from that tribe. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 68. Judah chosen and Zion loved close the long reversal: after Ephraim's failure and Shiloh's loss, the establishment moves to the tribe and mountain God delights in. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 69. He built his sanctuary like high palaces — stately above princes' palaces and firm as the earth while it was to stand, a house God himself is said to build. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 70. He chose David also his servant, and took him from the sheepfolds, is God setting a good government over them: he set a monarchy, and a monarch after his own heart — he chose David his servant out of all the thousands of Israel, and put the sceptre into his hand, out of whose loins Christ was to come, and who was to be a type of him. The meanness of his beginning: his extraction indeed was great, for he descended from the prince of the tribe of Judah, but his education was poor. He was bred not a scholar, not a soldier, but a shepherd. He was taken from the sheep-folds, as Moses was — for God delights to put honour upon the humble and diligent, to raise the poor out of the dust and to set them among princes, and sometimes he finds those most fit for public action that have spent the beginning of their time in solitude and contemplation. The Son of David was upbraided with the obscurity of his original: Is not this the carpenter? David was taken, he does not say from leading the rams, but from following the ewes, especially those great with young, which intimated that of all the good properties of a shepherd he was most remarkable for his tenderness and compassion to those of his flock that most needed his care. This temper of mind fitted him for government, and made him a type of Christ, who, when he feeds his flock like a shepherd, does with a particular care gently lead those that are with young. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 71. From following the ewes great with young he brought him to feed Jacob his people, and Israel his inheritance, is the greatness of David's advancement: God preferred him to feed Jacob his people. It was a great honour that God put upon him, in advancing him to be a king, especially to be king over Jacob and Israel, God's peculiar people, near and dear to him — but withal it was a great trust reposed in him when he was charged with the government of those that were God's own inheritance. God advanced him to the throne that he might feed them, not that he might feed himself, that he might do good, not that he might make his family great. It is the charge given to all the under-shepherds, both magistrates and ministers, that they feed the flock of God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
