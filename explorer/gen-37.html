The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Genesis 37 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 And Jacob dwelt in the land wherein his father was a stranger, in the land of Canaan.

2 These are the generations of Jacob. Joseph, being seventeen years old, was feeding the flock with his brethren; and the lad was with the sons of Bilhah, and with the sons of Zilpah, his father’s wives: and Joseph brought unto his father their evil report.

3 Now Israel loved Joseph more than all his children, because he was the son of his old age: and he made him a coat of many colours.

4 And when his brethren saw that their father loved him more than all his brethren, they hated him, and could not speak peaceably unto him.

5 And Joseph dreamed a dream, and he told it his brethren: and they hated him yet the more.

6 And he said unto them, Hear, I pray you, this dream which I have dreamed:

7 For, behold, we were binding sheaves in the field, and, lo, my sheaf arose, and also stood upright; and, behold, your sheaves stood round about, and made obeisance to my sheaf.

8 And his brethren said to him, Shalt thou indeed reign over us? or shalt thou indeed have dominion over us? And they hated him yet the more for his dreams, and for his words.

9 And he dreamed yet another dream, and told it his brethren, and said, Behold, I have dreamed a dream more; and, behold, the sun and the moon and the eleven stars made obeisance to me.

10 And he told it to his father, and to his brethren: and his father rebuked him, and said unto him, What is this dream that thou hast dreamed? Shall I and thy mother and thy brethren indeed come to bow down ourselves to thee to the earth?

11 And his brethren envied him; but his father observed the saying.

12 And his brethren went to feed their father’s flock in Shechem.

13 And Israel said unto Joseph, Do not thy brethren feed the flock in Shechem? come, and I will send thee unto them. And he said to him, Here am I.

14 And he said to him, Go, I pray thee, see whether it be well with thy brethren, and well with the flocks; and bring me word again. So he sent him out of the vale of Hebron, and he came to Shechem.

15 And a certain man found him, and, behold, he was wandering in the field: and the man asked him, saying, What seekest thou?

16 And he said, I seek my brethren: tell me, I pray thee, where they feed their flocks.

17 And the man said, They are departed hence; for I heard them say, Let us go to Dothan. And Joseph went after his brethren, and found them in Dothan.

18 And when they saw him afar off, even before he came near unto them, they conspired against him to slay him.

19 And they said one to another, Behold, this dreamer cometh.

20 Come now therefore, and let us slay him, and cast him into some pit, and we will say, Some evil beast hath devoured him: and we shall see what will become of his dreams.

21 And Reuben heard it, and he delivered him out of their hands; and said, Let us not kill him.

22 And Reuben said unto them, Shed no blood, but cast him into this pit that is in the wilderness, and lay no hand upon him; that he might rid him out of their hands, to deliver him to his father again.

23 And it came to pass, when Joseph was come unto his brethren, that they stript Joseph out of his coat, his coat of many colours that was on him;

24 And they took him, and cast him into a pit: and the pit was empty, there was no water in it.

25 And they sat down to eat bread: and they lifted up their eyes and looked, and, behold, a company of Ishmeelites came from Gilead with their camels bearing spicery and balm and myrrh, going to carry it down to Egypt.

26 And Judah said unto his brethren, What profit is it if we slay our brother, and conceal his blood?

27 Come, and let us sell him to the Ishmeelites, and let not our hand be upon him; for he is our brother and our flesh. And his brethren were content.

28 Then there passed by Midianites merchantmen; and they drew and lifted up Joseph out of the pit, and sold Joseph to the Ishmeelites for twenty pieces of silver: and they brought Joseph into Egypt.

29 And Reuben returned unto the pit; and, behold, Joseph was not in the pit; and he rent his clothes.

30 And he returned unto his brethren, and said, The child is not; and I, whither shall I go?

31 And they took Joseph’s coat, and killed a kid of the goats, and dipped the coat in the blood;

32 And they sent the coat of many colours, and they brought it to their father; and said, This have we found: know now whether it be thy son’s coat or no.

33 And he knew it, and said, It is my son’s coat; an evil beast hath devoured him; Joseph is without doubt rent in pieces.

34 And Jacob rent his clothes, and put sackcloth upon his loins, and mourned for his son many days.

35 And all his sons and all his daughters rose up to comfort him; but he refused to be comforted; and he said, For I will go down into the grave unto my son mourning. Thus his father wept for him.

36 And the Midianites sold him into Egypt unto Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh’s, and captain of the guard.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Jacob's favouritism toward Joseph, shown in a coat of many colours, and Joseph's dreams of sheaves and stars bowing to him, earn his brothers' hatred. Sent to check on them, Joseph is stripped, thrown in a pit, sold to passing traders, and carried into Egypt, while Jacob is deceived by a bloodied coat into mourning him as dead. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Jacob dwelt in Canaan, the land where his father had been a stranger. JFB notes Jacob sojourned at Mamre near Hebron, in the same manner and for the same motives as Abraham and Isaac before him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Joseph, seventeen years old, was feeding the flock with his brothers, alongside the sons of Bilhah and Zilpah, and he brought their evil report to his father. JFB explains Joseph held a chief shepherd's oversight, so he reported not as a tale-bearer but as a faithful steward. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Israel loved Joseph more than all his children, because he was the son of his old age, and made him a coat of many colours. JFB notes the Hebrew phrase can also mean a wise son, and the patched coloured coat was a dress of distinction. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. When his brothers saw their father's greater love, they hated Joseph and could not speak peaceably to him. JFB notes withholding the usual peace greeting was an unmistakable sign of hostility, and Henry calls Jacob's open partiality great indiscretion in a family by different mothers. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Joseph dreamed a dream and told it to his brothers, and they hated him yet more. JFB notes dreams were much attended to in ancient times, and this dream was evidently symbolical, its meaning easily discerned. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Joseph asked his brothers to hear the dream he had dreamed. The telling itself, not just the dream, sharpened the division. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. In the dream they were binding sheaves, and Joseph's sheaf rose and stood upright while theirs bowed to it. The farm image made the meaning plain to everyone who heard it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. His brothers answered with scorn: would he indeed reign and have dominion over them, and they hated him more for his dreams and words. Henry notes they rightly understood the dream, yet committed crimes to defeat it, and thereby became instruments of accomplishing it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Joseph dreamed yet another dream: sun, moon, and eleven stars bowed to him. The second dream, under different emblems, made fulfilment seem certain, as JFB notes from the pattern of repeated dreams. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Joseph told this dream to his father and brothers, and his father rebuked him, asking if father, mother, and brothers would indeed bow to him. The rebuke shows even Jacob felt the claim's boldness, though he did not dismiss it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. His brothers envied him, but his father observed the saying. Henry notes Joseph was given the prospect of advancement early, to support him under long troubles to come, though he dreamed of preferment and not of imprisonment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. His brothers went to feed their father's flock in Shechem. JFB notes Shechem's vale was richly watered, worth a journey of some fifty miles from Hebron for the sake of its herbage. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Israel asked Joseph if his brothers were not feeding the flock in Shechem, and said he would send him to them, and Joseph answered, here am I. Henry praises Joseph's ready obedience: the best-beloved child should be the most ready to obey. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Jacob sent Joseph from the vale of Hebron to see whether it was well with his brothers and the flocks, and to bring word back, and Joseph came to Shechem. The errand was a father's care, not a spy's mission. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. A man found Joseph wandering in the field and asked what he sought. The wandering shows Joseph arrived at Shechem to find his brothers already gone. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Joseph said he sought his brothers and asked where they fed their flocks. His plain answer kept the errand open and honest. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. The man said they had left for Dothan, having been heard to say, let us go to Dothan, and Joseph followed and found them there. JFB notes Dothan means two wells, and the move may have been forced by the hatred their name had earned after the Shechem massacre. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. When they saw Joseph afar off, before he came near, they conspired to kill him. Henry stresses the deliberation: this was malice aforethought, in cold blood, not a sudden quarrel. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. They said to one another, behold, this dreamer comes. JFB notes the phrase is literally master of dreams, a bitter sneer, since false claims to dreams were detested as a kind of blasphemy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Their plan was to kill him, cast him into a pit, and say an evil beast had devoured him, and then see what would become of his dreams. The lie was planned together with the murder. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. Reuben heard it and delivered Joseph from their hands, saying they should not kill him. Henry notes Reuben, the firstborn with most reason to be jealous, proved Joseph's best friend, and God overruled all for his own purpose. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Reuben proposed instead to cast Joseph into a pit in the wilderness and lay no hand on him, secretly intending to rescue him and return him to his father. JFB notes the pits were dry wells, and Reuben's plan was thwarted so that deliverance came by other means. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. When Joseph came to his brothers, they stripped him of his coat of many colours. JFB pictures his astonishment at the cold reception and rough usage from brothers he had approached in unsuspecting affection. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. They took him and cast him into a pit, which was empty with no water in it. Henry calls their tender mercies cruel: they left him to perish with hunger and cold. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. They sat down to eat bread, then looked up and saw a company of Ishmaelites from Gilead, with camels bearing spicery, balm, and myrrh, going down to Egypt. JFB notes the brothers' cool indifference at table, and that the caravan was a mixed Arab trading company bound for Egypt's embalming trade. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. Judah asked what profit there was in killing their brother and concealing his blood. The question shifted the plan from murder to sale, without softening the betrayal. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. Judah proposed selling Joseph to the Ishmaelites and not laying hands on him, since he was their brother and flesh, and his brothers agreed. JFB notes they had no wish to commit more crime than necessary for their end, and knew the merchants trafficked in slaves. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. Midianite merchants passed by, drew Joseph out of the pit, and sold him to the Ishmaelites for twenty pieces of silver, who brought him into Egypt. JFB notes the sum, if in shekels, was small, and providence used both brothers and merchants, each following his own course, to work out God's purposes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. Reuben returned to the pit, found Joseph gone, and rent his clothes. JFB notes Reuben had taken a roundabout route to rescue Joseph secretly, and found his excellent intentions painfully defeated. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. Reuben returned to his brothers and cried, the child is not, and I, where shall I go? His distress was for his father as much as for himself, since he would have to face Jacob without Joseph. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 31. They took Joseph's coat, killed a kid of the goats, and dipped the coat in blood. Henry notes one sin taught them another: to hide the crime with lying, since he who covers his sin shall not prosper long. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 32. They sent the coat to their father, asking him to know whether it was his son's coat or not. JFB marvels that their sneer, calling Joseph thy son's coat, did not awaken Jacob's suspicion, but extreme grief is blind. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 33. Jacob knew the coat and concluded an evil beast had devoured Joseph, who was without doubt torn in pieces. The brothers' deception worked because Jacob read the bloody coat as proof, not as a claim to be tested. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 34. Jacob rent his clothes, put sackcloth on his loins, and mourned for his son many days. JFB explains the rent garment and coarse black sackcloth as the common signs of Eastern mourning. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 35. All his sons and daughters rose to comfort him, but he refused comfort, saying he would go down to the grave mourning for his son. JFB notes he meant not the earth, since Joseph was supposed torn in pieces, but the place of departed souls where he expected to meet his son. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 36. The Midianites sold Joseph in Egypt to Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh and captain of the guard. Henry closes the chapter in wonder at providence: brothers, merchants, and Potiphar each got their ends, yet God's design — Israel's descent into Egypt and eventual deliverance — was already in train. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
