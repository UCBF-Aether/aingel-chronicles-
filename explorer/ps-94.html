The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Psalms 94 (King James Version (1611) for Psalms 1–150; Charles Thomson, The Septuagint (1808) for Psalm 151)

1 O Lord God, to whom vengeance belongeth; O God, to whom vengeance belongeth, shew thyself.

2 Lift up thyself, thou judge of the earth: render a reward to the proud.

3 LORD, how long shall the wicked, how long shall the wicked triumph?

4 How long shall they utter and speak hard things? and all the workers of iniquity boast themselves?

5 They break in pieces thy people, O LORD, and afflict thine heritage.

6 They slay the widow and the stranger, and murder the fatherless.

7 Yet they say, The LORD shall not see, neither shall the God of Jacob regard it.

8 Understand, ye brutish among the people: and ye fools, when will ye be wise?

9 He that planted the ear, shall he not hear? he that formed the eye, shall he not see?

10 He that chastiseth the heathen, shall not he correct? he that teacheth man knowledge, shall not he know?

11 The LORD knoweth the thoughts of man, that they are vanity.

12 Blessed is the man whom thou chastenest, O LORD, and teachest him out of thy law;

13 That thou mayest give him rest from the days of adversity, until the pit be digged for the wicked.

14 For the LORD will not cast off his people, neither will he forsake his inheritance.

15 But judgment shall return unto righteousness: and all the upright in heart shall follow it.

16 Who will rise up for me against the evildoers? or who will stand up for me against the workers of iniquity?

17 Unless the LORD had been my help, my soul had almost dwelt in silence.

18 When I said, My foot slippeth; thy mercy, O LORD, held me up.

19 In the multitude of my thoughts within me thy comforts delight my soul.

20 Shall the throne of iniquity have fellowship with thee, which frameth mischief by a law?

21 They gather themselves together against the soul of the righteous, and condemn the innocent blood.

22 But the LORD is my defence; and my God is the rock of my refuge.

23 And he shall bring upon them their own iniquity, and shall cut them off in their own wickedness; yea, the LORD our God shall cut them off.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

A plea to the God to whom vengeance belongs to show himself and lift up himself as judge of the earth against proud workers of iniquity who break God's people in pieces, slay widow, stranger and fatherless, and say the LORD shall not see. The psalm answers that claim — he who planted the ear shall hear, he who formed the eye shall see — and blesses the man God chastens and teaches out of his law, since the LORD will not cast off his people. It ends in personal trust: when the speaker's foot slipped, God's mercy held him up, and the LORD, his defence and rock of refuge, will bring the wicked's own iniquity upon them. Interpreted Psalm text above; study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Vengeance belonging to God and Judge of the earth are titles for bold appeal: the throne is the last refuge of oppressed innocence, and the prayer asks first that God show himself, since enemies thought him withdrawn, while self-vengeance would usurp his prerogative. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 2. Lift up thyself, thou judge of the earth: render a reward to the proud, is what the appeal asks of God: first, that he would glorify himself, and get honour to his own name. Wicked persecutors thought God had withdrawn and had forsaken the earth. Lord, say they, show thyself — make them know that thou art and that thou art ready to show thyself strong on the behalf of those whose hearts are upright with thee. The enemies thought God was conquered because his people were. Lord, say they, lift up thyself, be thou exalted in thy own strength. Lift up thyself, to be seen, to be feared — and suffer not thy name to be trampled upon and run down. Second, that he would mortify the oppressors: render a reward to the proud — that is, reckon with them for all their insolence, and the injuries they have done to thy people. These prayers are prophecies, which speak terror to all the sons of violence. The righteous God will deal with them according to their merits. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 3. 'How long shall the wicked triumph?' is a modest pleading with God about the continuance of persecution — when shall this wickedness come to an end? The enemies complained of are wicked indeed, workers of the worst iniquity, lost to honour and virtue, cruel to the innocent and hating the righteous whose goodness shames and condemns them. They are insolent, taking pleasure in magnifying themselves, talking high and big, boasting as if their tongues and hands were their own and they were accountable to none, as if the day were theirs — but there will come a day of reckoning for all their hard speeches against God, his truths, his ways and his people. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 4. How long shall they utter and speak hard things? and all the workers of iniquity boast themselves? is the insolence of the oppressors: they are insolent, and take a pleasure in magnifying themselves. They talk high and talk big; they triumph; they speak loud things; they boast themselves, as if their tongues were their own and their hands too, and they were accountable to none for what they say or do, and as if the day were their own, and they doubted not but to carry the cause against God and religion. Those that speak highly of themselves, that triumph and boast, are apt to speak hardly of others; but there will come a day of reckoning for all their hard speeches which ungodly sinners have spoken against God, his truths, and ways, and people. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 5. Breaking God's people in pieces is impious pleasure in running down God's people because they are his: breaking their assemblies, estates, families and persons in pieces, doing all they can to grieve, crush, run down and root them out. Henry turns it into a plea in intercession: Lord, it is thine — thou hast a property in it, it is thy heritage, thou hast a pleasure in it, and out of it the rent of thy glory in this world issues — and wilt thou suffer wicked men to trample upon it thus? Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 6. Slaying the widow and the stranger and murdering the fatherless is inhumanity toward those least able to help themselves: not only oppressing and impoverishing, but slaying the widow and stranger, not only neglecting the fatherless and making a prey of them, but murdering them because they are weak and exposed and sometimes lie at their mercy. Those whom they should protect from injury they are most injurious to, perhaps because God has taken them into his particular care — who would think it possible that children of men should be thus barbarous? Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 7. 'The Lord shall not see' is the atheistical thought discovered beneath the persecution: though the cry of their wickedness is very great and loud, and they rebel against the light of nature and their own consciences, they have the confidence to say God will not only wink at small faults but shut his eyes at great ones too, or that they have managed it so artfully, under colour of justice and religion, that it will not be adjudged murder. Thus they deny God's government of the world, banter his covenant with his people, and set the judgment to come at defiance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 8. 'Understand, ye brutish' is a fair reasoning with the atheists for their conviction and conversion, to prevent their confusion: though they set up for wits, philosophers and politicians, they are really the brutish among the people — if they would but understand, they would believe. God speaks as if he thought the time long till men would be men and show themselves so by understanding and considering, asking fools when they will be so wise as to know that God sees and regards all they say and do, and to speak and act accordingly as those that must give account — for none are so bad but means should be tried whether they may not yet be made wise while there is life, there is hope. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 9. He that planted the ear and formed the eye proves God's omniscience from creation: the formation of human bodies proves there is a God, and proves also that God has infinitely and transcendently in himself all those perfections that are in any creature. Could he give a perfection to a creature which he has not in himself? The powers of nature are all derived from the God of nature, so by knowledge of our own bodies and organs of sense we may be led toward knowledge of God — if we can see and hear, much more can he, and we are accountable to him for our use of sight and hearing received from him, unlike the heathen gods who had eyes and saw not. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 10. He that chastiseth the heathen, shall not he correct? he that teacheth man knowledge, shall not he know? is argued from the works of providence and of grace: from the works of providence — he that chastises the heathen for their polytheism and idolatry, shall not he much more correct his own people for their atheism and profaneness? He that chastises the children of men for oppressing and wronging one another, shall not he correct those that profess to be his own children, and call themselves so, and yet persecute those that are really so? Shall not we be under his correction, under whose government the whole world is? Does he regard as King of nations, and shall he not much more regard as the God of Jacob? Dr. Hammond gives another very probably sense of this: He that instructs the nations — that is, gives them his law — shall not he correct, that is, shall not he judge them according to that law, and call them to an account for their violations of it? In vain was the law given if there will not be a judgment upon it. And it is true that the same word signifies to chastise and to instruct, because chastisement is intended for instruction and instruction should go along with chastisement. From the works of grace: he that teaches man knowledge, shall he not know? He not only, as the God of nature, has given the light of reason, but, as the God of grace, has given the light of revelation, has shown man what is true wisdom and understanding — and he that does this, shall he not know? The flowing of the streams is a certain sign of the fulness of the fountain. If all knowledge is from God, no doubt all knowledge is in God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 11. The LORD knoweth the thoughts of man, that they are vanity, is the general doctrine of God's omniscience applied: from this general doctrine of God's omniscience, the psalmist not only confutes the atheists, who said, The Lord shall not see — he will not take cognizance of what we do — but awakens us all to consider that God will take cognizance even of what we think: the Lord knows the thoughts of man, that they are vanity. First, he knows those thoughts in particular, concerning God's conniving at the wickedness of the wicked, and knows them to be vain, and laughs at the folly of those who by such fond conceits buoy themselves up in sin. Second, he knows all the thoughts of the children of men, and knows them to be, for the most part, vain, that the imaginations of the thoughts of men's hearts are evil, only evil, and that continually. Even in good thoughts there is a fickleness and inconstancy which may well be called vanity. It concerns us to keep a strict guard upon our thoughts, because God takes particular notice of them. Thoughts are words to God, and vain thoughts are provocations. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 12. Blessed is the man God chastens because he looks above the instruments of trouble and eyes the hand of God, which gives it another name and puts quite another colour upon it: the enemies aim at breaking God's people in pieces, but the truth is that God by them chastens his people as a father the son in whom he delights, the persecutors being only the rod he makes use of. The afflictions of saints are fatherly chastenings designed for instruction, reformation and improvement, and when the teachings of word and Spirit go along with the rebukes of providence they are both marks of adoption and means of sanctification — for it is not the chastening itself that does good, but the teaching that goes along with it and is the exposition of it, the law being the best expositor of providence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 13. That thou mayest give him rest from the days of adversity, until the pit be digged for the wicked, is the promise that God's people shall see through their sufferings: that thou mayest give him rest from the days of adversity. Note, first, there is a rest remaining for the people of God after the days of their adversity, which, though they may be many and long, shall be numbered and finished in due time, and shall not last always. He that sends the trouble will send the rest, that he may comfort them according to the time that he has afflicted them. Second, God therefore teaches his people by their troubles, that he may prepare them for deliverance, and so give them rest from their troubles, that, being reformed, they may be relieved, and that the affliction, having done its work, may be removed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 14. Though God's people may be cast down, they shall certainly not be cast off: whatever their friends do, God will not cast them off nor throw them out of his covenant or out of his care, and will not forsake them because they are his inheritance, which he will not quit his title to nor suffer himself to be disseised of — the comfort Paul took to himself in Romans 11. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 15. Judgment returning to righteousness means that, bad as things are, they shall mend, and though out of course they shall return to their due and ancient channel: the seeming disorders of providence — for real ones there never were — shall be rectified, God's government, which sometimes looks at a distance from righteousness while the wicked prosper and the best men meet the worst usage, returning to righteousness either in this world or at the furthest in the judgment of the great day, which will set all to rights. Then all the upright in heart shall follow it with praises and entire satisfaction, returning to a prosperous and flourishing condition, shining forth out of obscurity and accommodating themselves to providence's dispensations. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 16. Who will rise up for me against the evildoers? or who will stand up for me against the workers of iniquity? is the distressed looking about for help, and being disappointed: Who will rise up for me against the evil-doers? Have I any friend who, in love to me, will appear for me? Has justice any friend who, in a pious indignation at unrighteousness, will plead my injured cause? He looked, but there was none to save, there was none to uphold. Note, when on the side of the oppressors there is power it is no marvel if the oppressed have no comforter, none that dare own them, or speak a good word for them. When St. Paul was brought before Nero's throne of iniquity no man stood by him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 17. Unless the Lord had been his help, the psalmist's soul had almost dwelt in silence — he was at his wits' end, knowing not what to say or do, in his own apprehensions at his life's end, ready to drop into the grave, that land of silence, as Paul received a sentence of death within himself. God helps at a dead lift: when he had almost dwelt in silence, the Lord kept him alive and kept him in heart, and unless he had made God his help by trusting him and expecting relief from him, he could never have kept possession of his own soul — but living by faith kept his head above water, gave him breath and something to say. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 18. When I said, My foot slippeth; thy mercy, O LORD, held me up, is God's goodness as the great support of sinking spirits: When I said, My foot slips — into sin, into ruin, into despair — then thy mercy, O Lord! held me up, kept me from falling, and defeated the design of those who consulted to cast me down from my excellency. We are beholden not only to God's power, but to his pity, for spiritual supports: thy mercy, the gifts of thy mercy and my hope in thy mercy, held me up. God's right hand sustains his people when they look on their right hand and on their left and there is none to uphold; and we are then prepared for his gracious supports when we are sensible of our own weakness and inability to stand by our own strength, and come to God, to acknowledge it, and to tell him how our foot slips. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 19. In the multitude of his thoughts — noisy like a multitude, crowding and jostling one another, unruly and ungovernable, sorrowful, solicitous and timorous thoughts about his case, its construction, the course he should take and its likely issue — God's comforts delight the soul, never more delightful than when they come in so seasonably to silence unquiet thoughts and keep the mind easy. The world's comforts give but little delight to a soul hurried with melancholy thoughts, songs to a heavy heart; but God's comforts reach the soul and not the fancy only, bringing peace and pleasure which the smiles of the world cannot give and its frowns cannot take away. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 20. Shall the throne of iniquity have fellowship with thee, which frameth mischief by a law? is the humble expostulation with God over the throne of iniquity: Lord, shall the throne of iniquity have fellowship with thee? Wilt thou countenance and support these tyrants in their wickedness? We know thou wilt not. A throne has fellowship with God when it is a throne of justice and answers the end of the erecting of it: for by him kings reign, and when they reign for him their judgments are his, and he owns them as his ministers, and whoever resist them, or rise up against them, shall receive to themselves damnation; but, when it becomes a throne of iniquity, it has no longer fellowship with God. Far be it from the just and holy God that he should be the patron of unrighteousness, even in princes and those that sit in thrones, yea, though they be the thrones of the house of David. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 21. They gather themselves together against the soul of the righteous, and condemn the innocent blood, is the framed mischief carried into execution: these workers of iniquity, having framed mischief by a law, take care to see the law executed — for they gather themselves together against the soul of the righteous, who dare not keep the statutes of Omri nor the law of the house of Ahab — and they condemn the innocent blood for violating their decrees. See an instance in Daniel's enemies: they framed mischief by a law when they obtained an impious edict against prayer, and, when Daniel would not obey it, they assembled together against him and condemned his innocent blood to the lions. The best benefactors of mankind have often been thus treated, under colour of law and justice, as the worst of malefactors. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 22. When none else will, nor can, nor dare shelter him, the Lord is his defence, to preserve him from the evil of his troubles, from sinking under them and being ruined by them, and his God is the rock of his refuge, in whose clefts he may take shelter and on whose top he may set his feet, out of danger's reach. God is his people's refuge, to whom they may flee, in whom they are safe and may be secure, the rock of their refuge so strong, so firm, impregnable and immovable as a rock — natural fastnesses sometimes exceeding artificial fortifications. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 23. And he shall bring upon them their own iniquity, and shall cut them off in their own wickedness; yea, the LORD our God shall cut them off, is God reckoning with the injurious: he shall render to them their own iniquity — he shall deal with them according to their deserts, and that very mischief which they did and designed against God's people shall be brought upon themselves: it follows, he shall cut them off in their wickedness. A man cannot be more miserable than his own wickedness will make him if God visit it upon him: it will cut him in the remembrance of it; it will cut him off in the recompence of it. This the psalm concludes with the triumphant assurance of: Yea, the Lord our God, who takes our part and owns us for his, shall cut them off from any fellowship with him, and so shall make them completely miserable — and their pomp and power shall stand them in no stead. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

Note, first, there is a rest remaining for the people of God after the days of their adversity, which, though they may be many and long, shall be numbered and finished in due time, and shall not last always. He that sends the trouble will send the rest, that he may comfort them according to the time that he has afflicted them. Second, God therefore teaches his people by their troubles, that he may prepare them for deliverance, and so give them rest from their troubles, that, being reformed, they may be relieved, and that the affliction, having done its work, may be removed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 14. Though God's people may be cast down, they shall certainly not be cast off: whatever their friends do, God will not cast them off nor throw them out of his covenant or out of his care, and will not forsake them because they are his inheritance, which he will not quit his title to nor suffer himself to be disseised of — the comfort Paul took to himself in Romans 11. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 15. Judgment returning to righteousness means that, bad as things are, they shall mend, and though out of course they shall return to their due and ancient channel: the seeming disorders of providence — for real ones there never were — shall be rectified, God's government, which sometimes looks at a distance from righteousness while the wicked prosper and the best men meet the worst usage, returning to righteousness either in this world or at the furthest in the judgment of the great day, which will set all to rights. Then all the upright in heart shall follow it with praises and entire satisfaction, returning to a prosperous and flourishing condition, shining forth out of obscurity and accommodating themselves to providence's dispensations. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 16. Who will rise up for me against the evildoers? or who will stand up for me against the workers of iniquity? is the distressed looking about for help, and being disappointed: Who will rise up for me against the evil-doers? Have I any friend who, in love to me, will appear for me? Has justice any friend who, in a pious indignation at unrighteousness, will plead my injured cause? He looked, but there was none to save, there was none to uphold. Note, when on the side of the oppressors there is power it is no marvel if the oppressed have no comforter, none that dare own them, or speak a good word for them. When St. Paul was brought before Nero's throne of iniquity no man stood by him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 17. Unless the Lord had been his help, the psalmist's soul had almost dwelt in silence — he was at his wits' end, knowing not what to say or do, in his own apprehensions at his life's end, ready to drop into the grave, that land of silence, as Paul received a sentence of death within himself. God helps at a dead lift: when he had almost dwelt in silence, the Lord kept him alive and kept him in heart, and unless he had made God his help by trusting him and expecting relief from him, he could never have kept possession of his own soul — but living by faith kept his head above water, gave him breath and something to say. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 18. When I said, My foot slippeth; thy mercy, O LORD, held me up, is God's goodness as the great support of sinking spirits: When I said, My foot slips — into sin, into ruin, into despair — then thy mercy, O Lord! held me up, kept me from falling, and defeated the design of those who consulted to cast me down from my excellency. We are beholden not only to God's power, but to his pity, for spiritual supports: thy mercy, the gifts of thy mercy and my hope in thy mercy, held me up. God's right hand sustains his people when they look on their right hand and on their left and there is none to uphold; and we are then prepared for his gracious supports when we are sensible of our own weakness and inability to stand by our own strength, and come to God, to acknowledge it, and to tell him how our foot slips. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 19. In the multitude of his thoughts — noisy like a multitude, crowding and jostling one another, unruly and ungovernable, sorrowful, solicitous and timorous thoughts about his case, its construction, the course he should take and its likely issue — God's comforts delight the soul, never more delightful than when they come in so seasonably to silence unquiet thoughts and keep the mind easy. The world's comforts give but little delight to a soul hurried with melancholy thoughts, songs to a heavy heart; but God's comforts reach the soul and not the fancy only, bringing peace and pleasure which the smiles of the world cannot give and its frowns cannot take away. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 20. Shall the throne of iniquity have fellowship with thee, which frameth mischief by a law? is the humble expostulation with God over the throne of iniquity: Lord, shall the throne of iniquity have fellowship with thee? Wilt thou countenance and support these tyrants in their wickedness? We know thou wilt not. A throne has fellowship with God when it is a throne of justice and answers the end of the erecting of it: for by him kings reign, and when they reign for him their judgments are his, and he owns them as his ministers, and whoever resist them, or rise up against them, shall receive to themselves damnation; but, when it becomes a throne of iniquity, it has no longer fellowship with God. Far be it from the just and holy God that he should be the patron of unrighteousness, even in princes and those that sit in thrones, yea, though they be the thrones of the house of David. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 21. They gather themselves together against the soul of the righteous, and condemn the innocent blood, is the framed mischief carried into execution: these workers of iniquity, having framed mischief by a law, take care to see the law executed — for they gather themselves together against the soul of the righteous, who dare not keep the statutes of Omri nor the law of the house of Ahab — and they condemn the innocent blood for violating their decrees. See an instance in Daniel's enemies: they framed mischief by a law when they obtained an impious edict against prayer, and, when Daniel would not obey it, they assembled together against him and condemned his innocent blood to the lions. The best benefactors of mankind have often been thus treated, under colour of law and justice, as the worst of malefactors. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 22. When none else will, nor can, nor dare shelter him, the Lord is his defence, to preserve him from the evil of his troubles, from sinking under them and being ruined by them, and his God is the rock of his refuge, in whose clefts he may take shelter and on whose top he may set his feet, out of danger's reach. God is his people's refuge, to whom they may flee, in whom they are safe and may be secure, the rock of their refuge so strong, so firm, impregnable and immovable as a rock — natural fastnesses sometimes exceeding artificial fortifications. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 23. And he shall bring upon them their own iniquity, and shall cut them off in their own wickedness; yea, the LORD our God shall cut them off, is God reckoning with the injurious: he shall render to them their own iniquity — he shall deal with them according to their deserts, and that very mischief which they did and designed against God's people shall be brought upon themselves: it follows, he shall cut them off in their wickedness. A man cannot be more miserable than his own wickedness will make him if God visit it upon him: it will cut him in the remembrance of it; it will cut him off in the recompence of it. This the psalm concludes with the triumphant assurance of: Yea, the Lord our God, who takes our part and owns us for his, shall cut them off from any fellowship with him, and so shall make them completely miserable — and their pomp and power shall stand them in no stead. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
