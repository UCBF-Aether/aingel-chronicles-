The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Genesis 2 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Thus the heavens and the earth were finished, and all the host of them.

2 And on the seventh day God ended his work which he had made; and he rested on the seventh day from all his work which he had made.

3 And God blessed the seventh day, and sanctified it: because that in it he had rested from all his work which God created and made.

4 These are the generations of the heavens and of the earth when they were created, in the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens,

5 And every plant of the field before it was in the earth, and every herb of the field before it grew: for the LORD God had not caused it to rain upon the earth, and there was not a man to till the ground.

6 But there went up a mist from the earth, and watered the whole face of the ground.

7 And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.

8 And the LORD God planted a garden eastward in Eden; and there he put the man whom he had formed.

9 And out of the ground made the LORD God to grow every tree that is pleasant to the sight, and good for food; the tree of life also in the midst of the garden, and the tree of knowledge of good and evil.

10 And a river went out of Eden to water the garden; and from thence it was parted, and became into four heads.

11 The name of the first is Pison: that is it which compasseth the whole land of Havilah, where there is gold;

12 And the gold of that land is good: there is bdellium and the onyx stone.

13 And the name of the second river is Gihon: the same is it that compasseth the whole land of Ethiopia.

14 And the name of the third river is Hiddekel: that is it which goeth toward the east of Assyria. And the fourth river is Euphrates.

15 And the LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to dress it and to keep it.

16 And the LORD God commanded the man, saying, Of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat:

17 But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it: for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die.

18 And the LORD God said, It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him an help meet for him.

19 And out of the ground the LORD God formed every beast of the field, and every fowl of the air; and brought them unto Adam to see what he would call them: and whatsoever Adam called every living creature, that was the name thereof.

20 And Adam gave names to all cattle, and to the fowl of the air, and to every beast of the field; but for Adam there was not found an help meet for him.

21 And the LORD God caused a deep sleep to fall upon Adam, and he slept: and he took one of his ribs, and closed up the flesh instead thereof;

22 And the rib, which the LORD God had taken from man, made he a woman, and brought her unto the man.

23 And Adam said, This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh: she shall be called Woman, because she was taken out of Man.

24 Therefore shall a man leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave unto his wife: and they shall be one flesh.

25 And they were both naked, the man and his wife, and were not ashamed.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

After creation is finished and the seventh day is blessed and set apart, the chapter tells the making of humanity again in closer detail: man formed and placed in the garden of Eden, the trees and rivers named, animals brought to be named, and woman formed as his companion. It ends with man and wife together and unashamed. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Heaven, earth and all their host were finished: creation was complete, not ongoing. Henry stresses God ceased from creating, though he still upholds what he made. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. On the seventh day God ended his work and rested. Henry is clear God did not rest from weariness, but as one well pleased, marking the work done. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. God blessed the seventh day and set it apart as holy, because he rested in it. This is the beginning of Sabbath keeping: one day in seven kept for holy rest and holy work. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. This verse turns from the broad creation account to the detailed story of earth and humanity. The name LORD God now appears, pointing to God's personal, covenant dealings with people. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Before any plant of the field had grown, there was no rain and no man to till the ground. The scene is set as a world waiting for the gardener God will place in it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. A mist went up and watered the whole face of the ground. JFB and Henry take this as God's early provision of moisture before rain and farming began. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. God formed man from dust and breathed into him the breath of life, and man became a living soul. The two parts matter: a body from the earth, and life directly from God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. God planted a garden in Eden, in the east, and put the man there. Eden is presented as a prepared home, not a wilderness man had to tame first. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. God made every pleasant and good tree grow there, including the tree of life and the tree of knowledge of good and evil. Henry notes the garden supplied both delight and a test of obedience. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. A river flowed out of Eden to water the garden and divided into four heads. The garden was well watered at its source, a picture of abundance rather than scarcity. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. The first river, Pison, is described as circling Havilah, a land of gold. JFB identifies these rivers as real ancient waterways, grounding Eden in a known geography. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Havilah's gold is called good, and bdellium and onyx are named there. The detail shows Eden's region as richly provided, not barren. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. The second river, Gihon, circles the land of Cush. Naming each river in turn traces the water outward from Eden into the wider world. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. The third river is Hiddekel, flowing east of Assyria, and the fourth is the Euphrates. These familiar names anchor the garden's rivers to lands readers later knew. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. God put the man in the garden to dress it and keep it. Work came before the fall: tending the garden was a dignified calling, not a punishment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. God freely allowed the man to eat of every tree in the garden. The command begins with generous permission, not with prohibition. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. Only the tree of knowledge of good and evil was forbidden, on pain of death. Henry stresses the test was small in itself, which makes obedience a matter of trust in God's word. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. God said it was not good for man to be alone, and promised a helper fit for him. Companionship is presented as part of God's good design, not an afterthought. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. God brought the beasts and birds to Adam to see what he would name them, and the name he gave stuck. Naming shows man's authority over the creatures and his wisdom in discerning their kinds. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Adam named all cattle, birds and beasts, but found no helper fit for himself among them. No animal could supply the companionship and likeness a human partner would. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. God caused a deep sleep to fall on Adam and took a rib from his side, closing up the flesh. The making of woman is God's own surgery, done while Adam rested in trust. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. From the rib God made a woman and brought her to the man. Henry famously notes she was taken from his side, to stand beside him as an equal companion, not from head or foot. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. Adam recognised her as bone of his bones and flesh of his flesh, and called her Woman because she was taken out of man. The name marks both sameness and distinction: she shares his nature, yet is a distinct person. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. Therefore a man leaves father and mother and cleaves to his wife, and they become one flesh. This states the marriage pattern: a new household, a lasting bond, and a union deeper than kinship alone. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. Both were naked and not ashamed. Before sin, there was no shame between them, because there was no guilt to hide. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Investigation (editorial — an open question, not a conclusion)

Genesis 2 and Genesis 1: two creation accounts

This section investigates a question readers often ask, it does not settle it. Genesis 2 tells the making of humanity in close detail, man formed and placed in Eden, animals named, woman formed as companion, after first noting that creation was finished and the seventh day blessed. Genesis 1 tells the same beginning in a broad six-day sequence, from light to humanity, with God speaking each realm into place. Interpreted Chapter text of Genesis 1 and Genesis 2; study notes for both chapters

The two accounts share the same God, the same created order, and the same humanity made for life with God. They differ in shape and focus: Genesis 2 narrows to one garden and one pair, speaking of God forming and planting, while Genesis 1 moves day by day across the whole world and closes by calling everything very good. Interpreted Chapter text of Genesis 1 and Genesis 2

Whether the two chapters are two tellings of one event, a broad account followed by a detailed one, or something else is not established by the sources consulted for this page, and no direct dependence or shared authorship is implied here.
