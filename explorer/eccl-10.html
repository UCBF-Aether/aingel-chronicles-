The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Ecclesiastes 10 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Dead flies cause the ointment of the apothecary to send forth a stinking savour: so doth a little folly him that is in reputation for wisdom and honour.

2 A wise man’s heart is at his right hand; but a fool’s heart at his left.

3 Yea also, when he that is a fool walketh by the way, his wisdom faileth him, and he saith to every one that he is a fool.

4 If the spirit of the ruler rise up against thee, leave not thy place; for yielding pacifieth great offences.

5 There is an evil which I have seen under the sun, as an error which proceedeth from the ruler:

6 Folly is set in great dignity, and the rich sit in low place.

7 I have seen servants upon horses, and princes walking as servants upon the earth.

8 He that diggeth a pit shall fall into it; and whoso breaketh an hedge, a serpent shall bite him.

9 Whoso removeth stones shall be hurt therewith; and he that cleaveth wood shall be endangered thereby.

10 If the iron be blunt, and he do not whet the edge, then must he put to more strength: but wisdom is profitable to direct.

11 Surely the serpent will bite without enchantment; and a babbler is no better.

12 The words of a wise man’s mouth are gracious; but the lips of a fool will swallow up himself.

13 The beginning of the words of his mouth is foolishness: and the end of his talk is mischievous madness.

14 A fool also is full of words: a man cannot tell what shall be; and what shall be after him, who can tell him?

15 The labour of the foolish wearieth every one of them, because he knoweth not how to go to the city.

16 Woe to thee, O land, when thy king is a child, and thy princes eat in the morning!

17 Blessed art thou, O land, when thy king is the son of nobles, and thy princes eat in due season, for strength, and not for drunkenness!

18 By much slothfulness the building decayeth; and through idleness of the hands the house droppeth through.

19 A feast is made for laughter, and wine maketh merry: but money answereth all things.

20 Curse not the king, no not in thy thought; and curse not the rich in thy bedchamber: for a bird of the air shall carry the voice, and that which hath wings shall tell the matter.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Small folly spoils a reputation like dead flies in ointment, and the chapter runs through folly in high places, careless words, sloth that lets the house decay, and rulers young or noble. Its warnings end with a practical one: do not curse the king even in thought, for a bird may carry the word. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Dead flies cause the ointment of the apothecary to send forth a stinking savour, so does a little folly him that is in reputation for wisdom and honour, because one small folly spoils a great reputation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. A wise man's heart is at his right hand, but a fool's heart at his left, because wisdom directs a man aright in his course, while folly leads him crosswise to everything. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. When he that is a fool walks by the way, his wisdom fails him, and he says to everyone that he is a fool, because folly betrays itself in a man's very carriage and speech. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. If the spirit of the ruler rise up against thee, leave not thy place, for yielding pacifies great offences, because a calm refusal to quit one's post disarms anger that resistance would inflame. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. There is an evil seen under the sun, as an error which proceeds from the ruler, namely folly set in great dignity while the rich sit in low place, because promotion often follows favour rather than merit. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Folly is set in great dignity, and the rich sit in low place, which is the world turned upside down, where worth is debased and emptiness exalted. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. He has seen servants upon horses, and princes walking as servants upon the earth, because the order of ranks is often inverted by the caprice of rulers. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. He that digs a pit shall fall into it, and whoso breaks a hedge, a serpent shall bite him, because mischief plotted against others recoils upon the plotter. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Whoso removes stones shall be hurt therewith, and he that cleaves wood shall be endangered thereby, because even lawful labour carries hazards that wisdom must foresee. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. If the iron be blunt and he do not whet the edge, then must he put to more strength, but wisdom is profitable to direct, because skill saves the strength that mere force wastes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Surely the serpent will bite without enchantment, and a babbler is no better, because a tongue left uncharmed does the same harm as a serpent left uncharmed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. The words of a wise man's mouth are gracious, but the lips of a fool will swallow up himself, because wise speech wins favour while foolish speech destroys the speaker. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. The beginning of the words of his mouth is foolishness, and the end of his talk is mischievous madness, because folly grows worse as it goes, from idle talk to dangerous talk. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. A fool also is full of words, yet a man cannot tell what shall be, and what shall be after him, who can tell him, because the fool multiplies words precisely where knowledge fails. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. The labour of the foolish wearies every one of them, because he knows not how to go to the city, because folly cannot manage even the plainest road, and wears itself out on what wisdom does easily. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Woe to thee, O land, when thy king is a child, and thy princes eat in the morning, because a realm ruled by appetite and immaturity is given to excess from the start of the day. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. Blessed art thou, O land, when thy king is the son of nobles, and thy princes eat in due season, for strength and not for drunkenness, because rulers who eat for strength govern, while rulers who eat for pleasure only consume. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. By much slothfulness the building decays, and through idleness of the hands the house drops through, because a house, like a kingdom, falls when its keepers will not work. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. A feast is made for laughter, and wine makes merry, but money answers all things, because in the world's reckoning wealth is treated as the universal provision, though it cannot answer the soul. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Curse not the king, no not in thy thought, and curse not the rich in thy bedchamber, for a bird of the air shall carry the voice, and that which has wings shall tell the matter, because secret disloyalty finds a way to be reported. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
