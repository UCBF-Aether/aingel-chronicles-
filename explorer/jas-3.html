The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — James 3 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 My brethren, be not many masters, knowing that we shall receive the greater condemnation.

2 For in many things we offend all. If any man offend not in word, the same is a perfect man, and able also to bridle the whole body.

3 Behold, we put bits in the horses’ mouths, that they may obey us; and we turn about their whole body.

4 Behold also the ships, which though they be so great, and are driven of fierce winds, yet are they turned about with a very small helm, whithersoever the governor listeth.

5 Even so the tongue is a little member, and boasteth great things. Behold, how great a matter a little fire kindleth!

6 And the tongue is a fire, a world of iniquity: so is the tongue among our members, that it defileth the whole body, and setteth on fire the course of nature; and it is set on fire of hell.

7 For every kind of beasts, and of birds, and of serpents, and of things in the sea, is tamed, and hath been tamed of mankind:

8 But the tongue can no man tame; it is an unruly evil, full of deadly poison.

9 Therewith bless we God, even the Father; and therewith curse we men, which are made after the similitude of God.

10 Out of the same mouth proceedeth blessing and cursing. My brethren, these things ought not so to be.

11 Doth a fountain send forth at the same place sweet water and bitter?

12 Can the fig tree, my brethren, bear olive berries? either a vine, figs? so can no fountain both yield salt water and fresh.

13 Who is a wise man and endued with knowledge among you? let him shew out of a good conversation his works with meekness of wisdom.

14 But if ye have bitter envying and strife in your hearts, glory not, and lie not against the truth.

15 This wisdom descendeth not from above, but is earthly, sensual, devilish.

16 For where envying and strife is, there is confusion and every evil work.

17 But the wisdom that is from above is first pure, then peaceable, gentle, and easy to be intreated, full of mercy and good fruits, without partiality, and without hypocrisy.

18 And the fruit of righteousness is sown in peace of them that make peace.

  • Interpreted — a reasoned explanation or proposed relationship.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Not many should become teachers, James warns, since teachers face stricter judgment and everyone stumbles in speech. The tongue, small as a bit or a rudder, boasts great things, sets the whole course of life on fire, and cannot be tamed, blessing God and cursing people made in his likeness from the same mouth. True wisdom is contrasted with earthly wisdom: the wisdom from above is pure, peaceable, gentle, full of mercy and good fruits, while envy and strife breed confusion and every evil work. What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Be not many masters, knowing we shall receive the greater condemnation. Henry's caution against proud behaviour opens here: those who take the teacher's place invite stricter judgment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 2. In many things we offend all, and if any offend not in word, he is a perfect man able also to bridle the whole body. We are taught to dread an unruly tongue as one of the greatest evils, Henry says. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 3. Bits in horses' mouths turn about their whole body. The tongue, small as a bit, steers the whole course of a life, which is why its government matters so much. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 4. Ships, though great and driven of fierce winds, are turned about with a very small helm. So the tongue, though little, directs great affairs, and the affairs of mankind are thrown into confusion by the tongues of men. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 5. The tongue is a little member that boasts great things, and a little fire kindles a great matter. Every age and every condition of life, private or public, affords examples of this mischief. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 6. The tongue is a fire, a world of iniquity, defiling the whole body and setting on fire the course of nature, itself set on fire of hell. Hell has more to do in promoting the fire of the tongue than men generally think, Henry warns. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 7. Every kind of beasts, birds, serpents, and sea creatures is tamed and has been tamed of mankind, but the tongue can no man tame. No man can tame it without divine grace and assistance; the apostle does not call it impossible, but extremely difficult. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 8. The tongue is an unruly evil, full of deadly poison. Other sins decay with age, Henry notes, but this many times gets worse, as we grow more froward and fretful when natural strength decays. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 9. With the tongue we bless God, even the Father, and with it we curse men made after the similitude of God. That man's tongue confutes itself, Henry says, which at one time pretends to adore God and at another condemns even good men for not using the same words. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 10. Out of the same mouth proceed blessing and cursing, which ought not so to be. True religion will not admit of contradictions, Henry insists; how many sins would be prevented if men would always be consistent! Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 11. Does a fountain send forth at the same place sweet water and bitter? Pious and edifying language is the genuine produce of a sanctified heart, and none who understand Christianity expect curses, lies, boastings, and revilings from a true believer's mouth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 12. Can the fig tree bear olive berries, or a vine figs? So no fountain can yield both salt water and fresh. We look for the fruit of one tree from another no more than we expect such contradictions from a believer. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 13. Who is wise and endued with knowledge among you? Let him show out of a good conversation his works with meekness of wisdom. He who thinks well or talks well is not wise in Scripture's sense, Henry says, if he does not live and act well. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 14. If ye have bitter envying and strife in your hearts, glory not and lie not against the truth. Those who live in malice, envy, and contention live in confusion and are liable to be hurried to any evil work. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 15. This wisdom descends not from above, but is earthly, sensual, devilish. Such wisdom springs up from earthly principles, acts on earthly motives, and is intent on serving earthly purposes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 16. Where envying and strife is, there is confusion and every evil work. The wisdom that is lifted up in envy and contention stands far from Christian love. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 17. The wisdom from above is first pure, then peaceable, gentle, easy to be entreated, full of mercy and good fruits, without partiality and without hypocrisy. It has no disguise or deceit, Henry says; it is sincere, open, steady, uniform, and consistent with itself. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 18. The fruit of righteousness is sown in peace of them that make peace. May the purity, peace, gentleness, teachableness, and mercy shown in all our actions, and the fruits of righteousness abounding in our lives, prove that God has bestowed this excellent gift. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
