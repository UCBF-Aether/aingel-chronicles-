The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Isaiah 28 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Woe to the crown of pride, to the drunkards of Ephraim, whose glorious beauty is a fading flower, which are on the head of the fat valleys of them that are overcome with wine!

2 Behold, the Lord hath a mighty and strong one, which as a tempest of hail and a destroying storm, as a flood of mighty waters overflowing, shall cast down to the earth with the hand.

3 The crown of pride, the drunkards of Ephraim, shall be trodden under feet:

4 And the glorious beauty, which is on the head of the fat valley, shall be a fading flower, and as the hasty fruit before the summer; which when he that looketh upon it seeth, while it is yet in his hand he eateth it up.

5 In that day shall the LORD of hosts be for a crown of glory, and for a diadem of beauty, unto the residue of his people,

6 And for a spirit of judgment to him that sitteth in judgment, and for strength to them that turn the battle to the gate.

7 But they also have erred through wine, and through strong drink are out of the way; the priest and the prophet have erred through strong drink, they are swallowed up of wine, they are out of the way through strong drink; they err in vision, they stumble in judgment.

8 For all tables are full of vomit and filthiness, so that there is no place clean.

9 Whom shall he teach knowledge? and whom shall he make to understand doctrine? them that are weaned from the milk, and drawn from the breasts.

10 For precept must be upon precept, precept upon precept; line upon line, line upon line; here a little, and there a little:

11 For with stammering lips and another tongue will he speak to this people.

12 To whom he said, This is the rest wherewith ye may cause the weary to rest; and this is the refreshing: yet they would not hear.

13 But the word of the LORD was unto them precept upon precept, precept upon precept; line upon line, line upon line; here a little, and there a little; that they might go, and fall backward, and be broken, and snared, and taken.

14 Wherefore hear the word of the LORD, ye scornful men, that rule this people which is in Jerusalem.

15 Because ye have said, We have made a covenant with death, and with hell are we at agreement; when the overflowing scourge shall pass through, it shall not come unto us: for we have made lies our refuge, and under falsehood have we hid ourselves:

16 Therefore thus saith the Lord GOD, Behold, I lay in Zion for a foundation a stone, a tried stone, a precious corner stone, a sure foundation: he that believeth shall not make haste.

17 Judgment also will I lay to the line, and righteousness to the plummet: and the hail shall sweep away the refuge of lies, and the waters shall overflow the hiding place.

18 And your covenant with death shall be disannulled, and your agreement with hell shall not stand; when the overflowing scourge shall pass through, then ye shall be trodden down by it.

19 From the time that it goeth forth it shall take you: for morning by morning shall it pass over, by day and by night: and it shall be a vexation only to understand the report.

20 For the bed is shorter than that a man can stretch himself on it: and the covering narrower than that he can wrap himself in it.

21 For the LORD shall rise up as in mount Perazim, he shall be wroth as in the valley of Gibeon, that he may do his work, his strange work; and bring to pass his act, his strange act.

22 Now therefore be ye not mockers, lest your bands be made strong: for I have heard from the Lord GOD of hosts a consumption, even determined upon the whole earth.

23 Give ye ear, and hear my voice; hearken, and hear my speech.

24 Doth the plowman plow all day to sow? doth he open and break the clods of his ground?

25 When he hath made plain the face thereof, doth he not cast abroad the fitches, and scatter the cummin, and cast in the principal wheat and the appointed barley and the rie in their place?

26 For his God doth instruct him to discretion, and doth teach him.

27 For the fitches are not threshed with a threshing instrument, neither is a cart wheel turned about upon the cummin; but the fitches are beaten out with a staff, and the cummin with a rod.

28 Bread corn is bruised; because he will not ever be threshing it, nor break it with the wheel of his cart, nor bruise it with his horsemen.

29 This also cometh forth from the LORD of hosts, which is wonderful in counsel, and excellent in working.

How to read the labels

Every substantive historical claim in the editorial panels below carries one of three labels, plus a source reference:

  • Documented — directly supported by a cited source.
  • Interpreted — a reasoned explanation or proposed relationship.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

The first of a long prophecy on Ephraim's fall and Judah's folly: the drunkards of Ephraim wear a fading flower as their crown of pride, but God lays a tried cornerstone in Zion for those who trust. A farming parable closes the chapter — the husbandman threshes each grain differently, as God adapts his judgments to each people's need. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Woe to the crown of pride, to the drunkards of Ephraim, whose glorious beauty is a fading flower, which are on the head of the fat valleys of them that are overcome with wine! Chapters 28–33 form almost one continuous prophecy on Ephraim's destruction, Judah's impiety and folly, the Egyptian league, Assyrian straits, and Jehovah's deliverance, with chapter 28 referring to just before Hezekiah's sixth year. The proud crown of the drunkards is Samaria, capital of Ephraim, drunkards literally and metaphorically, like drunkards rushing to their own destruction, and its glorious beauty is a fading flower, for at feasts brows were wreathed with flowers, and Samaria on a hill surrounded by rich valleys as a garland is fading because Ephraim is close to ruin, fulfilled in 721 B.C. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Behold, the Lord has a mighty and strong one, which as a tempest of hail and a destroying storm, as a flood of mighty waters overflowing, will cast down to the earth with the hand. The strong one is the Assyrian, and he will cast down Ephraim and Samaria its crown with violence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. The crown of pride, the drunkards of Ephraim, will be trodden under feet. The crown of the drunkards will be trodden underfoot by the Assyrian flood. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. The glorious beauty, which is on the head of the fat valley, will be a fading flower, and as the hasty fruit before the summer, which when he that looks upon it sees it, while it is yet in his hand he eats it up. The fading flower, their glorious beauty on the head of the fertile valley, will be as the early fig, ripening in June as a delicacy while common figs ripen in August; the Assyrian Shalmaneser will seize it immediately, without delay, describing his eagerness not merely to conquer but utterly to destroy Samaria, whereas other conquered cities were often spared. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. In that day the Lord of hosts will be for a crown of glory, and for a diadem of beauty, unto the residue of his people. The prophet now turns to Judah: a gracious promise to the remnant, the residue, with a warning lest through like sins Judah share Samaria's fate. The crown here stands against Ephraim's fading crown, and the residue is primarily Judah in Hezekiah's prosperous reign, antitypically the elect of God; as God is their crown and diadem, so they are called his, a beautiful reciprocity. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. And for a spirit of judgment to him that sits in judgment, and for strength to them that turn the battle to the gate. Jehovah will inspire Judah's magistrates with justice and her soldiers with strength of spirit, so that her defenders not only repel the foe but drive him to the gates of his own cities. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. But they also have erred through wine, and through strong drink are out of the way; the priest and the prophet have erred through strong drink, they are swallowed up of wine, they are out of the way through strong drink, they err in vision, they stumble in judgment. Though Judah is to survive Ephraim's fall, yet they also have perpetrated like sins, which must be chastised: erred and out of the way mean stagger and reel, repeated to express the frequency of the vice, and if ministers of religion sin so grievously, how much more the other rulers. The prophet errs even in the sacred function of declaring God's revealed will, and the priest stumbles in judgment, though priests administered the law and were forbidden wine before entering the tabernacle. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Whom will he teach knowledge, and whom will he make to understand doctrine? Them that are weaned from the milk, and drawn from the breasts. Here the drunkards scoffingly comment on Isaiah's warnings: whom does Isaiah presume to teach, and whom will he make to understand instruction — does he take us to be just weaned children? Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. For precept must be upon precept, precept upon precept, line upon line, line upon line, here a little, and there a little. The scoffers mimic Isaiah's constant repetition as if to little children: precept upon precept, line — a rule or law — upon line, with the Hebrew repetition of sounds expressing their scorn of his stammering speech. God's mode of teaching offends sinners' pride by its simplicity, but as stammerers by drunkenness and children in knowledge of God, they needed to be spoken to in children's language and with stammering lips, a just and merciful retribution. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. For with stammering lips and another tongue he will speak to this people. This is Isaiah's reply to the scoffers: their drunken questions will be answered by severe lessons from God conveyed through the Assyrians and Babylonians, whose dialect, though Semitic like Hebrew, sounded to the Jews like the speech of stammerers; to those who will not understand, God will speak still more unintelligibly. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. To whom he said: This is the rest wherewith you may cause the weary to rest, and this is the refreshing, yet they would not hear. Rather, He, Jehovah, who has said to them: this is the rest — primarily rest from national warlike preparations, the Jews being weary through preceding calamities such as the Syro-Israelite invasion, but spiritually the rest found in obeying those very precepts they jeered at — yet they would not hear. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. But the word of the Lord was unto them precept upon precept, precept upon precept, line upon line, line upon line, here a little, and there a little, that they might go, and fall backward, and be broken, and snared, and taken. Therefore, because they would not hear, God's word became to them precept upon precept as a stumbling-block, by defect of will, and they go and fall — an image apt for drunkards who in trying to go forward fall backward — and are broken, snared, and taken. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Therefore hear the word of the Lord, you scornful men, that rule this people which is in Jerusalem. The scornful men are the scoffers of verses 9–10, now addressed as rulers of Jerusalem's people. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Because you have said: We have made a covenant with death, and with hell are we at agreement; when the overflowing scourge passes through, it will not come unto us, for we have made lies our refuge, and under falsehood have we hid ourselves. They said this virtually in conduct if not in words: there may be a tacit reference to confidence in their covenant with the Assyrians in Hezekiah's early prosperous reign, as if it ensured Judah from evil whatever befell Ephraim, but the full meaning applies to all lulled in false security spiritually, for only the godly are in covenant with death. The overflowing scourge is the hostile Assyrian armies like an overwhelming flood passing through Judea on their way to Egypt, and lies are what Isaiah calls their sentiments by their true name, though they did not use the words. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Therefore thus says the Lord God: Behold, I lay in Zion for a foundation a stone, a tried stone, a precious corner stone, a sure foundation; he that believes will not make haste. Literally, Behold Me as Him who has laid the stone in His divine counsel, for none save God could lay it; the stone is Jesus Christ, for Hezekiah or the temple do not realise the full significance, but only in type point to Him, in whom the prophecy receives its exhaustive accomplishment. The stone is tried by the devil, by men, and even by God, a stone of tested solidity to bear the vast superstructure of man's redemption, and its tested righteousness gives its peculiar merit to His vicarious sacrifice. Though a scourge visits Judea, God's gracious purpose as to the elect remnant and His kingdom centred on Zion will not fail, because it rests on Messiah. Precious means literally of preciousness — He is preciousness — the corner-stone is laid where two walls meet and connects them, often costly, and he that believes will not make haste in fleeing alarm, or be ashamed, for he who rests on Him will not have the shame of disappointment nor flee in sudden panic. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. Judgment also will I lay to the line, and righteousness to the plummet, and the hail will sweep away the refuge of lies, and the waters will overflow the hiding place. The line is the measuring-line of the plummet, and God will appoint judgment for the rule and justice for the plummet: as the corner-stone stands most perpendicular and exactly proportioned, so Jehovah, while holding out grace to believers in the Foundation-stone, will judge the scoffers according to the exact justice of the law, and hail, divine judgment, will sweep away their refuge of lies while waters overflow their hiding place. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. Your covenant with death will be disannulled, and your agreement with hell will not stand; when the overflowing scourge passes through, then you will be trodden down by it. The covenant will be disannulled, obliterated as letters traced on a waxen tablet are obliterated by passing the stylus over it, and trodden down passes from the scourge metaphor to the thing meant, the army which treads down its enemies. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. From the time that it goes forth it will take you, for morning by morning it will pass over, by day and by night, and it will be a vexation only to understand the report. As often as it comes over, it will overtake them, like a flood returning from time to time, for frequent hostile invasions will assail Judah after the ten tribes' deportation, and it will be a terror even to hear the mere report of it — or hard treatment only will make them understand instruction, since they scorned the prophet's simple way and must be taught by severe adversity. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. For the bed is shorter than that a man can stretch himself on it, and the covering narrower than that he can wrap himself in it. This is proverbial: they will find all their sources of confidence fail them, and all will be hopeless perplexity in their affairs. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. For the Lord will rise up as in Mount Perazim, and be wroth as in the valley of Gibeon, that he may do his work, his strange work, and bring to pass his act, his strange act. At Perazim in the valley of Rephaim, Jehovah by David broke forth as waters do and made a breach among the Philistines, as Perazim means, expressing sudden and complete overthrow, and at Gibeon he likewise overthrew David's enemies; his work is strange as being against his own people, for judgment is not what God delights in, though necessary, and his work is punishing the guilty. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Now therefore be not mockers, lest your bands be made strong, for I have heard from the Lord God of hosts a consumption, even determined upon the whole earth. The mockers are guilty of the sin of verses 9–10, and their bands are their Assyrian bondage, Judah then being tributary to Assyria, or lest their punishment be made still more severe; the consumption is the determined destruction of chapters 10 and 24. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. Give ear, and hear my voice; hearken, and hear my speech. The prophet calls attention to the following illustration from husbandry: as the husbandman does his different kinds of work each in its right time and due proportion, so God adapts His measures to varying exigencies — now mercy, now judgments, now punishing sooner, now later — answering the scoff that His judgments, being put off so long, would never come; His object is not to destroy His people any more than the farmer's object in threshing is to destroy his crop, vindicating God's strange work in punishing His people. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. Does the plowman plow all day to sow? Does he open and break the clods of his ground? The plowman is not always ploughing, emphatically not all day: he also sows, and that in accordance with sure rules. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. When he has made plain the face thereof, does he not cast abroad the fitches, and scatter the cummin, and cast in the principal wheat and the appointed barley and the rye in their place? When the surface of the ground is made plain and level by harrowing, he casts abroad the fitches — rather dill or fennel, Nigella with black seed easily beaten out, used as condiment and medicine — and scatters cummin used the same way, and plants the wheat in rows, for wheat was thought to yield the largest crop planted sparingly, and barley in its appointed place, and rye in the field's border. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. For his God does instruct him to discretion, and does teach him. God first taught man the due rules of husbandry, instructing him to discretion. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. For the fitches are not threshed with a threshing instrument, neither is a cart wheel turned about upon the cummin, but the fitches are beaten out with a staff, and the cummin with a rod. The husbandman uses the same discretion in threshing: dill and cummin, leguminous and tender grains, are beaten out not with the heavy corn-drag but with a staff, for heavy instruments would crush and injure the seed; the cart wheel is two iron wheels armed with iron teeth like a saw joined by a wooden axle, and the corn-drag is three or four wooden cylinders armed with iron teeth or flint stones underneath, joined like a sledge, cutting straw for fodder as well as separating corn. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. Bread corn is bruised, because he will not ever be threshing it, nor break it with the wheel of his cart, nor bruise it with his horsemen. Bread corn is threshed with the corn-drag, as contrasted with dill and cummin beaten with the staff, or trodden out by cattle hoofs on the threshing-floor; but though threshed with the heavy instrument, yet he will not always be thus threshing it, nor drive over it continually with the wheel, nor bruise it with his horses. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. This also comes forth from the Lord of hosts, who is wonderful in counsel, and excellent in working. The skill wherewith the husbandman duly adjusts his modes of threshing is given by God, as well as the skill wherewith he tills and sows; therefore God must also be able to adapt His modes of treatment to the several moral needs of His creatures. His object in sending tribulation — from the Latin tribulum, a threshing instrument — is to sever the moral chaff from the wheat, not to crush utterly, and His judgments are usually in the line of our offences, so by the nature of the judgments we may usually ascertain the nature of the sin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

The covenant will be disannulled, obliterated as letters traced on a waxen tablet are obliterated by passing the stylus over it, and trodden down passes from the scourge metaphor to the thing meant, the army which treads down its enemies. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. From the time that it goes forth it will take you, for morning by morning it will pass over, by day and by night, and it will be a vexation only to understand the report. As often as it comes over, it will overtake them, like a flood returning from time to time, for frequent hostile invasions will assail Judah after the ten tribes' deportation, and it will be a terror even to hear the mere report of it — or hard treatment only will make them understand instruction, since they scorned the prophet's simple way and must be taught by severe adversity. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. For the bed is shorter than that a man can stretch himself on it, and the covering narrower than that he can wrap himself in it. This is proverbial: they will find all their sources of confidence fail them, and all will be hopeless perplexity in their affairs. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. For the Lord will rise up as in Mount Perazim, and be wroth as in the valley of Gibeon, that he may do his work, his strange work, and bring to pass his act, his strange act. At Perazim in the valley of Rephaim, Jehovah by David broke forth as waters do and made a breach among the Philistines, as Perazim means, expressing sudden and complete overthrow, and at Gibeon he likewise overthrew David's enemies; his work is strange as being against his own people, for judgment is not what God delights in, though necessary, and his work is punishing the guilty. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Now therefore be not mockers, lest your bands be made strong, for I have heard from the Lord God of hosts a consumption, even determined upon the whole earth. The mockers are guilty of the sin of verses 9–10, and their bands are their Assyrian bondage, Judah then being tributary to Assyria, or lest their punishment be made still more severe; the consumption is the determined destruction of chapters 10 and 24. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. Give ear, and hear my voice; hearken, and hear my speech. The prophet calls attention to the following illustration from husbandry: as the husbandman does his different kinds of work each in its right time and due proportion, so God adapts His measures to varying exigencies — now mercy, now judgments, now punishing sooner, now later — answering the scoff that His judgments, being put off so long, would never come; His object is not to destroy His people any more than the farmer's object in threshing is to destroy his crop, vindicating God's strange work in punishing His people. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. Does the plowman plow all day to sow? Does he open and break the clods of his ground? The plowman is not always ploughing, emphatically not all day: he also sows, and that in accordance with sure rules. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. When he has made plain the face thereof, does he not cast abroad the fitches, and scatter the cummin, and cast in the principal wheat and the appointed barley and the rye in their place? When the surface of the ground is made plain and level by harrowing, he casts abroad the fitches — rather dill or fennel, Nigella with black seed easily beaten out, used as condiment and medicine — and scatters cummin used the same way, and plants the wheat in rows, for wheat was thought to yield the largest crop planted sparingly, and barley in its appointed place, and rye in the field's border. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. For his God does instruct him to discretion, and does teach him. God first taught man the due rules of husbandry, instructing him to discretion. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. For the fitches are not threshed with a threshing instrument, neither is a cart wheel turned about upon the cummin, but the fitches are beaten out with a staff, and the cummin with a rod. The husbandman uses the same discretion in threshing: dill and cummin, leguminous and tender grains, are beaten out not with the heavy corn-drag but with a staff, for heavy instruments would crush and injure the seed; the cart wheel is two iron wheels armed with iron teeth like a saw joined by a wooden axle, and the corn-drag is three or four wooden cylinders armed with iron teeth or flint stones underneath, joined like a sledge, cutting straw for fodder as well as separating corn. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. Bread corn is bruised, because he will not ever be threshing it, nor break it with the wheel of his cart, nor bruise it with his horsemen. Bread corn is threshed with the corn-drag, as contrasted with dill and cummin beaten with the staff, or trodden out by cattle hoofs on the threshing-floor; but though threshed with the heavy instrument, yet he will not always be thus threshing it, nor drive over it continually with the wheel, nor bruise it with his horses. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. This also comes forth from the Lord of hosts, who is wonderful in counsel, and excellent in working. The skill wherewith the husbandman duly adjusts his modes of threshing is given by God, as well as the skill wherewith he tills and sows; therefore God must also be able to adapt His modes of treatment to the several moral needs of His creatures. His object in sending tribulation — from the Latin tribulum, a threshing instrument — is to sever the moral chaff from the wheat, not to crush utterly, and His judgments are usually in the line of our offences, so by the nature of the judgments we may usually ascertain the nature of the sin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
