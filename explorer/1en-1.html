The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — 1 Enoch 1 (R. H. Charles, The Book of Enoch (London: SPCK, 1917; reprint of his 2nd edition, 1912); Project Gutenberg no. 77935)

1 The words of the blessing of Enoch, wherewith he blessed the elect 〚and〛 righteous, who will be living in the day of tribulation, when all the wicked 〚and godless〛 are to be removed.

2 And he took up his parable and said—Enoch a righteous man, whose eyes were opened by God, saw the vision of the Holy One in the heavens, 〚which〛 the angels showed me, and from them I heard everything, and from them I understood as I saw, but not for this generation, but for a remote one which is for to come.

3 Concerning the elect I said, and took up my parable concerning them: The Holy Great One will come forth from His dwelling,

4 And the eternal God will tread upon the earth, (even) on Mount Sinai, [And appear from His camp] And appear in the strength of His might from the heaven ⌜of heavens⌝.

5 And all shall be smitten with fear, And the Watchers shall quake, And great fear and trembling shall seize them unto the ends of the earth.

6 And the high mountains shall be shaken, And the high hills shall be made low, And shall melt like wax before the flame

7 And the earth shall be ⌜wholly⌝ rent in sunder, And all that is upon the earth shall perish, And there shall be a judgement upon all (men).

8 But with the righteous He will make peace, And will protect the elect, And mercy shall be upon them. And they shall all belong to God, And they shall be prospered, And they shall ⌜all⌝ be blessed. ⌜And He will help them all⌝, And light shall appear unto them, ⌜And He will make peace with them⌝.

9 And behold! He cometh with ten thousands of ⌜His⌝ holy ones To execute judgement upon all, And to destroy ⌜all⌝ the ungodly: And to convict all flesh Of all the works ⌜of their ungodliness⌝ which they have ungodly committed, ⌜And of all the hard things which⌝ ungodly sinners ⌜have spoken⌝ against Him.

How to read the labels

Every substantive historical claim in the editorial panels below carries one of three labels, plus a source reference:

  • Documented — directly supported by a cited source.
  • Interpreted — a reasoned explanation or proposed relationship.
  • Unresolved — evidence is insufficient to establish the claim.

A claim does NOT become Documented merely because a commentary states it. Commentary views are attributed to their authors and labeled Interpreted unless a primary textual source directly supports the claim. Where evidence runs out, the page says Unresolved and stops — it does not fill the gap.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

In plain language, this chapter opens as a blessing and a warning together. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter The speaker, Enoch, blesses one group — called “the elect and righteous” in verse 1 — and announces a coming day when the wicked will be removed, the righteous protected, and judgement carried out. The two sides of that one hope run through the whole chapter: peace and mercy for the righteous (verse 8) and judgement on the ungodly (verse 9).

The vision is deliberately placed in the future. Verse 2 says Enoch saw it “not for this generation, but for a remote one which is for to come.” Documented Chapter text, verse 2, above Interpreted In other words, the chapter presents itself as a message for readers living long after Enoch, not as a report of something already finished in his own day.

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. The book opens as a blessing for the elect and righteous who will be living in the day of tribulation, when the wicked and godless are removed. Its whole hope is that the present order, where the wicked seem triumphant, gives way to a new age in which the righteous are vindicated. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 2. Enoch's vision is not for his own generation but for a remote one yet to come. Apocalyptic writing of this kind claims divine secrets, hidden from the beginning, revealed to God-fearing men so that they may foresee the march of future events. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 8. Against the judgement to come, the righteous are promised peace, protection, and mercy: they shall belong to God, be prospered and blessed, and light shall appear to them. The introduction singles this passage out as the book's hope expressed most beautifully. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 9. The Holy One comes with ten thousands of his holy ones to execute judgement upon all, destroying the ungodly and convicting all flesh of ungodly deeds and hard speeches against him. Judgement is the other side of the book's hope: the present world must be judged before the new age dawns. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley

Unfamiliar terms

  • The elect. Interpreted As used here, “the elect” means the chosen righteous people whom the blessing is for (verses 1 and 8). The chapter does not give a further definition of the word. Chapter text, above
  • The Watchers. Documented Verse 5 names “the Watchers” among those who will quake in fear when the Holy Great One comes forth. Unresolved Within chapter 1 alone, the chapter does not define who the Watchers are in full; this page does not import a fuller definition from elsewhere to fill that gap. Chapter text, verse 5, above
  • “Took up his parable.” Interpreted This is old-fashioned wording for taking up a discourse, speech, or solemn utterance — Enoch begins to speak in verses 2–3. It is not a claim that the chapter is a fictional story. Chapter text, verses 2–3, above
  • “Ten thousands of his holy ones.” Interpreted Verse 9 pictures the coming One accompanied by a vast host. The phrase reads as a picture of overwhelming majesty and judgement, not as a head-count this page can verify. Chapter text, verse 9, above

A connection inside the text’s own world: Jude 14–15

The letter of Jude, in the King James text of the Standard corpus, contains wording closely paralleling 1 Enoch 1:9:

14 And Enoch also, the seventh from Adam, prophesied of these, saying, Behold, the Lord cometh with ten thousands of his saints,

15 To execute judgment upon all, and to convince all that are ungodly among them of all their ungodly deeds which they have ungodly committed, and of all their hard speeches which ungodly sinners have spoken against him.

Jude 14–15, King James Version, Standard corpus (jude.json).

Documented Jude 14 attributes the saying to “Enoch also, the seventh from Adam,” and Jude 15 repeats the judgement language of 1 Enoch 1:9 — executing judgement upon all, and convicting the ungodly of ungodly deeds and hard speeches against him. Interpreted That close wording match is why readers connect the two passages. This page states only that textual parallel; it does not claim that Jude quotes Charles’s 1917 English wording, since Jude here is in the King James Version and 1 Enoch here is in Charles’s translation.

EmphasisReadingLabel
Hope firstThe chapter’s centre is the blessing: peace, protection, mercy, and light for the righteous (verse 8).Interpreted
Judgement firstThe chapter’s centre is the coming judgement: fear, shaking, and conviction of the ungodly (verses 5–7, 9).Interpreted

Both readings draw on verses actually in the chapter. Interpreted The disagreement is one of emphasis, not of different texts: the chapter holds blessing and judgement together, and a reader who stresses only one side will miss the other.

Enoch – Genesis: an investigation, not a conclusion (editorial investigation — not a conclusion)

This section investigates a possible connection. It does not conclude one. No parallel below implies direct dependence, shared authorship, or historical certainty.

Parallel or differenceWhat each text saysLabel
Both open with God’s majestyGenesis 1 opens with God creating; 1 Enoch 1 opens with the Holy Great One coming forth from His dwelling to judge and to bless. The shared theme is God’s sovereign power over heaven and earth.Interpreted
Different kind of chapterGenesis 1 is a creation account; 1 Enoch 1 is a blessing and judgement vision for a remote generation. Their purposes differ even where their language about God’s power overlaps.Interpreted
Enoch the person, in a different chapterEnoch appears in the Genesis 5 genealogy, a different chapter from Genesis 1. The Enoch who speaks in 1 Enoch 1 must not be conflated with the Genesis 1 creation account itself.Documented Standard corpus, gen.json, chapter 5 genealogy names Enoch

Genesis 1:1, in the King James text of the Standard corpus, reads:

1 In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth.

Genesis 1:1, King James Version, Standard corpus (gen.json).

Interpreted Set beside 1 Enoch 1:3 (“The Holy Great One will come forth from His dwelling”), the parallel is thematic: both chapters present a God whose action reaches the whole earth. Interpreted The difference matters as much as the parallel: Genesis 1 narrates making, while 1 Enoch 1 announces coming judgement and blessing. Neither the parallel nor the difference, on the evidence presented here, proves that one text depends on the other.
