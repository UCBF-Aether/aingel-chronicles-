The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Titus 1 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Paul, a servant of God, and an apostle of Jesus Christ, according to the faith of God’s elect, and the acknowledging of the truth which is after godliness;

2 In hope of eternal life, which God, that cannot lie, promised before the world began;

3 But hath in due times manifested his word through preaching, which is committed unto me according to the commandment of God our Saviour;

4 To Titus, mine own son after the common faith: Grace, mercy, and peace, from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ our Saviour.

5 For this cause left I thee in Crete, that thou shouldest set in order the things that are wanting, and ordain elders in every city, as I had appointed thee:

6 If any be blameless, the husband of one wife, having faithful children not accused of riot or unruly.

7 For a bishop must be blameless, as the steward of God; not selfwilled, not soon angry, not given to wine, no striker, not given to filthy lucre;

8 But a lover of hospitality, a lover of good men, sober, just, holy, temperate;

9 Holding fast the faithful word as he hath been taught, that he may be able by sound doctrine both to exhort and to convince the gainsayers.

10 For there are many unruly and vain talkers and deceivers, specially they of the circumcision:

11 Whose mouths must be stopped, who subvert whole houses, teaching things which they ought not, for filthy lucre’s sake.

12 One of themselves, even a prophet of their own, said, The Cretians are alway liars, evil beasts, slow bellies.

13 This witness is true. Wherefore rebuke them sharply, that they may be sound in the faith;

14 Not giving heed to Jewish fables, and commandments of men, that turn from the truth.

15 Unto the pure all things are pure: but unto them that are defiled and unbelieving is nothing pure; but even their mind and conscience is defiled.

16 They profess that they know God; but in works they deny him, being abominable, and disobedient, and unto every good work reprobate.

Every substantive historical claim in the editorial panels below carries one of three labels, plus a source reference:

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Paul presents his apostleship as service toward the faith of God's elect and knowledge of truth leading to godliness, and reminds Titus why he was left in Crete: to set in order what remains and appoint elders in every town, blameless, faithful in household and doctrine, able to encourage and refute. Many unruly talkers, especially of the circumcision, must be silenced, and Paul quotes a Cretan prophet's own harsh verdict on Cretans. What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Paul presents himself as God's servant and Christ's apostle, whose work exists to advance the faith of God's elect and a knowledge of truth that leads to godliness. JFB notes the apostleship is described by its purpose: faith and godly knowledge, not self-importance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. That faith rests on the hope of eternal life, promised by God who cannot lie and purposed before time began. The promise was hidden in God's eternal purpose and is now made known in the gospel. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. In God's own appointed seasons he made his word known through preaching, a trust committed to Paul by God's command. Henry stresses that faith comes by hearing this preached word. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Titus is Paul's genuine son in the faith they share in common, a faith embracing Gentile and Jew alike. The greeting of grace, mercy, and peace traces every blessing to God's free favour and its fruit in pardon and peace. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Titus was left in Crete to finish unfinished work: setting things in order and appointing elders in every city, as Paul had directed. JFB notes elder and bishop name the same office, one of dignity, the other of oversight of the flock. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. An elder must be blameless, the husband of one wife, with believing children who are not accused of riot or disobedience. JFB stresses reputation: one who could not lead his own household in faith could hardly lead others. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. A bishop, as God's steward over God's household, must be blameless, and therefore not self-willed, quick-tempered, given to wine, violent, or greedy for gain. The greater the Master, JFB notes, the greater the character required in his servant. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Positively, he must love hospitality and love what is good, and be sober, just, holy, and self-controlled. Henry describes these as the spirit and practice fitting one who must be an example of good works. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. He must hold firmly to the faithful word he was taught, so that he can both encourage with sound doctrine and refute those who contradict it. Sound teaching, not novelty, is the elder's chief weapon. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Many in Crete were unruly, empty talkers and deceivers, especially from the circumcision party. Their talk ran against the faithful word of verse 9, and corrupted Judaism was the source of the evil. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Their mouths must be stopped, because they overturn whole households by teaching what they ought not, for shameful gain. Henry calls this serving a worldly interest under a pretence of religion. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Paul quotes a Cretan prophet of their own, Epimenides, who called Cretans always liars, evil beasts, and slow bellies. The witness stings precisely because it comes from one of themselves. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. This testimony is true, so Titus must rebuke them sharply, not from anger but so that they may become sound in the faith. Henry adds that even the sharpest reproof must aim at the good of the reproved. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Soundness means not giving heed to Jewish fables and human commandments that turn people away from the truth. Such teaching was profitless and tended away from godliness, not toward it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. To the pure all things are pure, because purity lies not in outward things themselves but in the heart of the user. To the defiled and unbelieving, by contrast, nothing is pure, for even mind and conscience are defiled. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. They profess to know God but deny him by their works, being detestable, disobedient, and unfit for any good work. Henry warns against being quick to fix this charge on others while careless that it might apply to ourselves. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Unfamiliar terms

  • bishop, as steward. overseer entrusted with God’s household, who must be blameless and hold fast the faithful word (verse 7) Interpreted Editorial gloss on the Text panel above
  • a prophet of their own. a Cretan prophet, named by the study note as Epimenides, whose verdict on Cretans Paul quotes (verse 12) Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
