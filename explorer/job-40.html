The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 40 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Moreover the LORD answered Job, and said,

2 Shall he that contendeth with the Almighty instruct him? he that reproveth God, let him answer it.

3 Then Job answered the LORD, and said,

4 Behold, I am vile; what shall I answer thee? I will lay mine hand upon my mouth.

5 Once have I spoken; but I will not answer: yea, twice; but I will proceed no further.

6 Then answered the LORD unto Job out of the whirlwind, and said,

7 Gird up thy loins now like a man: I will demand of thee, and declare thou unto me.

8 Wilt thou also disannul my judgment? wilt thou condemn me, that thou mayest be righteous?

9 Hast thou an arm like God? or canst thou thunder with a voice like him?

10 Deck thyself now with majesty and excellency; and array thyself with glory and beauty.

11 Cast abroad the rage of thy wrath: and behold every one that is proud, and abase him.

12 Look on every one that is proud, and bring him low; and tread down the wicked in their place.

13 Hide them in the dust together; and bind their faces in secret.

14 Then will I also confess unto thee that thine own right hand can save thee.

15 Behold now behemoth, which I made with thee; he eateth grass as an ox.

16 Lo now, his strength is in his loins, and his force is in the navel of his belly.

17 He moveth his tail like a cedar: the sinews of his stones are wrapped together.

18 His bones are as strong pieces of brass; his bones are like bars of iron.

19 He is the chief of the ways of God: he that made him can make his sword to approach unto him.

20 Surely the mountains bring him forth food, where all the beasts of the field play.

21 He lieth under the shady trees, in the covert of the reed, and fens.

22 The shady trees cover him with their shadow; the willows of the brook compass him about.

23 Behold, he drinketh up a river, and hasteth not: he trusteth that he can draw up Jordan into his mouth.

24 He taketh it with his eyes: his nose pierceth through snares.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

God pauses for Job’s reply — shall he that contends with the Almighty instruct him? — and Job answers: behold, I am vile; I lay my hand upon my mouth; I have spoken once, yea twice, but will proceed no further. God answers a second time: wilt thou disannul my judgment and condemn me, that thou mayest be righteous? Deck yourself with majesty, abase the proud, tread down the wicked — and then consider behemoth, chief of the ways of God, eating grass as an ox, whose strength no man can tame. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Moreover the LORD answered Job, and said. JFB introduces God's second address: he had paused for a reply, but Job was silent. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Shall he that contends with the Almighty instruct him? He that reproves God, let him answer it. JFB renders he that contendeth as Job, who had so often expressed a wish to contend with God, or rebuketh: does Job now still wish to set God right, and must he answer the questions God has asked? Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Then Job answered the LORD, and said. JFB notes the Lord here is JEHOVAH, now addressed by a humbled Job. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Behold, I am vile; what shall I answer thee? I will lay mine hand upon my mouth. JFB explains that it is a very different thing to vindicate ourselves before God from what it is before men: Job could do the latter, not the former, and laying hand upon mouth means having no plea to offer. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Once have I spoken, but I will not answer: yea, twice, but I will proceed no further. JFB renders once and twice as oftentimes, more than once, with I have spoken as namely against God, and not answer as not pleading against him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Then answered the LORD unto Job out of the whirlwind, and said. JFB notes again the Lord is JEHOVAH, speaking a second time from the whirlwind. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Gird up thy loins now like a man: God will demand of Job, and Job shall declare unto him. JFB notes that since Job has not only spoken against God but accused him of injustice, God challenges him to try whether he could govern the world as God by his power does, and punish the proud and wicked. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Wilt thou also disannul God's judgment? Wilt thou condemn him, that thou mayest be righteous? JFB explains that Job would not only contend with God but set aside his judgment or justice in the government of the world, and condemn as declaring God unrighteous in order that Job may be accounted righteous. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Hast thou an arm like God? Or canst thou thunder with a voice like him? JFB renders arm as God's omnipotence, and thunder as God's voice. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Deck thyself now with majesty and excellency, and array thyself with glory and beauty. JFB asks whether Job has power and majesty like God's to enable him to judge and govern the world. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Cast abroad the rage of thy wrath, and behold every one that is proud, and abase him. JFB renders rage as pouring out the redundant floods of wrath, and behold as trying whether Job, as God, by a mere glance can abase the proud. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Look on every one that is proud, and bring him low, and tread down the wicked in their place. JFB renders proud as high, and in their place as on the spot, suddenly, before they can move from their place. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Hide them in the dust together, and bind their faces in secret. JFB explains abase and remove them out of the sight of men, with bind faces as shutting up their persons, or rather the custom of binding a cloth over the faces of persons about to be executed, and in secret as consigning them to darkness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Then will God also confess unto Job that his own right hand can save him. JFB renders confess as extol: since Job cannot do these works, he must instead of censuring extol God's government, and thine own hand save as eternal salvation by Christ. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Behold now behemoth, which God made with thee; he eats grass as an ox. JFB explains that God shows that if Job cannot bring under control the lower animals, of which he selects the two most striking, behemoth on land and leviathan in the water, much less is he capable of governing the world: behemoth agrees in part with the hippopotamus and in part with the elephant, but exactly with neither, being rather a poetical personification of the great Pachydermata, with the hippopotamus predominant, and made with thee as God made thyself, yet how great the difference. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Lo now, his strength is in his loins, and his force is in the navel of his belly. JFB renders navel as muscles of his belly, the weakest point of the elephant, therefore not meant. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. He moves his tail like a cedar: the sinews of his stones are wrapped together. JFB explains like a cedar as the tempest bends the cedar, so it can move its smooth thick tail, though the cedar implies straightness and length such as do not apply to the river horse's short tail, and stones as thighs, wrapped as firmly twisted together like a thick rope. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. His bones are as strong pieces of brass; his bones are like bars of iron. JFB renders strong as tubes of copper. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. He is the chief of the ways of God: he that made him can make his sword to approach unto him. JFB explains chief of the works of God, so ways, and can make his sword to approach as has furnished him with his sword, the sickle-like teeth with which he cuts down grain. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Surely the mountains bring him forth food, where all the beasts of the field play. JFB notes the mountain is not his usual haunt, and beasts play as a graphic trait: though armed with such teeth, he lets the beasts play near him unhurt, for his food is grass. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. He lies under the shady trees, in the covert of the reed, and fens. JFB renders lieth as leading an inactive life, and shady trees as lotus bushes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. The shady trees cover him with their shadow; the willows of the brook compass him about. JFB again translates shady trees as lotus bushes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. Behold, he drinks up a river, and hastens not: he trusts that he can draw up Jordan into his mouth. JFB renders it as though a river be violent, he trembles not, for though living on land he can live in the water too, and is secure though a Jordan swell up to his mouth, with Jordan used for any great river as a poetical generalization. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. He takes it with his eyes: his nose pierces through snares. JFB renders it as will any take him by open force, before his eyes, or pierce his nose with cords? No, he can only be taken by guile, and in a pitfall. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Unfamiliar terms

  • Behemoth (verse 15). A great land creature God presents as chief of his ways: he eats grass as an ox, his strength is in his loins, his bones like bars of iron, and no man can tame him. The study note leans toward the hippopotamus as predominant among the great Pachydermata, while saying behemoth agrees exactly with neither hippopotamus nor elephant, so no single known species is settled. Unresolved Study note on verse 15, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
