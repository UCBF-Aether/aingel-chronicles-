The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Genesis 3 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Now the serpent was more subtil than any beast of the field which the LORD God had made. And he said unto the woman, Yea, hath God said, Ye shall not eat of every tree of the garden?

2 And the woman said unto the serpent, We may eat of the fruit of the trees of the garden:

3 But of the fruit of the tree which is in the midst of the garden, God hath said, Ye shall not eat of it, neither shall ye touch it, lest ye die.

4 And the serpent said unto the woman, Ye shall not surely die:

5 For God doth know that in the day ye eat thereof, then your eyes shall be opened, and ye shall be as gods, knowing good and evil.

6 And when the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was pleasant to the eyes, and a tree to be desired to make one wise, she took of the fruit thereof, and did eat, and gave also unto her husband with her; and he did eat.

7 And the eyes of them both were opened, and they knew that they were naked; and they sewed fig leaves together, and made themselves aprons.

8 And they heard the voice of the LORD God walking in the garden in the cool of the day: and Adam and his wife hid themselves from the presence of the LORD God amongst the trees of the garden.

9 And the LORD God called unto Adam, and said unto him, Where art thou?

10 And he said, I heard thy voice in the garden, and I was afraid, because I was naked; and I hid myself.

11 And he said, Who told thee that thou wast naked? Hast thou eaten of the tree, whereof I commanded thee that thou shouldest not eat?

12 And the man said, The woman whom thou gavest to be with me, she gave me of the tree, and I did eat.

13 And the LORD God said unto the woman, What is this that thou hast done? And the woman said, The serpent beguiled me, and I did eat.

14 And the LORD God said unto the serpent, Because thou hast done this, thou art cursed above all cattle, and above every beast of the field; upon thy belly shalt thou go, and dust shalt thou eat all the days of thy life:

15 And I will put enmity between thee and the woman, and between thy seed and her seed; it shall bruise thy head, and thou shalt bruise his heel.

16 Unto the woman he said, I will greatly multiply thy sorrow and thy conception; in sorrow thou shalt bring forth children; and thy desire shall be to thy husband, and he shall rule over thee.

17 And unto Adam he said, Because thou hast hearkened unto the voice of thy wife, and hast eaten of the tree, of which I commanded thee, saying, Thou shalt not eat of it: cursed is the ground for thy sake; in sorrow shalt thou eat of it all the days of thy life;

18 Thorns also and thistles shall it bring forth to thee; and thou shalt eat the herb of the field;

19 In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread, till thou return unto the ground; for out of it wast thou taken: for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return.

20 And Adam called his wife’s name Eve; because she was the mother of all living.

21 Unto Adam also and to his wife did the LORD God make coats of skins, and clothed them.

22 And the LORD God said, Behold, the man is become as one of us, to know good and evil: and now, lest he put forth his hand, and take also of the tree of life, and eat, and live for ever:

23 Therefore the LORD God sent him forth from the garden of Eden, to till the ground from whence he was taken.

24 So he drove out the man; and he placed at the east of the garden of Eden Cherubims, and a flaming sword which turned every way, to keep the way of the tree of life.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

The serpent questions God's word, the woman and then the man eat from the forbidden tree, and blame follows on every side. Judgment falls on serpent, woman and man, enmity is set between the serpent's offspring and the woman's, and the man and woman are sent out of Eden, with cherubim and a flaming sword guarding the way to the tree of life. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. The serpent, more subtle than other beasts, questioned God's word to the woman. JFB stresses the real serpent was the tool of Satan, who attacked when the woman was alone and cast doubt on God's goodness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. The woman answered that they could eat freely of the garden's trees. She rightly began with God's generosity, even as the tempter tried to narrow her view to the one exception. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. She added that the forbidden tree must not be eaten or even touched, lest they die. JFB notes the softened wording, lest ye die for surely ye shall die, already shows the tempter's doubt taking hold. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. The serpent flatly denied the penalty: ye shall not surely die. This is the tempter's open contradiction of God's word, replacing warning with a promise of impunity. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. He promised their eyes would be opened and they would be as gods, knowing good and evil. JFB notes the promise was half true in a terrible way: they would learn evil by experience, not gain godlike wisdom. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Seeing the tree as good for food, pleasant to the eyes and desirable for wisdom, the woman ate and gave to her husband, who also ate. JFB traces the pattern of all temptation: outward attraction, inward desire, then the act that enslaves. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Their eyes were opened, and they knew they were naked, so they sewed fig leaves together. Knowledge came, but as shame and a useless self-made covering, not as the wisdom promised. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Hearing God's voice walking in the garden in the cool of the day, they hid among the trees. JFB notes God's approach was the familiar, gentle one they had welcomed before; guilt now made them flee it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. God called, Where art thou? The question is not for God's information but for Adam's conscience, inviting confession rather than hiding. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Adam said he heard God's voice and was afraid because he was naked, so he hid. JFB calls this an evasive confession: it admits fear and shame but not yet the sin itself. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. God asked who told him he was naked, and whether he had eaten of the forbidden tree. The questions press Adam from the symptom, shame, to the cause, disobedience. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Adam blamed the woman God had given him, saying she gave him the fruit and he ate. JFB notes he blames God as well as Eve, turning a gift into an excuse. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Eve blamed the serpent, saying it beguiled her and she ate. Both answers shift blame outward, and JFB stresses the sin was far more than eating: it was self-love, ingratitude and preference of the creature over the Creator. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. God pronounced doom first on the serpent, cursed above all cattle and beasts, to go on its belly and eat dust. JFB reads a double curse: on the creature itself, and on the spiritual serpent whose power would be destroyed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. God put enmity between the serpent and the woman, and between their seeds, promising the woman's seed would bruise the serpent's head while it bruised his heel. JFB reads this as the first promise of the Messiah, whose fatal blow to Satan would cost him suffering. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. To the woman God greatly multiplied sorrow in childbearing, and set her desire toward her husband who would rule over her. JFB notes her place changes from equal partner to humble subjection, with pain added to motherhood. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. To Adam God cursed the ground for his sake: in sorrow he would eat of it all his life. Work that was once easy becomes toil, because the ground itself now resists the worker. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. Thorns and thistles would spring up, yet he would eat the herb of the field. The ground's fruitfulness is now mixed with weeds, so food requires clearing as well as planting. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. In the sweat of his face he would eat bread until he returned to dust, for dust he was and to dust he would return. JFB notes man did not die at once, but his body was changed toward dissolution and his fellowship with God was already broken. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Adam called his wife Eve, because she was the mother of all living. JFB reads the name as hope: even after the sentence, she is named for life and for the promised Saviour to come through her. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. God made coats of skins and clothed them. JFB sees divine institution of sacrifice here: covering now requires a life given, pointing to worship through a Redeemer rather than fig leaves. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. God said man had become as one of us, knowing good and evil, and must not now take also of the tree of life. JFB reads this not as irony but as compassion over what sin had made of a man once formed in God's image. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. So God sent man out of Eden to till the ground from which he was taken. Exile is both judgment and mercy: he may not seize back by eating what obedience had forfeited. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. God placed cherubim and a flaming sword at the east of Eden to keep the way to the tree of life. JFB renders it as God dwelling between the cherubim, so that access to life now runs through mediation, as later at the mercy seat. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
