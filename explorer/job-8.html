The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 8 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Then answered Bildad the Shuhite, and said,

2 How long wilt thou speak these things? and how long shall the words of thy mouth be like a strong wind?

3 Doth God pervert judgment? or doth the Almighty pervert justice?

4 If thy children have sinned against him, and he have cast them away for their transgression;

5 If thou wouldest seek unto God betimes, and make thy supplication to the Almighty;

6 If thou wert pure and upright; surely now he would awake for thee, and make the habitation of thy righteousness prosperous.

7 Though thy beginning was small, yet thy latter end should greatly increase.

8 For enquire, I pray thee, of the former age, and prepare thyself to the search of their fathers:

9 (For we are but of yesterday, and know nothing, because our days upon earth are a shadow:)

10 Shall not they teach thee, and tell thee, and utter words out of their heart?

11 Can the rush grow up without mire? can the flag grow without water?

12 Whilst it is yet in his greenness, and not cut down, it withereth before any other herb.

13 So are the paths of all that forget God; and the hypocrite’s hope shall perish:

14 Whose hope shall be cut off, and whose trust shall be a spider’s web.

15 He shall lean upon his house, but it shall not stand: he shall hold it fast, but it shall not endure.

16 He is green before the sun, and his branch shooteth forth in his garden.

17 His roots are wrapped about the heap, and seeth the place of stones.

18 If he destroy him from his place, then it shall deny him, saying, I have not seen thee.

19 Behold, this is the joy of his way, and out of the earth shall others grow.

20 Behold, God will not cast away a perfect man, neither will he help the evil doers:

21 Till he fill thy mouth with laughing, and thy lips with rejoicing.

22 They that hate thee shall be clothed with shame; and the dwelling place of the wicked shall come to nought.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Bildad answers more harshly than Eliphaz: God does not pervert justice, so if Job’s children sinned, God cast them away for it — and if Job himself is pure and upright, God will wake for him and make his habitation prosperous. Appealing to the former age, he says the godless are like a rush that grows only in mire and withers while still green, and like a spider’s web that cannot hold the man who leans on it. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Bildad the Shuhite answers, more severe and coarse than Eliphaz. JFB frames the whole speech as Bildad's address, pressing Job's guilt from his sufferings. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. How long will Job speak these things, and how long shall his words be like a strong wind? JFB reads the wind as disregarding restraints and daring against God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Does God pervert judgment, or does the Almighty pervert justice? JFB notes the repeated pervert galling Job, and that Bildad assumes Job's guilt from his sufferings, as if Job would have God let his sins go unpunished. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. If Job's children have sinned against God, he has cast them away for their transgression. JFB renders if as since, and calls this most cutting to the bereaved father: God leaves sinners to the consequence of their sin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. If Job would seek God betimes and make supplication to the Almighty, God would respond. JFB explains betimes as early, making God the first and chief anxiety. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. If Job were pure and upright, God would surely awake for him and make his righteous habitation prosperous. JFB notes awake means arise to his help, and that Bildad assumes Job's former habitation was one of guilt. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Though Job's beginning was small, his latter end should greatly increase. JFB reads the beginning as the start of his new happiness after restoration, and the latter end as his greatly increased end. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Inquire of the former age, and prepare to the search of their fathers. JFB notes the sages of olden time reached ages beyond Job's contemporaries and could give testimony from fuller experience. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. We are but of yesterday and know nothing, because our days on earth are a shadow. JFB explains yesterday as a recent race, knowing nothing compared with the ancients because of life's brevity, with knowledge handed down in proverbs and tradition. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Shall not the fathers teach Job and utter words out of their heart? JFB notes Bildad answers Job's teach me with inquire of the fathers, whose words are well-considered, from observation and reflection, not merely from the mouth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Can the rush grow without mire, or the flag grow without water? JFB identifies the rush as papyrus, growing only in marshy places: so the godless thrives only in external prosperity, with no inward stability. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. While the rush is yet green and not cut down, it withers before any other herb. JFB explains that before ripening for the scythe it withers suddenly once moisture is gone, as ruin seizes the godless in the zenith of prosperity. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. So are the paths of all who forget God, and the hypocrite's hope shall perish. JFB notes forgetting God is the distinguishing trait of the godless, and the paths are their ways. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. The hypocrite's hope shall be cut off, and his trust shall be a spider's web. JFB explains the confidence as laid in ruins, to accord with the spider's house metaphor. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. He shall lean on his house but it shall not stand; he shall hold it fast but it shall not endure. JFB notes the spider holds fast by its web and is sustained by it, but the godless is not sustained by the thin web he rests on, fine-spun from his own inventions. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. The godless is green before the sun, and his branch shoots forth in his garden. JFB reads the sun as smiling fortune, during which the hypocrite wondrously progresses like rank weeds spreading over stones and walls. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. His roots are wrapped about the heap and he sees the place of stones. JFB explains the house of stones as the garden wall, which the creeping parasite plant figuratively sees and regards as it spreads over it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. If God destroys him from his place, then it shall deny him, saying it never saw him. JFB notes the personified place is ashamed of the withered weeds, as the godless man's nearest friends disown him when he falls. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. Behold, this is the joy of his way, and out of the earth shall others grow. JFB calls it bitter irony: this is the hypocrite's joy at last, and a fresh crop of weeds always springs up where the old were torn away. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Behold, God will not cast away a perfect man, neither will he help evildoers. JFB notes Bildad regards Job as righteous but fallen into sin: if he repents, God will not cast him away, but those who persevere in sin God will not take by the hand. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. Till God fills Job's mouth with laughing and his lips with rejoicing. JFB renders till as to the point that: God's blessing on the repentant will keep increasing until it reaches this joy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. They who hate Job shall be clothed with shame, and the dwelling of the wicked shall come to naught. JFB identifies Job's haters as the wicked, shamed at the failure of their hope that he would utterly perish, because they instead come to naught. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
