The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Psalms 39 (King James Version (1611) for Psalms 1–150; Charles Thomson, The Septuagint (1808) for Psalm 151)

1 I said, I will take heed to my ways, that I sin not with my tongue: I will keep my mouth with a bridle, while the wicked is before me.

2 I was dumb with silence, I held my peace, even from good; and my sorrow was stirred.

3 My heart was hot within me, while I was musing the fire burned: then spake I with my tongue,

4 LORD, make me to know mine end, and the measure of my days, what it is: that I may know how frail I am.

5 Behold, thou hast made my days as an handbreadth; and mine age is as nothing before thee: verily every man at his best state is altogether vanity. Selah.

6 Surely every man walketh in a vain shew: surely they are disquieted in vain: he heapeth up riches, and knoweth not who shall gather them.

7 And now, Lord, what wait I for? my hope is in thee.

8 Deliver me from all my transgressions: make me not the reproach of the foolish.

9 I was dumb, I opened not my mouth; because thou didst it.

10 Remove thy stroke away from me: I am consumed by the blow of thine hand.

11 When thou with rebukes dost correct man for iniquity, thou makest his beauty to consume away like a moth: surely every man is vanity. Selah.

12 Hear my prayer, O LORD, and give ear unto my cry; hold not thy peace at my tears: for I am a stranger with thee, and a sojourner, as all my fathers were.

13 O spare me, that I may recover strength, before I go hence, and be no more.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

The bridled tongue and the handbreadth life: silence, even from good speech, stirs sorrow until musing fire breaks out, make me know my end. Days are a handbreadth, every man at his best is vanity, riches heaped for unknown gatherers. Hope turns to God alone, with a plea for sparing before he goes hence and is no more. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown; chapter text

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. I said, I will take heed to my ways, that I sin not with my tongue; I will keep my mouth with a bridle while the wicked is before me: the tongue is bridled precisely because the wicked watch for a slip. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. I was dumb with silence, held my peace even from good, and my sorrow was stirred: silence kept even from good speech stirred sorrow rather than easing it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. My heart was hot within me; while I was musing the fire burned; then I spoke with my tongue: musing kindled fire, and fire broke silence — thought became speech. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Lord, make me to know my end, and the measure of my days, what it is, that I may know how frail I am: the prayer for knowledge of his end is a prayer to know frailty. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Behold, you have made my days as a handbreadth, and my age is as nothing before you; verily every man at his best state is altogether vanity: a handbreadth measures life, and even man's best state is vanity before God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Surely every man walks in a vain show; surely they are disquieted in vain; he heaps up riches and knows not who shall gather them: life as a show, disquiet in vain, and riches heaped for an unknown heir — vanity in three forms. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. And now, Lord, what wait I for? My hope is in you: from vanity he turns to the only wait worth waiting — hope in God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Deliver me from all my transgressions; make me not the reproach of the foolish: deliverance from transgressions, not from trouble alone, is prayed for, lest he become the fool's reproach. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. I was dumb, I opened not my mouth, because you did it: silence returns, now as submission — God did it, therefore he will not plead against it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Remove your stroke away from me; I am consumed by the blow of your hand: submission in verse 9 does not cancel the plea in verse 10 — he still asks the stroke's removal, for he is consumed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. When you with rebukes correct man for iniquity, you make his beauty consume away like a moth; surely every man is vanity: rebuke eats beauty as a moth eats cloth, and the lesson repeats — every man is vanity. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Hear my prayer, Lord, and give ear to my cry; hold not your peace at my tears, for I am a stranger with you, and a sojourner, as all my fathers were: tears are pleaded, and strangerhood with God — a guest, like his fathers — is the ground of welcome. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. O spare me, that I may recover strength, before I go hence and be no more: sparing is asked for recovery of strength before departure, for after going hence there is no more of this life's service. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
