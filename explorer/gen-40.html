The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Genesis 40 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 And it came to pass after these things, that the butler of the king of Egypt and his baker had offended their lord the king of Egypt.

2 And Pharaoh was wroth against two of his officers, against the chief of the butlers, and against the chief of the bakers.

3 And he put them in ward in the house of the captain of the guard, into the prison, the place where Joseph was bound.

4 And the captain of the guard charged Joseph with them, and he served them: and they continued a season in ward.

5 And they dreamed a dream both of them, each man his dream in one night, each man according to the interpretation of his dream, the butler and the baker of the king of Egypt, which were bound in the prison.

6 And Joseph came in unto them in the morning, and looked upon them, and, behold, they were sad.

7 And he asked Pharaoh’s officers that were with him in the ward of his lord’s house, saying, Wherefore look ye so sadly to day?

8 And they said unto him, We have dreamed a dream, and there is no interpreter of it. And Joseph said unto them, Do not interpretations belong to God? tell me them, I pray you.

9 And the chief butler told his dream to Joseph, and said to him, In my dream, behold, a vine was before me;

10 And in the vine were three branches: and it was as though it budded, and her blossoms shot forth; and the clusters thereof brought forth ripe grapes:

11 And Pharaoh’s cup was in my hand: and I took the grapes, and pressed them into Pharaoh’s cup, and I gave the cup into Pharaoh’s hand.

12 And Joseph said unto him, This is the interpretation of it: The three branches are three days:

13 Yet within three days shall Pharaoh lift up thine head, and restore thee unto thy place: and thou shalt deliver Pharaoh’s cup into his hand, after the former manner when thou wast his butler.

14 But think on me when it shall be well with thee, and shew kindness, I pray thee, unto me, and make mention of me unto Pharaoh, and bring me out of this house:

15 For indeed I was stolen away out of the land of the Hebrews: and here also have I done nothing that they should put me into the dungeon.

16 When the chief baker saw that the interpretation was good, he said unto Joseph, I also was in my dream, and, behold, I had three white baskets on my head:

17 And in the uppermost basket there was of all manner of bakemeats for Pharaoh; and the birds did eat them out of the basket upon my head.

18 And Joseph answered and said, This is the interpretation thereof: The three baskets are three days:

19 Yet within three days shall Pharaoh lift up thy head from off thee, and shall hang thee on a tree; and the birds shall eat thy flesh from off thee.

20 And it came to pass the third day, which was Pharaoh’s birthday, that he made a feast unto all his servants: and he lifted up the head of the chief butler and of the chief baker among his servants.

21 And he restored the chief butler unto his butlership again; and he gave the cup into Pharaoh’s hand:

22 But he hanged the chief baker: as Joseph had interpreted to them.

23 Yet did not the chief butler remember Joseph, but forgat him.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

In prison Joseph interprets the dreams of Pharaoh's cupbearer and baker: in three days the cupbearer will be restored to his place and the baker hanged. Both come true exactly, but the restored cupbearer forgets Joseph, leaving him two more years in prison. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. After these things, Pharaoh's butler and baker offended their lord the king of Egypt. JFB notes both were officers of great rank: the butler oversaw the royal vineyards and cellars, and the baker supervised all provision for the royal table. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Pharaoh was angry with his two officers, the chief butler and the chief baker. Their high standing made the offence a court matter, not a household quarrel. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. He put them in ward in the house of the captain of the guard, in the prison where Joseph was bound. JFB notes they were held until their case could be investigated, in an outer part of Potiphar's house where the royal prison stood. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. The captain of the guard charged Joseph with them, and he served them, and they continued a season in ward. JFB notes it was Potiphar himself, not merely the keeper, who gave Joseph this charge, as if by now satisfied of Joseph's innocence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Each of them dreamed a dream in one night, each according to its interpretation, the butler and the baker of Egypt's king, bound in prison. Henry notes it was not so much the prison as their dreams that made them sad, and JFB stresses the providence in both dreaming on the same night. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Joseph came to them in the morning, looked on them, and saw they were sad. Henry praises this compassion: it is often a relief to those in trouble simply to be noticed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Joseph asked Pharaoh's officers why they looked so sad that day. The question opened the conversation that led to the interpretations. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. They said they had dreamed a dream with no interpreter, and Joseph answered that interpretations belong to God, and asked them to tell him their dreams. JFB notes Joseph first directed them to God, then volunteered, through divine help, to aid them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. The chief butler told his dream: in his dream a vine was before him. The dream's setting, JFB notes, pictures the king at exercise attended by his butler with a cooling draught. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. In the vine were three branches, which budded, blossomed, and brought forth ripe grapes. The growth from branch to ripe cluster is the dream's hopeful movement. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Pharaoh's cup was in his hand, and he took the grapes, pressed them into the cup, and gave the cup to Pharaoh. JFB notes everything was done in the king's presence: the cup washed, the juice pressed into it, and the cup handed over, lightly resting on the fingertips. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Joseph said the three branches are three days. The interpretation turns the dream's threefold pattern into a short, fixed time. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Within three days Pharaoh would lift up the butler's head and restore him to his place, and he would deliver Pharaoh's cup as before. Henry notes the butler's dream foretold advancement, and Joseph gave the good news plainly. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Joseph asked the butler to think on him when it was well with him, show him kindness, mention him to Pharaoh, and bring him out of that house. JFB notes the earnest appeal reveals a sadness and longing for release that even Joseph's piety could not dispel. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Joseph explained that he had been stolen from the land of the Hebrews, and here too had done nothing to deserve the dungeon. Henry notes Joseph states his innocence mildly, without reflecting on his brothers who sold him or on his mistress and master. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. When the chief baker saw the interpretation was good, he told his dream too: he had three white baskets on his head. Hope raised by another's good news moved him to speak. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. In the uppermost basket were all manner of bakemeats for Pharaoh, and the birds ate them out of the basket on his head. JFB explains the baskets were wicker, carried piled on the head from kitchen to dining rooms, and birds snatching food was a frequent occurrence in Egyptian palaces. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. Joseph answered that the three baskets are three days. The same number that promised restoration to the butler now frames a darker outcome. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. Within three days Pharaoh would lift the baker's head from off him, hang him on a tree, and birds would eat his flesh. JFB notes Joseph described an Egyptian form of execution: decapitation, then the headless body gibbeted by the highway until devoured by birds. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. On the third day, Pharaoh's birthday, he made a feast for all his servants and lifted up the heads of the chief butler and chief baker among them. JFB notes the birthday was a holiday season honoured by free pardon to prisoners, and Henry adds that Joseph's interpretations came to pass on the very day fixed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. He restored the chief butler to his butlership, and the butler gave the cup into Pharaoh's hand. The dream's picture — cup pressed and handed to the king — became fact. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. But he hanged the chief baker, as Joseph had interpreted to them. Henry notes Joseph was not at fault for bringing the baker no better tidings: an interpreter cannot make the message otherwise than it is. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. Yet the chief butler did not remember Joseph, but forgot him. JFB calls this human nature — men forget in prosperity those who were companions in adversity — while Henry adds that Joseph learned by disappointment to trust in God only. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
