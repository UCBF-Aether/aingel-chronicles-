Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Wisdom of Solomon 2 (King James Version, Apocrypha; public-domain text as received)

1 For the ungodly said, reasoning with themselves, but not aright, Our life is short and tedious, and in the death of a man there is no remedy: neither was there any man known to have returned from the grave.

2 For we are born at all adventure: and we shall be hereafter as though we had never been: for the breath in our nostrils is as smoke, and a little spark in the moving of our heart:

3 Which being extinguished, our body shall be turned into ashes, and our spirit shall vanish as the soft air,

4 And our name shall be forgotten in time, and no man shall have our works in remembrance, and our life shall pass away as the trace of a cloud, and shall be dispersed as a mist, that is driven away with the beams of the sun, and overcome with the heat thereof.

5 For our time is a very shadow that passeth away; and after our end there is no returning: for it is fast sealed, so that no man cometh again.

6 Come on therefore, let us enjoy the good things that are present: and let us speedily use the creatures like as in youth.

7 Let us fill ourselves with costly wine and ointments: and let no flower of the spring pass by us:

8 Let us crown ourselves with rosebuds, before they be withered:

9 Let none of us go without his part of our voluptuousness: let us leave tokens of our joyfulness in every place: for this is our portion, and our lot is this.

10 Let us oppress the poor righteous man, let us not spare the widow, nor reverence the ancient gray hairs of the aged.

11 Let our strength be the law of justice: for that which is feeble is found to be nothing worth.

12 Therefore let us lie in wait for the righteous; because he is not for our turn, and he is clean contrary to our doings: he upbraideth us with our offending the law, and objecteth to our infamy the transgressings of our education.

13 He professeth to have the knowledge of God: and he calleth himself the child of the Lord.

14 He was made to reprove our thoughts.

15 He is grievous unto us even to behold: for his life is not like other men's, his ways are of another fashion.

16 We are esteemed of him as counterfeits: he abstaineth from our ways as from filthiness: he pronounceth the end of the just to be blessed, and maketh his boast that God is his father.

17 Let us see if his words be true: and let us prove what shall happen in the end of him.

18 For if the just man be the son of God, he will help him, and deliver him from the hand of his enemies.

19 Let us examine him with despitefulness and torture, that we may know his meekness, and prove his patience.

20 Let us condemn him with a shameful death: for by his own saying he shall be respected.

21 Such things they did imagine, and were deceived: for their own wickedness hath blinded them.

22 As for the mysteries of God, they kn ew them not: neither hoped they for the wages of righteousness, nor discerned a reward for blameless souls.

23 For God created man to be immortal, and made him to be an image of his own eternity.

24 Nevertheless through envy of the devil came death into the world: and they that do hold of his side do find it.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

The ungodly reason aloud: life is short and by chance, the body returns to ash, the spirit vanishes, so enjoy costly wine, rosebuds and pleasure while they last, and oppress the poor righteous man whose life reproves them. They plot to test and kill the just man, but their reasoning is self-blinding: God created humanity to be immortal, and death entered through the devil's envy. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing George Leo Haydock

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. The ungodly reason with themselves, but not aright: life is short and tedious, death has no remedy, and none was known to return from the grave. Haydock identifies this as the epicureans' reasoning, which rejects resurrection and future reward. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 2. We are born at all adventure, and shall be hereafter as though we had never been; breath is smoke, and a spark moves the heart. Haydock notes the epicurean fancy that man arose by chance, like a spark, and vanishes like smoke. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 3. When the spark is extinguished, the body turns to ashes and the spirit vanishes as soft air. Haydock's reading is that this materialist account leaves the soul no more endurance than air. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 4. Our name shall be forgotten in time, no man remembers our works, and life passes like a cloud's trace, scattered as mist before the sun. Haydock notes the same reasoning concludes that nothing of man survives to be remembered. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 5. Our days are the passing of a shadow, with no return after the end, sealed and none returning. Haydock compares the sealed state to one in a tomb, and notes the epicureans were convinced of nature's fragility yet drew the false inference that present enjoyment is all. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 6. Come therefore, let us enjoy the good things that are, and use the creature speedily as in youth. Haydock observes that denial of future reward produces the epicure's life of indulgence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 7. Let us fill ourselves with costly wine and ointments, and let no flower of spring pass by us. Haydock notes youth is life's spring, and the voluptuous wrongly argue from transience to use rather than to contempt of such things. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 8. Let us crown ourselves with rosebuds before they wither. Haydock reads this as the same plea: seize pleasure before it fades, because, on their view, nothing follows. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 9. Let none of us go without his part of voluptuousness, leaving tokens of joy everywhere, for this is our portion and lot. Haydock's point is that the epicurean lot is deliberately chosen pleasure, shared as a programme. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 10. Let us oppress the poor righteous man, spare not the widow, nor reverence the ancient's grey hairs. Haydock notes that maxims of oppression follow from the denial of judgment: the weak have no protector on this view. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 11. Let our strength be the law of justice, for what is feeble is found worth nothing. Haydock calls these strange maxims, which few dare proclaim though many act by them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 12. Let us lie in wait for the righteous, because he is unprofitable to us, contrary to our doings, and upbraids us with offences against the law. Haydock notes infidels are not content with riot; they persecute the just, and this passage strikingly foreshadows the treatment of Christ. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 13. He professes to have God's knowledge and calls himself God's child, and his life reproves our thoughts. Haydock observes that prophets, and Christ above all, irritated sinners precisely by speaking in God's name. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 14. He is grievous to behold, for his life is unlike others' and his ways are different. Haydock's reading is that mere difference of life is enough to offend those wedded to pleasure. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 15. We are esteemed by him as counterfeits, he abstains from our ways as from filthiness, and pronounces the righteous end blessed, boasting God as father. Haydock notes the just man's judgment on their ways, and his hope, are what they resent. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 16. Let us examine him with despitefulness and torture, that we may know his meekness and prove his patience. Haydock's point is that the persecutors propose a test of the very virtues they hate. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 17. Let us condemn him to a shameful death, for by his own sayings he shall be respected. Haydock reads this as the persecutors' appeal to the just man's own claim of divine protection. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 18. Such things they imagined, and were deceived, for their own wickedness blinded them. Haydock notes the blindness is self-inflicted: wickedness darkens the mind that reasons so. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 19. They did not know God's mysteries, nor hope for the reward of holiness, nor discern the honour of blameless souls. Haydock's point is that ignorance of the mysteries, not lack of evidence, is their ruin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 20. They shall be examined by their own words, for the just man's claim shall be put to proof. Haydock notes the persecutors will be judged by the very words and test they proposed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 21. These things they thought and erred, for their wickedness blinded them, and they knew not God's secrets. Haydock stresses the repeated moral: error follows wickedness, not the other way round. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 22. They knew not God's mysteries, nor hoped for holiness's reward, nor discerned the prize of blameless souls. Haydock observes that disbelief of mysteries leads both to dissolute life and to persecution of the just. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 23. God created man to be immortal, and made him in the image of his own eternity. Haydock's contrast is sharp: against the epicurean account of chance, man is made for immortality. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 24. Through the devil's envy death entered the world, and those of his side experience it. Haydock traces the envy to the fallen angel who grudged man's destined honour and strove to be like the Most High. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
