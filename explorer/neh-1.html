The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Nehemiah 1 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 The words of Nehemiah the son of Hachaliah. And it came to pass in the month Chisleu, in the twentieth year, as I was in Shushan the palace,

2 That Hanani, one of my brethren, came, he and certain men of Judah; and I asked them concerning the Jews that had escaped, which were left of the captivity, and concerning Jerusalem.

3 And they said unto me, The remnant that are left of the captivity there in the province are in great affliction and reproach: the wall of Jerusalem also is broken down, and the gates thereof are burned with fire.

4 And it came to pass, when I heard these words, that I sat down and wept, and mourned certain days, and fasted, and prayed before the God of heaven,

5 And said, I beseech thee, O LORD God of heaven, the great and terrible God, that keepeth covenant and mercy for them that love him and observe his commandments:

6 Let thine ear now be attentive, and thine eyes open, that thou mayest hear the prayer of thy servant, which I pray before thee now, day and night, for the children of Israel thy servants, and confess the sins of the children of Israel, which we have sinned against thee: both I and my father’s house have sinned.

7 We have dealt very corruptly against thee, and have not kept the commandments, nor the statutes, nor the judgments, which thou commandedst thy servant Moses.

8 Remember, I beseech thee, the word that thou commandedst thy servant Moses, saying, If ye transgress, I will scatter you abroad among the nations:

9 But if ye turn unto me, and keep my commandments, and do them; though there were of you cast out unto the uttermost part of the heaven, yet will I gather them from thence, and will bring them unto the place that I have chosen to set my name there.

10 Now these are thy servants and thy people, whom thou hast redeemed by thy great power, and by thy strong hand.

11 O LORD, I beseech thee, let now thine ear be attentive to the prayer of thy servant, and to the prayer of thy servants, who desire to fear thy name: and prosper, I pray thee, thy servant this day, and grant him mercy in the sight of this man. For I was the king’s cupbearer.

How to read the labels

Every substantive historical claim in the editorial panels below carries one of three labels, plus a source reference:

  • Documented — directly supported by a cited source.
  • Interpreted — a reasoned explanation or proposed relationship.
  • Unresolved — evidence is insufficient to establish the claim.

A claim does NOT become Documented merely because a commentary states it. Commentary views are attributed to their authors and labeled Interpreted unless a primary textual source directly supports the claim. Where evidence runs out, the page says Unresolved and stops — it does not fill the gap.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

What the chapter says, in plain language

Nehemiah, serving at the Persian court in Shushan, hears from Hanani that the returned Jews in Jerusalem are in great affliction, the city wall broken down and its gates burned. He weeps, fasts, and prays a long prayer of confession, owning his own sins and his people's, and pleading the word given through Moses: scattering for unfaithfulness, but gathering again for those who return and keep God's commandments. He closes by asking for favour with the king, whose cupbearer he is. Interpreted Chapter text and study notes for this chapter

Unfamiliar terms

  • Cupbearer. the court servant who tasted and served the king's wine, a post of rank and frequent access to the king (study note on verse 11). Interpreted Study note for this chapter
  • Chisleu. the month named in verse 1, which the study note places at the close of November and most of December. Interpreted Study note for this chapter
  • Remnant. those left of the captivity who had returned to the province of Judah. Interpreted Study note for this chapter

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Nehemiah, son of Hachaliah, writes from Shushan the palace in the month Chisleu, which JFB places at the close of November and most of December. Henry notes his modesty: he first says only that he was in Shushan, holding back his high court preferment until the story requires it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Hanani, called his brother though JFB notes the term likely means only a kinsman, came with certain men of Judah, and Nehemiah asked after the escaped Jews and Jerusalem. Henry stresses that neither distance nor his own dignity cooled his concern for brethren in distress. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. The report was bleak: the remnant in the province lived in great affliction and reproach, the wall of Jerusalem was broken down, and the gates were burned with fire. Henry adds that the temple was built and reform begun, yet this one good work remained undone, leaving the people exposed and despised. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. On hearing this, Nehemiah sat down and wept, mourned for days, fasted, and prayed before the God of heaven. Henry reads this as consecrated sorrow: he poured out his complaint to God, easing his spirit and seeking direction before forming any plan to help his people. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. His prayer opens with holy awe and confidence: the LORD God of heaven is the great and terrible God, yet he keeps covenant and mercy for those who love him and observe his commandments. Henry notes the address matches Daniel's prayer, joining fear of God's majesty with trust in his promised grace. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 6. He asks God to let his ear be attentive and his eyes open to the prayer offered day and night, and confesses plainly, "I and my father's house have sinned." Henry observes that owning his own share, not merely Israel's, is what humbles the confession. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 7. The confession sharpens: they have dealt very corruptly against God and have not kept the commandments, statutes, and judgments commanded through Moses. Henry calls this the malignity of sin: a corruption of ourselves and an affront to God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 8. He pleads the word God commanded Moses: if they were unfaithful, he would scatter them among the nations. Henry notes the scattering had indeed been fulfilled, with Israel widely dispersed, and Nehemiah now puts God in remembrance of his own settled rule. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 9. The other half of Moses' word is the hope: if they return and keep God's commandments, he will gather them even from the farthest part of heaven to the place he chose for his name. Henry calls promises like this our best pleas in prayer, the word on which God has caused us to hope. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 10. He pleads their relation to God: these are his servants and his people, redeemed by his great power and strong hand. Henry argues that the God whose power redeemed them of old has lost none of that power, and should perfect the redemption he began. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 11. The prayer closes with a particular petition: prosper Nehemiah this day and grant him mercy in the sight of "this man," the king. JFB notes the cupbearer's office was one of rank and great influence through frequent access to the royal presence, and Henry adds that favour with men is comfortable only when seen as springing from God's mercy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
