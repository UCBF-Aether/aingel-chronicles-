The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Numbers 19 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 And the LORD spake unto Moses and unto Aaron, saying,

2 This is the ordinance of the law which the LORD hath commanded, saying, Speak unto the children of Israel, that they bring thee a red heifer without spot, wherein is no blemish, and upon which never came yoke:

3 And ye shall give her unto Eleazar the priest, that he may bring her forth without the camp, and one shall slay her before his face:

4 And Eleazar the priest shall take of her blood with his finger, and sprinkle of her blood directly before the tabernacle of the congregation seven times:

5 And one shall burn the heifer in his sight; her skin, and her flesh, and her blood, with her dung, shall he burn:

6 And the priest shall take cedar wood, and hyssop, and scarlet, and cast it into the midst of the burning of the heifer.

7 Then the priest shall wash his clothes, and he shall bathe his flesh in water, and afterward he shall come into the camp, and the priest shall be unclean until the even.

8 And he that burneth her shall wash his clothes in water, and bathe his flesh in water, and shall be unclean until the even.

9 And a man that is clean shall gather up the ashes of the heifer, and lay them up without the camp in a clean place, and it shall be kept for the congregation of the children of Israel for a water of separation: it is a purification for sin.

10 And he that gathereth the ashes of the heifer shall wash his clothes, and be unclean until the even: and it shall be unto the children of Israel, and unto the stranger that sojourneth among them, for a statute for ever.

11 He that toucheth the dead body of any man shall be unclean seven days.

12 He shall purify himself with it on the third day, and on the seventh day he shall be clean: but if he purify not himself the third day, then the seventh day he shall not be clean.

13 Whosoever toucheth the dead body of any man that is dead, and purifieth not himself, defileth the tabernacle of the LORD; and that soul shall be cut off from Israel: because the water of separation was not sprinkled upon him, he shall be unclean; his uncleanness is yet upon him.

14 This is the law, when a man dieth in a tent: all that come into the tent, and all that is in the tent, shall be unclean seven days.

15 And every open vessel, which hath no covering bound upon it, is unclean.

16 And whosoever toucheth one that is slain with a sword in the open fields, or a dead body, or a bone of a man, or a grave, shall be unclean seven days.

17 And for an unclean person they shall take of the ashes of the burnt heifer of purification for sin, and running water shall be put thereto in a vessel:

18 And a clean person shall take hyssop, and dip it in the water, and sprinkle it upon the tent, and upon all the vessels, and upon the persons that were there, and upon him that touched a bone, or one slain, or one dead, or a grave:

19 And the clean person shall sprinkle upon the unclean on the third day, and on the seventh day: and on the seventh day he shall purify himself, and wash his clothes, and bathe himself in water, and shall be clean at even.

20 But the man that shall be unclean, and shall not purify himself, that soul shall be cut off from among the congregation, because he hath defiled the sanctuary of the LORD: the water of separation hath not been sprinkled upon him; he is unclean.

21 And it shall be a perpetual statute unto them, that he that sprinkleth the water of separation shall wash his clothes; and he that toucheth the water of separation shall be unclean until even.

22 And whatsoever the unclean person toucheth shall be unclean; and the soul that toucheth it shall be unclean until even.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

This chapter gives the ordinance of the red heifer and the law of corpse uncleanness. A red heifer without spot or blemish, on which no yoke has come, is slain outside the camp and wholly burnt with cedar, hyssop, and scarlet, and its ashes are kept for the water of purification. Anyone who touches a dead body is unclean seven days and must be purified with that water on the third day and the seventh; if he neglects it he defiles the tabernacle and is cut off. Whatever an unclean person touches, and whoever touches it, is likewise unclean until evening. Interpreted Editorial overview, grounded in the chapter text and its study notes

Unfamiliar terms

  • Water of purification. Water mixed with the ashes of the red heifer, sprinkled on the third and seventh days to cleanse defilement by the dead. Interpreted Chapter text in the Scripture panel above

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 2. This is the ordinance of the law: Israel was to bring a red heifer without spot, wherein is no blemish, and upon which never came yoke. JFB notes this is the only case where the victim's colour is specified, and the choice opposed Egyptian superstition: Egyptians sacrificed a red bull with great care to Typhon, their evil being, so sex and colour together were chosen to eradicate a favourite Egyptian superstition from Israel's mind. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. They gave her to Eleazar the priest, that he might bring her forth without the camp and slay her before his face. JFB notes Eleazar, the deputy high priest, was selected because the duty entailed temporary defilement from which the acting high priest was to be preserved, and leading her without the camp followed the law for sin-laden victims, typical of Christ. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Eleazar took of her blood with his finger and sprinkled it seven times directly before the tabernacle. JFB explains before as towards or near: either he carried blood to the tabernacle door, or, turning his face toward the sacred edifice while disqualified from approaching close, he presented an expiatory sacrifice looking to the mercy seat. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. One burned the heifer in his sight: her skin, flesh, blood and dung. Henry notes the heifer was wholly burned, typifying the painful sufferings of Christ in soul and body, as a sacrifice made by fire to satisfy God's justice for man's sin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 6. The priest took cedar wood, hyssop and scarlet, and cast it into the midst of the burning of the heifer. JFB notes every part was consumed except the sprinkled blood, and the ingredients mixed with the ashes were the same as those used in sprinkling lepers. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Then the priest washed his clothes, bathed his flesh in water, and afterward came into the camp, yet was unclean until the evening. JFB notes the ceremonies show the imperfection of the Levitical priesthood, while typifying Christ's condition when expiating our sins. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. He that burned her washed his clothes in water, bathed his flesh in water, and was unclean until the evening. Even the burner, who handled the purification itself, contracted defilement: the rite cleansed others while marking all its ministers unclean till evening. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. A man that is clean gathered up the ashes of the heifer and laid them up without the camp in a clean place, for the congregation, for a water of separation, a purification for sin. Henry notes the ashes were laid up as a purification for sin, a type of the purification Christ made by his death, and his blood is laid up for us in word and sacraments as a fountain of merit for cleansing consciences by faith. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 10. He that gathered the ashes washed his clothes and was unclean until the evening, and it was a perpetual statute to Israel and the stranger sojourning among them. The gatherer, like priest and burner, was defiled by the holy work, yet the statute stood for ever, for native and stranger alike. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. He that touches the dead body of any man shall be unclean seven days. JFB notes this law shows the water's uses: in a population where bereavement defiled whole households and mortality occurred daily, the water of separation must have been in constant requisition, and Jewish writers say a red heifer was sacrificed yearly and its ashes distributed through all Israel's cities. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. He shall purify himself with it on the third day, and on the seventh day he shall be clean, but if he purify not himself on the third day, then on the seventh day he shall not be clean. JFB notes the third day has no natural or moral ground, and is generally taken as typical of Christ's resurrection, by whom his people are sanctified, while seven days show sanctification is progressive and incomplete till the eternal Sabbath. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Whosoever touches the dead body of any man that is dead, and purifies not himself, defiles the tabernacle of the Lord, and that soul shall be cut off from Israel, because the water of separation was not sprinkled on him, and he shall be unclean. Knowing, presumptuous neglect of the sprinkling was punished by excommunication, not by a further washing. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. This is the law when a man dies in a tent: all that come into the tent, and all that is in the tent, shall be unclean seven days. JFB notes the minute instances served religious and sanitary ends: they prevented an Egyptianised Israel from keeping mummied ancestors in their houses, ensured speedy interment, kept burial places distant, and removed infectious corpses and unburied strangers fallen in battle. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Every open vessel, which has no covering bound upon it, is unclean. The tent's vessels shared the tent's defilement unless sealed: pollution spread to what the dead air could reach, and only a bound covering shut it out. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Whosoever touches one slain with a sword in the open fields, or a dead body, or a bone of a man, or a grave, shall be unclean seven days. The law reached beyond the tent to the battlefield and the burial ground: sword, corpse, bone and grave all defiled alike, because death itself, not merely dying at home, was the wages of sin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. For an unclean person they took of the ashes of the burnt heifer of purification for sin, and running water was put thereto in a vessel. Henry joins the two elements: as the ashes signified Christ's merit, so the running water signified the power and grace of the Holy Spirit, compared to rivers of living water, by whose work Christ's righteousness is applied for cleansing. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 18. A clean person took hyssop, dipped it in the water, and sprinkled it upon the tent, all the vessels, and the persons that were there, and upon him that touched a bone, or one slain, or one dead, or a grave. The sprinkling was by a clean hand with hyssop, the lowly plant of application, reaching tent, vessel and person alike. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. The clean person sprinkled upon the unclean on the third day and on the seventh day, and on the seventh day he purified him, and he washed his clothes, bathed himself in water, and was clean at evening. The double sprinkling, third and seventh day, closed with washing: purification was a process over days, not a single moment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. But the man that shall be unclean and shall not purify himself, that soul shall be cut off from the midst of the congregation, because he has defiled the sanctuary of the Lord, and the water of separation has not been sprinkled upon him. Refusal of the appointed cleansing left the sanctuary itself defiled in the reckoning: the unclean man's presence among the congregation profaned what the water would have cleansed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. It shall be a perpetual statute to them, that he that sprinkles the water of separation shall wash his clothes, and he that touches the water shall be unclean until evening. JFB calls the opposite effects singular: the water cleansed one person and defiled another, teaching that its efficacy was not inherent in itself but arose from divine appointment, as ordinances are effectual not by virtue in them or their minister, but through God's grace communicated thereby. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Whatsoever the unclean person touches shall be unclean, and the soul that touches it shall be unclean until evening. Henry draws the moral the institutions teach: the defiling nature of sin is shown here, and we are warned to avoid evil communications, since uncleanness spreads by contact. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
