The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 39 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Knowest thou the time when the wild goats of the rock bring forth? or canst thou mark when the hinds do calve?

2 Canst thou number the months that they fulfil? or knowest thou the time when they bring forth?

3 They bow themselves, they bring forth their young ones, they cast out their sorrows.

4 Their young ones are in good liking, they grow up with corn; they go forth, and return not unto them.

5 Who hath sent out the wild ass free? or who hath loosed the bands of the wild ass?

6 Whose house I have made the wilderness, and the barren land his dwellings.

7 He scorneth the multitude of the city, neither regardeth he the crying of the driver.

8 The range of the mountains is his pasture, and he searcheth after every green thing.

9 Will the unicorn be willing to serve thee, or abide by thy crib?

10 Canst thou bind the unicorn with his band in the furrow? or will he harrow the valleys after thee?

11 Wilt thou trust him, because his strength is great? or wilt thou leave thy labour to him?

12 Wilt thou believe him, that he will bring home thy seed, and gather it into thy barn?

13 Gavest thou the goodly wings unto the peacocks? or wings and feathers unto the ostrich?

14 Which leaveth her eggs in the earth, and warmeth them in dust,

15 And forgetteth that the foot may crush them, or that the wild beast may break them.

16 She is hardened against her young ones, as though they were not hers: her labour is in vain without fear;

17 Because God hath deprived her of wisdom, neither hath he imparted to her understanding.

18 What time she lifteth up herself on high, she scorneth the horse and his rider.

19 Hast thou given the horse strength? hast thou clothed his neck with thunder?

20 Canst thou make him afraid as a grasshopper? the glory of his nostrils is terrible.

21 He paweth in the valley, and rejoiceth in his strength: he goeth on to meet the armed men.

22 He mocketh at fear, and is not affrighted; neither turneth he back from the sword.

23 The quiver rattleth against him, the glittering spear and the shield.

24 He swalloweth the ground with fierceness and rage: neither believeth he that it is the sound of the trumpet.

25 He saith among the trumpets, Ha, ha; and he smelleth the battle afar off, the thunder of the captains, and the shouting.

26 Doth the hawk fly by thy wisdom, and stretch her wings toward the south?

27 Doth the eagle mount up at thy command, and make her nest on high?

28 She dwelleth and abideth on the rock, upon the crag of the rock, and the strong place.

29 From thence she seeketh the prey, and her eyes behold afar off.

30 Her young ones also suck up blood: and where the slain are, there is she.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

God’s questions continue through the wild creatures, none of which man tames or provides for: the wild goats that bring forth on the rocks, the wild ass free in the wilderness, the unicorn that will not serve at your crib, the peacock and ostrich, the horse that laughs at fear in battle, the hawk that stretches her wings southward, and the eagle that builds on the crag and whose young suck up blood where the slain are. Each creature is governed by a wisdom that is not man’s. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Knowest thou the time when the wild goats of the rock bring forth? Or canst thou mark when the hinds do calve? JFB notes even wild beasts, cut off from all care of man, are cared for by God at their seasons of greatest need: their instinct comes direct from God and guides them to help themselves in parturition, the very time when the herdsman is most anxious for his herds, with wild goats as ibex, and hinds as fawns, most timid and defenceless animals, yet cared for by God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Canst thou number the months that they fulfil? Or knowest thou the time when they bring forth? JFB notes they bring forth with ease and do not need to reckon the months of pregnancy, as the shepherd does for his flocks. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. They bow themselves, they bring forth their young ones, they cast out their sorrows. JFB explains bow themselves as in parturition, bending on their knees, bring forth literally as causing their young to cleave the womb and break forth, and sorrows as their young ones, the cause of their momentary pains. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Their young ones are in good liking, they grow up with corn, they go forth, and return not unto them. JFB renders in good liking as in good condition, growing up strong, with corn as in the field, without man's care, and return not as being able to provide for themselves. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Who has sent out the wild ass free? Or who has loosed the bands of the wild ass? JFB notes two different Hebrew words are used for the same animal, the ass of the woods and the wild ass, and that man can rob animals of freedom but not, as God, give freedom combined with subordination to fixed laws. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Whose house God has made the wilderness, and the barren land his dwellings. JFB renders barren literally as salt, that is, unfruitful. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. He scorns the multitude of the city, neither regards he the crying of the driver. JFB renders multitude as din, which the wild ass sets at defiance, being far away from it in the freedom of the wilderness, and driver as who urges on the tame ass to work: the wild ass is the symbol of uncontrolled freedom in the East. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. The range of the mountains is his pasture, and he searches after every green thing. JFB renders range literally as searching, that which it finds by searching being his pasture. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Will the unicorn be willing to serve thee, or abide by thy crib? JFB explains unicorn from the Hebrew reem, conveying loftiness and power, with the rhinoceros perhaps the original type, and the buffalo the animal referred to here from the contrast to the tame ox used in ploughing, and abide literally as passing the night. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Canst thou bind the unicorn with his band in the furrow? Or will he harrow the valleys after thee? JFB explains his band as fastened to the horns, its chief strength lying in the head and shoulders, and after thee as obedient to thee, willing to follow instead of being goaded on before thee. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Wilt thou trust him, because his strength is great? Or wilt thou leave thy labour to him? JFB renders labour as rustic work. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Wilt thou believe him, that he will bring home thy seed, and gather it into thy barn? JFB renders believe as trust, seed as produce, and into thy barn as gathering the contents of the threshing-floor, the corn threshed on it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Gavest thou the goodly wings unto the peacocks? Or wings and feathers unto the ostrich? JFB renders it as the wing of the ostrich hen, literally the crying bird, referring to its night cries vibrating joyously, with the vibrating, quivering wing serving for sail and oar at once, characteristic of the ostrich in full course, its white and black feathers like the stork's. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Which leaves her eggs in the earth, and warms them in dust, and forgets that the foot may crush them, or that the wild beast may break them. JFB notes that unlike the stork she leaves her eggs, hence called by the Arabs the impious bird, yet she lays them with great care and hatches them, but in hot countries the eggs do not need so constant incubation, so she often leaves them; God describes her as she seems to man, implying that though she may seem foolishly to neglect her young, yet really she is guided by a sure instinct from God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. And forgets that the foot may crush them, or that the wild beast may break them. The ostrich's apparent neglect is part of the same instinct described in the preceding verse. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. She is hardened against her young ones, as though they were not hers: her labour is in vain without fear. JFB notes that on a slight noise she often forsakes her eggs and returns not, as if hardened towards her young, with her labour in producing eggs in vain, yet without disquietude about her young, unlike other birds. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. Because God has deprived her of wisdom, neither has he imparted to her understanding. JFB notes the Arab proverb, foolish as an ostrich, yet her very seeming want of wisdom is not without wise design of God, though man cannot see it, just as in the trials of the godly, which seem so unreasonable to Job, there lies hid a wise design. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. What time she lifts up herself on high, she scorns the horse and his rider. JFB notes that notwithstanding her deficiencies she has distinguishing excellences: she lifts herself for running, she cannot mount in the air, and proudly scorns the horse and rider. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. Hast thou given the horse strength? Hast thou clothed his neck with thunder? JFB notes the allusion to the horse suggests his description: Arab poets delight in praising the horse, yet it is not mentioned among Job's possessions, and at the time it seems to have been chiefly used for war, with thunder poetically for majesty, or the trembling, quivering mane. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Canst thou make him afraid as a grasshopper? The glory of his nostrils is terrible. JFB renders make afraid as canst thou, as God does, make him spring as the locust, noting the comparison between locusts and war-horses, and nostrils as snorting furiously. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. He paws in the valley, and rejoices in his strength: he goes on to meet the armed men. JFB renders valley as where the battle is joined, and goes on as goes forth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. He mocks at fear, and is not affrighted, neither turns he back from the sword. The war-horse's courage carries him forward where others would flee. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. The quiver rattles against him, the glittering spear and the shield. JFB renders quiver as for the arrows it contains, directed against him, and glittering spear literally as glittering of the spear, like lightning of the spear, with shield rather as lance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. He swallows the ground with fierceness and rage: neither believes he that it is the sound of the trumpet. JFB explains swalloweth as fretting with impatience, drawing the ground towards him with his hoof as if he would swallow it, and neither believes as he will not stand still when the note of the trumpet sounds. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. He says among the trumpets, Ha, ha, and he smells the battle afar off, the thunder of the captains, and the shouting. JFB renders saith as poetically applied to his mettlesome neighing, whereby he shows his love of the battle, and smelleth as snuffing or discerning, with thunder as thundering voice. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. Does the hawk fly by thy wisdom, and stretch her wings toward the south? JFB explains the instinct by which some birds migrate to warmer climes before winter, with rapid flying peculiarly characterizing the whole hawk genus. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. Does the eagle mount up at thy command, and make her nest on high? JFB notes the eagle flies highest of all birds, thence called the bird of heaven. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. She dwells and abides on the rock, upon the crag of the rock, and the strong place. JFB renders abideth as securely, occupying the same abode mostly for life, with crag literally as tooth, and strong place as citadel or fastness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. From thence she seeks the prey, and her eyes behold afar off. JFB notes the eagle descries its prey at an astonishing distance, by sight rather than smell. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. Her young ones also suck up blood, and where the slain are, there is she. JFB notes the food of young eagles is the blood of victims brought by the parent when they are still too feeble to devour flesh, and that the vulture, which chiefly feeds on carcasses, is probably included in the eagle genus. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
