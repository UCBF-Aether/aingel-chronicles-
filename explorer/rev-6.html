The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Revelation 6 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 And I saw when the Lamb opened one of the seals, and I heard, as it were the noise of thunder, one of the four beasts saying, Come and see.

2 And I saw, and behold a white horse: and he that sat on him had a bow; and a crown was given unto him: and he went forth conquering, and to conquer.

3 And when he had opened the second seal, I heard the second beast say, Come and see.

4 And there went out another horse that was red: and power was given to him that sat thereon to take peace from the earth, and that they should kill one another: and there was given unto him a great sword.

5 And when he had opened the third seal, I heard the third beast say, Come and see. And I beheld, and lo a black horse; and he that sat on him had a pair of balances in his hand.

6 And I heard a voice in the midst of the four beasts say, A measure of wheat for a penny, and three measures of barley for a penny; and see thou hurt not the oil and the wine.

7 And when he had opened the fourth seal, I heard the voice of the fourth beast say, Come and see.

8 And I looked, and behold a pale horse: and his name that sat on him was Death, and Hell followed with him. And power was given unto them over the fourth part of the earth, to kill with sword, and with hunger, and with death, and with the beasts of the earth.

9 And when he had opened the fifth seal, I saw under the altar the souls of them that were slain for the word of God, and for the testimony which they held:

10 And they cried with a loud voice, saying, How long, O Lord, holy and true, dost thou not judge and avenge our blood on them that dwell on the earth?

11 And white robes were given unto every one of them; and it was said unto them, that they should rest yet for a little season, until their fellowservants also and their brethren, that should be killed as they were, should be fulfilled.

12 And I beheld when he had opened the sixth seal, and, lo, there was a great earthquake; and the sun became black as sackcloth of hair, and the moon became as blood;

13 And the stars of heaven fell unto the earth, even as a fig tree casteth her untimely figs, when she is shaken of a mighty wind.

14 And the heaven departed as a scroll when it is rolled together; and every mountain and island were moved out of their places.

15 And the kings of the earth, and the great men, and the rich men, and the chief captains, and the mighty men, and every bondman, and every free man, hid themselves in the dens and in the rocks of the mountains;

16 And said to the mountains and rocks, Fall on us, and hide us from the face of him that sitteth on the throne, and from the wrath of the Lamb:

17 For the great day of his wrath is come; and who shall be able to stand?

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

The Lamb opens six seals. A white horse goes forth conquering, which Henry reads as the pure gospel; then red war takes peace from the earth, black famine weighs out bread by measure, and pale Death with Hell following kills by sword, hunger, death and beasts. Under the fifth seal the souls of the slain cry, How long, and are given white robes and told to rest a little season; at the sixth, earthquake, darkened sun and blood moon, falling stars and a departing heaven drive all ranks to hide from the face of him on the throne and from the wrath of the Lamb. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, Matthew Henry

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. The Lamb opened the first seal, and one of the living creatures called with a voice like thunder, "Come and see." Henry notes Christ now enters on opening and accomplishing God's purposes, with a minister of the church summoning John to observe what appears. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 2. John saw a white horse, whose rider had a bow, received a crown, and went forth conquering and to conquer. Henry notes the white horse is the pure gospel riding swiftly, the bow's sharp arrows are convictions reaching at a distance, the crown is the earnest of victory, and Christ keeps conquering in every age, in his people's sanctification and over the world. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 3. When the Lamb opened the second seal, the second living creature said, "Come and see." Henry groups the next three seals as desolating judgments avenging the covenant on those who refuse or abuse the gospel. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 4. Another horse, red, went out, and its rider was given power to take peace from the earth, so that men should kill one another, with a great sword given to him. Henry notes war takes away peace, one of earth's greatest blessings, and sets men who should love and help one another upon killing one another. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 5. When the third seal was opened, the third living creature said, "Come and see," and John saw a black horse, whose rider had a pair of balances in his hand. Henry reads the black horse as famine, with men eating bread by weight, as threatened in Leviticus. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 6. A voice in the midst of the living creatures said, "A measure of wheat for a penny, and three measures of barley for a penny; and see thou hurt not the oil and the wine." Henry explains the measure was a single quart and the penny a large sum, so this was famine, falling hardest on the poor, while the rich's dainties were spared, though dainties cannot replace the staff of life. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 7. When the fourth seal was opened, the fourth living creature said, "Come and see." Henry continues the chain of judgments: war draws famine after it, and famine draws pestilence, since God is never at a loss for ways to punish a wicked people. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 8. John saw a pale horse, whose rider's name was Death, and Hell followed with him, given power over a fourth part of the earth to kill with sword, hunger, death, and the beasts of the earth. Henry calls Death the king of terrors, pestilence reigning over a nation, with hell, eternal misery, following impenitent death, and notes the beasts prey on the remnant left in a wasted land. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 9. At the fifth seal John saw under the altar the souls of those slain for the word of God and for the testimony they held. Henry notes persecutors can only kill the body, while the souls live near Christ in heaven, received not by their own death but by the Lamb's sacrifice, for the noblest cause: faith in God's word and confession of it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 10. They cried with a loud voice, "How long, O Lord, holy and true, dost thou not judge and avenge our blood on them that dwell on the earth?" Henry notes even perfected spirits retain a proper resentment of wrong, yet commit vengeance to God, desiring judgment for God's honour and the terror of others rather than avenging themselves. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 11. White robes were given to each of them, and they were told to rest yet for a little season, until their fellowservants and brethren, who should be killed as they were, should be fulfilled. Henry notes the robes are victory and honour, an abundant recompense, and that when the appointed number of martyrs is complete, God will take just revenge on persecutors and give the troubled rest. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 12. At the sixth seal there was a great earthquake, the sun became black as sackcloth of hair, and the moon became as blood. Henry refers this to the revolutions at Constantine's fall of paganism, or, with great probability, to Jerusalem's destruction as an emblem of the general judgment, with foundations shaken and rulers fallen. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 13. The stars of heaven fell to the earth, as a fig tree casts her untimely figs when shaken by a mighty wind. Henry reads the falling stars as men of note and influence in lower spheres, in a general desolation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 14. The heaven departed as a scroll when it is rolled together, and every mountain and island was moved out of its place. Henry takes this as the perishing of the ecclesiastical state and a judgment astonishing all nations, high and seemingly secure alike. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 15. The kings of the earth, great men, rich men, chief captains, mighty men, bondmen, and freemen hid themselves in the dens and rocks of the mountains. Henry notes no authority, grandeur, riches, valour, or strength could support men then, and even slaves with nothing to lose were amazed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 16. They said to the mountains and rocks, "Fall on us, and hide us from the face of him that sitteth on the throne, and from the wrath of the Lamb." Henry notes their terror made them wish for non-being, and that the wrath of the Lamb is exceedingly dreadful, since if the Redeemer who appeases God's wrath is himself the enemy, no friend remains to plead. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 17. For the great day of his wrath is come, and who shall be able to stand? Henry concludes that as men have their day of opportunity and seasons of grace, so God has his day of righteous wrath, when the most stout-hearted sinners cannot stand before him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry

Unfamiliar terms

  • Balances, penny, measure. Famine measures: Henry explains the measure as a single quart and the penny a large sum, so a quart of wheat costs a day's pay. Interpreted Study note, verse 6, Matthew Henry
  • Pale horse. The fourth seal's horse, whose rider's name is Death, with Hell following — pestilence reigning over a nation, in Henry's note. Interpreted Study note, verse 8, Matthew Henry

Connections to other passages

  • The famine measure: men eating bread by weight, as threatened in Leviticus (verse 5, Henry). Interpreted Study note for this chapter
