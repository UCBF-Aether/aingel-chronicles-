The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Psalms 81 (King James Version (1611) for Psalms 1–150; Charles Thomson, The Septuagint (1808) for Psalm 151)

1 Sing aloud unto God our strength: make a joyful noise unto the God of Jacob.

2 Take a psalm, and bring hither the timbrel, the pleasant harp with the psaltery.

3 Blow up the trumpet in the new moon, in the time appointed, on our solemn feast day.

4 For this was a statute for Israel, and a law of the God of Jacob.

5 This he ordained in Joseph for a testimony, when he went out through the land of Egypt: where I heard a language that I understood not.

6 I removed his shoulder from the burden: his hands were delivered from the pots.

7 Thou calledst in trouble, and I delivered thee; I answered thee in the secret place of thunder: I proved thee at the waters of Meribah. Selah.

8 Hear, O my people, and I will testify unto thee: O Israel, if thou wilt hearken unto me;

9 There shall no strange god be in thee; neither shalt thou worship any strange god.

10 I am the LORD thy God, which brought thee out of the land of Egypt: open thy mouth wide, and I will fill it.

11 But my people would not hearken to my voice; and Israel would none of me.

12 So I gave them up unto their own hearts’ lust: and they walked in their own counsels.

13 Oh that my people had hearkened unto me, and Israel had walked in my ways!

14 I should soon have subdued their enemies, and turned my hand against their adversaries.

15 The haters of the LORD should have submitted themselves unto him: but their time should have endured for ever.

16 He should have fed them also with the finest of the wheat: and with honey out of the rock should I have satisfied thee.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

A feast-day call to sing aloud and sound the trumpet opens into God's own voice recalling the Exodus: the burden lifted, deliverance in trouble, and the test at Meribah. The command is to have no strange god, and the psalm laments that Israel would not listen and was given up to its own counsels — then wishes aloud that, had they listened, God would have subdued their enemies and fed them with the finest wheat and honey out of the rock. Interpreted Psalm text above; study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. God as strength and God of Jacob are the two lenses for praise: covenant protector who fights battles, and strength of every Israelite for service, suffering, and conflict. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 2. Take a psalm, bring the timbrel, the pleasant harp with the psaltery — music is commanded for worship. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 3. Trumpet at new moon and appointed feast marks times set not for God to meet us, since he is always ready, but for us to meet one another in joint praise. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 4. This was a statute for Israel and a law of the God of Jacob, not a mere custom. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 5. Testimony ordained in Joseph keeps Egypt in memory as evidence: a strange language heard there makes the trouble grievous, and therefore the deliverance glorious. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 6. Shoulder freed from burden and hands from pots name slavery as a sensible mercy: deliverance from baskets of clay is to be held in everlasting remembrance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 7. Called in trouble, answered from thunder's secret place, proved at Meribah: Henry reads Sinai's terrors and the pillar's fire as favours, and Meribah as man's badness set against God's continuing goodness as foils. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 8. Hear, O my people, and I will testify unto thee, O Israel, if thou wilt hearken unto me, is God demanding diligent and serious attention: Hear, O my people! and who should hear me if my people will not? I have heard and answered thee; now wilt thou hear me? Hear what is said with the greatest solemnity and the most unquestionable certainty, for it is what I will testify unto thee. Do not only give me the hearing, but hearken unto me, that is, be advised by me, be ruled by me. Nothing could be more reasonably nor more justly expected, and yet God puts an if upon it: If thou wilt hearken unto me. It is thy interest to do so, and yet it is questionable whether thou wilt or no; for thy neck is an iron sinew. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 9. Strange gods are strange precisely because a people with the true and living God should hanker after no other: jealousy here is God's refusal to share glory. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 10. The preface to the commandments becomes the argument: Redeemer first, then law, and the promise is a mouth opened wide filled, since divine pleasures satisfy without surfeiting, unlike pleasures of sense. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 11. Henry charges Israel with high contempt of both lawgiver and benefactor: they heard God's voice as no other people did, yet would not be ruled by it, and they would none of him as their God, though fairly invited into covenant. The fault was purely wilfulness — not that they might not or could not, but that they would not be religious. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 12. So I gave them up unto their own hearts' lust, and they walked in their own counsels, is God justifying himself in the spiritual judgments he had brought upon them: So I gave them up unto their own hearts' lusts, which would be more dangerous enemies and more mischievous oppressors to them than any of the neighbouring nations ever were. God withdrew his Spirit from them, took off the bridle of restraining grace, left them to themselves, and justly; they will do as they will, and therefore let them do as they will. Ephraim is joined to idols; let him alone. It is a righteous thing with God to give those up to their own hearts' lusts that indulge them, and give up themselves to be led by them; for why should his Spirit always strive? His grace is his own, and he is debtor to no man, and yet, as he never gave his grace to any that could say they deserved it, so he never took it away from any but such as had first forfeited it: They would none of me, so I gave them up; let them take their course. And see what follows: They walked in their own counsels, in the way of their heart and in the sight of their eye, both in their worships and in their conversations. I left them to do as they would, and then they did all that was ill; they walked in their own counsels, and not according to the counsels of God and his advice. God therefore was not the author of their sin; he left them to the lusts of their own hearts and the counsels of their own heads; if they do not well, the blame must lie upon their own hearts and the blood upon their own heads. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 13. Oh that my people had hearkened unto me, and Israel had walked in my ways, is God testifying his good-will to them in wishing they had done well for themselves: he saw how sad their case was, and how sure their ruin, when they were delivered up to their own lusts. That is worse than being given up to Satan, which may be in order to reformation and to salvation; but to be delivered up to their own hearts' lusts is to be sealed under condemnation. He that is filthy, let him be filthy still. What fatal precipices will not these hurry a man to! Now here God looks upon them with pity, and shows that it was with reluctance that he thus abandoned them to their folly and fate. How shall I give thee up, Ephraim? So here, O that my people had hearkened! Thus Christ lamented the obstinacy of Jerusalem: If thou hadst known. The expressions here are very affecting, designed to show how unwilling God is that any should perish and desirous that all should come to repentance — he delights not in the ruin of sinful persons or nations — and also what enemies sinners are to themselves and what an aggravation it will be of their misery that they might have been happy upon such easy terms. The duty God required from them as the condition of all this mercy: he expected no more than that they should hearken to him, as a scholar to his teacher, to receive his instructions — as a servant to his master, to receive his commands; and that they should walk in his ways, those ways of the Lord which are right and pleasant, that they should observe the institutions of his ordinances and attend the intimations of his providence. There was nothing unreasonable in this. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 14. Had they hearkened, God would soon have subdued their enemies with a mere turn of his hand, without a tedious war, and the submission of haters would have honoured both God and Israel. Henry lays the withholding squarely on their neglect: national disobedience retards deliverance, and it is sin that makes troubles long and salvation slow. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 15. Haters submitting with time enduring forever is the counterfactual blessing: obedience would have turned enemies to submission and made their submission lasting, not fleeting. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 16. Finest wheat and honey from rock are the blessings obedience would have secured: Henry's good-will wish shows what keeping close to God would have fed a people with, against the leanness of their own counsels. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
