The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 12 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 And Job answered and said,

2 No doubt but ye are the people, and wisdom shall die with you.

3 But I have understanding as well as you; I am not inferior to you: yea, who knoweth not such things as these?

4 I am as one mocked of his neighbour, who calleth upon God, and he answereth him: the just upright man is laughed to scorn.

5 He that is ready to slip with his feet is as a lamp despised in the thought of him that is at ease.

6 The tabernacles of robbers prosper, and they that provoke God are secure; into whose hand God bringeth abundantly.

7 But ask now the beasts, and they shall teach thee; and the fowls of the air, and they shall tell thee:

8 Or speak to the earth, and it shall teach thee: and the fishes of the sea shall declare unto thee.

9 Who knoweth not in all these that the hand of the LORD hath wrought this?

10 In whose hand is the soul of every living thing, and the breath of all mankind.

11 Doth not the ear try words? and the mouth taste his meat?

12 With the ancient is wisdom; and in length of days understanding.

13 With him is wisdom and strength, he hath counsel and understanding.

14 Behold, he breaketh down, and it cannot be built again: he shutteth up a man, and there can be no opening.

15 Behold, he withholdeth the waters, and they dry up: also he sendeth them out, and they overturn the earth.

16 With him is strength and wisdom: the deceived and the deceiver are his.

17 He leadeth counsellors away spoiled, and maketh the judges fools.

18 He looseth the bond of kings, and girdeth their loins with a girdle.

19 He leadeth princes away spoiled, and overthroweth the mighty.

20 He removeth away the speech of the trusty, and taketh away the understanding of the aged.

21 He poureth contempt upon princes, and weakeneth the strength of the mighty.

22 He discovereth deep things out of darkness, and bringeth out to light the shadow of death.

23 He increaseth the nations, and destroyeth them: he enlargeth the nations, and straiteneth them again.

24 He taketh away the heart of the chief of the people of the earth, and causeth them to wander in a wilderness where there is no way.

25 They grope in the dark without light, and he maketh them to stagger like a drunken man.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Job answers Zophar with irony: no doubt you are the people, and wisdom shall die with you — yet he has understanding as well as they, and knows their commonplaces. Facts, he says, run against their rule: the tabernacles of robbers prosper, and the beasts, birds, earth, and fishes all teach that the hand of the LORD has wrought this. With God, not merely with the aged, is wisdom — he leads counsellors and judges away spoiled, loosens kings, and increases and destroys nations as he wills. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Job answers Zophar, turning the friends' wisdom back on them with irony. The reply claims equal understanding and exposes their commonplaces. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. No doubt you are the people, and wisdom shall die with you. JFB calls this ironical, as if all the world's wisdom were concentrated in them and would expire when they expired. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Job also has understanding, is not inferior to them, and knows such things as these. JFB explains not inferior as not vanquished in argument, and such things as commonplace maxims pompously adduced. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Job is as one mocked of his neighbour, who calls upon God and God answers him, yet the just upright man is laughed to scorn. JFB notes the friends' unfounded accusations were a mockery, answering Zophar's word mockest, and renders neighbour as Job himself calling on God for a favourable answer. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. He who is ready to slip with his feet is as a lamp despised in the thought of him who is at ease. JFB gives the torch image: the secure traveller contemptuously throws aside the torch that guided his uncertain steps, as Job's friends now mock the helper they once gladly used. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. The tabernacles of robbers prosper, and they who provoke God are secure, into whose hand God brings abundantly. JFB notes fact opposes Zophar's theory that wickedness causes insecurity, and renders the last clause as those who make a god of their own hand, regarding might as their only rule. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Ask the beasts and they shall teach you, and the fowls of the air shall tell you. JFB notes beasts, birds, fishes, and plants teach that the violent live most securely: the vulture more securely than the dove, the lion than the ox. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Speak to the earth and it shall teach you, and the fishes of the sea shall declare it to you. JFB renders speak to the earth as the shrubs of the earth, joining the plant world to the animal witnesses. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Who among all these does not know that the hand of the LORD has wrought this? JFB notes that in all these cases agency must be referred to Jehovah, and that this is the only undisputed passage in the poetical part where the name Jehovah occurs. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. In God's hand is the soul of every living thing and the breath of all mankind. JFB explains soul as animal life, and notes man is subjected to the same laws as the lower animals. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Does not the ear try words, as the mouth tastes meat? JFB compares tasting meats with trying words: each chooses according to taste, and Job may borrow the ancients' proverbs that suit his case as the friends borrow those suiting theirs. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. With the ancient is wisdom, and in length of days understanding. JFB notes ancient means aged, and Job concedes the point only to turn it: with God, not merely with the aged, is true wisdom. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. With God is wisdom and strength, and he has counsel and understanding. JFB contrasts this with the ancient is wisdom: Job quotes an ancient saying suiting his argument, that with God is true wisdom, and man's part is to bow to his unchangeable decrees. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. If God breaks down, it cannot be built again; if he shuts up a man, there can be no opening. JFB notes Job refers to Zophar's shut up, now as God's absolute sovereignty. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. If God withholds the waters they dry up, and if he sends them out they overturn the earth. JFB probably sees an allusion to the flood, God's sovereign control over waters that bless or destroy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. With God are strength and wisdom, and the deceived and the deceiver are his. Both sides of human deception fall under the same sovereign hand. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. God leads counsellors away spoiled, and makes judges fools. The wisest human counsel is stripped bare before him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. God loosens the bond of kings and girds their loins with a girdle. JFB explains the bond as the authority with which kings bind subjects, replaced by the cord of captives instead of the royal girdle. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. God leads princes away spoiled and overthrows the mighty. JFB renders princes as priests, so even sacred ministers are not exempt from reversals, and the mighty as firm-rooted in power. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. God removes away the speech of the trusty and takes away the understanding of the aged. JFB renders the trusty as those secure in eloquence, and understanding as taste, the insight experience gives the aged. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. God pours contempt upon princes and weakens the strength of the mighty. JFB explains weakening as loosening the girdle: Orientals gird flowing garments for action, so loosening destroys their power in the people's eyes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. God discovers deep things out of darkness and brings out to light the shadow of death. What is hidden from men is open before him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. God increases the nations and destroys them, enlarges them and straitens them again. JFB notes straiteneth literally means leadeth in, that is, reduces. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. God takes away the heart of the chief of the people and causes them to wander in a wilderness where there is no way. JFB explains heart as intelligence, and the wilderness as figurative, not a reference to Israel's desert wanderings. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. They grope in the dark without light, and God makes them stagger like a drunken man. The loss of intelligence leaves leaders groping and reeling. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Where the speakers disagree

The friends claim the wisdom of the ancients: the righteous are restored, the godless wither. Job answers that he knows their maxims as well as they do, and that observation breaks their rule — robbers prosper and beasts, birds, and fishes all show the same sovereign hand at work. Both sides appeal to God’s power; they differ on what it proves about Job. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
