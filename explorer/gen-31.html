The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Genesis 31 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 And he heard the words of Laban’s sons, saying, Jacob hath taken away all that was our father’s; and of that which was our father’s hath he gotten all this glory.

2 And Jacob beheld the countenance of Laban, and, behold, it was not toward him as before.

3 And the LORD said unto Jacob, Return unto the land of thy fathers, and to thy kindred; and I will be with thee.

4 And Jacob sent and called Rachel and Leah to the field unto his flock,

5 And said unto them, I see your father’s countenance, that it is not toward me as before; but the God of my father hath been with me.

6 And ye know that with all my power I have served your father.

7 And your father hath deceived me, and changed my wages ten times; but God suffered him not to hurt me.

8 If he said thus, The speckled shall be thy wages; then all the cattle bare speckled: and if he said thus, The ringstraked shall be thy hire; then bare all the cattle ringstraked.

9 Thus God hath taken away the cattle of your father, and given them to me.

10 And it came to pass at the time that the cattle conceived, that I lifted up mine eyes, and saw in a dream, and, behold, the rams which leaped upon the cattle were ringstraked, speckled, and grisled.

11 And the angel of God spake unto me in a dream, saying, Jacob: And I said, Here am I.

12 And he said, Lift up now thine eyes, and see, all the rams which leap upon the cattle are ringstraked, speckled, and grisled: for I have seen all that Laban doeth unto thee.

13 I am the God of Bethel, where thou anointedst the pillar, and where thou vowedst a vow unto me: now arise, get thee out from this land, and return unto the land of thy kindred.

14 And Rachel and Leah answered and said unto him, Is there yet any portion or inheritance for us in our father’s house?

15 Are we not counted of him strangers? for he hath sold us, and hath quite devoured also our money.

16 For all the riches which God hath taken from our father, that is ours, and our children’s: now then, whatsoever God hath said unto thee, do.

17 Then Jacob rose up, and set his sons and his wives upon camels;

18 And he carried away all his cattle, and all his goods which he had gotten, the cattle of his getting, which he had gotten in Padanaram, for to go to Isaac his father in the land of Canaan.

19 And Laban went to shear his sheep: and Rachel had stolen the images that were her father’s.

20 And Jacob stole away unawares to Laban the Syrian, in that he told him not that he fled.

21 So he fled with all that he had; and he rose up, and passed over the river, and set his face toward the mount Gilead.

22 And it was told Laban on the third day that Jacob was fled.

23 And he took his brethren with him, and pursued after him seven days’ journey; and they overtook him in the mount Gilead.

24 And God came to Laban the Syrian in a dream by night, and said unto him, Take heed that thou speak not to Jacob either good or bad.

25 Then Laban overtook Jacob. Now Jacob had pitched his tent in the mount: and Laban with his brethren pitched in the mount of Gilead.

26 And Laban said to Jacob, What hast thou done, that thou hast stolen away unawares to me, and carried away my daughters, as captives taken with the sword?

27 Wherefore didst thou flee away secretly, and steal away from me; and didst not tell me, that I might have sent thee away with mirth, and with songs, with tabret, and with harp?

28 And hast not suffered me to kiss my sons and my daughters? thou hast now done foolishly in so doing.

29 It is in the power of my hand to do you hurt: but the God of your father spake unto me yesternight, saying, Take thou heed that thou speak not to Jacob either good or bad.

30 And now, though thou wouldest needs be gone, because thou sore longedst after thy father’s house, yet wherefore hast thou stolen my gods?

31 And Jacob answered and said to Laban, Because I was afraid: for I said, Peradventure thou wouldest take by force thy daughters from me.

32 With whomsoever thou findest thy gods, let him not live: before our brethren discern thou what is thine with me, and take it to thee. For Jacob knew not that Rachel had stolen them.

33 And Laban went into Jacob’s tent, and into Leah’s tent, and into the two maidservants’ tents; but he found them not. Then went he out of Leah’s tent, and entered into Rachel’s tent.

34 Now Rachel had taken the images, and put them in the camel’s furniture, and sat upon them. And Laban searched all the tent, but found them not.

35 And she said to her father, Let it not displease my lord that I cannot rise up before thee; for the custom of women is upon me. And he searched but found not the images.

36 And Jacob was wroth, and chode with Laban: and Jacob answered and said to Laban, What is my trespass? what is my sin, that thou hast so hotly pursued after me?

37 Whereas thou hast searched all my stuff, what hast thou found of all thy household stuff? set it here before my brethren and thy brethren, that they may judge betwixt us both.

38 This twenty years have I been with thee; thy ewes and thy she goats have not cast their young, and the rams of thy flock have I not eaten.

39 That which was torn of beasts I brought not unto thee; I bare the loss of it; of my hand didst thou require it, whether stolen by day, or stolen by night.

40 Thus I was; in the day the drought consumed me, and the frost by night; and my sleep departed from mine eyes.

41 Thus have I been twenty years in thy house; I served thee fourteen years for thy two daughters, and six years for thy cattle: and thou hast changed my wages ten times.

42 Except the God of my father, the God of Abraham, and the fear of Isaac, had been with me, surely thou hadst sent me away now empty. God hath seen mine affliction and the labour of my hands, and rebuked thee yesternight.

43 And Laban answered and said unto Jacob, These daughters are my daughters, and these children are my children, and these cattle are my cattle, and all that thou seest is mine: and what can I do this day unto these my daughters, or unto their children which they have born?

44 Now therefore come thou, let us make a covenant, I and thou; and let it be for a witness between me and thee.

45 And Jacob took a stone, and set it up for a pillar.

46 And Jacob said unto his brethren, Gather stones; and they took stones, and made an heap: and they did eat there upon the heap.

47 And Laban called it Jegarsahadutha: but Jacob called it Galeed.

48 And Laban said, This heap is a witness between me and thee this day. Therefore was the name of it called Galeed;

49 And Mizpah; for he said, The LORD watch between me and thee, when we are absent one from another.

50 If thou shalt afflict my daughters, or if thou shalt take other wives beside my daughters, no man is with us; see, God is witness betwixt me and thee.

51 And Laban said to Jacob, Behold this heap, and behold this pillar, which I have cast betwixt me and thee:

52 This heap be witness, and this pillar be witness, that I will not pass over this heap to thee, and that thou shalt not pass over this heap and this pillar unto me, for harm.

53 The God of Abraham, and the God of Nahor, the God of their father, judge betwixt us. And Jacob sware by the fear of his father Isaac.

54 Then Jacob offered sacrifice upon the mount, and called his brethren to eat bread: and they did eat bread, and tarried all night in the mount.

55 And early in the morning Laban rose up, and kissed his sons and his daughters, and blessed them: and Laban departed, and returned unto his place.

How to read the labels

Every substantive historical claim in the editorial panels below carries one of three labels, plus a source reference:

  • Documented — directly supported by a cited source.
  • Interpreted — a reasoned explanation or proposed relationship.
  • Unresolved — evidence is insufficient to establish the claim.

A claim does NOT become Documented merely because a commentary states it. Commentary views are attributed to their authors and labeled Interpreted unless a primary textual source directly supports the claim. Where evidence runs out, the page says Unresolved and stops — it does not fill the gap.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Hearing Laban's sons accuse him of taking their father's wealth, and warned by God, Jacob flees secretly with his wives, children and flocks. Laban pursues to Gilead, God warns him in a dream, the two men air their grievances, and part with a covenant marked by a heap of stones, Mizpah, each going his own way. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Jacob learned, apparently by report, that Laban's sons resented his prosperity and claimed it came from their father's wealth. Henry's point is that overvaluing wealth breeds envy, even between kin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Laban's changed face told Jacob the relationship had soured, though Jacob had kept a good conscience. JFB notes that prosperity often stirs envy, and a good name cannot always be kept in a censorious world. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. God, not resentment, set Jacob's move in motion: he was told to return to his own land with the promise of God's presence. JFB draws the lesson to seek God's direction in journeys and settlements, not to act from fretfulness alone. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Jacob called Rachel and Leah to him at the flock, since he could not leave it, to tell them his settled plan. Henry and JFB both stress that a husband should consult his wives rather than act in secret. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Jacob pointed to two things: Laban's changed attitude, and the God of his father who had stayed with him through it. The contrast frames the whole speech — human favour failed, divine faithfulness did not. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Jacob reminded his wives that he had served Laban with all his strength, so his case rested on faithful service, not on scheming. JFB notes he laid out his grounds of dissatisfaction before mentioning God's blessing and command. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Laban had repeatedly changed Jacob's wages, yet God had not allowed him to harm Jacob. The verse shows a pattern: human unfairness met by divine protection. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Whatever wage Laban named, the flocks bore in Jacob's favour, so the outcome pointed away from Laban's control. Henry reads this as God overruling a dishonest bargain. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Jacob's conclusion was plain: God had taken the cattle from Laban and given them to him. The wealth was therefore a gift of providence, not a theft. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. In a dream at mating time Jacob saw the marked rams, a picture confirming that the increase came by God's ordering. The dream prepared him to trust the coming move. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. The angel of God called Jacob by name in the dream, and Jacob answered that he was listening. The personal call marks this as direction, not mere fancy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Jacob was told to notice that all the rams were marked, because God had seen all that Laban was doing to him. Nothing in Laban's dealing had escaped God's notice. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. God identified himself as the God of Bethel, where Jacob had anointed a pillar and made a vow, and now called him to leave and return home. Henry's lesson is to remember past vows and communion with God when a hard decision comes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Rachel and Leah answered together, fully approving Jacob's plan, since they had grievances of their own. JFB, quoting Henry, notes that a true helpmeet does not hinder a husband in what God calls him to do. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. The sisters felt treated as strangers: their father had sold them and used up the price. That sense of being cut off from any share at home made leaving easier to accept. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. They agreed that the riches God had taken from their father belonged to them and their children, and urged Jacob simply to do what God had said. Their consent rested on God's word, not on gain alone. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. Jacob set his family on camels for the journey, in the quick, simple way of pastoral people moving camp. JFB pictures tents struck, baggage stowed, and a plain emptied within hours. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. Jacob carried away only his own cattle and goods, the increase God had given him in Padan-aram. JFB stresses he took nothing of Laban's to make up for his losses. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. While Laban was away shearing sheep, Rachel stole her father's household images. Henry sees here how deep the family's drift toward idolatry ran, even after leaving idolatrous Chaldea. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Jacob left without telling Laban, so the departure was secret. JFB judges the secrecy prudent, since open notice might have meant detention by force or trickery. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. Jacob crossed the river and headed for the hill country of Gilead, putting distance between his household and Laban's. The direction marks a clean break toward Canaan. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Laban heard of the flight on the third day and at once gave chase. Unencumbered, he travelled fast, while Jacob's large household and flocks moved slowly. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. Laban caught up with Jacob after seven days at Mount Gilead, bringing his kinsmen with him. The meeting was therefore a confrontation, not a quiet farewell. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. God came to Laban in a dream by night and warned him not to speak to Jacob either good or bad. Henry's point is that God can bridle a wicked man's malice without changing his heart. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. Laban overtook Jacob where Jacob had pitched his tent in the hills, and the two camps faced each other. The dream had already limited what Laban could do there. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. Laban charged Jacob with stealing away and carrying off his daughters as if they were captives. JFB notes he said nothing of the wages charge, and his complaint about a proper send-off rang hollow after his own conduct. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. Laban claimed he would have sent Jacob off with music and celebration if only he had been told. JFB calls this hypocritical cant, given how Laban had treated Jacob. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. Laban added that he had not even been allowed to kiss his children goodbye, and called Jacob's course foolish. The charge tried to turn a flight from unfairness into a family offence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. Laban admitted he had power to hurt Jacob, but said the God of Jacob's father had warned him the night before. Restraint here came from God's warning, not from Laban's goodwill. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. Laban then raised his grave charge: Jacob had stolen his gods. JFB explains these teraphim as small human figures kept as superstitious talismans, not objects of true worship. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 31. Jacob answered that fear had driven him: he thought Laban might seize his daughters by force. His reply is honest about his motive, even though it does not cover Rachel's theft. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 32. Confident of his innocence and unaware of Rachel's act, Jacob boldly invited a search and pronounced a severe penalty on whoever held the images. JFB notes the boldness came from a clear conscience about his own conduct. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 33. Laban searched Jacob's tent, Leah's tent, and the servants' tents, and found nothing. The fruitless search began to clear Jacob, tent by tent. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 34. Rachel had hidden the images in the camel's saddle-bag and sat on them, so Laban's search of her tent found nothing. JFB notes the pack saddle was commonly used as a seat or cushion. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 35. Rachel excused herself from rising by pleading the custom of women, and Laban searched without finding the images. Henry treats the whole episode as part of the household's tangled idolatry and deceit. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 36. With the search over, Jacob's anger broke out and he demanded what offence justified so hot a pursuit. JFB notes that, passion running high, his charges ranged widely over twenty years of grievance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 37. Jacob challenged Laban to produce anything of his found among Jacob's goods and let both companies judge between them. The appeal to witnesses shows Jacob wanted an open verdict, not a shouting match. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 38. For twenty years Jacob's care had kept Laban's ewes from miscarrying, and he had not eaten the rams of the flock. JFB notes that in the East females were seldom killed for food except when barren, so this was careful, honest shepherding. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 39. When animals were torn by beasts, Jacob bore the loss himself instead of bringing the carcass to Laban. JFB explains that shepherds were held responsible for losses unless they could prove wild beasts caused them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 40. Jacob described days of drought and nights of frost, with sleep driven from his eyes, as the price of his service. JFB adds that great and long provocation ruffles even disciplined tempers, and allows much for Jacob here. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 41. In twenty years Jacob had served fourteen for his daughters and six for his cattle, while Laban changed his wages ten times. The summary turns a long quarrel into a single, checkable account. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 42. Jacob credited his survival to the God of Abraham and the fear of Isaac: without God, Laban would have sent him away empty. Henry notes Jacob speaks of God as his father's God, counting himself unworthy yet beloved for his father's sake. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 43. Laban answered by claiming everything in sight — daughters, children, flocks — as his own, yet admitted he could do nothing about it that day. Henry notes he could neither justify himself nor condemn Jacob, so he changed tack. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 44. Laban proposed a covenant between them, with something to stand as witness. JFB describes the ancient ratification: a circle of stones for seats, a pillar set upright, a sacrifice, and a shared feast of reconciliation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 45. Jacob set up a stone as a pillar, the first visible mark of the agreement. In a time when writing was little used, a stone monument kept the memory of a bargain. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 46. Jacob told his kinsmen to gather stones into a heap, and they ate together on the heap. Henry notes that eating and drinking together was the ancient token of hearty reconciliation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 47. Laban and Jacob each named the heap in his own tongue, both names meaning a heap of witness. The double naming shows two peoples making one agreement. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 48. Laban declared the heap a witness between them that day, which fixed the name Galeed. The place itself was to remind both sides of what they had promised. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 49. The place was also called Mizpah, from Laban's prayer that the Lord would watch between them when they were apart. The name turns absence itself into a reason to trust God's oversight. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 50. The covenant bound Jacob not to ill-treat Laban's daughters or take other wives, with God as witness even where no man was present. Henry's lesson is that God judges between contending parties, and wrongdoing is at the wrongdoer's peril. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 51. Laban pointed to the heap and pillar he had set between them as the boundary mark of the agreement. Both markers were to stand where both men could see them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 52. Heap and pillar were to witness that neither man would cross them to harm the other. JFB notes the difference in their oaths: Laban appealed to the God of Abraham and Nahor, while Jacob swore by the fear of Isaac, wary of the idolatry in Nahor's line. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 53. Laban invoked the God of Abraham, Nahor, and their father as judge, and Jacob swore by the fear of his father Isaac. The two oaths reveal two different religious standpoints under one treaty. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 54. Jacob offered sacrifice on the mountain and invited his kinsmen to eat bread, and they stayed the night there. Henry notes that peace with God puts true comfort into peace with friends. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 55. Early in the morning Laban kissed and blessed his children and grandchildren, then went home. Henry closes the chapter with the remark that God is often better to us than our fears, overruling men's spirits in our favour. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

JFB notes he said nothing of the wages charge, and his complaint about a proper send-off rang hollow after his own conduct. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. Laban claimed he would have sent Jacob off with music and celebration if only he had been told. JFB calls this hypocritical cant, given how Laban had treated Jacob. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. Laban added that he had not even been allowed to kiss his children goodbye, and called Jacob's course foolish. The charge tried to turn a flight from unfairness into a family offence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. Laban admitted he had power to hurt Jacob, but said the God of Jacob's father had warned him the night before. Restraint here came from God's warning, not from Laban's goodwill. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. Laban then raised his grave charge: Jacob had stolen his gods. JFB explains these teraphim as small human figures kept as superstitious talismans, not objects of true worship. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 31. Jacob answered that fear had driven him: he thought Laban might seize his daughters by force. His reply is honest about his motive, even though it does not cover Rachel's theft. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 32. Confident of his innocence and unaware of Rachel's act, Jacob boldly invited a search and pronounced a severe penalty on whoever held the images. JFB notes the boldness came from a clear conscience about his own conduct. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 33. Laban searched Jacob's tent, Leah's tent, and the servants' tents, and found nothing. The fruitless search began to clear Jacob, tent by tent. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 34. Rachel had hidden the images in the camel's saddle-bag and sat on them, so Laban's search of her tent found nothing. JFB notes the pack saddle was commonly used as a seat or cushion. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 35. Rachel excused herself from rising by pleading the custom of women, and Laban searched without finding the images. Henry treats the whole episode as part of the household's tangled idolatry and deceit. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 36. With the search over, Jacob's anger broke out and he demanded what offence justified so hot a pursuit. JFB notes that, passion running high, his charges ranged widely over twenty years of grievance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 37. Jacob challenged Laban to produce anything of his found among Jacob's goods and let both companies judge between them. The appeal to witnesses shows Jacob wanted an open verdict, not a shouting match. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 38. For twenty years Jacob's care had kept Laban's ewes from miscarrying, and he had not eaten the rams of the flock. JFB notes that in the East females were seldom killed for food except when barren, so this was careful, honest shepherding. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 39. When animals were torn by beasts, Jacob bore the loss himself instead of bringing the carcass to Laban. JFB explains that shepherds were held responsible for losses unless they could prove wild beasts caused them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 40. Jacob described days of drought and nights of frost, with sleep driven from his eyes, as the price of his service. JFB adds that great and long provocation ruffles even disciplined tempers, and allows much for Jacob here. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 41. In twenty years Jacob had served fourteen for his daughters and six for his cattle, while Laban changed his wages ten times. The summary turns a long quarrel into a single, checkable account. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 42. Jacob credited his survival to the God of Abraham and the fear of Isaac: without God, Laban would have sent him away empty. Henry notes Jacob speaks of God as his father's God, counting himself unworthy yet beloved for his father's sake. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 43. Laban answered by claiming everything in sight — daughters, children, flocks — as his own, yet admitted he could do nothing about it that day. Henry notes he could neither justify himself nor condemn Jacob, so he changed tack. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 44. Laban proposed a covenant between them, with something to stand as witness. JFB describes the ancient ratification: a circle of stones for seats, a pillar set upright, a sacrifice, and a shared feast of reconciliation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 45. Jacob set up a stone as a pillar, the first visible mark of the agreement. In a time when writing was little used, a stone monument kept the memory of a bargain. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 46. Jacob told his kinsmen to gather stones into a heap, and they ate together on the heap. Henry notes that eating and drinking together was the ancient token of hearty reconciliation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 47. Laban and Jacob each named the heap in his own tongue, both names meaning a heap of witness. The double naming shows two peoples making one agreement. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 48. Laban declared the heap a witness between them that day, which fixed the name Galeed. The place itself was to remind both sides of what they had promised. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 49. The place was also called Mizpah, from Laban's prayer that the Lord would watch between them when they were apart. The name turns absence itself into a reason to trust God's oversight. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 50. The covenant bound Jacob not to ill-treat Laban's daughters or take other wives, with God as witness even where no man was present. Henry's lesson is that God judges between contending parties, and wrongdoing is at the wrongdoer's peril. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 51. Laban pointed to the heap and pillar he had set between them as the boundary mark of the agreement. Both markers were to stand where both men could see them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 52. Heap and pillar were to witness that neither man would cross them to harm the other. JFB notes the difference in their oaths: Laban appealed to the God of Abraham and Nahor, while Jacob swore by the fear of Isaac, wary of the idolatry in Nahor's line. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 53. Laban invoked the God of Abraham, Nahor, and their father as judge, and Jacob swore by the fear of his father Isaac. The two oaths reveal two different religious standpoints under one treaty. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 54. Jacob offered sacrifice on the mountain and invited his kinsmen to eat bread, and they stayed the night there. Henry notes that peace with God puts true comfort into peace with friends. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 55. Early in the morning Laban kissed and blessed his children and grandchildren, then went home. Henry closes the chapter with the remark that God is often better to us than our fears, overruling men's spirits in our favour. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
