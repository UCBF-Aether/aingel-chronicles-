The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Matthew 5 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 And seeing the multitudes, he went up into a mountain: and when he was set, his disciples came unto him:

2 And he opened his mouth, and taught them, saying,

3 Blessed are the poor in spirit: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.

4 Blessed are they that mourn: for they shall be comforted.

5 Blessed are the meek: for they shall inherit the earth.

6 Blessed are they which do hunger and thirst after righteousness: for they shall be filled.

7 Blessed are the merciful: for they shall obtain mercy.

8 Blessed are the pure in heart: for they shall see God.

9 Blessed are the peacemakers: for they shall be called the children of God.

10 Blessed are they which are persecuted for righteousness’ sake: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.

11 Blessed are ye, when men shall revile you, and persecute you, and shall say all manner of evil against you falsely, for my sake.

12 Rejoice, and be exceeding glad: for great is your reward in heaven: for so persecuted they the prophets which were before you.

13 Ye are the salt of the earth: but if the salt have lost his savour, wherewith shall it be salted? it is thenceforth good for nothing, but to be cast out, and to be trodden under foot of men.

14 Ye are the light of the world. A city that is set on an hill cannot be hid.

15 Neither do men light a candle, and put it under a bushel, but on a candlestick; and it giveth light unto all that are in the house.

16 Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven.

17 Think not that I am come to destroy the law, or the prophets: I am not come to destroy, but to fulfil.

18 For verily I say unto you, Till heaven and earth pass, one jot or one tittle shall in no wise pass from the law, till all be fulfilled.

19 Whosoever therefore shall break one of these least commandments, and shall teach men so, he shall be called the least in the kingdom of heaven: but whosoever shall do and teach them, the same shall be called great in the kingdom of heaven.

20 For I say unto you, That except your righteousness shall exceed the righteousness of the scribes and Pharisees, ye shall in no case enter into the kingdom of heaven.

21 Ye have heard that it was said by them of old time, Thou shalt not kill; and whosoever shall kill shall be in danger of the judgment:

22 But I say unto you, That whosoever is angry with his brother without a cause shall be in danger of the judgment: and whosoever shall say to his brother, Raca, shall be in danger of the council: but whosoever shall say, Thou fool, shall be in danger of hell fire.

23 Therefore if thou bring thy gift to the altar, and there rememberest that thy brother hath ought against thee;

24 Leave there thy gift before the altar, and go thy way; first be reconciled to thy brother, and then come and offer thy gift.

25 Agree with thine adversary quickly, whiles thou art in the way with him; lest at any time the adversary deliver thee to the judge, and the judge deliver thee to the officer, and thou be cast into prison.

26 Verily I say unto thee, Thou shalt by no means come out thence, till thou hast paid the uttermost farthing.

27 Ye have heard that it was said by them of old time, Thou shalt not commit adultery:

28 But I say unto you, That whosoever looketh on a woman to lust after her hath committed adultery with her already in his heart.

29 And if thy right eye offend thee, pluck it out, and cast it from thee: for it is profitable for thee that one of thy members should perish, and not that thy whole body should be cast into hell.

30 And if thy right hand offend thee, cut it off, and cast it from thee: for it is profitable for thee that one of thy members should perish, and not that thy whole body should be cast into hell.

31 It hath been said, Whosoever shall put away his wife, let him give her a writing of divorcement:

32 But I say unto you, That whosoever shall put away his wife, saving for the cause of fornication, causeth her to commit adultery: and whosoever shall marry her that is divorced committeth adultery.

33 Again, ye have heard that it hath been said by them of old time, Thou shalt not forswear thyself, but shalt perform unto the Lord thine oaths:

34 But I say unto you, Swear not at all; neither by heaven; for it is God’s throne:

35 Nor by the earth; for it is his footstool: neither by Jerusalem; for it is the city of the great King.

36 Neither shalt thou swear by thy head, because thou canst not make one hair white or black.

37 But let your communication be, Yea, yea; Nay, nay: for whatsoever is more than these cometh of evil.

38 Ye have heard that it hath been said, An eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth:

39 But I say unto you, That ye resist not evil: but whosoever shall smite thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also.

40 And if any man will sue thee at the law, and take away thy coat, let him have thy cloak also.

41 And whosoever shall compel thee to go a mile, go with him twain.

42 Give to him that asketh thee, and from him that would borrow of thee turn not thou away.

43 Ye have heard that it hath been said, Thou shalt love thy neighbour, and hate thine enemy.

44 But I say unto you, Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you, and persecute you;

45 That ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven: for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust.

46 For if ye love them which love you, what reward have ye? do not even the publicans the same?

47 And if ye salute your brethren only, what do ye more than others? do not even the publicans so?

48 Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect.

How to read the labels

Every substantive historical claim in the editorial panels below carries one of three labels, plus a source reference:

  • Documented — directly supported by a cited source.
  • Interpreted — a reasoned explanation or proposed relationship.
  • Unresolved — evidence is insufficient to establish the claim.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

The Sermon on the Mount begins with the Beatitudes, blessing the poor in spirit, those who mourn, the meek, those who hunger for righteousness, the merciful, the pure in heart, the peacemakers, and the persecuted, and then calls the disciples the salt of the earth and the light of the world. Jesus says he came not to destroy the law but to fulfil it, and presses its demands inward: anger, lust, divorce, oaths, retaliation, and finally love of enemies and perfection as the Father in heaven is perfect. What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Seeing the multitudes, he went up into a mountain near the Sea of Galilee, and when he was set, his disciples came unto him — a large circle attracted by his preaching and miracles, besides the smaller band of devoted adherents. None will find happiness in this world or the next who do not seek it from Christ by the rule of his word. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. He opened his mouth, a solemn way of arousing attention for something weighty, and taught them. He taught what was the evil they should abhor and what the good they should seek and abound in. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. “Blessed” here points to inward happiness in a lofty sense. The poor in spirit are those who in their deepest consciousness realize their entire need — self-emptying conviction that before God we are void of everything — and theirs already is the kingdom of heaven, for the very sense of their poverty is begun riches. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. They that mourn are the complement of the poor in spirit: poverty of spirit says “I am undone,” mourning breaks forth, “Woe is me, for I am undone.” These are mourners in Zion, and they shall be comforted — even now with beauty for ashes, and at last when God wipes away all tears. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. The meek, of lowly and gentle carriage, quietly submitting to God, bearing insult and returning a soft answer, shall inherit the earth — the promise of Psalm 37:11, the land as the Old Testament evidence of God's favour. The meek are the only rightful occupants of a foot of ground here, and heirs of all coming things. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. They which hunger and thirst after righteousness crave, with the keenest of appetites, spiritual and entire conformity to God's law, the possession of which is true saintship. They shall be filled — saturated — not merely supplied, though the full satisfaction awaits the waking in his likeness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. The merciful shall obtain mercy, by what Bengel calls the gracious requital of the kingdom. The Christian stands between a mercy received and a mercy yet needed: forgiven ten thousand talents, he forgives his brother's hundred pence, and the foretastes of recompense here give way to perfection at the great white throne. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. The pure in heart shall see God. Heart purity begins in a conscience purged from dead works and a heart sprinkled from an evil conscience; with light within, the inner man looks on God with complacency, while the full beatific vision — seeing him as he is — is reserved for glory. Without holiness no man shall see the Lord. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. The peacemakers not only study peace but diffuse it, and shall be called the children of God. This beatitude alone could hardly find its ground in the Old Testament, for God as the God of peace was fully revealed only when Christ made peace by the blood of the cross; peace-receivers become peace-diffusers, and by the family likeness are recognized as his children. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. They persecuted for righteousness' sake are blessed — the eighth beatitude is supplementary, denoting merely the possessors of the seven preceding features, on account of which they are persecuted. The seven characters run counter to the spirit of the world at every point, and theirs is the kingdom of heaven, the same promise as the first. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Blessed are ye when men revile you to your face, persecute you, and say all manner of evil against you falsely, for my sake. Where he before said “for righteousness' sake,” he now identifies himself and his cause with righteousness — language no mere prophet could use, but fitting for Righteousness incarnate. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Rejoice and be exceeding glad — exult, even leap — for great is your reward in heaven, for so persecuted they the prophets before you. You do but serve yourselves heirs to their character and sufferings, and the reward will be common. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Ye are the salt of the earth, to preserve it from corruption, season its insipidity, and freshen it. Mankind under unrestrained evil nature is entirely corrupt, and the remedy is the active presence of disciples among their fellows; but if the salt lose its savour, wherewith shall it be salted? It is thenceforth good for nothing but to be cast out and trodden under foot — indignant exclusion and contempt for a profession that begets expectation and disappoints it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Ye are the light of the world — his own distinctive title, applied to disciples only as they shine with his light by his Spirit dwelling in them. Salt operates internally in the mass; sunlight operates externally, irradiating all it reaches. A city set on a hill cannot be hid, nor was it built except to be seen. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Neither do men light a lamp and put it under a bushel, a dry measure, but on a lampstand, that it may shine unto all in the house. The familiar household utensils make the point plain: light is lit to be conspicuous, not covered. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Let your light so shine before men that they may see your good works and glorify your Father in heaven. What is between God and our souls is kept to ourselves, but what is open to men's sight must suit our profession; men seeing the life of Christ's disciples glorify the Father for redeeming and transforming earth's sinful children. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. Think not I am come to destroy the law or the prophets — the authority and principles of the Old Testament. He came not to subvert or annul, but to fulfil: to establish them, unfold them, embody them in living form, and enshrine them in the reverence, affection and character of men. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. Verily I say unto you — here first occurs that august expression of supreme legislative authority — till heaven and earth pass, one jot, the smallest Hebrew letter, and one tittle, the little stroke distinguishing letters, shall in no wise pass from the law till all be fulfilled. The enduring stability of the great moral and spiritual truths of the Old Testament is thus expressed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. Whosoever shall break — dissolve or annul — one of the least commandments and teach men so shall be called least in the kingdom of heaven: not exclusion, but a degraded position in the present stage of the kingdom, retributively reduced to the dishonour to which his system brought God's law. Whoever does and teaches them shall be called great, raised to the honour to which he exalts the law. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Except your righteousness exceed the righteousness of the scribes and Pharisees, ye shall in no case enter the kingdom of heaven. The superiority required is in kind, not degree: outward, formal righteousness must give way to inward, vital, spiritual righteousness, for entrance depends solely on having the character God demands. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. Ye have heard it was said by them of old time, “Thou shalt not kill,” with whosoever kills in danger of the judgment — the inferior local courts. Thus the commandment was reduced from a holy law of the heart-searching God to a mere criminal statute taking cognizance only of outward actions, as the Jewish teachers taught that nothing but actual murder was forbidden. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. But I say unto you, in the authoritative tone of the Lawgiver himself: causeless anger is heart-murder, hatred in the bud, and “Raca,” a scornful word from pride, and “Thou fool,” a spiteful word from hatred, express degrees of contempt answerable at the divine tribunal. The three punishments — judgment, council, hell-fire, alluding to the valley of Hinnom — are gradations of divine retribution, not temporal penalties. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. Therefore, if thou bring thy gift to the altar and there remember thy brother hath aught of just complaint against thee, the application follows: Christian love and peace must be preserved, and a quarrel confessed and made up, for until this is done we are unfit for communion with God in holy ordinances. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. Leave thy gift before the altar, go thy way, first be reconciled to thy brother — get him to dismiss his grudge against thee — and then come and offer thy gift. Even before the offering is presented, reconciliation is to be sought; the early church accordingly made up differences before communion. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. Agree with thine adversary quickly, whiles thou art in the way with him to the magistrate, lest he deliver thee to the judge and the judge to the officer. While we are alive we are in the way to God's judgment-seat; after death it will be too late, so seek peace with God without delay. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. Verily, thou shalt by no means come out thence till thou hast paid the uttermost farthing, a fractional Roman coin. The language is drawn from human legal procedure but deals with a higher Adversary, a higher tribunal and a higher sentence: the offender has another party to deal with besides the man wronged on earth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. Ye have heard, “Thou shalt not commit adultery” — but the traditional perverters restricted the breach to acts of criminal intercourse between or with married persons exclusively. Christ now dissipates such delusions, showing the full spiritual meaning of the commandment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. Whosoever looketh on a woman to lust after her, with full consent of will to feed unholy desire, hath committed adultery with her already in his heart. The range extends to all forms of impurity, for the counsels that follow were intended for all, married or unmarried. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. If thy right eye, the readier and dearer, offend thee — be a trap-spring or occasion of stumbling — pluck it out with indignant promptitude, heedless of cost. It is not execution upon the bodily organ, for putting out the eyes would not quench the lust; we are to strike at the root of unholy dispositions and cut off the occasions that stimulate them, lest the whole body be cast into hell. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. If thy right hand, the organ of action to which the eye excites, offend thee, cut it off and cast it from thee, for the same reason. The repetition in identical terms of such stern truths is characteristic of our Lord's teaching; victory over the heart's desires must be attended with painful exertions, but it must be done. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 31. It hath been said, whosoever shall put away his wife, let him give her a writing of divorcement — a legal check on reckless separation, quoted from Deuteronomy 24. The one legitimate ground in that enactment was “some uncleanness,” conjugal infidelity; Shammai read it so, but Hillel stretched it to anything offensive to the husband, a view fitted to minister to caprice. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 32. But I say, whosoever puts away his wife, saving for fornication, causeth her to commit adultery, driving her into it if she marries again; and whosoever marries her that is divorced committeth adultery, for if the commandment is broken by one party it must be by the other also. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 33. Again ye have heard, thou shalt not forswear thyself, but perform unto the Lord thine oaths — not the precise words of Exodus 20:7, but what it was currently understood to condemn, false swearing. Solemn oaths in court or on proper occasions, taken with due reverence, are not hereby condemned; it is swearing in common intercourse and on frivolous occasions that is meant, for every oath carries an appeal to God whether his name is uttered or not. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 34. Swear not at all, neither by heaven, for it is God's throne (Isaiah 66:1). God's name is profaned quite as really by swearing by his throne as if his name had been uttered, because it is instantly suggested by the mention of it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 35. Nor by the earth, for it is his footstool, neither by Jerusalem, for it is the city of the great King (Psalm 48:2). The worse men are, the less they are bound by oaths; the better they are, the less need there is for them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 36. Neither shalt thou swear by thy head, because thou canst not make one hair white or black. In swearing by our own head the objection lies in its being beyond our control, profanely assumed to have a stability which it has not. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 37. Let your communication be Yea, yea; Nay, nay — a simple Yes and No sufficing in ordinary intercourse. Whatsoever is more cometh of evil: the habit of confirming words by oath springs from, and aggravates, the vicious root of untruthfulness, while a disciple's plain Yes and No soon come to be relied on more than others' solemn asseverations. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 38. Ye have heard, an eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth (Exodus 21, Leviticus 24, Deuteronomy 19). This law of retribution was designed to take vengeance out of private hands and commit it to the magistrate, but was abused in the opposite way to the Decalogue: a judicial regulation was held as warrant for taking redress into one's own hands, contrary to Proverbs 20:22 and 24:29. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 39. But I say, resist not evil: whosoever smites thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also. Christ's own meek yet dignified bearing when smitten (John 18) is the best comment: not literally inviting another blow, but a preparedness to submit meekly to a further indignity without retaliation. Suffer any injury that can be borne, for the sake of peace. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 40. If any man will sue thee at law and take away thy coat, the inner garment taken in pledge for debt, let him have thy cloak also, the outer and costlier garment which could not be retained overnight from the poor because they used it for a bed covering. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 41. Whosoever shall compel thee to go a mile, go with him twain — an allusion to Roman practice of obliging people to furnish attendance for government despatches, often at great inconvenience. The demand is readiness to submit to unreasonable demands of whatever kind rather than raise quarrels with all their resulting evils. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 42. Give to him that asketh thee, the sense of unreasonable asking being implied, and from him that would borrow of thee turn not thou away — a graphic expression of unfeeling refusal to relieve a brother in extremity. Simple borrowing is meant, usury being forbidden among the Jews, and “owe no man anything” is not absolute, else the commendations of lending to a necessitous brother would have no application. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 43. Ye have heard, thou shalt love thy neighbour (Leviticus 19:18) — to which the corrupt teachers added, “and hate thine enemy,” as if a legitimate inference, instead of a detestable gloss. The Jewish teachers understood “neighbour” only as those of their own country, nation and religion. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 44. But I say, love your enemies — moral love, the benignant, compassionate outgoings of desire for another's good — bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you and persecute you. The best commentary is the bright example of him who gave them; yet he is here only the incomparable Interpreter of the law in force from the beginning. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 45. That ye may be the children of your Father in heaven — that ye may show yourselves to be such by resembling him — for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust. Henry adds that we must render good for evil, which speaks a nobler principle than most men act by. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 46. If ye love them which love you, what reward have ye? Do not even the publicans the same? Henry's group comment presses the point: while many render good for good, Christians must render good for evil, and more is expected from the followers of Christ than from others. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 47. If ye salute your brethren only, what do ye more than others? Do not even the publicans so? Others salute their own party, way and opinion, but Christians must not so confine their respect; they must do all the real kindness they can to all, especially to their souls. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 48. Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect. It is the duty of Christians to desire, aim at, and press towards perfection in grace and holiness, conforming themselves to the example of their heavenly Father — more being expected from, and found in, the followers of Christ than in others. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry

Heart purity begins in a conscience purged from dead works and a heart sprinkled from an evil conscience; with light within, the inner man looks on God with complacency, while the full beatific vision — seeing him as he is — is reserved for glory. Without holiness no man shall see the Lord. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. The peacemakers not only study peace but diffuse it, and shall be called the children of God. This beatitude alone could hardly find its ground in the Old Testament, for God as the God of peace was fully revealed only when Christ made peace by the blood of the cross; peace-receivers become peace-diffusers, and by the family likeness are recognized as his children. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. They persecuted for righteousness' sake are blessed — the eighth beatitude is supplementary, denoting merely the possessors of the seven preceding features, on account of which they are persecuted. The seven characters run counter to the spirit of the world at every point, and theirs is the kingdom of heaven, the same promise as the first. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Blessed are ye when men revile you to your face, persecute you, and say all manner of evil against you falsely, for my sake. Where he before said “for righteousness' sake,” he now identifies himself and his cause with righteousness — language no mere prophet could use, but fitting for Righteousness incarnate. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Rejoice and be exceeding glad — exult, even leap — for great is your reward in heaven, for so persecuted they the prophets before you. You do but serve yourselves heirs to their character and sufferings, and the reward will be common. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Ye are the salt of the earth, to preserve it from corruption, season its insipidity, and freshen it. Mankind under unrestrained evil nature is entirely corrupt, and the remedy is the active presence of disciples among their fellows; but if the salt lose its savour, wherewith shall it be salted? It is thenceforth good for nothing but to be cast out and trodden under foot — indignant exclusion and contempt for a profession that begets expectation and disappoints it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Ye are the light of the world — his own distinctive title, applied to disciples only as they shine with his light by his Spirit dwelling in them. Salt operates internally in the mass; sunlight operates externally, irradiating all it reaches. A city set on a hill cannot be hid, nor was it built except to be seen. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Neither do men light a lamp and put it under a bushel, a dry measure, but on a lampstand, that it may shine unto all in the house. The familiar household utensils make the point plain: light is lit to be conspicuous, not covered. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Let your light so shine before men that they may see your good works and glorify your Father in heaven. What is between God and our souls is kept to ourselves, but what is open to men's sight must suit our profession; men seeing the life of Christ's disciples glorify the Father for redeeming and transforming earth's sinful children. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. Think not I am come to destroy the law or the prophets — the authority and principles of the Old Testament. He came not to subvert or annul, but to fulfil: to establish them, unfold them, embody them in living form, and enshrine them in the reverence, affection and character of men. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. Verily I say unto you — here first occurs that august expression of supreme legislative authority — till heaven and earth pass, one jot, the smallest Hebrew letter, and one tittle, the little stroke distinguishing letters, shall in no wise pass from the law till all be fulfilled. The enduring stability of the great moral and spiritual truths of the Old Testament is thus expressed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. Whosoever shall break — dissolve or annul — one of the least commandments and teach men so shall be called least in the kingdom of heaven: not exclusion, but a degraded position in the present stage of the kingdom, retributively reduced to the dishonour to which his system brought God's law. Whoever does and teaches them shall be called great, raised to the honour to which he exalts the law. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Except your righteousness exceed the righteousness of the scribes and Pharisees, ye shall in no case enter the kingdom of heaven. The superiority required is in kind, not degree: outward, formal righteousness must give way to inward, vital, spiritual righteousness, for entrance depends solely on having the character God demands. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. Ye have heard it was said by them of old time, “Thou shalt not kill,” with whosoever kills in danger of the judgment — the inferior local courts. Thus the commandment was reduced from a holy law of the heart-searching God to a mere criminal statute taking cognizance only of outward actions, as the Jewish teachers taught that nothing but actual murder was forbidden. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. But I say unto you, in the authoritative tone of the Lawgiver himself: causeless anger is heart-murder, hatred in the bud, and “Raca,” a scornful word from pride, and “Thou fool,” a spiteful word from hatred, express degrees of contempt answerable at the divine tribunal. The three punishments — judgment, council, hell-fire, alluding to the valley of Hinnom — are gradations of divine retribution, not temporal penalties. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. Therefore, if thou bring thy gift to the altar and there remember thy brother hath aught of just complaint against thee, the application follows: Christian love and peace must be preserved, and a quarrel confessed and made up, for until this is done we are unfit for communion with God in holy ordinances. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. Leave thy gift before the altar, go thy way, first be reconciled to thy brother — get him to dismiss his grudge against thee — and then come and offer thy gift. Even before the offering is presented, reconciliation is to be sought; the early church accordingly made up differences before communion. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. Agree with thine adversary quickly, whiles thou art in the way with him to the magistrate, lest he deliver thee to the judge and the judge to the officer. While we are alive we are in the way to God's judgment-seat; after death it will be too late, so seek peace with God without delay. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. Verily, thou shalt by no means come out thence till thou hast paid the uttermost farthing, a fractional Roman coin. The language is drawn from human legal procedure but deals with a higher Adversary, a higher tribunal and a higher sentence: the offender has another party to deal with besides the man wronged on earth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. Ye have heard, “Thou shalt not commit adultery” — but the traditional perverters restricted the breach to acts of criminal intercourse between or with married persons exclusively. Christ now dissipates such delusions, showing the full spiritual meaning of the commandment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. Whosoever looketh on a woman to lust after her, with full consent of will to feed unholy desire, hath committed adultery with her already in his heart. The range extends to all forms of impurity, for the counsels that follow were intended for all, married or unmarried. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. If thy right eye, the readier and dearer, offend thee — be a trap-spring or occasion of stumbling — pluck it out with indignant promptitude, heedless of cost. It is not execution upon the bodily organ, for putting out the eyes would not quench the lust; we are to strike at the root of unholy dispositions and cut off the occasions that stimulate them, lest the whole body be cast into hell. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. If thy right hand, the organ of action to which the eye excites, offend thee, cut it off and cast it from thee, for the same reason. The repetition in identical terms of such stern truths is characteristic of our Lord's teaching; victory over the heart's desires must be attended with painful exertions, but it must be done. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 31. It hath been said, whosoever shall put away his wife, let him give her a writing of divorcement — a legal check on reckless separation, quoted from Deuteronomy 24. The one legitimate ground in that enactment was “some uncleanness,” conjugal infidelity; Shammai read it so, but Hillel stretched it to anything offensive to the husband, a view fitted to minister to caprice. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 32. But I say, whosoever puts away his wife, saving for fornication, causeth her to commit adultery, driving her into it if she marries again; and whosoever marries her that is divorced committeth adultery, for if the commandment is broken by one party it must be by the other also. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 33. Again ye have heard, thou shalt not forswear thyself, but perform unto the Lord thine oaths — not the precise words of Exodus 20:7, but what it was currently understood to condemn, false swearing. Solemn oaths in court or on proper occasions, taken with due reverence, are not hereby condemned; it is swearing in common intercourse and on frivolous occasions that is meant, for every oath carries an appeal to God whether his name is uttered or not. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 34. Swear not at all, neither by heaven, for it is God's throne (Isaiah 66:1). God's name is profaned quite as really by swearing by his throne as if his name had been uttered, because it is instantly suggested by the mention of it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 35. Nor by the earth, for it is his footstool, neither by Jerusalem, for it is the city of the great King (Psalm 48:2). The worse men are, the less they are bound by oaths; the better they are, the less need there is for them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 36. Neither shalt thou swear by thy head, because thou canst not make one hair white or black. In swearing by our own head the objection lies in its being beyond our control, profanely assumed to have a stability which it has not. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 37. Let your communication be Yea, yea; Nay, nay — a simple Yes and No sufficing in ordinary intercourse. Whatsoever is more cometh of evil: the habit of confirming words by oath springs from, and aggravates, the vicious root of untruthfulness, while a disciple's plain Yes and No soon come to be relied on more than others' solemn asseverations. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 38. Ye have heard, an eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth (Exodus 21, Leviticus 24, Deuteronomy 19). This law of retribution was designed to take vengeance out of private hands and commit it to the magistrate, but was abused in the opposite way to the Decalogue: a judicial regulation was held as warrant for taking redress into one's own hands, contrary to Proverbs 20:22 and 24:29. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 39. But I say, resist not evil: whosoever smites thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also. Christ's own meek yet dignified bearing when smitten (John 18) is the best comment: not literally inviting another blow, but a preparedness to submit meekly to a further indignity without retaliation. Suffer any injury that can be borne, for the sake of peace. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 40. If any man will sue thee at law and take away thy coat, the inner garment taken in pledge for debt, let him have thy cloak also, the outer and costlier garment which could not be retained overnight from the poor because they used it for a bed covering. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 41. Whosoever shall compel thee to go a mile, go with him twain — an allusion to Roman practice of obliging people to furnish attendance for government despatches, often at great inconvenience. The demand is readiness to submit to unreasonable demands of whatever kind rather than raise quarrels with all their resulting evils. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 42. Give to him that asketh thee, the sense of unreasonable asking being implied, and from him that would borrow of thee turn not thou away — a graphic expression of unfeeling refusal to relieve a brother in extremity. Simple borrowing is meant, usury being forbidden among the Jews, and “owe no man anything” is not absolute, else the commendations of lending to a necessitous brother would have no application. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 43. Ye have heard, thou shalt love thy neighbour (Leviticus 19:18) — to which the corrupt teachers added, “and hate thine enemy,” as if a legitimate inference, instead of a detestable gloss. The Jewish teachers understood “neighbour” only as those of their own country, nation and religion. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 44. But I say, love your enemies — moral love, the benignant, compassionate outgoings of desire for another's good — bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you and persecute you. The best commentary is the bright example of him who gave them; yet he is here only the incomparable Interpreter of the law in force from the beginning. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 45. That ye may be the children of your Father in heaven — that ye may show yourselves to be such by resembling him — for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust. Henry adds that we must render good for evil, which speaks a nobler principle than most men act by. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Matthew Henry

  • Verse 46. If ye love them which love you, what reward have ye? Do not even the publicans the same? Henry's group comment presses the point: while many render good for good, Christians must render good for evil, and more is expected from the followers of Christ than from others. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 47. If ye salute your brethren only, what do ye more than others? Do not even the publicans so? Others salute their own party, way and opinion, but Christians must not so confine their respect; they must do all the real kindness they can to all, especially to their souls. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 48. Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect. It is the duty of Christians to desire, aim at, and press towards perfection in grace and holiness, conforming themselves to the example of their heavenly Father — more being expected from, and found in, the followers of Christ than in others. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
