The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Ezra 4 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Now when the adversaries of Judah and Benjamin heard that the children of the captivity builded the temple unto the LORD God of Israel;

2 Then they came to Zerubbabel, and to the chief of the fathers, and said unto them, Let us build with you: for we seek your God, as ye do; and we do sacrifice unto him since the days of Esarhaddon king of Assur, which brought us up hither.

3 But Zerubbabel, and Jeshua, and the rest of the chief of the fathers of Israel, said unto them, Ye have nothing to do with us to build an house unto our God; but we ourselves together will build unto the LORD God of Israel, as king Cyrus the king of Persia hath commanded us.

4 Then the people of the land weakened the hands of the people of Judah, and troubled them in building,

5 And hired counsellors against them, to frustrate their purpose, all the days of Cyrus king of Persia, even until the reign of Darius king of Persia.

6 And in the reign of Ahasuerus, in the beginning of his reign, wrote they unto him an accusation against the inhabitants of Judah and Jerusalem.

7 And in the days of Artaxerxes wrote Bishlam, Mithredath, Tabeel, and the rest of their companions, unto Artaxerxes king of Persia; and the writing of the letter was written in the Syrian tongue, and interpreted in the Syrian tongue.

8 Rehum the chancellor and Shimshai the scribe wrote a letter against Jerusalem to Artaxerxes the king in this sort:

9 Then wrote Rehum the chancellor, and Shimshai the scribe, and the rest of their companions; the Dinaites, the Apharsathchites, the Tarpelites, the Apharsites, the Archevites, the Babylonians, the Susanchites, the Dehavites, and the Elamites,

10 And the rest of the nations whom the great and noble Asnapper brought over, and set in the cities of Samaria, and the rest that are on this side the river, and at such a time.

11 This is the copy of the letter that they sent unto him, even unto Artaxerxes the king; Thy servants the men on this side the river, and at such a time.

12 Be it known unto the king, that the Jews which came up from thee to us are come unto Jerusalem, building the rebellious and the bad city, and have set up the walls thereof, and joined the foundations.

13 Be it known now unto the king, that, if this city be builded, and the walls set up again, then will they not pay toll, tribute, and custom, and so thou shalt endamage the revenue of the kings.

14 Now because we have maintenance from the king’s palace, and it was not meet for us to see the king’s dishonour, therefore have we sent and certified the king;

15 That search may be made in the book of the records of thy fathers: so shalt thou find in the book of the records, and know that this city is a rebellious city, and hurtful unto kings and provinces, and that they have moved sedition within the same of old time: for which cause was this city destroyed.

16 We certify the king that, if this city be builded again, and the walls thereof set up, by this means thou shalt have no portion on this side the river.

17 Then sent the king an answer unto Rehum the chancellor, and to Shimshai the scribe, and to the rest of their companions that dwell in Samaria, and unto the rest beyond the river, Peace, and at such a time.

18 The letter which ye sent unto us hath been plainly read before me.

19 And I commanded, and search hath been made, and it is found that this city of old time hath made insurrection against kings, and that rebellion and sedition have been made therein.

20 There have been mighty kings also over Jerusalem, which have ruled over all countries beyond the river; and toll, tribute, and custom, was paid unto them.

21 Give ye now commandment to cause these men to cease, and that this city be not builded, until another commandment shall be given from me.

22 Take heed now that ye fail not to do this: why should damage grow to the hurt of the kings?

23 Now when the copy of king Artaxerxes’ letter was read before Rehum, and Shimshai the scribe, and their companions, they went up in haste to Jerusalem unto the Jews, and made them to cease by force and power.

24 Then ceased the work of the house of God which is at Jerusalem. So it ceased unto the second year of the reign of Darius king of Persia.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

When the adversaries of Judah and Benjamin hear the returned exiles are building the temple, they offer to build with them, claiming to seek the same God — and Zerubbabel, Jeshua, and the chief fathers refuse: the house is theirs alone to build. The adversaries then weaken their hands, hire counsellors, and write letters to Artaxerxes accusing Jerusalem of rebellion, until the king orders the work to cease — and it ceases until the second year of Darius. What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. When the adversaries of Judah and Benjamin heard that the returned exiles were building the temple, they took notice at once. Henry notes opposition begins the moment God's work visibly resumes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. The adversaries offered to build with them, claiming they sought the same God and had sacrificed to him since Esarhaddon brought them up. JFB notes the offer was a snare: their worship was mixed, and union would have corrupted the work it pretended to help. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Zerubbabel, Jeshua and the chief fathers refused: 'Ye have nothing to do with us to build an house unto our God.' Henry defends the refusal: the temple was Israel's by Cyrus's commission, and a mixed building company would have made a mixed religion. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Then the people of the land weakened the hands of Judah and troubled them in building. Open help refused, the adversaries turned to discouragement, the usual second weapon against God's work. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. They hired counsellors against them to frustrate their purpose all the days of Cyrus, even until Darius. JFB notes the long, paid lobbying at court: opposition to the temple was a sustained political campaign, not a passing quarrel. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. In the beginning of Ahasuerus's reign they wrote an accusation against Judah and Jerusalem. Henry notes the accusers waited for a new king, hoping a new reign would mean a new policy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. In the days of Artaxerxes, Bishlam, Mithredath, Tabeel and their companions wrote to the king, the letter written in the Syrian tongue and interpreted in Syrian. JFB notes the language detail shows this was a formal state paper, not a private complaint. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Rehum the chancellor and Shimshai the scribe wrote a letter against Jerusalem to Artaxerxes. The highest provincial officials now lead the charge, giving private enmity the weight of office. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. The letter is signed by Rehum, Shimshai and their companions, a roll of peoples settled in Samaria: Dinaites, Apharsathchites, Tarpelites, Apharsites, Archevites, Babylonians, Susanchites, Dehavites and Elamites. JFB identifies them as the mixed colonies planted by Assyria in the empty land. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. The rest of the nations whom the great and noble Asnapper brought over and set in Samaria and beyond the river join the complaint. Henry notes the accusers boast of an Assyrian king's settlement as their title, against Israel's title from God and Cyrus. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. This is the copy of the letter sent to Artaxerxes, from his servants beyond the river. The record preserves the enemy's own words, so that the reader can judge the charge on its face. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. The charge is that the Jews come from the king to Jerusalem are building 'the rebellious and the bad city,' setting up its walls and joining its foundations. JFB notes the slander turns temple-building into city-fortifying, a political threat rather than a religious work. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. They warn that if the city is built and the walls set up, toll, tribute and custom will cease, damaging the king's revenue. Henry notes the appeal is to the king's purse, the argument most likely to move a court. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Because they have maintenance from the king's palace, they say, it is not meet for them to see the king's dishonour, so they have certified the king. JFB notes the pretended loyalty: servants of the palace pose as guardians of the king's honour while serving their own hatred. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. They urge a search in the records of his fathers, which will show Jerusalem a rebellious city, hurtful to kings and provinces, moving sedition of old time, for which it was destroyed. Henry notes the half-truth: Jerusalem had rebelled, but against Babylon, not against Persia, and its punishment was God's, not a standing treason. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. They certify that if the city is built and its walls set up, the king will have no portion beyond the river. The warning escalates from lost revenue to lost empire, the standard exaggeration of accusers. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. The king sent an answer to Rehum, Shimshai and their companions in Samaria and beyond the river: 'Peace.' Henry notes the irony: a greeting of peace introduces an order that will stop God's work by force. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. 'The letter which ye sent unto us hath been plainly read before me.' The king's answer begins with procedure, showing the accusation was formally received and read in council. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. Search was made, and it was found that Jerusalem of old time had made insurrection against kings, with rebellion and sedition therein. JFB notes the records did show revolts, but the king reads them without distinguishing the kings or the causes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Mighty kings had ruled over Jerusalem and over all countries beyond the river, to whom toll, tribute and custom were paid. The king's fear is fed by Jerusalem's former greatness, not its present weakness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. The king commands these men to cease, and the city not to be built, until another commandment is given from him. Henry notes the saving clause: the stop is not final in form, and God will use that opening in a later reign. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. 'Take heed now that ye fail not to do this: why should damage grow to the hurt of the kings?' The order presses for immediate obedience, leaving no room for appeal or delay. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. When the king's letter was read before Rehum, Shimshai and their companions, they went up in haste to Jerusalem and made the Jews cease by force and power. JFB notes the haste: enemies of God's work lose no time in executing a favourable decree. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. Then the work of the house of God at Jerusalem ceased, until the second year of Darius. Henry notes the long pause: from Artaxerxes back to Darius the narrative leaps years, and the temple stood unfinished while the people waited on God's time. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
