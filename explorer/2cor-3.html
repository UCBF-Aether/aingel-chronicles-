The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — 2 Corinthians 3 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Do we begin again to commend ourselves? or need we, as some others, epistles of commendation to you, or letters of commendation from you?

2 Ye are our epistle written in our hearts, known and read of all men:

3 Forasmuch as ye are manifestly declared to be the epistle of Christ ministered by us, written not with ink, but with the Spirit of the living God; not in tables of stone, but in fleshy tables of the heart.

4 And such trust have we through Christ to God-ward:

5 Not that we are sufficient of ourselves to think any thing as of ourselves; but our sufficiency is of God;

6 Who also hath made us able ministers of the new testament; not of the letter, but of the spirit: for the letter killeth, but the spirit giveth life.

7 But if the ministration of death, written and engraven in stones, was glorious, so that the children of Israel could not stedfastly behold the face of Moses for the glory of his countenance; which glory was to be done away:

8 How shall not the ministration of the spirit be rather glorious?

9 For if the ministration of condemnation be glory, much more doth the ministration of righteousness exceed in glory.

10 For even that which was made glorious had no glory in this respect, by reason of the glory that excelleth.

11 For if that which is done away was glorious, much more that which remaineth is glorious.

12 Seeing then that we have such hope, we use great plainness of speech:

13 And not as Moses, which put a vail over his face, that the children of Israel could not stedfastly look to the end of that which is abolished:

14 But their minds were blinded: for until this day remaineth the same vail untaken away in the reading of the old testament; which vail is done away in Christ.

15 But even unto this day, when Moses is read, the vail is upon their heart.

16 Nevertheless when it shall turn to the Lord, the vail shall be taken away.

17 Now the Lord is that Spirit: and where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is liberty.

18 But we all, with open face beholding as in a glass the glory of the Lord, are changed into the same image from glory to glory, even as by the Spirit of the LORD.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Paul asks whether he needs letters of commendation like others, since the Corinthians themselves are his letter, written not with ink but with the Spirit on fleshy tables of the heart. He contrasts the old covenant's letter, which kills and whose glory faded, with the new covenant's Spirit, which gives life, and says that where the Spirit of the Lord is there is liberty, as believers with unveiled face are changed into Christ's image from glory to glory. What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Do we begin again to commend ourselves? or need we, as some others, epistles of commendation to you, or letters of commendation from you? Henry notes Paul asks the question his critics ask, and the answer is no: he needs no letters, because Corinth itself is his letter. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Ye are our epistle written in our hearts, known and read of all men. JFB notes the Corinthians are a letter Paul carries in his heart, yet also read by all men, since their changed lives are public evidence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Forasmuch as ye are manifestly declared to be the epistle of Christ ministered by us, written not with ink, but with the Spirit of the living God; not in tables of stone, but in fleshy tables of the heart. Henry notes the contrast: Moses' law on stone, Christ's epistle by the Spirit on hearts, and Paul is only the minister who delivered it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. And such trust have we through Christ to God-ward. JFB notes the trust is through Christ and toward God: Paul's confidence about his ministry rests on neither himself nor Corinth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Not that we are sufficient of ourselves to think any thing as of ourselves; but our sufficiency is of God. Henry notes this answers chapter 2's question: no man is sufficient, but God makes ministers sufficient, and the sufficiency is his gift. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Who also has made us able ministers of the new testament; not of the letter, but of the spirit: for the letter kills, but the spirit gives life. JFB notes the new covenant's ministry is of the Spirit: the letter alone, the law without the Spirit, condemns and kills, while the Spirit gives life. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. But if the ministration of death, written and engraven in stones, was glorious, so that the children of Israel could not steadfastly behold the face of Moses for the glory of his countenance; which glory was to be done away. Henry notes even the killing ministry had glory, shining on Moses' face, though it was a passing glory. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. How shall not the ministration of the spirit be rather glorious? JFB notes the argument from the lesser to the greater: if death's ministry shone, life's ministry must shine far more. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. For if the ministration of condemnation be glory, much more does the ministration of righteousness exceed in glory. Henry notes the two ministries are named by their effects: condemnation and righteousness, and righteousness exceeds as life exceeds death. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. For even that which was made glorious had no glory in this respect, by reason of the glory that excels. JFB notes the old glory is eclipsed, not denied: beside the gospel's glory it counts as no glory, as a candle beside the sun. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. For if that which is done away was glorious, much more that which remains is glorious. Henry notes permanence is the final contrast: what is abolished was glorious, but what remains is glorious for ever. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Seeing then that we have such hope, we use great plainness of speech. JFB notes hope produces plainness: ministers of a remaining glory need no veil and no hiding. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. And not as Moses, which put a vail over his face, that the children of Israel could not steadfastly look to the end of that which is abolished. Henry notes Moses veiled a fading glory so Israel could not see its end; the gospel ministry unveils what lasts. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. But their minds were blinded: for until this day remains the same vail untaken away in the reading of the old testament; which vail is done away in Christ. JFB notes the veil has moved from Moses' face to the readers' minds: it lifts only in Christ, when the old testament is read through him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. But even unto this day, when Moses is read, the vail is upon their heart. Henry notes the veil is now on the heart, not the page: the words are read, but their meaning is hidden until the heart turns. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Nevertheless when it shall turn to the Lord, the vail shall be taken away. JFB notes the remedy is a turning, not a new argument: when the heart turns to the Lord, the veil that hid the old covenant falls. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. Now the Lord is that Spirit: and where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is liberty. Henry notes liberty is the Spirit's mark: where he rules, there is freedom from the letter's bondage and from the veil's blindness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. But we all, with open face beholding as in a glass the glory of the Lord, are changed into the same image from glory to glory, even as by the Spirit of the LORD. JFB notes the beholding itself transforms: open-faced, in a mirror, believers are changed into Christ's image, glory by glory, by the Spirit's work. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Unfamiliar terms

Plain-language glosses for words and phrases in this chapter that a modern reader may find unfamiliar. They are editorial aids, not Scripture.

  • epistles of commendation. Letters of recommendation carried by travelling teachers; Paul says he needs none for Corinth. Interpreted Chapter text and study notes for this chapter
  • fleshy tables. Tables of the heart, as opposed to tables of stone; Paul's image for the Spirit's writing in believers. Interpreted Chapter text and study notes for this chapter
  • veil. The covering Moses put over his face, and, for Paul, the covering over hearts that is removed in Christ. Interpreted Chapter text and study notes for this chapter
