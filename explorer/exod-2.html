Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Exodus 2 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 And there went a man of the house of Levi, and took to wife a daughter of Levi.

2 And the woman conceived, and bare a son: and when she saw him that he was a goodly child, she hid him three months.

3 And when she could not longer hide him, she took for him an ark of bulrushes, and daubed it with slime and with pitch, and put the child therein; and she laid it in the flags by the river’s brink.

4 And his sister stood afar off, to wit what would be done to him.

5 And the daughter of Pharaoh came down to wash herself at the river; and her maidens walked along by the river’s side; and when she saw the ark among the flags, she sent her maid to fetch it.

6 And when she had opened it, she saw the child: and, behold, the babe wept. And she had compassion on him, and said, This is one of the Hebrews’ children.

7 Then said his sister to Pharaoh’s daughter, Shall I go and call to thee a nurse of the Hebrew women, that she may nurse the child for thee?

8 And Pharaoh’s daughter said to her, Go. And the maid went and called the child’s mother.

9 And Pharaoh’s daughter said unto her, Take this child away, and nurse it for me, and I will give thee thy wages. And the woman took the child, and nursed it.

10 And the child grew, and she brought him unto Pharaoh’s daughter, and he became her son. And she called his name Moses: and she said, Because I drew him out of the water.

11 And it came to pass in those days, when Moses was grown, that he went out unto his brethren, and looked on their burdens: and he spied an Egyptian smiting an Hebrew, one of his brethren.

12 And he looked this way and that way, and when he saw that there was no man, he slew the Egyptian, and hid him in the sand.

13 And when he went out the second day, behold, two men of the Hebrews strove together: and he said to him that did the wrong, Wherefore smitest thou thy fellow?

14 And he said, Who made thee a prince and a judge over us? intendest thou to kill me, as thou killedst the Egyptian? And Moses feared, and said, Surely this thing is known.

15 Now when Pharaoh heard this thing, he sought to slay Moses. But Moses fled from the face of Pharaoh, and dwelt in the land of Midian: and he sat down by a well.

16 Now the priest of Midian had seven daughters: and they came and drew water, and filled the troughs to water their father’s flock.

17 And the shepherds came and drove them away: but Moses stood up and helped them, and watered their flock.

18 And when they came to Reuel their father, he said, How is it that ye are come so soon to day?

19 And they said, An Egyptian delivered us out of the hand of the shepherds, and also drew water enough for us, and watered the flock.

20 And he said unto his daughters, And where is he? why is it that ye have left the man? call him, that he may eat bread.

21 And Moses was content to dwell with the man: and he gave Moses Zipporah his daughter.

22 And she bare him a son, and he called his name Gershom: for he said, I have been a stranger in a strange land.

23 And it came to pass in process of time, that the king of Egypt died: and the children of Israel sighed by reason of the bondage, and they cried, and their cry came up unto God by reason of the bondage.

24 And God heard their groaning, and God remembered his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob.

25 And God looked upon the children of Israel, and God had respect unto them.

How to read the labels

Every substantive historical claim in the editorial panels below carries one of three labels, plus a source reference:

  • Documented — directly supported by a cited source.
  • Interpreted — a reasoned explanation or proposed relationship.
  • Unresolved — evidence is insufficient to establish the claim.

A claim does NOT become Documented merely because a commentary states it. Commentary views are attributed to their authors and labeled Interpreted unless a primary textual source directly supports the claim. Where evidence runs out, the page says Unresolved and stops — it does not fill the gap.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

A Levite couple hide their baby son, then set him in a basket of bulrushes by the river, where Pharaoh's daughter finds and adopts him. Grown, Moses kills an Egyptian who is beating a Hebrew, flees to Midian after the deed is known, marries Zipporah, and tends Jethro's flock. When Israel groans under bondage, God hears, remembers his covenant, and looks upon them. Interpreted Plain-language summary of the chapter text, checked against the study notes for this chapter

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. A man of Levi married a daughter of Levi, so Moses was born of a priestly line on both sides. Henry notes that God raised his deliverer from the very tribe that would afterward serve at the altar. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. The mother hid her son three months because she saw he was a goodly child. JFB reads her faith as seeing in the child's beauty a pledge of God's purpose, while Henry stresses that parental love here served providence, not mere fondness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. When hiding failed, she set the child in an ark of bulrushes daubed with slime and pitch by the river's brink. Henry observes that faith did its utmost and then committed the child to God, rather than sitting idle under Pharaoh's edict. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. His sister stood afar off to watch what would be done to him. JFB notes Miriam's watchful station by the river, the quiet human means by which providence guided the child into Pharaoh's own house. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Pharaoh's daughter came to wash at the river and saw the ark among the flags. Henry marks the providence: the very house that ordered the slaughter became the shelter of the deliverer. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 6. Opening the ark, she saw the weeping babe and had compassion, knowing him to be a Hebrew child. JFB notes that compassion overcame both her father's edict and the prejudice against a Hebrew infant. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. The sister offered to call a Hebrew nurse, a proposal the princess accepted. Henry observes how providence put the very words into a child's mouth that secured Moses a godly nursing. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 8. The maiden fetched the child's own mother, so Moses was nursed by her who had risked all for him. JFB notes the irony and mercy: Pharaoh's house paid the mother to raise the child Pharaoh had condemned. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Pharaoh's daughter hired the mother to nurse the child, promising wages. Henry draws the lesson that God often makes even enemies pay for the upbringing of his servants. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 10. When the child grew, his mother brought him to Pharaoh's daughter, who adopted him and named him Moses, because she drew him out of the water. JFB notes the Egyptian name fits the deliverance, and that Moses thus learned both Hebrew faith at home and Egyptian learning at court. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Grown to manhood, Moses went out to his brethren and saw their burdens. Henry reads this as the first stirrings of his calling: he refused court ease to share his people's affliction. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 12. Seeing an Egyptian smiting a Hebrew, Moses slew the Egyptian and hid him in the sand. JFB and Henry agree his zeal was right in its aim but rash in its manner, done without a call or commission and therefore hidden rather than owned. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. The next day Moses found two Hebrews striving and asked the wrongdoer why he smote his fellow. Henry notes the sad sight: the oppressed quarrelling among themselves, a wound to their common cause. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 14. The offender retorted, asking who made Moses a prince and judge, and whether he would kill him as he killed the Egyptian. JFB observes that Israel's rejection of Moses here foreshadowed their later rejection of him as deliverer. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Moses feared, for the thing was surely known, and Pharaoh sought to slay him. Henry notes that a good deed done in a wrong way brings fear rather than peace, and Moses fled from Egypt. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 16. Reuel, priest of Midian, had seven daughters who came to draw water and fill the troughs for their father's flock. Henry notes the daughters' industry in a shepherd's household, a humble station for the future lawgiver's kin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 17. When shepherds drove the maidens away, Moses stood up, helped them, and watered their flock. JFB sees the same generous temper that had defended the Hebrew now defending strangers, and Henry notes that helpfulness opened his way in Midian. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. The daughters returned early to Reuel, who asked why they had come so soon. Their report of an Egyptian who delivered them set up Moses's introduction to the household. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 19. They told their father an Egyptian delivered them from the shepherds and drew water for the flock. Henry notes Moses was known by his dress as an Egyptian, though his heart was wholly Hebrew. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 20. Reuel asked why they had left the man behind and bade them call him to eat bread. JFB notes eastern hospitality here rewarded the stranger, and Henry observes that small kindnesses often open great doors. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 21. Moses was content to dwell with Reuel, who gave him Zipporah his daughter to wife. Henry notes Moses settled into a shepherd's life, the very training God would use for leading Israel. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 22. Zipporah bore a son whom Moses named Gershom, for he had been a stranger in a strange land. JFB notes the name kept his exile and his hope together: a stranger here, but heir of a promise elsewhere. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. In process of time the king of Egypt died, yet Israel's bondage continued and they sighed and cried under it. Henry notes that a change of princes brought no change of oppression, driving the people from man to God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 24. God heard their groaning and remembered his covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. JFB stresses that God's remembering is not forgetfulness repaired but covenant faithfulness acting in its season. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. God looked upon Israel and had respect unto them. Henry closes the chapter on this comfort: the people's cry reached heaven, and God's eye was already on their deliverance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry

Unfamiliar terms

  • ark of bulrushes. A small basket or chest woven from reeds, daubed with slime and pitch to keep water out; not the great ark of Noah. Interpreted Study note on verse 3, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
