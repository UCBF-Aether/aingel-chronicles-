The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 31 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 I made a covenant with mine eyes; why then should I think upon a maid?

2 For what portion of God is there from above? and what inheritance of the Almighty from on high?

3 Is not destruction to the wicked? and a strange punishment to the workers of iniquity?

4 Doth not he see my ways, and count all my steps?

5 If I have walked with vanity, or if my foot hath hasted to deceit;

6 Let me be weighed in an even balance that God may know mine integrity.

7 If my step hath turned out of the way, and mine heart walked after mine eyes, and if any blot hath cleaved to mine hands;

8 Then let me sow, and let another eat; yea, let my offspring be rooted out.

9 If mine heart have been deceived by a woman, or if I have laid wait at my neighbour’s door;

10 Then let my wife grind unto another, and let others bow down upon her.

11 For this is an heinous crime; yea, it is an iniquity to be punished by the judges.

12 For it is a fire that consumeth to destruction, and would root out all mine increase.

13 If I did despise the cause of my manservant or of my maidservant, when they contended with me;

14 What then shall I do when God riseth up? and when he visiteth, what shall I answer him?

15 Did not he that made me in the womb make him? and did not one fashion us in the womb?

16 If I have withheld the poor from their desire, or have caused the eyes of the widow to fail;

17 Or have eaten my morsel myself alone, and the fatherless hath not eaten thereof;

18 (For from my youth he was brought up with me, as with a father, and I have guided her from my mother’s womb;)

19 If I have seen any perish for want of clothing, or any poor without covering;

20 If his loins have not blessed me, and if he were not warmed with the fleece of my sheep;

21 If I have lifted up my hand against the fatherless, when I saw my help in the gate:

22 Then let mine arm fall from my shoulder blade, and mine arm be broken from the bone.

23 For destruction from God was a terror to me, and by reason of his highness I could not endure.

24 If I have made gold my hope, or have said to the fine gold, Thou art my confidence;

25 If I rejoice because my wealth was great, and because mine hand had gotten much;

26 If I beheld the sun when it shined, or the moon walking in brightness;

27 And my heart hath been secretly enticed, or my mouth hath kissed my hand:

28 This also were an iniquity to be punished by the judge: for I should have denied the God that is above.

29 If I rejoice at the destruction of him that hated me, or lifted up myself when evil found him:

30 Neither have I suffered my mouth to sin by wishing a curse to his soul.

31 If the men of my tabernacle said not, Oh that we had of his flesh! we cannot be satisfied.

32 The stranger did not lodge in the street: but I opened my doors to the traveller.

33 If I covered my transgressions as Adam, by hiding mine iniquity in my bosom:

34 Did I fear a great multitude, or did the contempt of families terrify me, that I kept silence, and went not out of the door?

35 Oh that one would hear me! behold, my desire is, that the Almighty would answer me, and that mine adversary had written a book.

36 Surely I would take it upon my shoulder, and bind it as a crown to me.

37 I would declare unto him the number of my steps; as a prince would I go near unto him.

38 If my land cry against me, or that the furrows likewise thereof complain;

39 If I have eaten the fruits thereof without money, or have caused the owners thereof to lose their life:

40 Let thistles grow instead of wheat, and cockle instead of barley. The words of Job are ended.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Job closes his defence with a long oath of innocence, inviting God to weigh him in an even balance: he made a covenant with his eyes, walked not in vanity, kept his servants’ cause, fed the poor, clothed the naked, trusted not in gold, and did not rejoice at his enemy’s fall. If he covered his transgressions as Adam, hiding iniquity in his bosom, let his adversary write a book against him — he would carry it on his shoulder as a crown and declare to God the number of his steps. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Job made a covenant with his eyes; why then should he think upon a maid? JFB explains think as casting a lustful look, which Job put out of the question by covenanting with his eyes against temptation, asserting his guard against being allured to sin by his senses. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. For what portion of God is there from above, and what inheritance of the Almighty from on high? JFB explains that had Job let his senses tempt him to sin, this is the portion he must have expected from God above. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Is not destruction to the wicked, and a strange punishment to the workers of iniquity? JFB calls this the answer to verse 2: strange as extraordinary, the expected lot of such sin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Does not God see Job's ways, and count all his steps? JFB notes that knowing this, Job could only have expected destruction had he committed this sin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. If Job has walked with vanity, or his foot has hasted to deceit, let God weigh him. JFB explains vanity as falsehood, in Job's abstinence from evil deeds. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Let God weigh Job in an even balance, that God may know his integrity. JFB renders it parenthetically: oh that God would weigh me, then would he know. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. If Job's step has turned out of the way, and his heart walked after his eyes, and any blot has cleaved to his hands. JFB connects this with verse 5, explaining the way as God's way, and heart after eyes as coveting what the eyes beheld. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Then let Job sow, and another eat, and his offspring be rooted out. JFB calls this the apodosis to verses 5 and 7, the curses he imprecates on himself if he did these things, with offspring as what he plants, his harvests. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. If Job's heart has been deceived by a woman, or he has laid wait at his neighbour's door. JFB introduces Job's assertion of innocence of adultery, with deceived as seduced, and laid wait until the husband went out. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Then let Job's wife grind unto another, and let others bow down upon her. JFB explains grind as turning the handmill, becoming the most abject slave and concubine. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. For this is a heinous crime, yea, an iniquity to be punished by the judges. JFB notes adultery was punished with death in earliest times and later, a sin against the community needing the judge's cognizance, whereas before Job spoke of sins against conscience. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. For it is a fire that consumes to destruction, and would root out all Job's increase. JFB notes no crime more provokes God to send destruction as consuming fire, and none so desolates the soul. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. If Job has despised the cause of his manservant or maidservant when they contended with him. JFB introduces Job's freedom from unfairness to servants and harshness to the needy, with despise as refusing to do them justice. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. What then shall Job do when God rises up, and when he visits, what shall he answer him? JFB explains parenthetically: had he despised his servants, what could he do when God arose to call him to account, and what answer when God visited to enquire? Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Did not he that made Job in the womb make his servant, and did not one fashion them in the womb? JFB notes slaveholders defend themselves by original inferiority, but common origin is the argument for brotherly love from master to servant. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. If Job has withheld the poor from their desire, or caused the eyes of the widow to fail. JFB explains fail as in vain expectation of relief. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. Or eaten his morsel himself alone, and the fatherless has not eaten thereof. JFB notes Arabian hospitality requires the stranger to be helped first and to the best, which Job did not violate. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. For from Job's youth the fatherless was brought up with him as with a father, and he guided the widow from his mother's womb. JFB explains parenthetically that he did the contrary to verses 16-17, with he as the orphan, and guided her as the widow, by advice and protection. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. If Job has seen any perish for want of clothing, or any poor without covering. JFB explains perish as ready to perish, the perishing man Job clothed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. If his loins have not blessed Job, and if he has not been warmed with the fleece of his sheep. JFB explains loins poetically as thanking him: the loins before naked, when clad by him, wished him every blessing. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. If Job has lifted up his hand against the fatherless, when he saw his help in the gate. JFB explains when as because: he might calculate on a powerful party's help in the court at the gate if summoned by the injured fatherless. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Then let Job's arm fall from his shoulder blade, and his arm be broken from the bone. JFB calls this the apodosis to verses 13 and 16-21, alluding to Eliphaz's charge, with the first arm as shoulder and the second as forearm, broken from the bone or reed, the upper arm above the elbow. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. For destruction from God was a terror to Job, and by reason of his highness he could not endure. JFB explains this as the reason Job guarded against such sins: fear of God, though he could escape man's judgment, with highness as majestic might, against which he could avail nothing. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. If Job has made gold his hope, or said to the fine gold, you are my confidence. JFB introduces Job's freedom from trust in money, turning to duty toward God after duty toward himself and neighbour, since covetousness is covert idolatry transferring the heart from Creator to creature. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. If Job rejoiced because his wealth was great, and because his hand had gotten much. Trust in money is tested not only by hope placed in it but by rejoicing in its increase. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. If Job beheld the sun when it shined, or the moon walking in brightness. JFB explains Sabaism, worship of the heavenly hosts, the earliest false worship: sun, moon, and stars as visible representatives of the invisible God, worshipped on high places and roofs, with light worshipped as emanation before its embodiments. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. And Job's heart has been secretly enticed, or his mouth has kissed his hand. JFB explains enticed as away from God to idolatry, and kissing the hand as adoration literally: worshippers kissed the hand and threw the kiss toward the object of worship. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. This also were an iniquity to be punished by the judge, for Job would have denied the God above. JFB notes the Mosaic law later embodied the godly feeling against idolatry as treason against the Supreme King, deserving judicial penalties. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. If Job rejoiced at the destruction of him that hated him, or lifted up himself when evil found him. JFB explains lifted up as malicious triumph. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. Neither has Job suffered his mouth to sin by wishing a curse to his soul. JFB explains mouth literally as palate, and wishing as demanding his enemy's life by a curse, confirming chapter 6, with Job realizing the Gospel spirit before the law. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 31. If the men of Job's tabernacle said not, oh that we had of his flesh, we cannot be satisfied. JFB explains the household's wish to devour Job's enemy, which Job refrained from even wishing, and better as who was not satisfied with the meat Job provided, since he never let a poor man leave his gate unfed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 32. The stranger did not lodge in the street, but Job opened his doors to the traveller. JFB explains traveller literally as way, including wayfarers of every kind. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 33. If Job covered his transgressions as Adam, by hiding his iniquity in his bosom. JFB explains Adam as the representative of fallen man's propensity to concealment, with the same word for hiding used of Adam in Genesis 3, and Job not hiding his iniquity from God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 34. Did Job fear a great multitude, or did the contempt of families terrify him, that he kept silence and went not out of the door? JFB renders it as the apodosis: then let him be fear-stricken before a multitude, the just retribution for hiding sin from God being exposure before man, which Job had not suffered, being esteemed in assemblies. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 35. Oh that one would hear Job! Behold his desire is that the Almighty would answer him, and that his adversary had written a book. JFB explains his desire as his mark of subscription, originally a cross or Tau, and adversary as whoever judicially opposes him, with written a book as writing down his charge. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 36. Surely Job would take it upon his shoulder, and bind it as a crown to him. JFB notes that far from hiding the adversary's charge through fear, he would take it as public honour on his shoulder, and as a crown of distinction, not shame. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 37. Job would declare unto him the number of his steps, and as a prince would he go near unto him. JFB notes a good conscience imparts princely dignity before man and free assurance in approaching God, realized only through Christ, not in Job's way. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 38. If Job's land cries against him, or its furrows likewise complain. JFB explains the personification: complaints of unjustly ousted proprietors transferred to the lands themselves, if Job unjustly acquired lands or paid not the labourer for tillage and fruits. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 39. If Job has eaten its fruits without money, or caused the owners thereof to lose their life. JFB explains lose life not literally but harassed to death, until the owner gave up land gratis, or languished by loss of livelihood. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 40. Let thistles grow instead of wheat, and cockle instead of barley. The words of Job are ended. The self-curse closes his defence: if guilty of land wrong, let the land itself bear weeds instead of crops, and with this his words end. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
