The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Romans 14 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Him that is weak in the faith receive ye, but not to doubtful disputations.

2 For one believeth that he may eat all things: another, who is weak, eateth herbs.

3 Let not him that eateth despise him that eateth not; and let not him which eateth not judge him that eateth: for God hath received him.

4 Who art thou that judgest another man’s servant? to his own master he standeth or falleth. Yea, he shall be holden up: for God is able to make him stand.

5 One man esteemeth one day above another: another esteemeth every day alike. Let every man be fully persuaded in his own mind.

6 He that regardeth the day, regardeth it unto the Lord; and he that regardeth not the day, to the Lord he doth not regard it. He that eateth, eateth to the Lord, for he giveth God thanks; and he that eateth not, to the Lord he eateth not, and giveth God thanks.

7 For none of us liveth to himself, and no man dieth to himself.

8 For whether we live, we live unto the Lord; and whether we die, we die unto the Lord: whether we live therefore, or die, we are the Lord’s.

9 For to this end Christ both died, and rose, and revived, that he might be Lord both of the dead and living.

10 But why dost thou judge thy brother? or why dost thou set at nought thy brother? for we shall all stand before the judgment seat of Christ.

11 For it is written, As I live, saith the Lord, every knee shall bow to me, and every tongue shall confess to God.

12 So then every one of us shall give account of himself to God.

13 Let us not therefore judge one another any more: but judge this rather, that no man put a stumblingblock or an occasion to fall in his brother’s way.

14 I know, and am persuaded by the Lord Jesus, that there is nothing unclean of itself: but to him that esteemeth any thing to be unclean, to him it is unclean.

15 But if thy brother be grieved with thy meat, now walkest thou not charitably. Destroy not him with thy meat, for whom Christ died.

16 Let not then your good be evil spoken of:

17 For the kingdom of God is not meat and drink; but righteousness, and peace, and joy in the Holy Ghost.

18 For he that in these things serveth Christ is acceptable to God, and approved of men.

19 Let us therefore follow after the things which make for peace, and things wherewith one may edify another.

20 For meat destroy not the work of God. All things indeed are pure; but it is evil for that man who eateth with offence.

21 It is good neither to eat flesh, nor to drink wine, nor any thing whereby thy brother stumbleth, or is offended, or is made weak.

22 Hast thou faith? have it to thyself before God. Happy is he that condemneth not himself in that thing which he alloweth.

23 And he that doubteth is damned if he eat, because he eateth not of faith: for whatsoever is not of faith is sin.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Paul tells the strong to receive the weak in faith without judging over food and holy days, since each stands or falls to his own master, and every one of us shall give account of himself to God. The kingdom of God is not meat and drink but righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit, so believers should pursue what makes for peace and not destroy, with food, the brother for whom Christ died. What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Receive him who is weak in the faith, but not to doubtful disputations. Henry notes that the weak are to be welcomed, not interrogated into disputes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. One believes he may eat all things; another, who is weak, eats herbs. JFB stresses that both are believers; their difference is about conscience, not about Christ. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Let not him who eats despise him who eats not, and let not him who eats not judge him who eats, for God has received him. Henry observes that God has received both, so neither may reject the other. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Who are you to judge another man's servant? To his own master he stands or falls, and he shall be held up, for God is able to make him stand. JFB notes that the servant answers to his own master, not to a fellow servant. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. One man esteems one day above another; another esteems every day alike; let every man be fully persuaded in his own mind. Henry stresses that in indifferent matters conscience, fully persuaded, must rule. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. He who regards the day regards it to the Lord, and he who regards it not does so to the Lord; likewise he who eats eats to the Lord, giving thanks, and he who eats not does so to the Lord. JFB notes that both parties act for the Lord, which sanctifies their difference. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. None of us lives to himself, and no man dies to himself. Henry observes that the Christian life is not private property; it belongs to the Lord. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Whether we live, we live to the Lord, and whether we die, we die to the Lord; whether we live or die, we are the Lord's. JFB notes that life and death alike are lived under Christ's lordship. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. To this end Christ both died and rose and revived, that he might be Lord both of the dead and the living. Henry stresses that Christ's lordship spans both worlds. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Why do you judge your brother, or why do you set at nought your brother? For we shall all stand before Christ's judgment seat. JFB notes that a coming common judgment forbids present private judging. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. As it is written, as I live, says the Lord, every knee shall bow to me, and every tongue shall confess to God. Henry observes that universal confession will vindicate God's judgment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Every one of us shall give account of himself to God. JFB stresses the personal account: each answers for himself, not for his brother. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Let us not judge one another any more, but judge this rather, that no man put a stumbling block or occasion to fall in his brother's way. Henry notes the better judgment: to judge one's own conduct lest it trip another. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Paul knows and is persuaded by the Lord Jesus that nothing is unclean of itself, but to him who esteems anything unclean, to him it is unclean. JFB stresses that conscience makes a thing unclean to the man who believes it so. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. If your brother is grieved with your meat, you no longer walk charitably; do not destroy with your meat him for whom Christ died. Henry observes that love outweighs liberty when a brother's soul is at stake. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Let not your good be evil spoken of. JFB notes that even lawful liberty, used unlovingly, brings reproach on the gospel. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. God's kingdom is not meat and drink, but righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Ghost. Henry stresses that the kingdom's substance is inward and spiritual, not dietary. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. He who in these things serves Christ is acceptable to God and approved of men. JFB notes that kingdom service wins both God's acceptance and men's approval. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. Let us follow things that make for peace, and things that edify one another. Henry observes that peace and edification are the twin aims of Christian conduct. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. For meat do not destroy God's work; all things indeed are pure, but it is evil for the man who eats with offence. JFB notes that a pure thing becomes evil when it offends a brother. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. It is good neither to eat flesh, nor to drink wine, nor anything by which your brother stumbles, is offended, or made weak. Henry stresses that abstaining for a brother's sake is better than exercising liberty. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Have you faith? Have it to yourself before God. Happy is he who does not condemn himself in what he allows. JFB notes that a settled conscience before God is the happy man's possession. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. He who doubts is damned if he eats, because he eats not of faith, for whatever is not of faith is sin. Henry observes that acting against conscience, even in indifferent things, is sin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Unfamiliar terms

Plain-language glosses for words and phrases in this chapter that a modern reader may find unfamiliar. They are editorial aids, not Scripture.

  • weak in the faith. The believer whose conscience restricts what he eats or which days he observes; Paul says to receive him, not dispute him. Interpreted Chapter text and study notes for this chapter
  • doubtful disputations. Arguments over doubtful matters; Paul says the weak are to be received, but not to such disputations. Interpreted Chapter text and study notes for this chapter
  • judgment seat of Christ. The place where, Paul says, every one of us shall give account of himself to God. Interpreted Chapter text and study notes for this chapter
