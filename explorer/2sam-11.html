The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — 2 Samuel 11 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 And it came to pass, after the year was expired, at the time when kings go forth to battle, that David sent Joab, and his servants with him, and all Israel; and they destroyed the children of Ammon, and besieged Rabbah. But David tarried still at Jerusalem.

2 And it came to pass in an eveningtide, that David arose from off his bed, and walked upon the roof of the king’s house: and from the roof he saw a woman washing herself; and the woman was very beautiful to look upon.

3 And David sent and enquired after the woman. And one said, Is not this Bathsheba, the daughter of Eliam, the wife of Uriah the Hittite?

4 And David sent messengers, and took her; and she came in unto him, and he lay with her; for she was purified from her uncleanness: and she returned unto her house.

5 And the woman conceived, and sent and told David, and said, I am with child.

6 And David sent to Joab, saying, Send me Uriah the Hittite. And Joab sent Uriah to David.

7 And when Uriah was come unto him, David demanded of him how Joab did, and how the people did, and how the war prospered.

8 And David said to Uriah, Go down to thy house, and wash thy feet. And Uriah departed out of the king’s house, and there followed him a mess of meat from the king.

9 But Uriah slept at the door of the king’s house with all the servants of his lord, and went not down to his house.

10 And when they had told David, saying, Uriah went not down unto his house, David said unto Uriah, Camest thou not from thy journey? why then didst thou not go down unto thine house?

11 And Uriah said unto David, The ark, and Israel, and Judah, abide in tents; and my lord Joab, and the servants of my lord, are encamped in the open fields; shall I then go into mine house, to eat and to drink, and to lie with my wife? as thou livest, and as thy soul liveth, I will not do this thing.

12 And David said to Uriah, Tarry here to day also, and to morrow I will let thee depart. So Uriah abode in Jerusalem that day, and the morrow.

13 And when David had called him, he did eat and drink before him; and he made him drunk: and at even he went out to lie on his bed with the servants of his lord, but went not down to his house.

14 And it came to pass in the morning, that David wrote a letter to Joab, and sent it by the hand of Uriah.

15 And he wrote in the letter, saying, Set ye Uriah in the forefront of the hottest battle, and retire ye from him, that he may be smitten, and die.

16 And it came to pass, when Joab observed the city, that he assigned Uriah unto a place where he knew that valiant men were.

17 And the men of the city went out, and fought with Joab: and there fell some of the people of the servants of David; and Uriah the Hittite died also.

18 Then Joab sent and told David all the things concerning the war;

19 And charged the messenger, saying, When thou hast made an end of telling the matters of the war unto the king,

20 And if so be that the king’s wrath arise, and he say unto thee, Wherefore approached ye so nigh unto the city when ye did fight? knew ye not that they would shoot from the wall?

21 Who smote Abimelech the son of Jerubbesheth? did not a woman cast a piece of a millstone upon him from the wall, that he died in Thebez? why went ye nigh the wall? then say thou, Thy servant Uriah the Hittite is dead also.

22 So the messenger went, and came and shewed David all that Joab had sent him for.

23 And the messenger said unto David, Surely the men prevailed against us, and came out unto us into the field, and we were upon them even unto the entering of the gate.

24 And the shooters shot from off the wall upon thy servants; and some of the king’s servants be dead, and thy servant Uriah the Hittite is dead also.

25 Then David said unto the messenger, Thus shalt thou say unto Joab, Let not this thing displease thee, for the sword devoureth one as well as another: make thy battle more strong against the city, and overthrow it: and encourage thou him.

26 And when the wife of Uriah heard that Uriah her husband was dead, she mourned for her husband.

27 And when the mourning was past, David sent and fetched her to his house, and she became his wife, and bare him a son. But the thing that David had done displeased the LORD.

How to read the labels

Every substantive historical claim in the editorial panels below carries one of three labels, plus a source reference:

  • Documented — directly supported by a cited source.
  • Interpreted — a reasoned explanation or proposed relationship.
  • Unresolved — evidence is insufficient to establish the claim.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

While his army is away at war, David sees Bathsheba, takes her, and she conceives; to cover it he recalls her husband Uriah from the front, and when Uriah refuses the comforts of home, David has Joab place him where the fighting is fiercest so he is killed. The chapter ends with David taking Bathsheba as his wife, and the LORD's displeasure hanging over the deed. Interpreted Plain-language summary of the chapter text and its study notes

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. At the time when kings go forth to battle, David sends Joab and all Israel against Ammon and besieges Rabbah, but David tarries still at Jerusalem. Henry traces the sin to neglect of business: when we are out of the way of our duty, we are in the way of temptation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 2. In the evening David rises from his bed, walks on the roof, and sees a woman washing herself, very beautiful to look upon. Love of ease gave the tempter advantage, Henry notes, and a wandering eye let the sin in, as Eve's sin came in at the eye. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 3. David sends and enquires after the woman, and is told she is Bathsheba, daughter of Eliam, wife of Uriah the Hittite. The enquiry is the first step downhill; when men begin to do evil, Henry warns, they cannot soon stop. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 4. David sends messengers and takes her, lies with her, and she returns to her house. Henry counts the aggravations: David, a king entrusted with justice, wrongs Uriah, one of his own worthies now hazarding his life in the field, and Bathsheba, a lady of good reputation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 5. The woman conceives and sends to tell David, I am with child. The hidden work of darkness is coming to light, and Bathsheba's message puts David under pressure to protect her and conceal what was done. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 6. David sends to Joab, saying, Send me Uriah the Hittite, and Joab sends him. The plan is to father the child upon Uriah himself, so that no prosecutor remains to expose the wrong. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 7. When Uriah comes, David demands how Joab, the people, and the war prosper. The pretended concern for the army covers a design that has nothing to do with the war. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 8. David tells Uriah to go down to his house and wash his feet, and sends a mess of meat after him from the king. The gift is meant to make the night at home pleasant and complete the cover. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 9. But Uriah sleeps at the door of the king's house with his lord's servants and does not go down to his house. His firm resolution defeats the plot, and Providence, Henry says, keeps him to it so the design's failure may awaken David's conscience. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 10. Told Uriah did not go home, David asks why, since he came from his journey. The king's question exposes how much he needs Uriah to do the ordinary thing. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 11. Uriah answers that the ark, Israel, and Judah abide in tents, and Joab and the servants are encamped in the open fields, so shall he go home to eat and drink and lie with his wife? Henry calls this a noble, generous resolution of a public-spirited man, mortified to the delights of sense while the army endures hardness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 12. David tells Uriah to tarry today also, and tomorrow he will let him depart, so Uriah abides in Jerusalem that day and the morrow. The delay is a second chance for the cover to work. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 13. David calls Uriah, makes him eat and drink, and makes him drunk, yet at evening Uriah goes out to lie with his lord's servants and does not go home. Henry condemns making a man drunk as the devil's work, robbing a man of reason and drawing him into sin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 14. In the morning David writes a letter to Joab and sends it by Uriah's own hand. The bearer of his own death warrant is the man whose honour David has already destroyed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 15. The letter orders Joab to set Uriah in the forefront of the hottest battle and retire from him, that he may be smitten and die. Adulteries have often occasioned murders, Henry observes, and one wickedness must be covered and secured with another. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 16. Joab, observing the city, assigns Uriah to a place where he knows valiant men are. The general obeys an unjust order without protest, becoming party to the murder. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 17. The men of the city fight with Joab, some of David's servants fall, and Uriah the Hittite dies also. The innocent, valiant man ready to die for his prince's honour dies by his prince's hand. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 18. Joab sends and tells David all the things concerning the war. The report must now be managed, for the death it carries is no ordinary casualty. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 19. Joab charges the messenger that when he has finished telling the matters of the war, he must be ready for the king's wrath. The rehearsed message shows the killing was planned to look like a tactical error. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 20. If the king asks why they approached so near the city when they would shoot from the wall, the messenger is to answer with the example of Abimelech, slain by a millstone at Thebez. A precedent of folly is prepared to excuse the folly that killed Uriah. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 21. The rehearsal ends with the line, Thy servant Uriah the Hittite is dead also. The name Joab feared to report plainly is buried at the end of the message, as Uriah was buried in the battle. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 22. The messenger goes and shows David all Joab sent him for. The report is delivered exactly as ordered, and the king hears his own crime reported back as news. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 23. The messenger reports that the men prevailed, came out into the field, and Israel pursued them to the gate. The narrative of a hard fight sets up the deaths that follow. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 24. The shooters shot from the wall, some of the king's servants are dead, and Uriah the Hittite is dead also. The casualty list now includes the man David ordered killed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 25. David answers that the sword devours one as well as another, and bids Joab make the battle stronger against the city. The cold maxim treats a murdered man as a routine loss of war, and Henry notes how fleshly lusts harden the heart and sear the conscience. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 26. When Uriah's wife hears her husband is dead, she mourns for him. The grief is real, and it falls on a woman who does not yet know the king arranged the death. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 27. When mourning passes, David sends, fetches her to his house, and she becomes his wife and bears him a son, but the thing David did displeased the Lord. The marriage that seems to settle everything is the very thing God condemns, and Scripture's faithfulness in recording it, Henry says, is written for our learning, that he who thinks he stands may take heed lest he fall. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry

Unfamiliar terms
