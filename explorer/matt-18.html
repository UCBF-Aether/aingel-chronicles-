The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Matthew 18 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 At the same time came the disciples unto Jesus, saying, Who is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven?

2 And Jesus called a little child unto him, and set him in the midst of them,

3 And said, Verily I say unto you, Except ye be converted, and become as little children, ye shall not enter into the kingdom of heaven.

4 Whosoever therefore shall humble himself as this little child, the same is greatest in the kingdom of heaven.

5 And whoso shall receive one such little child in my name receiveth me.

6 But whoso shall offend one of these little ones which believe in me, it were better for him that a millstone were hanged about his neck, and that he were drowned in the depth of the sea.

7 Woe unto the world because of offences! for it must needs be that offences come; but woe to that man by whom the offence cometh!

8 Wherefore if thy hand or thy foot offend thee, cut them off, and cast them from thee: it is better for thee to enter into life halt or maimed, rather than having two hands or two feet to be cast into everlasting fire.

9 And if thine eye offend thee, pluck it out, and cast it from thee: it is better for thee to enter into life with one eye, rather than having two eyes to be cast into hell fire.

10 Take heed that ye despise not one of these little ones; for I say unto you, That in heaven their angels do always behold the face of my Father which is in heaven.

11 For the Son of man is come to save that which was lost.

12 How think ye? if a man have an hundred sheep, and one of them be gone astray, doth he not leave the ninety and nine, and goeth into the mountains, and seeketh that which is gone astray?

13 And if so be that he find it, verily I say unto you, he rejoiceth more of that sheep, than of the ninety and nine which went not astray.

14 Even so it is not the will of your Father which is in heaven, that one of these little ones should perish.

15 Moreover if thy brother shall trespass against thee, go and tell him his fault between thee and him alone: if he shall hear thee, thou hast gained thy brother.

16 But if he will not hear thee, then take with thee one or two more, that in the mouth of two or three witnesses every word may be established.

17 And if he shall neglect to hear them, tell it unto the church: but if he neglect to hear the church, let him be unto thee as an heathen man and a publican.

18 Verily I say unto you, Whatsoever ye shall bind on earth shall be bound in heaven: and whatsoever ye shall loose on earth shall be loosed in heaven.

19 Again I say unto you, That if two of you shall agree on earth as touching any thing that they shall ask, it shall be done for them of my Father which is in heaven.

20 For where two or three are gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them.

21 Then came Peter to him, and said, Lord, how oft shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? till seven times?

22 Jesus saith unto him, I say not unto thee, Until seven times: but, Until seventy times seven.

23 Therefore is the kingdom of heaven likened unto a certain king, which would take account of his servants.

24 And when he had begun to reckon, one was brought unto him, which owed him ten thousand talents.

25 But forasmuch as he had not to pay, his lord commanded him to be sold, and his wife, and children, and all that he had, and payment to be made.

26 The servant therefore fell down, and worshipped him, saying, Lord, have patience with me, and I will pay thee all.

27 Then the lord of that servant was moved with compassion, and loosed him, and forgave him the debt.

28 But the same servant went out, and found one of his fellowservants, which owed him an hundred pence: and he laid hands on him, and took him by the throat, saying, Pay me that thou owest.

29 And his fellowservant fell down at his feet, and besought him, saying, Have patience with me, and I will pay thee all.

30 And he would not: but went and cast him into prison, till he should pay the debt.

31 So when his fellowservants saw what was done, they were very sorry, and came and told unto their lord all that was done.

32 Then his lord, after that he had called him, said unto him, O thou wicked servant, I forgave thee all that debt, because thou desiredst me:

33 Shouldest not thou also have had compassion on thy fellowservant, even as I had pity on thee?

34 And his lord was wroth, and delivered him to the tormentors, till he should pay all that was due unto him.

35 So likewise shall my heavenly Father do also unto you, if ye from your hearts forgive not every one his brother their trespasses.

How to read the labels

Every substantive historical claim in the editorial panels below carries one of three labels, plus a source reference:

  • Documented — directly supported by a cited source.
  • Interpreted — a reasoned explanation or proposed relationship.
  • Unresolved — evidence is insufficient to establish the claim.

A claim does NOT become Documented merely because a commentary states it. Commentary views are attributed to their authors and labeled Interpreted unless a primary textual source directly supports the claim. Where evidence runs out, the page says Unresolved and stops — it does not fill the gap.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Asked who is greatest in the kingdom, Jesus sets a little child in the midst and warns against offending little ones, with the millstone saying and the cutting off of hand, foot and eye. He gives the lost sheep, the procedure for a brother who trespasses (alone, then with witnesses, then before the church), the binding and loosing, agreement in prayer, and, to Peter's “how oft, till seven times?”, the parable of the unforgiving servant who, forgiven ten thousand talents, throttles his fellow for a hundred pence. What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. At the same time came the disciples unto Jesus, saying, Who is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven? Christ spoke many words of his sufferings, but only one of his glory; yet the disciples fasten upon that, and overlook the others. Many love to hear and speak of privileges and glory, who are willing to pass by the thoughts of work and trouble. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 2. And Jesus called a little child unto him, and set him in the midst of them. Our Lord set a little child before them, solemnly assuring them that unless they were converted and made like little children, they could not enter his kingdom. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 3. And said, Verily I say unto you, Except ye be converted, and become as little children, ye shall not enter into the kingdom of heaven. Children, when very young, do not desire authority, do not regard outward distinctions, are free from malice, are teachable, and willingly dependent on their parents. It is true they soon begin to show other dispositions, and other ideas are taught them at an early age; but these are marks of childhood, and render them proper emblems of the lowly minds of true Christians. Surely we need to be daily renewed in the spirit of our minds, that we may become simple and humble, as little children, and willing to be the least of all. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 4. Whosoever therefore shall humble himself as this little child, the same is greatest in the kingdom of heaven. Greatness in the kingdom is measured downward: the lowliest is the greatest. Let us daily study this subject, and examine our own spirits. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 5. And whoso shall receive one such little child in my name receiveth me. To receive the lowly in Christ's name is to receive Christ himself; honour shown to his little ones is honour shown to him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 6. But whoso shall offend one of these little ones which believe in me, it were better for him that a millstone were hanged about his neck, and that he were drowned in the depth of the sea. Christ came into the world to save souls, and he will reckon severely with those who hinder the progress of others who are setting their faces heavenward. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 7. Woe unto the world because of offences! for it must needs be that offences come; but woe to that man by whom the offence cometh! Considering the cunning and malice of Satan, and the weakness and depravity of men's hearts, it is not possible but that there should be offences. God permits them for wise and holy ends, that those who are sincere, and those who are not, may be made known. Being told before that there will be seducers, tempters, persecutors, and bad examples, let us stand on our guard. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 8. Wherefore if thy hand or thy foot offend thee, cut them off, and cast them from thee: it is better for thee to enter into life halt or maimed, rather than having two hands or two feet to be cast into everlasting fire. We must, as far as lawfully we may, part with what we cannot keep without being entangled by it in sin. The outward occasions of sin must be avoided. If we live after the flesh, we must die. If we, through the Spirit, mortify the deeds of the body, we shall live. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 9. And if thine eye offend thee, pluck it out, and cast it from thee: it is better for thee to enter into life with one eye, rather than having two eyes to be cast into hell fire. The eye, the dearest and most useful member, must go rather than drag the whole man into hell; no loss is too great that saves the soul. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 10. Take heed that ye despise not one of these little ones; for I say unto you, That in heaven their angels do always behold the face of my Father which is in heaven. A difficult verse; but perhaps this may be more than an illustration: among men, those who nurse and rear the royal children, however humble in themselves, are allowed free entrance with their charge, and a degree of familiarity which even the highest state ministers dare not assume. Probably our Lord means that, in virtue of their charge over his disciples, the angels have errands to the throne, a welcome there, and a dear familiarity in dealing with his Father which is in heaven, which on their own matters they could not assume. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. For the Son of man is come to save that which was lost. A golden saying, once and again repeated in different forms. Here the connection seems to be, “Since the whole object and errand of the Son of man into the world is to save the lost, take heed lest, by causing offenses, ye lose the saved.” That this is the idea intended we may gather from verse 14. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. How think ye? if a man have an hundred sheep, and one of them be gone astray, doth he not leave the ninety and nine, and goeth into the mountains, and seeketh that which is gone astray? This is another of those pregnant sayings which our Lord uttered more than once. In Luke 15 the object is to show what the good Shepherd will do, when even one of his sheep is lost, to find it; here the object is to show, when found, how reluctant he is to lose it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. And if so be that he find it, verily I say unto you, he rejoiceth more of that sheep, than of the ninety and nine which went not astray. The joy over the found exceeds the quiet joy over the kept — not because the kept are loved less, but because recovery is sweeter than possession. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 14. Even so it is not the will of your Father which is in heaven, that one of these little ones should perish. How, then, can he but visit for those offenses which endanger the souls of these little ones? And shall any of us refuse attention to those whom the Son of God came to seek and to save? A father takes care of all his children, but is particularly tender of the little ones. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Moreover if thy brother shall trespass against thee, go and tell him his fault between thee and him alone: if he shall hear thee, thou hast gained thy brother. Probably our Lord had reference still to the late dispute, Who should be the greatest? After the rebuke — so gentle and captivating, yet so dignified and divine — under which they would doubtless be smarting, perhaps each would be saying, It was not I that began it. Be it so, says our Lord; but as such things will often arise, I will direct you how to proceed. First, neither harbor a grudge against your offending brother, nor break forth upon him in presence of the unbelieving; but take him aside, show him his fault, and if he own and make reparation for it, you have done more service to him than even justice to yourself. If a professed Christian is wronged by another, he ought not to complain of it to others, as is often done merely upon report, but to go to the offender privately, state the matter kindly, and show him his conduct. This would generally have all the desired effect with a true Christian, and the parties would be reconciled. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. But if he will not hear thee, then take with thee one or two more, that in the mouth of two or three witnesses every word may be established. Next, if private dealing fail, take two or three to witness how just your complaint is, and how brotherly your spirit in dealing with him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 17. And if he shall neglect to hear them, tell it unto the church: but if he neglect to hear the church, let him be unto thee as an heathen man and a publican. Again, if this fail, bring him before the Church or congregation to which both belong. Lastly, if even this fail, regard him as no longer a brother Christian, but as one “without” — as the Jews did Gentiles and publicans. The principles of these rules may be practised everywhere, and under all circumstances, though they are too much neglected by all. But how few try the method which Christ has expressly enjoined to all his disciples! Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 18. Verily I say unto you, Whatsoever ye shall bind on earth shall be bound in heaven: and whatsoever ye shall loose on earth shall be loosed in heaven. Here, what had been granted but a short time before to Peter only is plainly extended to all the Twelve; so that whatever it means, it means nothing peculiar to Peter, far less to his pretended successors at Rome. It has to do with admission to and rejection from the membership of the Church. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. Again I say unto you, That if two of you shall agree on earth as touching any thing that they shall ask, it shall be done for them of my Father which is in heaven. In all our proceedings we should seek direction in prayer; we cannot too highly prize the promises of God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. For where two or three are gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them. On this passage — so full of sublime encouragement to Christian union in action and prayer — observe first the connection in which it stands. Our Lord had been speaking of church meetings before which the obstinate perversity of a brother was in the last resort to be brought, and whose decision was to be final — such honour does the Lord of the Church put upon its lawful assemblies. But not these assemblies only does he deign to countenance and honour. For even two uniting to bring any matter before him shall find that they are not alone. Next, observe the premium here put upon union in prayer. As this cannot exist with fewer than two, so by letting it down so low as that number, he gives the utmost conceivable encouragement to union in this exercise. But what kind of union? Not an agreement merely to pray in concert, but to pray for some definite thing — anything they shall agree to ask in concert. At the same time, it is plain he had certain things at that moment in his eye: the Twelve had been falling out by the way about the miserable question of precedence in their Master's kingdom, and this had given rise — or was in danger of giving rise — to offenses perilous to their souls. “But now shows he unto them a more excellent way.” Let them bring all such matters — yea, and everything whatsoever by which either their own loving relationship to each other, or the good of his kingdom at large, might be affected — to their Father in heaven; and if they be but agreed in petitioning him about that thing, it shall be done for them. But further, it is not merely union in prayer for the same thing, but symphonious prayer, the prayer by kindred spirits, members of one family, servants of one Lord, constrained by the same love, fighting under one banner, cheered by assurances of the same victory; a living and loving union, whose voice in the divine ear is as the sound of many waters. What they ask on earth is done for them, says Jesus, “of MY FATHER” — not “your Father”; as is evident from what follows: “For where two or three are gathered together unto MY name” — the “My” is emphatic — “there am I in the midst of them.” As his name would prove a spell to draw together many clusters of his dear disciples, so if there should be but two or three, that will attract himself down into the midst of them; and related as he is to both the parties, the petitioners and the Petitioned — to the one on earth by the tie of his assumed flesh, and to the other in heaven by the tie of his eternal Spirit — their symphonious prayers on earth would thrill upward through him to heaven, be carried by him into the holiest of all, and so reach the Throne. Thus will he be the living Conductor of the prayer upward, and the answer downward. Wherever and whenever we meet in the name of Christ, we should consider him as present in the midst of us. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. Then came Peter to him, and said, Lord, how oft shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? till seven times? In the recent dispute, Peter had probably been an object of special envy, and his forwardness in continually answering for all the rest would likely be cast up to him — and if so, probably by Judas — notwithstanding his Master's commendations. And as such insinuations were perhaps made once and again, he wished to know how often and how long he was to stand it. Seven being the sacred and complete number, perhaps his meaning was, Is there to be a limit at which the needful forbearance will be full? Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Jesus saith unto him, I say not unto thee, Until seven times: but, Until seventy times seven. That is, so long as it shall be needed and sought: you are never to come to the point of refusing forgiveness sincerely asked. Though we live wholly on mercy and forgiveness, we are backward to forgive the offences of our brethren. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. Therefore is the kingdom of heaven likened unto a certain king, which would take account of his servants. With reference to this matter of forgiveness, the kingdom is likened to a king who would scrutinize the accounts of his revenue collectors. This parable shows how much provocation God has from his family on earth, and how untoward his servants are. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. And when he had begun to reckon, one was brought unto him, which owed him ten thousand talents. If Attic talents are here meant, ten thousand of them would amount to above a million and a half sterling; if Jewish talents, to a much larger sum. The debt of sin is so great that we are not able to pay it. See here what every sin deserves; this is the wages of sin, to be sold as a slave. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. But forasmuch as he had not to pay, his lord commanded him to be sold, and his wife, and his children, and all that he had, and payment to be made. It is the folly of many who are under strong convictions of their sins, to fancy they can make God satisfaction for the wrong they have done him; the reckoning only exposes the impossibility. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. The servant therefore fell down, and worshipped him, saying, Lord, have patience with me, and I will pay thee all. This was just an acknowledgment of the justice of the claim made against him, and a piteous imploration of mercy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. Then the lord of that servant was moved with compassion, and loosed him, and forgave him the debt. Payment being hopeless, the master is first moved with compassion; next, liberates his debtor from prison; and then cancels the debt freely. The master's wonderful clemency is the first of the three things in the parable. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. But the same servant went out, and found one of his fellowservants, which owed him an hundred pence: and he laid hands on him, and took him by the throat, saying, Pay me that thou owest. Mark the difference here. The first case is that of master and servant; in this case, both are on a footing of equality. If Jewish money is intended, this debt was to the other less than one to a million. He seized and throttled him; mark the mercilessness even of the tone. The servant's unreasonable severity toward his fellow-servant, notwithstanding his lord's clemency toward him, is the second thing in the parable. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. And his fellowservant fell down at his feet, and besought him, saying, Have patience with me, and I will pay thee all. The same attitude, and the same words which drew compassion from his master, are here employed towards himself by his fellow servant. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. And he would not: but went and cast him into prison, till he should pay the debt. Jesus here vividly conveys the intolerable injustice and impudence which even the servants saw in this act on the part of one so recently laid under the heaviest obligation to their common master. Not that we may make light of wronging our neighbour, for that is also a sin against God; but we should not aggravate our neighbour's wronging us, nor study revenge. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 31. So when his fellowservants saw what was done, they were very sorry, and came and told unto their lord all that was done. Let our complaints, both of the wickedness of the wicked, and of the afflictions of the afflicted, be brought to God, and left with him; the fellow servants did exactly this, carrying the wrong to the lord rather than avenging it themselves. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 32. Then his lord, after that he had called him, said unto him, O thou wicked servant, I forgave thee all that debt, because thou desiredst me. Before bringing down his vengeance upon him, he calmly points out to him how shamefully unreasonable and heartless his conduct was; which would give the punishment inflicted on him a double sting. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 33. Shouldest not thou also have had compassion on thy fellowservant, even as I had pity on thee? The greatness of sin magnifies the riches of pardoning mercy; and the comfortable sense of pardoning mercy does much to dispose our hearts to forgive our brethren. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 34. And his lord was wroth, and delivered him to the tormentors, till he should pay all that was due unto him. More than jailers, denoting the severity of the treatment which he thought such a case demanded. The master reproved his servant's cruelty — the third thing in the parable. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 35. So likewise shall my heavenly Father do also unto you, if ye from your hearts forgive not every one his brother their trespasses. We are not to suppose that God actually forgives men, and afterwards reckons their guilt to them to condemn them; but this latter part of the parable shows the false conclusions many draw as to their sins being pardoned, though their after-conduct shows that they never entered into the spirit, or experienced the sanctifying grace of the gospel. We do not forgive our offending brother aright, if we do not forgive from the heart. Yet this is not enough; we must seek the welfare even of those who offend us. How justly will those be condemned, who, though they bear the Christian name, persist in unmerciful treatment of their brethren! The humbled sinner relies only on free, abounding mercy, through the ransom of the death of Christ. Let us seek more and more for the renewing grace of God, to teach us to forgive others as we hope for forgiveness from him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

  • Verse 10. Take heed that ye despise not one of these little ones; for I say unto you, That in heaven their angels do always behold the face of my Father which is in heaven. A difficult verse; but perhaps this may be more than an illustration: among men, those who nurse and rear the royal children, however humble in themselves, are allowed free entrance with their charge, and a degree of familiarity which even the highest state ministers dare not assume. Probably our Lord means that, in virtue of their charge over his disciples, the angels have errands to the throne, a welcome there, and a dear familiarity in dealing with his Father which is in heaven, which on their own matters they could not assume. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. For the Son of man is come to save that which was lost. A golden saying, once and again repeated in different forms. Here the connection seems to be, “Since the whole object and errand of the Son of man into the world is to save the lost, take heed lest, by causing offenses, ye lose the saved.” That this is the idea intended we may gather from verse 14. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. How think ye? if a man have an hundred sheep, and one of them be gone astray, doth he not leave the ninety and nine, and goeth into the mountains, and seeketh that which is gone astray? This is another of those pregnant sayings which our Lord uttered more than once. In Luke 15 the object is to show what the good Shepherd will do, when even one of his sheep is lost, to find it; here the object is to show, when found, how reluctant he is to lose it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Even so it is not the will of your Father which is in heaven, that one of these little ones should perish. How, then, can he but visit for those offenses which endanger the souls of these little ones? And shall any of us refuse attention to those whom the Son of God came to seek and to save? A father takes care of all his children, but is particularly tender of the little ones. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Moreover if thy brother shall trespass against thee, go and tell him his fault between thee and him alone: if he shall hear thee, thou hast gained thy brother. Probably our Lord had reference still to the late dispute, Who should be the greatest? After the rebuke — so gentle and captivating, yet so dignified and divine — under which they would doubtless be smarting, perhaps each would be saying, It was not I that began it. Be it so, says our Lord; but as such things will often arise, I will direct you how to proceed. First, neither harbor a grudge against your offending brother, nor break forth upon him in presence of the unbelieving; but take him aside, show him his fault, and if he own and make reparation for it, you have done more service to him than even justice to yourself. If a professed Christian is wronged by another, he ought not to complain of it to others, as is often done merely upon report, but to go to the offender privately, state the matter kindly, and show him his conduct. This would generally have all the desired effect with a true Christian, and the parties would be reconciled. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. Verily I say unto you, Whatsoever ye shall bind on earth shall be bound in heaven: and whatsoever ye shall loose on earth shall be loosed in heaven. Here, what had been granted but a short time before to Peter only is plainly extended to all the Twelve; so that whatever it means, it means nothing peculiar to Peter, far less to his pretended successors at Rome. It has to do with admission to and rejection from the membership of the Church. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. Again I say unto you, That if two of you shall agree on earth as touching any thing that they shall ask, it shall be done for them of my Father which is in heaven. In all our proceedings we should seek direction in prayer; we cannot too highly prize the promises of God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. For where two or three are gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them. On this passage — so full of sublime encouragement to Christian union in action and prayer — observe first the connection in which it stands. Our Lord had been speaking of church meetings before which the obstinate perversity of a brother was in the last resort to be brought, and whose decision was to be final — such honour does the Lord of the Church put upon its lawful assemblies. But not these assemblies only does he deign to countenance and honour. For even two uniting to bring any matter before him shall find that they are not alone. Next, observe the premium here put upon union in prayer. As this cannot exist with fewer than two, so by letting it down so low as that number, he gives the utmost conceivable encouragement to union in this exercise. But what kind of union? Not an agreement merely to pray in concert, but to pray for some definite thing — anything they shall agree to ask in concert. At the same time, it is plain he had certain things at that moment in his eye: the Twelve had been falling out by the way about the miserable question of precedence in their Master's kingdom, and this had given rise — or was in danger of giving rise — to offenses perilous to their souls. “But now shows he unto them a more excellent way.” Let them bring all such matters — yea, and everything whatsoever by which either their own loving relationship to each other, or the good of his kingdom at large, might be affected — to their Father in heaven; and if they be but agreed in petitioning him about that thing, it shall be done for them. But further, it is not merely union in prayer for the same thing, but symphonious prayer, the prayer by kindred spirits, members of one family, servants of one Lord, constrained by the same love, fighting under one banner, cheered by assurances of the same victory; a living and loving union, whose voice in the divine ear is as the sound of many waters. What they ask on earth is done for them, says Jesus, “of MY FATHER” — not “your Father”; as is evident from what follows: “For where two or three are gathered together unto MY name” — the “My” is emphatic — “there am I in the midst of them.” As his name would prove a spell to draw together many clusters of his dear disciples, so if there should be but two or three, that will attract himself down into the midst of them; and related as he is to both the parties, the petitioners and the Petitioned — to the one on earth by the tie of his assumed flesh, and to the other in heaven by the tie of his eternal Spirit — their symphonious prayers on earth would thrill upward through him to heaven, be carried by him into the holiest of all, and so reach the Throne. Thus will he be the living Conductor of the prayer upward, and the answer downward. Wherever and whenever we meet in the name of Christ, we should consider him as present in the midst of us. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. Then came Peter to him, and said, Lord, how oft shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? till seven times? In the recent dispute, Peter had probably been an object of special envy, and his forwardness in continually answering for all the rest would likely be cast up to him — and if so, probably by Judas — notwithstanding his Master's commendations. And as such insinuations were perhaps made once and again, he wished to know how often and how long he was to stand it. Seven being the sacred and complete number, perhaps his meaning was, Is there to be a limit at which the needful forbearance will be full? Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Jesus saith unto him, I say not unto thee, Until seven times: but, Until seventy times seven. That is, so long as it shall be needed and sought: you are never to come to the point of refusing forgiveness sincerely asked. Though we live wholly on mercy and forgiveness, we are backward to forgive the offences of our brethren. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. Therefore is the kingdom of heaven likened unto a certain king, which would take account of his servants. With reference to this matter of forgiveness, the kingdom is likened to a king who would scrutinize the accounts of his revenue collectors. This parable shows how much provocation God has from his family on earth, and how untoward his servants are. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. And when he had begun to reckon, one was brought unto him, which owed him ten thousand talents. If Attic talents are here meant, ten thousand of them would amount to above a million and a half sterling; if Jewish talents, to a much larger sum. The debt of sin is so great that we are not able to pay it. See here what every sin deserves; this is the wages of sin, to be sold as a slave. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. But forasmuch as he had not to pay, his lord commanded him to be sold, and his wife, and his children, and all that he had, and payment to be made. It is the folly of many who are under strong convictions of their sins, to fancy they can make God satisfaction for the wrong they have done him; the reckoning only exposes the impossibility. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. The servant therefore fell down, and worshipped him, saying, Lord, have patience with me, and I will pay thee all. This was just an acknowledgment of the justice of the claim made against him, and a piteous imploration of mercy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. Then the lord of that servant was moved with compassion, and loosed him, and forgave him the debt. Payment being hopeless, the master is first moved with compassion; next, liberates his debtor from prison; and then cancels the debt freely. The master's wonderful clemency is the first of the three things in the parable. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. But the same servant went out, and found one of his fellowservants, which owed him an hundred pence: and he laid hands on him, and took him by the throat, saying, Pay me that thou owest. Mark the difference here. The first case is that of master and servant; in this case, both are on a footing of equality. If Jewish money is intended, this debt was to the other less than one to a million. He seized and throttled him; mark the mercilessness even of the tone. The servant's unreasonable severity toward his fellow-servant, notwithstanding his lord's clemency toward him, is the second thing in the parable. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. And his fellowservant fell down at his feet, and besought him, saying, Have patience with me, and I will pay thee all. The same attitude, and the same words which drew compassion from his master, are here employed towards himself by his fellow servant. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. And he would not: but went and cast him into prison, till he should pay the debt. Jesus here vividly conveys the intolerable injustice and impudence which even the servants saw in this act on the part of one so recently laid under the heaviest obligation to their common master. Not that we may make light of wronging our neighbour, for that is also a sin against God; but we should not aggravate our neighbour's wronging us, nor study revenge. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 32. Then his lord, after that he had called him, said unto him, O thou wicked servant, I forgave thee all that debt, because thou desiredst me. Before bringing down his vengeance upon him, he calmly points out to him how shamefully unreasonable and heartless his conduct was; which would give the punishment inflicted on him a double sting. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 34. And his lord was wroth, and delivered him to the tormentors, till he should pay all that was due unto him. More than jailers, denoting the severity of the treatment which he thought such a case demanded. The master reproved his servant's cruelty — the third thing in the parable. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 35. So likewise shall my heavenly Father do also unto you, if ye from your hearts forgive not every one his brother their trespasses. We are not to suppose that God actually forgives men, and afterwards reckons their guilt to them to condemn them; but this latter part of the parable shows the false conclusions many draw as to their sins being pardoned, though their after-conduct shows that they never entered into the spirit, or experienced the sanctifying grace of the gospel. We do not forgive our offending brother aright, if we do not forgive from the heart. Yet this is not enough; we must seek the welfare even of those who offend us. How justly will those be condemned, who, though they bear the Christian name, persist in unmerciful treatment of their brethren! The humbled sinner relies only on free, abounding mercy, through the ransom of the death of Christ. Let us seek more and more for the renewing grace of God, to teach us to forgive others as we hope for forgiveness from him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Investigation (editorial — a parallel question, not a conclusion)

Parallel. The lost sheep saying is told in Matthew and, in the note's terms, in Luke 15 as well. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Difference. The note calls this one of those pregnant sayings which Jesus uttered more than once, and in Luke 15 the object differs in setting and application. The parallel is therefore presented as a repeated saying, not as two copies of one occasion, and the note does not claim the occasions are the same.
