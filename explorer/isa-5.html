The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Isaiah 5 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Now will I sing to my wellbeloved a song of my beloved touching his vineyard. My wellbeloved hath a vineyard in a very fruitful hill:

2 And he fenced it, and gathered out the stones thereof, and planted it with the choicest vine, and built a tower in the midst of it, and also made a winepress therein: and he looked that it should bring forth grapes, and it brought forth wild grapes.

3 And now, O inhabitants of Jerusalem, and men of Judah, judge, I pray you, betwixt me and my vineyard.

4 What could have been done more to my vineyard, that I have not done in it? wherefore, when I looked that it should bring forth grapes, brought it forth wild grapes?

5 And now go to; I will tell you what I will do to my vineyard: I will take away the hedge thereof, and it shall be eaten up; and break down the wall thereof, and it shall be trodden down:

6 And I will lay it waste: it shall not be pruned, nor digged; but there shall come up briers and thorns: I will also command the clouds that they rain no rain upon it.

7 For the vineyard of the LORD of hosts is the house of Israel, and the men of Judah his pleasant plant: and he looked for judgment, but behold oppression; for righteousness, but behold a cry.

8 Woe unto them that join house to house, that lay field to field, till there be no place, that they may be placed alone in the midst of the earth!

9 In mine ears said the LORD of hosts, Of a truth many houses shall be desolate, even great and fair, without inhabitant.

10 Yea, ten acres of vineyard shall yield one bath, and the seed of an homer shall yield an ephah.

11 Woe unto them that rise up early in the morning, that they may follow strong drink; that continue until night, till wine inflame them!

12 And the harp, and the viol, the tabret, and pipe, and wine, are in their feasts: but they regard not the work of the LORD, neither consider the operation of his hands.

13 Therefore my people are gone into captivity, because they have no knowledge: and their honourable men are famished, and their multitude dried up with thirst.

14 Therefore hell hath enlarged herself, and opened her mouth without measure: and their glory, and their multitude, and their pomp, and he that rejoiceth, shall descend into it.

15 And the mean man shall be brought down, and the mighty man shall be humbled, and the eyes of the lofty shall be humbled:

16 But the LORD of hosts shall be exalted in judgment, and God that is holy shall be sanctified in righteousness.

17 Then shall the lambs feed after their manner, and the waste places of the fat ones shall strangers eat.

18 Woe unto them that draw iniquity with cords of vanity, and sin as it were with a cart rope:

19 That say, Let him make speed, and hasten his work, that we may see it: and let the counsel of the Holy One of Israel draw nigh and come, that we may know it!

20 Woe unto them that call evil good, and good evil; that put darkness for light, and light for darkness; that put bitter for sweet, and sweet for bitter!

21 Woe unto them that are wise in their own eyes, and prudent in their own sight!

22 Woe unto them that are mighty to drink wine, and men of strength to mingle strong drink:

23 Which justify the wicked for reward, and take away the righteousness of the righteous from him!

24 Therefore as the fire devoureth the stubble, and the flame consumeth the chaff, so their root shall be as rottenness, and their blossom shall go up as dust: because they have cast away the law of the LORD of hosts, and despised the word of the Holy One of Israel.

25 Therefore is the anger of the LORD kindled against his people, and he hath stretched forth his hand against them, and hath smitten them: and the hills did tremble, and their carcases were torn in the midst of the streets. For all this his anger is not turned away, but his hand is stretched out still.

26 And he will lift up an ensign to the nations from far, and will hiss unto them from the end of the earth: and, behold, they shall come with speed swiftly:

27 None shall be weary nor stumble among them; none shall slumber nor sleep; neither shall the girdle of their loins be loosed, nor the latchet of their shoes be broken:

28 Whose arrows are sharp, and all their bows bent, their horses’ hoofs shall be counted like flint, and their wheels like a whirlwind:

29 Their roaring shall be like a lion, they shall roar like young lions: yea, they shall roar, and lay hold of the prey, and shall carry it away safe, and none shall deliver it.

30 And in that day they shall roar against them like the roaring of the sea: and if one look unto the land, behold darkness and sorrow, and the light is darkened in the heavens thereof.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Isaiah sings a song about God's vineyard: planted on a fruitful hill, fenced, and tended with every care, it produced only wild grapes. The chapter then pronounces woes on greed, drunkenness, and those who call evil good, and ends with a foreign invader roaring in like the sea, leaving only darkness and sorrow over the land. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Isaiah sings a song concerning his Beloved's vineyard, planted on a very fruitful hill, isolated, secure, and sunny in aspect. The Beloved is Jehovah as God of the Jews, and the vineyard is the covenant people separated from the nations for his glory. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. The owner digged and trenched the ground, gathered out the stones, planted the choicest vine — the sorek, whose grapes had scarcely perceptible seeds — and built a watchtower and a wine-press hewn from rock. Yet it brought forth wild grapes, fetid fruit expressing offensive putrefaction, answering to the corrupt state of the Jews. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. God now appeals to the inhabitants of Jerusalem and men of Judah to judge between him and his vineyard, as Jesus later makes his hearers pass sentence on themselves. God condemns sinners out of their own mouth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. What more could have been done for the vineyard that was not done in it, God asks, amazed as it were at the unnatural fruit of so well-cared a vineyard. He has done all that could be done for sinners consistently with his justice and goodness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Now God will show what he will do to his vineyard: he will take away its hedge and break down its wall, so that it will be trodden down by wild beasts, his enemies. The vineyard had both hedge and wall, proof of the owner's care, and both protections will be removed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. He will lay it waste, unpruned and undigged, overgrown with briers and thorns, and command the clouds to rain no rain upon it. The withheld rain is antitypically the heaven-sent teachings of the prophets, withheld in gospel times. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Here Isaiah applies the parable: the vineyard of the Lord of hosts is the house of Israel, and the men of Judah his pleasant plant, the plant of his delight chosen as carefully as the sorek. He looked for judgment but behold oppression — in Hebrew, mishpat but mispat, bloodshed — and for righteousness but behold a cry, the cry of anarchy, covetousness, and dissipation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. The first woe falls on those who join house to house and lay field to field until there is no place left for anyone else. The jubilee restoration of possessions was intended as a guard against exactly this avarice. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. The Lord of hosts has revealed in Isaiah's ears that many houses, great and fair, will be desolate without inhabitant. The desolation comes on account of the national sins, turning seized estates into emptiness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Ten acres of vineyard will yield only one bath of wine, and a homer of seed only an ephah — eight bushels of seed yielding barely three pecks of produce. The land seized by greed will answer with meagre harvests. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. The second woe falls on those who rise early to follow strong drink and continue until night till wine inflames them. Banquets for revelry began earlier than usual, and drinking all day was regarded as especially shameful. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. At their feasts are harp, viol of twelve strings, tabret, pipe, and wine, but they do not regard the work of the Lord or consider the operation of his hands. Feasting commonly produces this blindness, and God's work here is his punishing of the guilty. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Therefore the people will go into captivity because they have no knowledge, their honourable men famished and their multitude dried up with thirst. The prophet sees the future as present, and the luxurious feasters will hunger and thirst in deportation and exile. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Therefore hell, the grave and unseen world of spirits, enlarges herself and opens her mouth without measure to receive the countless hosts that will perish. Their glory, multitude, pomp, and even the drunken reveller of Jerusalem will descend into it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. The mean man will be brought down, the mighty man humbled, and the eyes of the lofty humbled, as in chapter 2. All ranks alike — honourable and multitude — share the humiliation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. But the Lord of hosts will be exalted in judgment, and God who is holy will be sanctified in righteousness. God is exalted in men's view by his manifestation of justice in punishing the guilty, and regarded as holy by reason of his righteous dealings. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. Then lambs will feed after their manner and strangers will eat the waste places of the fat ones. The desolate land will become a vast pasturage for nomad shepherds, and figuratively the pious lambs will enjoy what the impious rich have lost, with Gentile strangers brought into the privileges the Jews fell from. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. The third woe falls on those who draw iniquity with cords of vanity and sin as with a cart rope, straining every nerve in obstinate perseverance in sin. An evil inclination begins like a fine hair-string and finishes like a cart-rope, as one sin draws on another until they bind themselves with great guilt. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. In defiance they say: let God make speed and hasten his work that they may see it, and let the counsel of the Holy One draw near that they may know it. Their language is defiance of God's threatened purpose to punish, like Lamech's boast of impunity. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. The fourth woe falls on those who call evil good and good evil, putting darkness for light and bitter for sweet. Their moral perception is darkened and undiscriminating, though sin is bitter even when it seems sweet for a time, while religion is sweet. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. The fifth woe falls on those wise in their own eyes and prudent in their own sight, who thought they knew better than the prophet and therefore rejected his warnings. Self-wise contempt for prophecy is itself the sin condemned. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. The sixth woe falls on corrupt judges, mighty to drink wine — a boast still not uncommon — and men of strength to mingle strong drink, spiced to make it intoxicating rather than mixed with water. Their drinking is joined to bribery, the means of their self-indulgence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. These judges justify the wicked for reward and take away the righteousness of the righteous, setting aside the just claims of those with a righteous cause. The two verses are closely joined, describing one corrupt bench. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. Therefore, as fire devours stubble and flame consumes chaff, their root will be rottenness and their blossom dust. The hidden source and the outward manifestation of prosperity perish together, because they have cast away the law of the Lord in its spirit while retaining its letter. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. Therefore the anger of the Lord is kindled against his people, his hand is stretched out, the hills tremble, and their carcasses are torn like dung in the streets. The trembling hills probably recall the earthquake in Uzziah's days, and yet for all this his anger is not turned away but his hand is stretched out still — a refrain repeated through chapters 9 and 10. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. He will lift up an ensign to the nations from far and hiss to them from the end of the earth, and they will come with speed swiftly. As bees are drawn from their hives by hissing, God will collect the nations round Judea to execute his judgments, chiefly the distant subject races of the Assyrian army. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. None of the invaders will be weary or stumble, none will slumber or sleep, neither will the girdle of their loins be loosed nor the latchet of their shoes broken. Ever ready for march or battle, they need no rest, and not even a sandal strap gives way to impede them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. Their arrows are sharp and all their bows bent, their horses' hoofs counted like flint — the ancients did not shoe horses — and their wheels like a whirlwind. The Assyrian army abounded in cavalry and chariots. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. Their roaring will be like a lion, like young lions roaring and laying hold of prey and carrying it away safe, with none to deliver it. The roaring is their battle cry. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. In that day they will roar against Judah like the roaring of the sea, and if one looks to the land, behold darkness and sorrow, and the light darkened in its heavens. Distress and hope may alternately succeed in an unsettled state, but here darkness and distress alone are visible, like mariners in a storm who cannot see the land. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
