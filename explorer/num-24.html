The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Numbers 24 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 And when Balaam saw that it pleased the LORD to bless Israel, he went not, as at other times, to seek for enchantments, but he set his face toward the wilderness.

2 And Balaam lifted up his eyes, and he saw Israel abiding in his tents according to their tribes; and the spirit of God came upon him.

3 And he took up his parable, and said, Balaam the son of Beor hath said, and the man whose eyes are open hath said:

4 He hath said, which heard the words of God, which saw the vision of the Almighty, falling into a trance, but having his eyes open:

5 How goodly are thy tents, O Jacob, and thy tabernacles, O Israel!

6 As the valleys are they spread forth, as gardens by the river’s side, as the trees of lign aloes which the LORD hath planted, and as cedar trees beside the waters.

7 He shall pour the water out of his buckets, and his seed shall be in many waters, and his king shall be higher than Agag, and his kingdom shall be exalted.

8 God brought him forth out of Egypt; he hath as it were the strength of an unicorn: he shall eat up the nations his enemies, and shall break their bones, and pierce them through with his arrows.

9 He couched, he lay down as a lion, and as a great lion: who shall stir him up? Blessed is he that blesseth thee, and cursed is he that curseth thee.

10 And Balak’s anger was kindled against Balaam, and he smote his hands together: and Balak said unto Balaam, I called thee to curse mine enemies, and, behold, thou hast altogether blessed them these three times.

11 Therefore now flee thou to thy place: I thought to promote thee unto great honour; but, lo, the LORD hath kept thee back from honour.

12 And Balaam said unto Balak, Spake I not also to thy messengers which thou sentest unto me, saying,

13 If Balak would give me his house full of silver and gold, I cannot go beyond the commandment of the LORD, to do either good or bad of mine own mind; but what the LORD saith, that will I speak?

14 And now, behold, I go unto my people: come therefore, and I will advertise thee what this people shall do to thy people in the latter days.

15 And he took up his parable, and said, Balaam the son of Beor hath said, and the man whose eyes are open hath said:

16 He hath said, which heard the words of God, and knew the knowledge of the most High, which saw the vision of the Almighty, falling into a trance, but having his eyes open:

17 I shall see him, but not now: I shall behold him, but not nigh: there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel, and shall smite the corners of Moab, and destroy all the children of Sheth.

18 And Edom shall be a possession, Seir also shall be a possession for his enemies; and Israel shall do valiantly.

19 Out of Jacob shall come he that shall have dominion, and shall destroy him that remaineth of the city.

20 And when he looked on Amalek, he took up his parable, and said, Amalek was the first of the nations; but his latter end shall be that he perish for ever.

21 And he looked on the Kenites, and took up his parable, and said, Strong is thy dwellingplace, and thou puttest thy nest in a rock.

22 Nevertheless the Kenite shall be wasted, until Asshur shall carry thee away captive.

23 And he took up his parable, and said, Alas, who shall live when God doeth this!

24 And ships shall come from the coast of Chittim, and shall afflict Asshur, and shall afflict Eber, and he also shall perish for ever.

25 And Balaam rose up, and went and returned to his place: and Balak also went his way.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

This chapter gives Balaam's third and fourth oracles. Seeing that it pleases the LORD to bless Israel, Balaam no longer seeks enchantments but speaks as the Spirit comes on him, blessing Israel's tents, her king, and her strength, and warning that those who bless her are blessed and those who curse her are cursed. The fourth oracle looks further: a star shall come out of Jacob and a sceptre shall rise out of Israel, breaking the corners of Moab, with shorter oracles against Amalek, the Kenites, and ships from Chittim, before Balaam returns home and Balak goes his way. Interpreted Editorial overview, grounded in the chapter text and its study notes

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. When Balaam saw that it pleased the Lord to bless Israel, he went not, as at other times, to seek for enchantments, but set his face toward the wilderness. JFB notes his experience on the two former occasions had taught him these superstitious accompaniments were useless, so he now simply looked toward Israel's camp, either with a secret design to curse or to await the divine afflatus. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Balaam lifted up his eyes and saw Israel abiding in his tents according to their tribes, and the Spirit of God came upon him. JFB notes the tents lay in the orderly distribution of chapter 2, and before the regular ministry of prophets was instituted, God used various persons as instruments to reveal his will, and Balaam was one of them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. He took up his parable, saying, Balaam son of Beor has said, and the man whose eyes are open has said. JFB explains the man whose eyes are open is a seer, a prophet to whom the visioned future was disclosed, sometimes in sleep and frequently in a trance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. He has said, which heard the words of God, which saw the vision of the Almighty, falling into a trance, but having his eyes open. Henry warns many have their eyes open who have not their hearts open: they are enlightened but not sanctified, and knowledge that puffs men up with pride will but light them to hell, whither many go with their eyes open. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 5. How goodly are your tents, O Jacob, and your tabernacles, O Israel! JFB calls this a fine burst of admiration in highly poetical strains, noting travellers describe the beauty of a circular area of Bedouin tents in the desert, and how much more impressive Israel's immense camp over the plains must have looked from the heights of Abarim. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. As the valleys are they spread forth, as gardens by the river's side, as the trees of lign aloes which the Lord has planted, and as cedar trees beside the waters. JFB notes valleys means the brooks or watercourses of the mountains, and lign aloes, an aromatic shrub on his native Euphrates whose conical form resembles a tent, while the redundant imagery depicts Israel's humble origin, rapid progress and prosperity. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. He shall pour the water out of his buckets, and his seed shall be in many waters, and his king shall be higher than Agag, and his kingdom shall be exalted. JFB notes Agag was a title common to Amalekite kings, then the most powerful of the desert tribes, and Henry reads the verse as Israel's fruitfulness, increase, honour and advancement. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. God brought him forth out of Egypt; he has as it were the strength of a unicorn: he shall eat up the nations his enemies, and shall break their bones, and pierce them through with his arrows. Henry notes Balaam looks back on what had been done for them: their power and victory were already proved by the exodus and the conquests that followed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 9. He couched, he lay down as a lion, and as a great lion: who shall stir him up? Blessed is he that blesses you, and cursed is he that curses you. Henry notes the righteous are bold as a lion, not when assaulting others but when at rest, because God makes them dwell in safety, and God takes what is done to Israel, good or evil, as done to himself. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 10. Balak's anger was kindled against Balaam, and he smote his hands together, and said, I called you to curse my enemies, and behold, you have altogether blessed them these three times. JFB notes smiting the hands together is, among Oriental peoples, an indication of the most violent rage and of ignominious dismissal. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Therefore now flee to your place: I thought to promote you to great honour, but lo, the Lord has kept you back from honour. Balak's dismissal turned the promised honour into a reproach: the prophet who came for promotion left in disgrace, because the Lord, not Balak, controlled his words. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 12. Balaam said to Balak, spoke I not also to your messengers which you sent to me, saying, if Balak would give me his house full of silver and gold, I cannot go beyond the commandment of the Lord? Balaam's excuse was that he had warned the messengers from the start: no fee, however great, could move him beyond God's word. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 13. If Balak would give me his house full of silver and gold, I cannot go beyond the commandment of the Lord, to do either good or bad of my own mind; but what the Lord says, that will I speak. Henry calls this a very full excuse: God restrained him from saying what he would have said, and constrained him to say what he would not have uttered. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 14. And now, behold, I go to my people: come therefore, and I will advertise you what this people shall do to your people in the latter days. Before departing, Balaam offered a final advertisement, a prophecy of latter days, which would prove more dreadful to Balak than any curse he had sought. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 15. He took up his parable, and said, Balaam son of Beor has said, and the man whose eyes are open has said. The fourth parable opens with the same seer formula, now in a poetical prophecy style, marking a new and farther-reaching oracle. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. He has said, which heard the words of God, and knew the knowledge of the most High, which saw the vision of the Almighty, falling into a trance, but having his eyes open. Henry notes a man may be full of the knowledge of God yet utterly destitute of his grace, expressing great respect to God as Most High and Almighty while having no true fear of him, love to him, nor faith in him, and so go far toward heaven yet come short at last. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 17. I shall see him, but not now: I shall behold him, but not nigh: there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel, and shall smite the corners of Moab, and destroy all the children of Sheth. JFB renders the sight as prophetic, I do see or I have seen, like Abraham's sight, and explains the Star as glory and lustre and the Sceptre as power and authority, denoting primarily David but secondarily and pre-eminently the Messiah, with corners meaning the whole border country. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. And Edom shall be a possession, Seir also shall be a possession for his enemies, and Israel shall do valiantly. JFB notes this prophecy was accomplished by David, and Seir, seen in the south, is poetically used for Edom, with the double conquest of Moab and Edom in view. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. Out of Jacob shall come he that shall have dominion, and shall destroy him that remains of the city. JFB identifies the ruler as David, and particularly Christ, and the remnant of the city as those who flee from the field to fortified places. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. When he looked on Amalek, he took up his parable, and said, Amalek was the first of the nations, but his latter end shall be that he perish for ever. JFB notes Amalek's territory lay at the desert's remote extremity, and its latter end, perishing for ever, was fulfilled in its destruction under Saul and after. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. He looked on the Kenites, and took up his parable, and said, strong is your dwelling place, and you put your nest in a rock. JFB notes the Kenites were securely established among the clefts of the high rocks of En-gedi toward the west, yet Henry warns even a nest in a rock will not be a lasting security. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Nevertheless the Kenite shall be wasted, until Asshur shall carry you away captive. The security of the rock would fail by degrees: a succession of enemies would reduce the Kenites until the Assyrian invader carried them into captivity. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. He took up his parable, and said, alas, who shall live when God does this! JFB notes few shall escape the desolation that would send a Nebuchadnezzar to scourge all those regions, and Henry adds the revolutions of states and kingdoms are the Lord's doing, and those who live then will be as brands plucked out of the fire. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. And ships shall come from the coast of Chittim, and shall afflict Asshur, and shall afflict Eber, and he also shall perish for ever. JFB identifies Chittim with the Mediterranean countries, particularly Greece and Italy, whose powers overthrew Assyria, first the Greeks under Alexander and his successors, then the Romans, while Eber means the Hebrews' posterity, and the conqueror himself would also perish. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. Balaam rose up, and went and returned to his place, and Balak also went his way. JFB notes Balaam's place was Mesopotamia, to which, however, he did not return, since he would die among the Midianites, and Henry closes the vain attempt: instead of cursing the church, Balaam cursed Amalek the first and Rome the last enemy of the church. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
