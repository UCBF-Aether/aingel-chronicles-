The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 5 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Call now, if there be any that will answer thee; and to which of the saints wilt thou turn?

2 For wrath killeth the foolish man, and envy slayeth the silly one.

3 I have seen the foolish taking root: but suddenly I cursed his habitation.

4 His children are far from safety, and they are crushed in the gate, neither is there any to deliver them.

5 Whose harvest the hungry eateth up, and taketh it even out of the thorns, and the robber swalloweth up their substance.

6 Although affliction cometh not forth of the dust, neither doth trouble spring out of the ground;

7 Yet man is born unto trouble, as the sparks fly upward.

8 I would seek unto God, and unto God would I commit my cause:

9 Which doeth great things and unsearchable; marvellous things without number:

10 Who giveth rain upon the earth, and sendeth waters upon the fields:

11 To set up on high those that be low; that those which mourn may be exalted to safety.

12 He disappointeth the devices of the crafty, so that their hands cannot perform their enterprise.

13 He taketh the wise in their own craftiness: and the counsel of the froward is carried headlong.

14 They meet with darkness in the day time, and grope in the noonday as in the night.

15 But he saveth the poor from the sword, from their mouth, and from the hand of the mighty.

16 So the poor hath hope, and iniquity stoppeth her mouth.

17 Behold, happy is the man whom God correcteth: therefore despise not thou the chastening of the Almighty:

18 For he maketh sore, and bindeth up: he woundeth, and his hands make whole.

19 He shall deliver thee in six troubles: yea, in seven there shall no evil touch thee.

20 In famine he shall redeem thee from death: and in war from the power of the sword.

21 Thou shalt be hid from the scourge of the tongue: neither shalt thou be afraid of destruction when it cometh.

22 At destruction and famine thou shalt laugh: neither shalt thou be afraid of the beasts of the earth.

23 For thou shalt be in league with the stones of the field: and the beasts of the field shall be at peace with thee.

24 And thou shalt know that thy tabernacle shall be in peace; and thou shalt visit thy habitation, and shalt not sin.

25 Thou shalt know also that thy seed shall be great, and thine offspring as the grass of the earth.

26 Thou shalt come to thy grave in a full age, like as a shock of corn cometh in in his season.

27 Lo this, we have searched it, so it is; hear it, and know thou it for thy good.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Eliphaz continues: wrath kills the foolish, affliction does not spring from the dust without cause, and the remedy is to seek God and commit the cause to him. God, he says, does great unsearchable things, gives rain, lifts the lowly, and frustrates the crafty — and happy is the man whom God corrects, for God wounds and binds up. If Job turns, Eliphaz promises deliverance in every trouble, safety at home, and a grave in full age, like a shock of corn in its season. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Eliphaz asks whether Job will call on any holy one to answer him. JFB reads it as a challenge: after the revelation just given, Job cannot presume that God or any angel round his throne will vouchsafe a judicial reply to his rebellious complaint. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Wrath kills the foolish man, and envy slays the silly one. JFB explains the wrath and envy as Job's own fretful, passionate complaints, not God's wrath or envy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Eliphaz has seen the foolish taking root, yet suddenly his habitation is cursed. JFB reads the foolish as the wicked, spreading his root wide in prosperity until circumstances suddenly leave his dwelling desolate. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. The foolish man's children are far from safety and crushed in the gate, with none to deliver. JFB notes the gate was the place of judgment and public proceedings, and Eliphaz obliquely alludes to the calamity that cut off Job's children. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. The hungry eat up the wicked man's harvest, taking it even out of the thorns, and the thirsty swallow up his substance. JFB stresses how cleanly swept away the harvest is, since the gleaner does not grudge even grain hanging on thorn bushes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Affliction does not come forth from the dust, nor does trouble spring out of the ground. JFB explains that affliction does not grow like a weed of its own accord, hinting that its cause lay with Job himself. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Yet man is born to trouble, as the sparks fly upward. JFB reads this as God's appointment for sin, passing from Job's particular case to mankind's universal sin and suffering, as necessary as sparks flying upward. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Eliphaz's counsel is that Job should seek God and commit his cause to him. Since affliction is ordered by God on account of sin, the remedy is to turn humbly to God rather than complain. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. God does great things and unsearchable, marvellous things without number. The praise of God's incomprehensible works prepares the call to trust him rather than dispute him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. God gives rain on the earth and sends waters on the fields. His providence reaches the common, life-giving gifts as well as the great wonders. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. God sets the lowly on high, and those who mourn are lifted to safety. JFB connects this with God's unsearchable dealings: they aim to raise the humble and abase the proud, so Job ought to turn humbly to him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. God disappoints the devices of the crafty, so their hands cannot perform their enterprise. JFB notes the Hebrew combines wisdom and happiness in the idea of enduring existence that the crafty fail to secure. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. God takes the wise in their own craftiness, and the counsel of the froward is carried headlong. JFB notes Paul quotes this clause as written Scripture, and that the schemers' plans are precipitated before they ripen. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. The crafty meet with darkness in the daytime and grope at noonday as in the night. JFB reads this as judicial blindness sent upon keen men of the world. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. God saves the poor from the sword of their mouth and from the hand of the mighty. JFB explains the sword as the sword proceeding from their mouth, the weapon of slander and accusation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. So the poor has hope, and iniquity stops her mouth. JFB reads this as God's interposition for the poor, silencing the offending mouth at last. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. Happy is the man whom God corrects, so Job should not despise the Almighty's chastening. JFB cautions that the suffering itself is not joyous, but the righteousness of him who sends it and the end for which it is sent make it cause for thankfulness, not complaint. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. God makes sore and binds up, wounds and makes whole with his hands. JFB gives the image of binding a wound, the healing art of the time consisting much in external applications. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. God shall deliver Job in six troubles, and in seven no evil shall touch him. JFB explains the Hebrew idiom: a number is fixed on for emphasis, then raised by one, so the sense is deliverance in all possible troubles. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. In famine God shall redeem from death, and in war from the power of the sword. The sword is personified as a living agent whose hands are stayed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. Job shall be hidden from the scourge of the tongue and not afraid of destruction when it comes. The promise covers slander as well as open calamity. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. At destruction and famine Job shall laugh, and not be afraid of the beasts of the earth. JFB clarifies Eliphaz's meaning: not laughter in spite of calamity, but because those calamities shall not come upon him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. Job shall be in league with the stones of the field, and the beasts of the field shall be at peace with him. JFB explains that stones shall not hurt his soil's fertility nor wild beasts his fruits, a picture whose full realization is yet future. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. Job shall know his tabernacle is in peace, and shall visit his habitation and not sin. JFB renders it as resting in the assurance that his dwelling is an abode of peace, and that numbering his herd will not disappoint his expectations. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. Job shall know his seed is great and his offspring as the grass of the earth. JFB notes the grass is properly herb-bearing seed, a picture of abundant increase. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. Job shall come to his grave in a full age, like a shock of corn coming in its season. JFB explains full age as ripeness for death, not mere length of years, and the corn as lifted up and carried home as sheaves. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. Lo, this we have searched, so it is; hear it and know it for your good. JFB notes the closing appeal is literally for thyself: the searched-out truth is offered for Job's own benefit. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
