The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Revelation 9 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 And the fifth angel sounded, and I saw a star fall from heaven unto the earth: and to him was given the key of the bottomless pit.

2 And he opened the bottomless pit; and there arose a smoke out of the pit, as the smoke of a great furnace; and the sun and the air were darkened by reason of the smoke of the pit.

3 And there came out of the smoke locusts upon the earth: and unto them was given power, as the scorpions of the earth have power.

4 And it was commanded them that they should not hurt the grass of the earth, neither any green thing, neither any tree; but only those men which have not the seal of God in their foreheads.

5 And to them it was given that they should not kill them, but that they should be tormented five months: and their torment was as the torment of a scorpion, when he striketh a man.

6 And in those days shall men seek death, and shall not find it; and shall desire to die, and death shall flee from them.

7 And the shapes of the locusts were like unto horses prepared unto battle; and on their heads were as it were crowns like gold, and their faces were as the faces of men.

8 And they had hair as the hair of women, and their teeth were as the teeth of lions.

9 And they had breastplates, as it were breastplates of iron; and the sound of their wings was as the sound of chariots of many horses running to battle.

10 And they had tails like unto scorpions, and there were stings in their tails: and their power was to hurt men five months.

11 And they had a king over them, which is the angel of the bottomless pit, whose name in the Hebrew tongue is Abaddon, but in the Greek tongue hath his name Apollyon.

12 One woe is past; and, behold, there come two woes more hereafter.

13 And the sixth angel sounded, and I heard a voice from the four horns of the golden altar which is before God,

14 Saying to the sixth angel which had the trumpet, Loose the four angels which are bound in the great river Euphrates.

15 And the four angels were loosed, which were prepared for an hour, and a day, and a month, and a year, for to slay the third part of men.

16 And the number of the army of the horsemen were two hundred thousand thousand: and I heard the number of them.

17 And thus I saw the horses in the vision, and them that sat on them, having breastplates of fire, and of jacinth, and brimstone: and the heads of the horses were as the heads of lions; and out of their mouths issued fire and smoke and brimstone.

18 By these three was the third part of men killed, by the fire, and by the smoke, and by the brimstone, which issued out of their mouths.

19 For their power is in their mouth, and in their tails: for their tails were like unto serpents, and had heads, and with them they do hurt.

20 And the rest of the men which were not killed by these plagues yet repented not of the works of their hands, that they should not worship devils, and idols of gold, and silver, and brass, and stone, and of wood: which neither can see, nor hear, nor walk:

21 Neither repented they of their murders, nor of their sorceries, nor of their fornication, nor of their thefts.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

The fifth trumpet opens the bottomless pit, and locusts like horses prepared for battle torment men who lack God's seal for five months without killing them, under a king Henry describes as a fallen angel whose Hebrew name Abaddon and Greek Apollyon mean destroyer. The sixth trumpet looses four angels at the Euphrates and an army of two hundred thousand thousand horsemen kills a third of men by fire, smoke and brimstone — yet, Henry concludes, the survivors do not repent of idolatry, murders, sorceries, fornication or thefts. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, Matthew Henry

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. The fifth angel sounded, and John saw a star fall from heaven to the earth, given the key of the bottomless pit. Henry reads the star as an eminent churchman turned antichrist, who, having ceased to be Christ's minister and lost the church's keys, becomes the devil's turnkey by Christ's permission, to loose hell's powers against the churches. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 2. He opened the bottomless pit, and smoke arose as from a great furnace, darkening the sun and the air. Henry notes the devil carries on his designs by blinding men's eyes, extinguishing light and knowledge, and promoting ignorance and error, first deceiving men and then destroying them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 3. Out of the smoke came locusts upon the earth, given power as the scorpions of the earth have power. Henry calls them the devil's emissaries headed by antichrist, the rout of antichristian orders promoting superstition, idolatry, error, and cruelty, like a plague of Egypt. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 4. They were commanded not to hurt the grass, any green thing, or any tree, but only those men who do not have the seal of God in their foreheads. Henry notes God's electing, distinguishing grace preserves his people from total and final apostasy, while the unsealed are left to the infection. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 5. They were given power not to kill men but to torment them five months, with torment like a scorpion's sting. Henry explains the hurt was spiritual, not military: not a persecution by fire and sword but a secret poison in souls, robbing them first of purity and then of peace, limited to a short season. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 6. In those days men shall seek death and shall not find it, desiring to die, while death flees from them. Henry notes the sharpness of the poison: those feeling its malignity in their consciences would grow weary of life, for a wounded spirit who can bear? Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 7. The locusts' shapes were like horses prepared for battle, with crowns like gold on their heads and faces as the faces of men. Henry reads the crowns as counterfeit authority with a show of assured victory, and the men's faces as a show of wisdom and sagacity masking the spirit of devils. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 8. They had hair as the hair of women, and their teeth were as the teeth of lions. Henry notes the allurements of seeming beauty and gaudy worship to ensnare minds, joined to real cruelty beneath the tenderness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 9. They had breastplates as of iron, and the sound of their wings was as the sound of chariots of many horses running to battle. Henry notes the defence of earthly powers and the mighty noise of their movement from country to country, like an army on the march. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 10. They had tails like scorpions, with stings in their tails, and their power was to hurt men five months. Henry notes that though they first soothed and flattered with a fair appearance, the cup of their abominations would at length bite like a serpent and sting like an adder. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 11. They had a king over them, the angel of the bottomless pit, whose name in Hebrew is Abaddon, and in Greek Apollyon. Henry describes him as a fallen angel, prince and governor of the infernal regions, whose name means destroyer, his business and delight being the destruction of souls. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 12. One woe is past, and behold, two more woes come hereafter. Henry marks the end of one woe and the beginning of another, in the chain of judgments. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 13. The sixth angel sounded, and John heard a voice from the four horns of the golden altar before God. Henry notes the church's enemies are restrained until God gives the word to loose them, and that the voice from the altar frames the judgment that follows. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 14. The voice said to the sixth angel with the trumpet, "Loose the four angels which are bound in the great river Euphrates." Henry notes these instruments of anger lay far from Europe, at the Euphrates, where the Turkish power had its rise, so no danger was apprehended from them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 15. The four angels were loosed, prepared for an hour, a day, a month, and a year, to slay the third part of men. Henry notes the time is fixed for beginning and end, and the execution limited to a third part, since God makes man's wrath praise him and restrains the remainder. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 16. The number of the army of horsemen was two hundred thousand thousand, and John heard their number. Henry notes the vastness of the armies of the Mahomedan empire, with infantry left to guess, showing the Lord of hosts has vast armies at his command. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 17. John saw horses with breastplates of fire, jacinth, and brimstone, heads as the heads of lions, and out of their mouths issued fire, smoke, and brimstone. Henry notes their fierce, martial equipage, clad in bright and costly armour with all ensigns of courage, zeal, and resolution. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 18. By these three plagues was the third part of men killed: by the fire, smoke, and brimstone issuing from the horses' mouths. Henry takes this as artillery, great guns first used by the Turks at Constantinople, terrible and destructive, going only as far as their commission suffered. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 19. Their power was in their mouths and in their tails, for their tails were like serpents with heads, and with them they hurt. Henry parallels the two visions: as antichrist had spiritual scorpions poisoning minds, so the Turks had stings to hurt and kill the bodies of those who had murdered so many souls. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 20. The rest of the men not killed by these plagues yet repented not of the works of their hands, worshipping devils and idols of gold, silver, brass, stone, and wood, which cannot see, hear, or walk. Henry lists their idolatry first among the flagrant crimes: they would not cast away images that could do them no good. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 21. Neither did they repent of their murders, sorceries, fornication, or thefts. Henry concludes that impenitence under divine judgments is itself the ruin of sinners: though God revealed his wrath against these crimes, they persisted, obstinate and judicially hardened, until destroyed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry

Unfamiliar terms

  • Bottomless pit. The abyss whose key is given to the fallen star at the fifth trumpet, from which smoke and locusts come. Interpreted Study notes, verses 1–2, Matthew Henry
  • Abaddon, Apollyon. The locusts' king, angel of the pit: Abaddon in Hebrew, Apollyon in Greek, both meaning destroyer, in Henry's note. Interpreted Study note, verse 11, Matthew Henry
