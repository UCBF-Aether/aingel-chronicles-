The Text — Psalms 123 (King James Version (1611) for Psalms 1–150; Charles Thomson, The Septuagint (1808) for Psalm 151)

1 Unto thee lift I up mine eyes, O thou that dwellest in the heavens.

2 Behold, as the eyes of servants look unto the hand of their masters, and as the eyes of a maiden unto the hand of her mistress; so our eyes wait upon the LORD our God, until that he have mercy upon us.

3 Have mercy upon us, O LORD, have mercy upon us: for we are exceedingly filled with contempt.

4 Our soul is exceedingly filled with the scorning of those that are at ease, and with the contempt of the proud.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

A four-verse psalm of waiting: eyes lifted to the one who dwells in the heavens, as the eyes of servants look to the hand of their masters and a maiden's to her mistress, so the people's eyes wait on the LORD their God until he has mercy on them. The doubled plea, "have mercy upon us, O LORD, have mercy upon us," is grounded in contempt endured: souls exceedingly filled with the scorning of those at ease and the contempt of the proud. Interpreted Psalm text above; study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Henry lifts eyes to God dwelling in heavens, where glory manifests as King in court, prospect and power beholding calamities and able to send salvation — and to throne where injured innocency appeals, even when enemies ask Where is now your God, for our God is in the heavens. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 2. Henry waits as servants on a master's hand in six ways: directing hand appointing work, supplying hand giving daily bread and grace sufficient, assisting hand for strength, protecting hand righting wronged servants, correcting hand humbly submitted to without fleeing as Hagar did, and rewarding hand — true Christians looking to God as rewarder, not hypocrites to the world's hand — until mercy comes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 3. Henry sues mercy without prescribing or pleading merit: have mercy upon us, repeated, for with men tender mercies are cruel and mockings cruel, but with the Lord there is mercy, sovereign remedy for whatever troubles the church, all we need to relieve and make easy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 4. Henry is surfeited with contempt: souls exceedingly filled, enemies never jeering enough, a sword in bones — reproached though eyes are up to God, peaceable saints abused, excellent ones undervalued, by ease-living epicures serving bellies and proud defying God, magnifying themselves by vilifying — yet scorn has ever been God's people's lot, Ishmael mocking Isaac, and mercy with God comforts barbarously used names. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
