The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Psalms 104 (King James Version (1611) for Psalms 1–150; Charles Thomson, The Septuagint (1808) for Psalm 151)

1 Bless the LORD, O my soul. O LORD my God, thou art very great; thou art clothed with honour and majesty.

2 Who coverest thyself with light as with a garment: who stretchest out the heavens like a curtain:

3 Who layeth the beams of his chambers in the waters: who maketh the clouds his chariot: who walketh upon the wings of the wind:

4 Who maketh his angels spirits; his ministers a flaming fire:

5 Who laid the foundations of the earth, that it should not be removed for ever.

6 Thou coveredst it with the deep as with a garment: the waters stood above the mountains.

7 At thy rebuke they fled; at the voice of thy thunder they hasted away.

8 They go up by the mountains; they go down by the valleys unto the place which thou hast founded for them.

9 Thou hast set a bound that they may not pass over; that they turn not again to cover the earth.

10 He sendeth the springs into the valleys, which run among the hills.

11 They give drink to every beast of the field: the wild asses quench their thirst.

12 By them shall the fowls of the heaven have their habitation, which sing among the branches.

13 He watereth the hills from his chambers: the earth is satisfied with the fruit of thy works.

14 He causeth the grass to grow for the cattle, and herb for the service of man: that he may bring forth food out of the earth;

15 And wine that maketh glad the heart of man, and oil to make his face to shine, and bread which strengtheneth man’s heart.

16 The trees of the LORD are full of sap; the cedars of Lebanon, which he hath planted;

17 Where the birds make their nests: as for the stork, the fir trees are her house.

18 The high hills are a refuge for the wild goats; and the rocks for the conies.

19 He appointed the moon for seasons: the sun knoweth his going down.

20 Thou makest darkness, and it is night: wherein all the beasts of the forest do creep forth.

21 The young lions roar after their prey, and seek their meat from God.

22 The sun ariseth, they gather themselves together, and lay them down in their dens.

23 Man goeth forth unto his work and to his labour until the evening.

24 O LORD, how manifold are thy works! in wisdom hast thou made them all: the earth is full of thy riches.

25 So is this great and wide sea, wherein are things creeping innumerable, both small and great beasts.

26 There go the ships: there is that leviathan, whom thou hast made to play therein.

27 These wait all upon thee; that thou mayest give them their meat in due season.

28 That thou givest them they gather: thou openest thine hand, they are filled with good.

29 Thou hidest thy face, they are troubled: thou takest away their breath, they die, and return to their dust.

30 Thou sendest forth thy spirit, they are created: and thou renewest the face of the earth.

31 The glory of the LORD shall endure for ever: the LORD shall rejoice in his works.

32 He looketh on the earth, and it trembleth: he toucheth the hills, and they smoke.

33 I will sing unto the LORD as long as I live: I will sing praise to my God while I have my being.

34 My meditation of him shall be sweet: I will be glad in the LORD.

35 Let the sinners be consumed out of the earth, and let the wicked be no more. Bless thou the LORD, O my soul. Praise ye the LORD.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

A creation psalm: bless the LORD, O my soul, for God clothed with honour and majesty, covering himself with light, stretching out the heavens like a curtain, making clouds his chariot and walking on the wings of the wind, with angels as spirits and ministers as flaming fire. The earth is founded and bounded so the waters do not cover it again; springs run among hills giving drink to beasts and birds, grass grows for cattle and herb, wine, oil and bread for man, cedars for birds, high hills for wild goats, moon for seasons, night for forest beasts and day for man's labour. The sea holds innumerable creatures and leviathan made to play in it, and all wait on God for meat in due season — troubled when he hides his face, created anew when he sends forth his spirit. The speaker will sing as long as he lives, and closes by asking that sinners be consumed out of the earth. Interpreted Psalm text above; study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Blessing the Lord, O my soul, O Lord my God, thou art very great, clothed with honour and majesty, is David stirring himself up to take hold on God in religious service: come, my soul, where art thou, what art thou thinking of? Here is work to be done, good work, angels' work, set about it in good earnest, let all the powers and faculties be engaged and employed in it. He looks up to divine glory shining in the upper world, of which, though it is one of the things not seen, faith is the evidence, beginning with reverence and holy awe: O Lord my God, thou art very great. It is the joy of the saints that he who is their God is a great God, and the grandeur of the prince is the pride and pleasure of all his good subjects. God's majesty is set forth by the figure great princes covet in public appearances, whose equipage, even of eastern kings who most affected pomp, is but as a glow-worm's light compared with the sun going forth in his strength. Princes appear great in their robes — and what are God's robes? Clothed with honour and majesty, seen in his works, which proclaim him infinitely wise and good and all that is great. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 2. Light as garment reveals God under a similitude, since his face cannot be seen: nearest to spirit among visible things, light clothes the One who dwells in light, while heavens stretched like a curtain keep the dazzling upper world at compassionate distance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 3. Beams laid in waters, clouds as chariot, walking on wind's wings: fluid air and water held as firm as rafters show a presence-chamber reared by power, and a God who rides swiftly above opposition in uncommon providence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 4. Who maketh his angels spirits; his ministers a flaming fire, is God's retinue or train of attendants: he makes his angels spirits. This is quoted by the apostle to prove the pre-eminence of Christ above the angels. The angels are here said to be his angels and his ministers, for they are under his dominion and at his disposal; they are winds, and a flame of fire — that is, they appeared in wind and fire, so some — or they are as swift as winds, and pure as flames; or he makes them spirits, so the apostle quotes it. They are spiritual beings; and, whatever vehicles they may have proper to their nature, it is certain they have not bodies as we have. Being spirits, they are so much the further removed from the encumbrances of the human nature and so much the nearer allied to the glories of the divine nature. And they are bright, and quick, and ascending, as fire, as a flame of fire. In Ezekiel's vision they ran and returned like a flash of lightning. Thence they are called seraphim — burners. Whatever they are, they are what God made them, what he still makes them; they derive their being from him, having the being he gave them, are held in being by him, and he makes what use he pleases of them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 5. Laying the foundations of the earth, that it should not be removed for ever, means he has founded the earth, though he hung it upon nothing, balanced by its own weight, yet as immovable as if laid upon the surest foundations, built upon her basis, so that though it has received a dangerous shock by the sin of man and the malice of hell strikes at it, yet it shall not be removed for ever, that is, not till the end of time, when it must give way to the new earth. Hammond's paraphrase is worth noting: God has fixed so strange a place for the earth that, being a heavy body, one would think it should fall every minute — and yet, which way soever we imagine it to stir, it must, contrary to the nature of such a body, fall upwards, and so can have no possible ruin but by tumbling into heaven. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 6. Covering the earth with the deep as with a garment, the waters standing above the mountains, is God setting bounds to the sea at creation: at first the earth, the more ponderous body, would subside of course and was covered with the deep, the waters above the mountains, and so it was unfit to be, as designed, a habitation for man. Therefore on the third day God said, let the waters under heaven be gathered to one place and let the dry land appear — this command is here called his rebuke, as if given because he was displeased that the earth was thus covered with water and not fit for man to dwell on, and power went along with this word, so it is also called the voice of his thunder, a mighty voice producing strange effects. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 7. At thy rebuke the waters fled, at the voice of thy thunder they hasted away, as if made sensible they were out of their place — they fled and hasted away, calling, not in vain, to the rocks and mountains to cover them, as the waters saw thee, O God, and were afraid, even fluid bodies receiving the impression of God's terror. But was the Lord displeased against the rivers? No, it was for the salvation of his people. God rebuked the waters for man's sake, to prepare room for him, for men must not be made as the fishes of the sea — they must have air to breathe in. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 8. Up mountains, down valleys, to the founded place: waters retiring at rebuke teach obedience, since unstable waters acquiesce in assigned bounds, and shall man alone be obstinate, and teach contentment with providence's bounds for our habitation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 9. Setting a bound that the waters may not pass over, that they turn not again to cover the earth, is God keeping the sea within bounds: the waters are forbidden to pass the limits set them, they may not, and therefore they do not, turn again to cover the earth. Once they did, in Noah's flood, because God bade them, but never since, because he forbids them, having promised not to drown the world again. God himself glories in this instance of his power and uses it as an argument with us to fear him — and if duly considered it would keep the world in awe of the Lord and his goodness, that the waters of the sea would soon cover the earth if God did not restrain them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 10. He sendeth the springs into the valleys, which run among the hills, is God providing fresh water for their drink: he sends the springs into the valleys. There is water enough indeed in the sea, that is, enough to drown us, but not one drop to refresh us, be we ever so thirsty — it is all so salt; and therefore God has graciously provided water fit to drink. Naturalists dispute about the origin of fountains; but, whatever are their second causes, here is their first cause; it is God that sends the springs into the brooks, which walk by easy steps between the hills, and receive increase from the rain-water that descends from them. These give drink, not only to man, and those creatures that are immediately useful to him, but to every beast of the field; for where God has given life he provides a livelihood and takes care of all the creatures. Even the wild asses, though untameable and therefore of no use to man, are welcome to quench their thirst; and we have no reason to grudge it them, for we are better provided for, though born like the wild ass's colt. We have reason to thank God for the plenty of fair water with which he has provided the habitable part of his earth, which otherwise would not be habitable. That ought to be reckoned a great mercy the want of which would be a great affliction; and the more common it is the greater mercy it is. Water is common for all. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

  • Verse 12. By them shall the fowls of the heaven have their habitation, which sing among the branches, is God providing suitable habitations for the birds: some birds, by instinct, make their nests in the bushes near rivers: by the springs that run among the hills some of the fowls of heaven have their habitation, which sing among the branches. They sing, according to their capacity, to the honour of their Creator and benefactor, and their singing may shame our silence. Our heavenly Father feeds them, and therefore they are easy and cheerful, and take no thought for the morrow. The birds being made to fly above the earth, they make their nests on high, in the tops of trees; it should seem as if nature had an eye to this in planting the cedars of Lebanon, that they might be receptacles for the birds. Those that fly heavenward shall not want resting-places. The stork is particularly mentioned; the fir-trees, which are very high, are her house, her castle. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 13. He watereth the hills from his chambers: the earth is satisfied with the fruit of thy works, is God watering the hills from his store-chambers: the heavens drop fatness; they hear the earth, but God hears them. He waters the hills from his chambers, from those chambers spoken of before, the beams of which he lays in the waters, those store-chambers, the clouds that distil fruitful showers. The hills that are not watered by the rivers, as Egypt was by the Nile, are watered by the rain from heaven, which is called the river of God, as Canaan was. Thus the earth is satisfied with the fruit of his works, either with the rain it drinks in — the earth knows when it has enough; it is a pity that any man should not — or with the products it brings forth. It is a satisfaction to the earth to bear the fruit of God's works for the benefit of man, for thus it answers the end of its creation. The food which God brings forth out of the earth is the fruit of his works, which the earth is satisfied with. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 14. He causeth the grass to grow for the cattle, and herb for the service of man: that he may bring forth food out of the earth, is the various and valuable food God brings forth: for the cattle there is grass, and the beasts of prey, that live not on grass, feed on those that do; for man there is herb, a better sort of grass — and a dinner of herbs and roots is not to be despised; nay, he is furnished with wine, and oil, and bread. We may observe here, concerning our food, that which will help to make us both humble and thankful. To make us humble let us consider that we have a necessary dependence upon God for all the supports of this life — we live upon alms; we are at his finding, for our own hands are not sufficient for us — that our food comes all out of the earth, to remind us whence we ourselves were taken and whither we must return — and that therefore we must not think to live by bread alone, for that will feed the body only, but must look into the word of God for the meat that endures to eternal life. Let us also consider that we are in this respect fellow-commoners with the beasts; the same earth, the same spot of ground, that brings grass for the cattle, brings corn for man. To make us thankful let us consider, first, that God not only provides for us, but for our servants. The cattle that are of use to man are particularly taken care of; grass is made to grow in great abundance for them, when the young lions, that are not for the service of man, often lack and suffer hunger. Second, that our food is nigh us, and ready to us. Having our habitation on the earth, there we have our storehouse, and depend not on the merchant-ships that bring food from afar. Third, that we have even from the products of the earth, not only for necessity, but for ornament and delight, so good a Master do we serve. First, does nature call for something to support it, and repair its daily decays? Here is bread, which strengthens man's heart, and is therefore called the staff of life; let none who have that complain of want. Secondly, does nature go further, and covet something pleasant? Here is wine, that makes glad the heart, refreshes the spirits, and exhilarates them, when it is soberly and moderately used, that we may not only go through our business, but go through it cheerfully. It is a pity that that should be abused to overcharge the heart, and unfit men for their duty, which was given to revive their heart and quicken them in their duty. Thirdly, is nature yet more humoursome, and does it crave something for ornament too? Here is that also out of the earth — oil to make the face to shine, that the countenance may not only be cheerful but beautiful, and we may be the more acceptable to one another. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 15. Wine, oil, and bread are Henry's lesson in humility and thankfulness: we live upon alms from the earth we came from and return to, fellow-commoners with beasts for grass and corn, yet provided not only for need but for gladness and shining. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 16. The trees of the Lord full of sap, the cedars of Lebanon which he planted, show divine providence furnishing vegetables with their food as well as animals: not only men's trees, which they take care of in orchards, parks and enclosures, but God's trees, growing in wildernesses and taken care of only by his providence, are full of sap and want no nourishment. Even the cedars of Lebanon, an open forest, though high and bulky and requiring a great deal of sap to feed them, have enough from the earth — they are trees he planted, and therefore he will protect and provide for them. We may apply this to the trees of righteousness, the planting of the Lord in his vineyard: these are full of sap, for what God plants he will water, and those planted in the house of the Lord shall flourish in the courts of our God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 17. Where the birds make their nests, the stork having her home in the fir trees, shows God providing suitable habitations: to men God has given discretion to build for themselves and for serviceable cattle, but some creatures God more immediately provides a settlement for. Some birds by instinct make their nests in bushes near rivers, and by the springs running among the hills the fowls of heaven have their habitation and sing among the branches — singing, according to their capacity, to the honour of their Creator and benefactor, and their singing may shame our silence, for our heavenly Father feeds them and therefore they are easy and cheerful and take no thought for the morrow. The stork is particularly mentioned, and the fir-trees, which are very high, are her house, her castle. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 18. The high hills for the wild goats, the rocks a refuge for the conies, continue God's provision of habitations for the smaller beasts: the wild goats, having neither strength nor swiftness to secure themselves, are guided by instinct to the high hills, which are a refuge to them, and the rabbits, also helpless animals, find shelter in the rocks, where they can set beasts of prey at defiance. Does God provide thus for the inferior creatures — and will he not himself be a refuge and dwelling-place to his own people? Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 19. He appointed the moon for seasons: the sun knoweth his going down, is the constant revolutions and succession of day and night, and the dominion of sun and moon over them: the heathen were so affected with the light and influence of the sun and moon, and their serviceableness to the earth, that they worshipped them as deities; and therefore the scripture takes all occasions to show that the gods they worshipped are the creatures and servants of the true God: he appointed the moon for seasons, for the measuring of the months, the directing of the seasons for the business of the husbandman, and the governing of the tides. The full and change, the increase and decrease, of the moon, exactly observe the appointment of the Creator; so does the sun, for he keeps as punctually to the time and place of his going down as if he were an intellectual being and knew what he did. God herein consults the comfort of man. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 20. Thou makest darkness, and it is night: wherein all the beasts of the forest do creep forth, is the shadows of the evening befriending the repose of the night: thou makest darkness and it is night, which, though black, contributes to the beauty of nature, and is as a foil to the light of the day; and under the protection of the night all the beasts of the forest creep forth to feed, which they are afraid to do in the day, God having put the fear and dread of man upon every beast of the earth, which contributes as much to man's safety as to his honour. See how nearly allied those are to the disposition of the wild beasts who wait for the twilight and have fellowship with the unfruitful works of darkness; and compare to this the danger of ignorance and melancholy, which are both as darkness to the soul; when, in either of those ways, it is night, then all the beasts of the forest creep forth. Satan's temptations then assault us and have advantage against us. Then the young lions roar after their prey; and, as naturalists tell us, their roaring terrifies the timorous beasts so that they have not strength nor spirit to escape from them, which otherwise they might do, and so they become an easy prey to them. They are said to seek their meat from God, because it is not prepared for them by the care and forecast of man, but more immediately by the providence of God. The roaring of the young lions, like the crying of the young ravens, is interpreted asking their meat of God. Does God put this construction upon the language of mere nature, even in venomous creatures? and shall he not much more interpret favourably the language of grace in his own people, though it be weak and broken, groanings which cannot be uttered? Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

  • Verse 22. The sun ariseth, they gather themselves together, and lay them down in their dens, is the light of the morning befriending the business of the day: the sun arises — for, as he knows his going down, so, thanks be to God, he knows his rising again — and then the wild beasts betake themselves to their rest; even they have some society among them, for they gather themselves together and lay down in their dens, which is a great mercy to the children of men, that while they are abroad, as becomes honest travellers, between sun and sun, care is taken that they shall not be set upon by wild beasts, for they are then drawn out of the field, and the sluggard shall have no ground to excuse himself from the business of the day with this, that there is a lion in the way. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 23. Man goeth forth unto his work and to his labour until the evening, is man's day against the beasts' night: therefore then man goes forth to his work and to his labour. The beasts of prey creep forth with fear; man goes forth with boldness, as one that has dominion. The beasts creep forth to spoil and do mischief; man goes forth to work and do good. There is the work of every day, which is to be done in its day, which man must apply to every morning — for the lights are set up for us to work by, not to play by — and which he must stick to till evening; it will be time enough to rest when the night comes, in which no man can work. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 24. O Lord, how manifold are thy works, in wisdom hast thou made them all, the earth full of thy riches, is the psalmist breaking out into wonder in the midst of his discourse: God's works are numerous, various, of many kinds and many of every kind, and yet in wisdom he made them all. When men undertake many works of different kinds, commonly some are neglected and not done with due care — but God's works, though many and of very different kinds, are all made in wisdom and with the greatest exactness, with not the least flaw or defect. The works of art, the more closely looked upon with microscopes, appear the more rough; the works of nature through those glasses appear more fine and exact. They are all made in wisdom, for they are all made to answer the end they were designed to serve, the good of the universe, in order to the glory of the universal Monarch. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 25. This great and wide sea, wherein are things creeping innumerable, both small and great beasts, shows the replenishing of the ocean: as the earth is full of God's riches, well stocked with animals and those well provided for, so that seldom does any creature die merely for want of food, so is this great and wide sea, which seems a useless part of the globe, at least not to answer the room it takes up — yet God has appointed it its place and made it serviceable to man, both for navigation, there going the ships in which goods are conveyed to vastly distant countries speedily and much more cheaply than by land-carriage, and as his storehouse for fish. God made not the sea in vain, any more than the earth; he made it to be inherited, for there are things swimming innumerable, small and great, serving for man's dainty food. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 26. There go the ships: there is that leviathan, whom thou hast made to play therein, is the replenishing of the ocean: as the earth is full of God's riches, well stocked with animals, and those well provided for, so that it is seldom that any creature dies merely for want of food, so is this great and wide sea which seems a useless part of the globe, at least not to answer the room it takes up; yet God has appointed it its place and made it serviceable to man both for navigation — there go the ships, in which goods are conveyed, to countries vastly distant, speedily and much more cheaply than by land-carriage — and also to be his storehouse for fish. God made not the sea in vain, any more than the earth; he made it to be inherited, for there are things swimming innumerable, both small and great animals, which serve for man's dainty food. The whale is particularly mentioned in the history of the creation and is here called the leviathan. He is made to play in the sea; he has nothing to do, as man has, who goes forth to his work; he has nothing to fear, as the beasts have, that lie down in their dens; and therefore he plays with the waters. It is a pity that any of the children of men, who have nobler powers and were made for nobler purposes, should live as if they were sent into the world, like leviathan into the waters, to play therein, spending all their time in pastime. The leviathan is said to play in the waters, because he is so well armed against all assaults that he sets them at defiance and laughs at the shaking of a spear. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 27. These all wait upon thee, that thou mayest give them their meat in due season, shows God's seasonable and plentiful provision: God is a bountiful benefactor, giving them their meat, opening his hand and filling them with good, supporting the armies of heaven and earth, with even the meanest creatures not below his cognizance, open-handed in the gifts of his bounty, a great and good housekeeper providing for so large a family. And they are patient expectants from him: they seek their food according to the natural instinct God put into them and in the proper season for it, affecting no other food, nor at any other time, than nature has ordained. They do their part for obtaining it: what God gives them they gather, expecting not that providence should put it into their mouths, and what they gather they are satisfied with, filled with good, desiring no more than what God sees fit for them — which may shame our murmurings, discontent and dissatisfaction with our lot. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 28. That thou givest them they gather, thou openest thine hand, they are filled with good, is the creatures' part in that provision: what God gives they gather, and what they gather they are satisfied with, filled with good, desiring no more than God sees fit — the open hand of a bountiful housekeeper meeting patient, gathering expectants who will not murmur at their portion. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 29. Hiding thy face, they are troubled, taking away their breath, they die and return to their dust, shows God's absolute power and sovereign dominion, by which every species is continued though individuals daily die and drop off: thou hidest thy face, withdrawing thy supporting power and supplying bounty, and they are troubled immediately, for every creature has as necessary a dependence upon God's favours as every saint is sensible he has, saying with David, thou didst hide thy face and I was troubled. God's displeasure against this lower world for man's sin is the cause of all the vanity and burden the whole creation groans under. Thou takest away their breath, which is in thy hand, and then, and not till then, they die and return to their dust, to their first principles — the spirit of the beast, which goes downward, is at God's command, as well as the spirit of man, which goes upward. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 30. Sending forth thy spirit, they are created, and thou renewest the face of the earth, shows all preserved notwithstanding in succession: the same spirit, the same divine will and power by which they were all created at first, still preserves the several sorts of creatures in their being, place and usefulness, so that though one generation passes away another comes, and from time to time they are created, new ones rising up instead of the old, and this is a continual creation. Thus the face of the earth is renewed from day to day by the light of the sun, which beautifies it anew every morning, and from year to year by its products, which enrich it anew every spring and put quite another face upon it from what it had all winter. The world is as full of creatures as if none died, for the place of those that die is filled up — and this, the Jews say, is to be applied to the resurrection, which every spring is an emblem of, when a new world rises out of the ashes of the old one.   • Verse 31. The glory of the Lord shall endure for ever, the Lord shall rejoice in his works, praises him as a great God of matchless perfection and as a gracious God: his glory shall endure to the end of time in his works of creation and providence, and to eternity in the felicity and adorations of saints and angels — man's glory is fading, God's glory everlasting, creatures change but with the Creator there is no variableness. And the Lord shall rejoice in his works, continuing that complacency in the products of his own wisdom and goodness which he had when he saw everything he had made and behold it was very good, and rested the seventh day. We often do what, upon review, we cannot rejoice in, but are displeased at and wish undone again, blaming our own management — but God always rejoices in his works, because they are all done in wisdom. We regret our bounty and beneficence, but God never does; he rejoices in the works of his grace, for his gifts and callings are without repentance.   • Verse 33. I will sing unto the Lord as long as I live, I will sing praise to my God while I have my being, is the psalmist's own resolve to be much in praising him: sing unto the Lord, unto my God, praising him as Jehovah the Creator and as my God, a God in covenant with me, and this not now only but as long as I live and while I have my being.   • Verse 34. My meditation of him shall be sweet, I will be glad in the Lord, is his joy in the work: meditation shall be sweet because it shall be fixed and close, affecting and influencing, and therefore sweet — for thoughts of God will then be most pleasing when they are most powerful.   • Verse 35. Let the sinners be consumed out of the earth, and let the wicked be no more, is terror to the wicked closing the psalm: those that oppose the God of power and fight against him will certainly be consumed, for none can prosper that harden themselves against the Almighty; those that rebel against the light of such convincing evidence of God's being and refuse to serve him whom all the creatures serve will justly be consumed; and those that make that earth groan under the burden of their impieties, which God thus fills with his riches, deserve to be consumed out of it and that it should spue them out. Those who heartily desire to praise God themselves cannot but have a holy indignation at those who blaspheme and dishonour him, and a holy satisfaction in the prospect of their destruction and the honour God will get upon them. Even this ought to be the matter of their praise: while sinners are consumed out of the earth, let my soul bless the Lord that I am not cast away with the workers of iniquity but distinguished from them by special grace — and when the wicked are no more I hope to be praising God world without end, so praise ye the Lord, let all about me join in praising God. Hallelujah — the first time we meet with it, upon occasion of the destruction of the wicked, as the last time we meet with it is upon a similar occasion, when New-Testament Babylon is consumed. Blessing the Lord, O my soul, O Lord my God, thou art very great, clothed with honour and majesty, is David stirring himself up to take hold on God in religious service: come, my soul, where art thou, what art thou thinking of?   • Verse 14. We may observe here, concerning our food, that which will help to make us both humble and thankful. To make us humble let us consider that we have a necessary dependence upon God for all the supports of this life — we live upon alms; we are at his finding, for our own hands are not sufficient for us — that our food comes all out of the earth, to remind us whence we ourselves were taken and whither we must return — and that therefore we must not think to live by bread alone, for that will feed the body only, but must look into the word of God for the meat that endures to eternal life. Let us also consider that we are in this respect fellow-commoners with the beasts; the same earth, the same spot of ground, that brings grass for the cattle, brings corn for man. To make us thankful let us consider, first, that God not only provides for us, but for our servants. The cattle that are of use to man are particularly taken care of; grass is made to grow in great abundance for them, when the young lions, that are not for the service of man, often lack and suffer hunger. Second, that our food is nigh us, and ready to us. Having our habitation on the earth, there we have our storehouse, and depend not on the merchant-ships that bring food from afar. First, does nature call for something to support it, and repair its daily decays? Here is bread, which strengthens man's heart, and is therefore called the staff of life; let none who have that complain of want. Secondly, does nature go further, and covet something pleasant? Here is wine, that makes glad the heart, refreshes the spirits, and exhilarates them, when it is soberly and moderately used, that we may not only go through our business, but go through it cheerfully. It is a pity that that should be abused to overcharge the heart, and unfit men for their duty, which was given to revive their heart and quicken them in their duty. Thirdly, is nature yet more humoursome, and does it crave something for ornament too? Here is that also out of the earth — oil to make the face to shine, that the countenance may not only be cheerful but beautiful, and we may be the more acceptable to one another. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 29. Hiding thy face, they are troubled, taking away their breath, they die and return to their dust, shows God's absolute power and sovereign dominion, by which every species is continued though individuals daily die and drop off: thou hidest thy face, withdrawing thy supporting power and supplying bounty, and they are troubled immediately, for every creature has as necessary a dependence upon God's favours as every saint is sensible he has, saying with David, thou didst hide thy face and I was troubled. God's displeasure against this lower world for man's sin is the cause of all the vanity and burden the whole creation groans under. Thou takest away their breath, which is in thy hand, and then, and not till then, they die and return to their dust, to their first principles — the spirit of the beast, which goes downward, is at God's command, as well as the spirit of man, which goes upward. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 30. Sending forth thy spirit, they are created, and thou renewest the face of the earth, shows all preserved notwithstanding in succession: the same spirit, the same divine will and power by which they were all created at first, still preserves the several sorts of creatures in their being, place and usefulness, so that though one generation passes away another comes, and from time to time they are created, new ones rising up instead of the old, and this is a continual creation. Thus the face of the earth is renewed from day to day by the light of the sun, which beautifies it anew every morning, and from year to year by its products, which enrich it anew every spring and put quite another face upon it from what it had all winter. The world is as full of creatures as if none died, for the place of those that die is filled up — and this, the Jews say, is to be applied to the resurrection, which every spring is an emblem of, when a new world rises out of the ashes of the old one. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 31. The glory of the Lord shall endure for ever, the Lord shall rejoice in his works, praises him as a great God of matchless perfection and as a gracious God: his glory shall endure to the end of time in his works of creation and providence, and to eternity in the felicity and adorations of saints and angels — man's glory is fading, God's glory everlasting, creatures change but with the Creator there is no variableness. And the Lord shall rejoice in his works, continuing that complacency in the products of his own wisdom and goodness which he had when he saw everything he had made and behold it was very good, and rested the seventh day. We often do what, upon review, we cannot rejoice in, but are displeased at and wish undone again, blaming our own management — but God always rejoices in his works, because they are all done in wisdom. We regret our bounty and beneficence, but God never does; he rejoices in the works of his grace, for his gifts and callings are without repentance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 32. Look making earth tremble and touch making hills smoke recall Sinai and volcanoes as emblems: if a look and a touch do this to proud sinners, what will the weight of the heavy hand do, while God rejoices in works observant of him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 33. I will sing unto the Lord as long as I live, I will sing praise to my God while I have my being, is the psalmist's own resolve to be much in praising him: sing unto the Lord, unto my God, praising him as Jehovah the Creator and as my God, a God in covenant with me, and this not now only but as long as I live and while I have my being. Because we have our being from God and depend upon him for its support and continuance, as long as we live and have our being we must continue to praise God — and when we have no life, no being, on earth, we hope to have a better life and better being in a better world, and there to be doing this work in a better manner and in better company. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 34. My meditation of him shall be sweet, I will be glad in the Lord, is his joy in the work: meditation shall be sweet because it shall be fixed and close, affecting and influencing, and therefore sweet — for thoughts of God will then be most pleasing when they are most powerful. Divine meditation is a very sweet duty to all that are sanctified: I will be glad in the Lord, it shall be a pleasure to me to praise him, I will be glad of all opportunities to set forth his glory, and I will rejoice in the Lord always and in him only, for all my joys shall centre in him and in him they shall be full. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 35. Let the sinners be consumed out of the earth, and let the wicked be no more, is terror to the wicked closing the psalm: those that oppose the God of power and fight against him will certainly be consumed, for none can prosper that harden themselves against the Almighty; those that rebel against the light of such convincing evidence of God's being and refuse to serve him whom all the creatures serve will justly be consumed; and those that make that earth groan under the burden of their impieties, which God thus fills with his riches, deserve to be consumed out of it and that it should spue them out. Those who heartily desire to praise God themselves cannot but have a holy indignation at those who blaspheme and dishonour him, and a holy satisfaction in the prospect of their destruction and the honour God will get upon them. Even this ought to be the matter of their praise: while sinners are consumed out of the earth, let my soul bless the Lord that I am not cast away with the workers of iniquity but distinguished from them by special grace — and when the wicked are no more I hope to be praising God world without end, so praise ye the Lord, let all about me join in praising God. Hallelujah — the first time we meet with it, upon occasion of the destruction of the wicked, as the last time we meet with it is upon a similar occasion, when New-Testament Babylon is consumed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
