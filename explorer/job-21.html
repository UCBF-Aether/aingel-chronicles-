The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 21 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 But Job answered and said,

2 Hear diligently my speech, and let this be your consolations.

3 Suffer me that I may speak; and after that I have spoken, mock on.

4 As for me, is my complaint to man? and if it were so, why should not my spirit be troubled?

5 Mark me, and be astonished, and lay your hand upon your mouth.

6 Even when I remember I am afraid, and trembling taketh hold on my flesh.

7 Wherefore do the wicked live, become old, yea, are mighty in power?

8 Their seed is established in their sight with them, and their offspring before their eyes.

9 Their houses are safe from fear, neither is the rod of God upon them.

10 Their bull gendereth, and faileth not; their cow calveth, and casteth not her calf.

11 They send forth their little ones like a flock, and their children dance.

12 They take the timbrel and harp, and rejoice at the sound of the organ.

13 They spend their days in wealth, and in a moment go down to the grave.

14 Therefore they say unto God, Depart from us; for we desire not the knowledge of thy ways.

15 What is the Almighty, that we should serve him? and what profit should we have, if we pray unto him?

16 Lo, their good is not in their hand: the counsel of the wicked is far from me.

17 How oft is the candle of the wicked put out! and how oft cometh their destruction upon them! God distributeth sorrows in his anger.

18 They are as stubble before the wind, and as chaff that the storm carrieth away.

19 God layeth up his iniquity for his children: he rewardeth him, and he shall know it.

20 His eyes shall see his destruction, and he shall drink of the wrath of the Almighty.

21 For what pleasure hath he in his house after him, when the number of his months is cut off in the midst?

22 Shall any teach God knowledge? seeing he judgeth those that are high.

23 One dieth in his full strength, being wholly at ease and quiet.

24 His breasts are full of milk, and his bones are moistened with marrow.

25 And another dieth in the bitterness of his soul, and never eateth with pleasure.

26 They shall lie down alike in the dust, and the worms shall cover them.

27 Behold, I know your thoughts, and the devices which ye wrongfully imagine against me.

28 For ye say, Where is the house of the prince? and where are the dwelling places of the wicked?

29 Have ye not asked them that go by the way? and do ye not know their tokens,

30 That the wicked is reserved to the day of destruction? they shall be brought forth to the day of wrath.

31 Who shall declare his way to his face? and who shall repay him what he hath done?

32 Yet shall he be brought to the grave, and shall remain in the tomb.

33 The clods of the valley shall be sweet unto him, and every man shall draw after him, as there are innumerable before him.

34 How then comfort ye me in vain, seeing in your answers there remaineth falsehood?

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Job answers Zophar with observed fact against theory: the wicked live, become old, and are mighty in power; their children dance before them, their houses are safe from fear, and they spend their days in wealth and go down to the grave in a moment. If God lays up iniquity for the children, Job asks, why does the wicked man himself prosper and say to God, depart from us? The chapter is Job’s closest approach to the disputed question of whether prosperity marks the righteous. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Job answers, asking for a hearing before the friends mock again. The chapter is his closest approach to the disputed question: whether prosperity marks the true church. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Hear diligently Job's speech, and let this be your consolations. JFB notes that listening calmly will itself count as consolations, alluding to Eliphaz's boasted consolations Job felt as aggravations. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Suffer Job to speak, and after he has spoken, mock on. JFB renders it literally as begin your mockings after he speaks, accepting their scorn as the price of a hearing. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. As for Job, is his complaint to man? If it were so, why should his spirit not be troubled? JFB notes his difficulty is not with man but with God: why God afflicts him as if he were the guilty hypocrite alleged. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Mark Job, be astonished, and lay your hand upon your mouth. JFB explains the hand on the mouth as the gesture of silence, enough in Job's case to awe them into silence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Even when Job remembers, he is afraid, and trembling takes hold on his flesh. JFB asks whether they can wonder he broke out in complaints, when the struggle was not with men but with the Almighty, reconciling innocent woes with divine justice. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Why do the wicked live, become old, and grow mighty in power? JFB notes old opposes the friends' assertion that sinners are cut off early. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Their seed is established in their sight, and their offspring before their eyes. JFB notes this opposes Bildad and Eliphaz on cut-off children: the wicked's children flourish visibly. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Their houses are safe from fear, and the rod of God is not upon them. JFB renders it literally peace from fear: their house is peace itself, far removed from fear. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Their bull genders and fails not, their cow calves and casts not her calf. JFB explains the first clause as easy conception, the second as happy birth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. They send forth their little ones like a flock, and their children dance. JFB explains the little ones as lambkins sent out to happy sports under the skies, dancing not formally but skipping like lambs. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. They take the timbrel and harp, and rejoice at the sound of the organ. JFB renders take as lifting up the voice in song, timbrel as tambourine, and organ as pipe, so voice, strings, and winds enumerate all kinds of music. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. They spend their days in wealth, and in a moment go down to the grave. JFB notes wealth is old English for prosperity, and in a moment means not by lingering disease: lengthened life, prosperity, and a sudden painless death. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Therefore they say unto God, depart from us, for we desire not the knowledge of thy ways. JFB renders therefore as and yet: they say this not in words but virtually by conduct. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. What is the Almighty that we should serve him, and what profit should we have if we pray unto him? JFB notes sinners ask not what is right but what profits self, forgetting that wanting religion will cost self infinitely more. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Lo, their good is not in their hand; the counsel of the wicked is far from Job. JFB explains good is in God's hand, not theirs, which is Job's difficulty, and renders the second clause as may the counsel of the wicked be far from me, lest he be thought to admire it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. How often is the candle of the wicked put out, and how often does their destruction come upon them? JFB notes Job here quotes the friends' assertion to refute it, borrowing Bildad's lamp image to question its truth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. They are as stubble before the wind, and as chaff that the storm carries away. JFB notes Job alludes to Bildad's sentiment, using his own previous words against him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. God lays up the wicked's iniquity for his children, rewarding him so he shall know it. JFB calls equally questionable the friends' assertion that children are punished if the father is not, and that God rewards him here so he knows it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. His eyes shall see his destruction, and he shall drink of the wrath of the Almighty. JFB calls questionable the assertion that the sinner sees his own and his children's destruction in his lifetime, with drink as the cup of wrath. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. For what pleasure has he in his house after him, when the number of his months is cut off in the midst? JFB explains the friends' argument: what has he to do with his children when dead, unless his eyes see their destruction, and cut off as the allotted months fulfilled. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Shall any teach God knowledge, seeing he judges those that are high? JFB replies that in these assertions the friends try to teach God how he ought to deal with men, rather than prove he does so in fact, since God gives prosperity and adversity as he pleases on inscrutable principles. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. One dies in his full strength, being wholly at ease and quiet. JFB renders it literally in the bone of his perfection, the full strength of unimpaired prosperity. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. His breasts are full of milk, and his bones are moistened with marrow. JFB explains breasts as skins or vessels for fluids, or stations of herds near water, with the body compared to a well-watered field, abundant substance and vigorous health. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. And another dies in the bitterness of his soul, and never eats with pleasure. The same death meets opposite lives: fullness for one, bitterness without pleasure for the other. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. They shall lie down alike in the dust, and the worms shall cover them. Prosperous and bitter alike share dust and worms. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. Behold, Job knows the friends' thoughts, and the devices they wrongfully imagine against him. JFB notes their wrongful thoughts are stated but not honestly named: they insinuate his guilt without naming him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. For the friends say, where is the house of the prince, and where are the dwelling places of the wicked? JFB notes house refers to the fall of Job's eldest son's house, and prince in a bad sense as tyrant or oppressor, with dwelling places as pavilions of a great emir. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. Have you not asked them that go by the way, and do you not know their tokens? JFB notes Job appeals to travellers' observation, impartial witnesses unconnected with him, opposing Bildad and Zophar. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. The wicked is reserved to the day of destruction, and shall be brought forth to the day of wrath. JFB notes travellers testify the wicked is spared now and reserved hereafter: Job does not deny future punishment, only punishment in this life, with lots reversed hereafter. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 31. Who shall declare his way to his face, and who shall repay him what he has done? JFB asks who dares charge him openly in this present life, granting he shall be repaid hereafter. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 32. Yet shall he be brought to the grave, and shall remain in the tomb. JFB renders yet as and, with solemn pomp to the graves, and remain as watching on the tomb, his remembrance preserved by monument, opposing Bildad's forgotten name. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 33. The clods of the valley shall be sweet unto him, and every man shall draw after him, as innumerable before him. JFB notes the earth is light upon him and his repose sweet, sharing the common lot of mortals, with most men following in his steps. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 34. How then do the friends comfort Job in vain, seeing in their answers there remains falsehood? JFB explains falsehood as transgression: their consolations are contradicted by facts and betray evil intent against him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Where the speakers disagree

Zophar holds that the wicked’s triumph is brief and ruin certain. Job answers from what he sees: the wicked grow old and mighty, their children dance, their houses are safe, and they die in a moment without the terrors Zophar describes. The friends argue from doctrine about what must happen; Job argues from observation about what does happen. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
