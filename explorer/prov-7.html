The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Proverbs 7 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 My son, keep my words, and lay up my commandments with thee.

2 Keep my commandments, and live; and my law as the apple of thine eye.

3 Bind them upon thy fingers, write them upon the table of thine heart.

4 Say unto wisdom, Thou art my sister; and call understanding thy kinswoman:

5 That they may keep thee from the strange woman, from the stranger which flattereth with her words.

6 For at the window of my house I looked through my casement,

7 And beheld among the simple ones, I discerned among the youths, a young man void of understanding,

8 Passing through the street near her corner; and he went the way to her house,

9 In the twilight, in the evening, in the black and dark night:

10 And, behold, there met him a woman with the attire of an harlot, and subtil of heart.

11 (She is loud and stubborn; her feet abide not in her house:

12 Now is she without, now in the streets, and lieth in wait at every corner.)

13 So she caught him, and kissed him, and with an impudent face said unto him,

14 I have peace offerings with me; this day have I payed my vows.

15 Therefore came I forth to meet thee, diligently to seek thy face, and I have found thee.

16 I have decked my bed with coverings of tapestry, with carved works, with fine linen of Egypt.

17 I have perfumed my bed with myrrh, aloes, and cinnamon.

18 Come, let us take our fill of love until the morning: let us solace ourselves with loves.

19 For the goodman is not at home, he is gone a long journey:

20 He hath taken a bag of money with him, and will come home at the day appointed.

21 With her much fair speech she caused him to yield, with the flattering of her lips she forced him.

22 He goeth after her straightway, as an ox goeth to the slaughter, or as a fool to the correction of the stocks;

23 Till a dart strike through his liver; as a bird hasteth to the snare, and knoweth not that it is for his life.

24 Hearken unto me now therefore, O ye children, and attend to the words of my mouth.

25 Let not thine heart decline to her ways, go not astray in her paths.

26 For she hath cast down many wounded: yea, many strong men have been slain by her.

27 Her house is the way to hell, going down to the chambers of death.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Keep wisdom as a sister and understanding as kinswoman, the chapter urges, and then shows why in a street scene: a young man void of understanding passes the corner at twilight and is met, flattered, perfumed and persuaded by the loud, stubborn woman whose husband is away. He follows like an ox to slaughter, and the warning closes it: her house is the way to death, going down to its chambers. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Keeping words and laying up commandments is the safe laying-up Henry urges, for the word kept close guards against the fatal effects of passion and Satan's snares. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 2. Keeping commandments and living, with the law as the apple of the eye, means treasuring them as one cannot live without them, since the law in the heart is the eye of the soul. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 3. Binding them on the fingers and writing them on the table of the heart is letting the word dwell in us where it will always be at hand to be read. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 4. Calling wisdom sister and understanding kinswoman is the close kinship with God's word that confirms dread of sin and resolution against it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 5. Wisdom and understanding keep the son from the strange woman who flatters with her words, for the word dwelling in us is the guard Henry sets against her snares. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 6. Looking through the casement at the window opens Henry's affecting example of youthful lusts, a history or parable meant to show the danger of even going near temptation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 7. The youth void of understanding among the simple ones is the unwary figure of the example, whose disregard of God's commands is where self-ruined misery begins. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 8. Passing near her corner and going the way to her house is the first step Henry warns against, for we must ever avoid the neighbourhood of vice. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 9. Walking in twilight, evening, and black dark night shows the secrecy of the path, yet Henry's example is set plainly before the eyes so none may dare venture on it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 10. The woman in harlot's attire, subtil of heart, is the seducer whose arts the chapter displays, that the reader may see the snare before it is spread for him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 11. Loud and stubborn, her feet not abiding in her house, she embodies the restless vice whose neighbourhood Henry bids the reader ever avoid. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 12. Now without, now in the streets, lying in wait at every corner, she spreads snares for souls, and prayer to be kept from running into temptation is the defence Henry urges. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 13. Catching him and kissing him with an impudent face shows the boldness of the pleasant sin, which most easily gains the heart and closes it against repentance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 14. Her claim of peace offerings and vows paid is the pious colour given to sin, yet Henry's rule stands: do nothing till thou hast well considered the end of it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 15. Coming forth diligently to seek his face shows the tempter seeking the victim, and the reader who has seen another fall headlong should not dance on the same rock's edge. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 16. The bed decked with tapestry, carved works, and fine linen of Egypt is the delight sin offers, yet one hour of following anguish would outweigh a lifetime of such delights. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 17. Perfuming the bed with myrrh, aloes, and cinnamon is the pleasantness Henry warns makes the sin more dangerous, because it gains the heart before reason can weigh the end. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 18. Taking fill of love until morning and solacing in loves is the promised delight whose end Henry bids the reader consider before venturing. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 19. The goodman not at home, gone a long journey, is the false security of secrecy, yet the example is told precisely so the reader will not trust such cover. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 20. The bag of money taken and return at the day appointed prolongs the false security, but considering the end exposes it as a snare, not safety. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 21. With much fair speech she caused him to yield, and flattering lips forced him, showing how pleasant sins gain the heart and shut it against repentance. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 22. Going after her straightway, as an ox to slaughter or a fool to the stocks, is the headlong fall Henry pictures for one who ventures on temptation he has been warned against. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 23. Till a dart strikes through his liver, as a bird hastens to the snare not knowing it is for his life, the end of the pleasant sin is ruin the victim never weighed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 24. Hearkening now and attending to the words of the mouth is the application of the example, turning from the seducer's voice to the father's counsel. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 25. Letting not the heart decline to her ways nor going astray in her paths is the daily prayer Henry urges: to be kept from running into temptation rather than inviting the enemy's snares. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 26. She has cast down many wounded, and many strong men have been slain by her, proving the danger is not for the weak only, and none should dare the edge. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 27. Her house is the way to hell, going down to the chambers of death, the end Henry bids every reader consider before a single step toward it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
