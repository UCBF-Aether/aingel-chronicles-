The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Song of Songs 1 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 The song of songs, which is Solomon’s.

2 Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth: for thy love is better than wine.

3 Because of the savour of thy good ointments thy name is as ointment poured forth, therefore do the virgins love thee.

4 Draw me, we will run after thee: the king hath brought me into his chambers: we will be glad and rejoice in thee, we will remember thy love more than wine: the upright love thee.

5 I am black, but comely, O ye daughters of Jerusalem, as the tents of Kedar, as the curtains of Solomon.

6 Look not upon me, because I am black, because the sun hath looked upon me: my mother’s children were angry with me; they made me the keeper of the vineyards; but mine own vineyard have I not kept.

7 Tell me, O thou whom my soul loveth, where thou feedest, where thou makest thy flock to rest at noon: for why should I be as one that turneth aside by the flocks of thy companions?

8 If thou know not, O thou fairest among women, go thy way forth by the footsteps of the flock, and feed thy kids beside the shepherds’ tents.

9 I have compared thee, O my love, to a company of horses in Pharaoh’s chariots.

10 Thy cheeks are comely with rows of jewels, thy neck with chains of gold.

11 We will make thee borders of gold with studs of silver.

12 While the king sitteth at his table, my spikenard sendeth forth the smell thereof.

13 A bundle of myrrh is my well-beloved unto me; he shall lie all night betwixt my breasts.

14 My beloved is unto me as a cluster of camphire in the vineyards of Engedi.

15 Behold, thou art fair, my love; behold, thou art fair; thou hast doves’ eyes.

16 Behold, thou art fair, my beloved, yea, pleasant: also our bed is green.

17 The beams of our house are cedar, and our rafters of fir.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

A love dialogue opens the book: the beloved longs for her lover's presence, praises his name as poured-out ointment, and answers the daughters of Jerusalem about her sun-darkened skin, her work as keeper of vineyards, and her search for the shepherd she loves. Praise is exchanged, from Pharaoh's chariot horses to spikenard, myrrh and the vineyards of Engedi. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. This is the Song of songs, excellent above any others, Henry says, for it is wholly taken up with describing the excellences of Christ and the love between him and his redeemed people. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 2. Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth, for thy love is better than wine. The church, or rather the believer, speaks here as the spouse of the King, the Messiah, and the kisses of his mouth mean those assurances of pardon with which believers are favoured, filling them with peace and joy in believing. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 3. Because of the savour of thy good ointments, thy name is as ointment poured forth, therefore do the virgins love thee. The name of Christ is not like ointment sealed up, Henry says, but like ointment poured forth, denoting the freeness and fulness of his grace set forth by the gospel. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 4. Draw me, we will run after thee; the king has brought me into his chambers. Believers entreat Christ to draw them by the quickening influences of his Spirit, and the more clearly we discern his glory, the more sensible we are that we cannot follow him suitably, yet the more desirous of doing it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 5. I am black, but comely, O ye daughters of Jerusalem, as the tents of Kedar, as the curtains of Solomon. The believer is black, as defiled and sinful by nature, but comely, as renewed by divine grace to the holy image of God, Henry explains. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 6. Look not upon me because I am black, because the sun has looked upon me; my mother's children were angry with me and made me keeper of the vineyards, but mine own vineyard have I not kept. The blackness was owing to hard usage suffered, Henry notes, which caused her to neglect the care of her soul. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 7. Tell me, O thou whom my soul loveth, where thou feedest, where thou makest thy flock to rest at noon. Observe the title given to Christ, Henry says: those whose souls love Jesus may come to him boldly and humbly plead with him, and Christ has rest for them even at a noon-time of troubles. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 8. If thou know not, O thou fairest among women, go forth by the footsteps of the flock and feed thy kids beside the shepherds' tents. God is ready to answer prayer, Henry notes: follow the track, ask for the good old way, and sit under the direction of good ministers. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 9. I have compared thee, O my love, to a company of horses in Pharaoh's chariots. The Bridegroom gives high praises of his spouse, Henry says: in the sight of Christ believers are the excellent of the earth, fitted to be instruments for promoting his glory. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 10. Thy cheeks are comely with rows of jewels, thy neck with chains of gold. The spiritual gifts and graces Christ bestows on every true believer are described by the ornaments then in use, Henry explains. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 11. We will make thee borders of gold with studs of silver. The graces of the saints are many but depend upon each other, Henry notes, and he who is the Author will be the Finisher of the good work. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 12. While the king sits at his table, my spikenard sends forth its smell. The grace received from Christ's fulness springs forth into lively exercises of faith, affection, and gratitude, Henry says, yet Christ, not his gifts, is most precious to them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 13. A bundle of myrrh is my wellbeloved unto me; he shall lie all night betwixt my breasts. Believers resolve to lodge Christ in their hearts all the night, Henry says, during the continuance of the troubles of life. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 14. My beloved is unto me as a cluster of camphire in the vineyards of Engedi. The word translated camphire signifies atonement or propitiation, Henry notes: Christ is dear to all believers because he is the propitiation for their sins. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 15. Behold, thou art fair, my love; thou hast doves' eyes. Christ takes delight in the good work his grace has wrought on believers' souls, Henry says, and the spouse has a humble, modest eye, discovering simplicity and godly sincerity, eyes enlightened by the Holy Spirit, that blessed Dove. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 16. Behold, thou art fair, my beloved, yea, pleasant: also our bed is green. The church expresses her value for Christ, Henry notes: thou art the great Original, but I am but a faint and imperfect copy, and Christ is fair yet pleasant. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Verse 17. The beams of our house are cedar, and our rafters of fir. Whether the believer is in the courts of the Lord or in retirement, at daily labours or confined on a bed of sickness, Henry says, a sense of the divine presence will turn the place into a paradise. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)

Unfamiliar terms

Plain-language glosses for words in this chapter that modern readers may trip over. They are editorial explanation, not Scripture.

  • spikenard. A costly fragrant ointment, named in the chapter among the perfumes of the lovers' meeting. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • camphire. A fragrant cluster-plant of the vineyards of Engedi; Henry reads the word as signifying atonement or propitiation. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry (Concise)
  • Kedar. Named only in the comparison, as the tents of Kedar; the chapter gives no further account of the place, so its identification is left open here. Unresolved No source consulted for this page identifies Kedar further
