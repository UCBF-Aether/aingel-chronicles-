The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Genesis 32 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 And Jacob went on his way, and the angels of God met him.

2 And when Jacob saw them, he said, This is God’s host: and he called the name of that place Mahanaim.

3 And Jacob sent messengers before him to Esau his brother unto the land of Seir, the country of Edom.

4 And he commanded them, saying, Thus shall ye speak unto my lord Esau; Thy servant Jacob saith thus, I have sojourned with Laban, and stayed there until now:

5 And I have oxen, and asses, flocks, and menservants, and womenservants: and I have sent to tell my lord, that I may find grace in thy sight.

6 And the messengers returned to Jacob, saying, We came to thy brother Esau, and also he cometh to meet thee, and four hundred men with him.

7 Then Jacob was greatly afraid and distressed: and he divided the people that was with him, and the flocks, and herds, and the camels, into two bands;

8 And said, If Esau come to the one company, and smite it, then the other company which is left shall escape.

9 And Jacob said, O God of my father Abraham, and God of my father Isaac, the LORD which saidst unto me, Return unto thy country, and to thy kindred, and I will deal well with thee:

10 I am not worthy of the least of all the mercies, and of all the truth, which thou hast shewed unto thy servant; for with my staff I passed over this Jordan; and now I am become two bands.

11 Deliver me, I pray thee, from the hand of my brother, from the hand of Esau: for I fear him, lest he will come and smite me, and the mother with the children.

12 And thou saidst, I will surely do thee good, and make thy seed as the sand of the sea, which cannot be numbered for multitude.

13 And he lodged there that same night; and took of that which came to his hand a present for Esau his brother;

14 Two hundred she goats, and twenty he goats, two hundred ewes, and twenty rams,

15 Thirty milch camels with their colts, forty kine, and ten bulls, twenty she asses, and ten foals.

16 And he delivered them into the hand of his servants, every drove by themselves; and said unto his servants, Pass over before me, and put a space betwixt drove and drove.

17 And he commanded the foremost, saying, When Esau my brother meeteth thee, and asketh thee, saying, Whose art thou? and whither goest thou? and whose are these before thee?

18 Then thou shalt say, They be thy servant Jacob’s; it is a present sent unto my lord Esau: and, behold, also he is behind us.

19 And so commanded he the second, and the third, and all that followed the droves, saying, On this manner shall ye speak unto Esau, when ye find him.

20 And say ye moreover, Behold, thy servant Jacob is behind us. For he said, I will appease him with the present that goeth before me, and afterward I will see his face; peradventure he will accept of me.

21 So went the present over before him: and himself lodged that night in the company.

22 And he rose up that night, and took his two wives, and his two womenservants, and his eleven sons, and passed over the ford Jabbok.

23 And he took them, and sent them over the brook, and sent over that he had.

24 And Jacob was left alone; and there wrestled a man with him until the breaking of the day.

25 And when he saw that he prevailed not against him, he touched the hollow of his thigh; and the hollow of Jacob’s thigh was out of joint, as he wrestled with him.

26 And he said, Let me go, for the day breaketh. And he said, I will not let thee go, except thou bless me.

27 And he said unto him, What is thy name? And he said, Jacob.

28 And he said, Thy name shall be called no more Jacob, but Israel: for as a prince hast thou power with God and with men, and hast prevailed.

29 And Jacob asked him, and said, Tell me, I pray thee, thy name. And he said, Wherefore is it that thou dost ask after my name? And he blessed him there.

30 And Jacob called the name of the place Peniel: for I have seen God face to face, and my life is preserved.

31 And as he passed over Penuel the sun rose upon him, and he halted upon his thigh.

32 Therefore the children of Israel eat not of the sinew which shrank, which is upon the hollow of the thigh, unto this day: because he touched the hollow of Jacob’s thigh in the sinew that shrank.

How to read the labels

Every substantive historical claim in the editorial panels below carries one of three labels, plus a source reference:

  • Documented — directly supported by a cited source.
  • Interpreted — a reasoned explanation or proposed relationship.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

On his way home Jacob is met by angels, sends messengers and gifts ahead to Esau, and prays in fear of his brother's four hundred men. Left alone at the ford Jabbok, he wrestles through the night with a man, is renamed Israel, and limps away at sunrise, having seen God face to face and lived. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. As Jacob went on his way, angels of God met him, a reassuring sign as he returned to Canaan. JFB links this to the ladder vision, as a pledge of God's continued presence and protection. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Jacob called the place Mahanaim, meaning two camps, because God's host seemed encamped beside his own. JFB places it between Gilead and the Jabbok, near that brook. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Jacob sent messengers ahead to Esau in Seir, a prudent step to learn his brother's temper before meeting him. JFB notes the road lay near the wild district where Esau had settled in power. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. The message was humble and respectful: Jacob called Esau lord and himself servant, and said he had stayed with Laban until now. Henry notes that yielding and respectful speech goes far toward pacifying offence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Jacob listed his oxen, asses, flocks, and servants to show he returned with abundance and needed nothing from Esau. JFB reads the message as paying respectful homage, not begging for help. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. The messengers reported that Esau was coming to meet Jacob with four hundred men, leaving his intentions unclear. JFB notes Esau's studied reserve gave Jacob reason to dread the worst, especially with a helpless family to protect. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Jacob was greatly afraid and distressed, and divided his people, flocks, herds, and camels into two companies. Henry observes that lively fear can live alongside humble confidence in God's power and promise. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. His plan was that if Esau struck one company, the other might escape. The division was prudence under fear, not a loss of trust. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Jacob prayed to the God of Abraham and Isaac, the Lord who had told him to return to his country and kindred. JFB calls this the first recorded example of prayer in the Bible: short, earnest, and aimed directly at the occasion. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Jacob confessed he was unworthy of the least of God's mercies, recalling that he had crossed Jordan with only his staff and now had become two companies. Henry counts this thankful acknowledgement and humble confession as the pattern of true prayer. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. He pleaded for deliverance from Esau, fearing his brother would strike mothers and children alike. The prayer states the fear plainly and leaves the whole affair to God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Jacob rested his plea on God's own word: that God would do him good and make his seed as countless as sand. Henry notes that the best we can say to God in prayer is what he has said to us. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. That night Jacob selected a present for Esau from what he had at hand. JFB stresses that prayer did not replace prudence: Jacob combined earnest prayer with active, foresighted effort. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. The present was large and varied — two hundred she-goats, twenty he-goats, two hundred ewes, and twenty rams. Its size showed the respect due to a superior and the seriousness of Jacob's wish for peace. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Thirty milch camels with their colts, forty cows, ten bulls, twenty she-asses, and ten foals completed the gift. JFB notes the milch camels alone were of immense value, since she-camels were a chief part of Arab wealth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Jacob put each drove in his servants' hands separately, with space between drove and drove. JFB explains the prudence: the gift would look more imposing, Esau's anger would have time to cool between droves, and a refusal of the first could warn the rest. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. The foremost servant was told exactly what to say when Esau asked whose he was, where he was going, and whose the droves were. JFB notes every servant was given the same words, so the message would clearly come from Jacob himself. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. Each servant was to say the droves were Jacob's present to his lord Esau, and that Jacob himself was behind them. The repeated, identical message was meant to impress Esau step by step. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. The second, third, and all following droves received the same command, so Esau would hear one consistent account. Uniformity, not variety, was the point. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. They were also to add that Jacob was behind them, keeping his own approach after the gift. Jacob hoped the present going before him would appease Esau before they met face to face. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. The present went on ahead, while Jacob himself lodged that night with his company. JFB notes he lodged only part of the night, restless before the crossing. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. That night Jacob took his two wives, two maidservants, and eleven sons across the ford of the Jabbok. JFB identifies the Jabbok with the modern Zerka, a stream running from Gilead into the Jordan. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. Having sent his family and all he had across the brook, Jacob was left alone. JFB pictures him returning to the north bank to seek God's blessing in silent prayer on the means he had set in motion. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. Alone, Jacob wrestled with a man until daybreak. JFB notes the wrestler is called both angel and God, and is widely taken to be the angel of the covenant appearing to encourage his distressed servant. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. When the man saw he did not prevail, he touched Jacob's hip, and it was put out of joint as they wrestled. The injury marked the struggle as real and costly, not a mere contest of strength. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. At daybreak the man said, let me go, but Jacob answered that he would not let go unless he was blessed. JFB notes Jacob knew whom he wrestled with and clung to God's promise to do him good. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. Asked his name, Jacob answered plainly, Jacob. The question brought his old history — the name tied to grasping — into the open before the new name was given. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. His name was changed to Israel, because as a prince he had power with God and with men and had prevailed. Henry notes the new name celebrates not craft but true valour in prayer. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. Jacob asked the man's name, but was answered with a question and a blessing instead. JFB explains the refusal kept Jacob from being puffed up by his victory or supposing he could command the angel. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. Jacob called the place Peniel, because he had seen God face to face and his life was preserved. Henry notes the name honours God's grace: Jacob had seen God's appearance and obtained his favour. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 31. As Jacob passed over Peniel the sun rose on him, and he limped on his thigh. JFB compares the lameness to a thorn in the flesh: a lasting reminder that the victory was granted in grace, not won by strength alone. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 32. Because of this, Israelites do not eat the sinew on the hip joint to this day. JFB explains the sinew as the nerve fastening the thigh bone in its socket, and the abstinence as a traditional usage rather than a Mosaic law. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Unfamiliar terms

  • Israel. Jacob's new name, because as a prince he had power with God and with men and had prevailed; Henry notes it celebrates not craft but true valour in prayer. Interpreted Study note on Genesis 32:28, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Peniel. The place Jacob so named because he had seen God face to face and his life was preserved. Interpreted Study note on Genesis 32:30, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
