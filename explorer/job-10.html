The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 10 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 My soul is weary of my life; I will leave my complaint upon myself; I will speak in the bitterness of my soul.

2 I will say unto God, Do not condemn me; shew me wherefore thou contendest with me.

3 Is it good unto thee that thou shouldest oppress, that thou shouldest despise the work of thine hands, and shine upon the counsel of the wicked?

4 Hast thou eyes of flesh? or seest thou as man seeth?

5 Are thy days as the days of man? are thy years as man’s days,

6 That thou enquirest after mine iniquity, and searchest after my sin?

7 Thou knowest that I am not wicked; and there is none that can deliver out of thine hand.

8 Thine hands have made me and fashioned me together round about; yet thou dost destroy me.

9 Remember, I beseech thee, that thou hast made me as the clay; and wilt thou bring me into dust again?

10 Hast thou not poured me out as milk, and curdled me like cheese?

11 Thou hast clothed me with skin and flesh, and hast fenced me with bones and sinews.

12 Thou hast granted me life and favour, and thy visitation hath preserved my spirit.

13 And these things hast thou hid in thine heart: I know that this is with thee.

14 If I sin, then thou markest me, and thou wilt not acquit me from mine iniquity.

15 If I be wicked, woe unto me; and if I be righteous, yet will I not lift up my head. I am full of confusion; therefore see thou mine affliction;

16 For it increaseth. Thou huntest me as a fierce lion: and again thou shewest thyself marvellous upon me.

17 Thou renewest thy witnesses against me, and increasest thine indignation upon me; changes and war are against me.

18 Wherefore then hast thou brought me forth out of the womb? Oh that I had given up the ghost, and no eye had seen me!

19 I should have been as though I had not been; I should have been carried from the womb to the grave.

20 Are not my days few? cease then, and let me alone, that I may take comfort a little,

21 Before I go whence I shall not return, even to the land of darkness and the shadow of death;

22 A land of darkness, as darkness itself; and of the shadow of death, without any order, and where the light is as darkness.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Job turns from the friends to God himself: do not condemn me, he pleads, but show me why you contend with me. The God whose hands made and fashioned him like clay, who poured him out as milk and clothed him with skin and bones, now seems to hunt him as a fierce lion and renew witnesses against him. He asks only for a little respite before he goes to the land of darkness, where light itself is as darkness. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Job's soul is weary of his life, so he will leave his complaint upon himself and speak in bitterness. JFB renders it as giving loose to his complaint, as he resolved in chapter 7. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Job will say to God: do not condemn me; show me why you contend with me. JFB explains the plea: do not treat me as guilty by mere sovereignty without showing the reasons. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Is it good for God to oppress, to despise the work of his hands, and shine on the counsel of the wicked? JFB notes Job cannot think God takes pleasure in using power to oppress the weak, and shine means favouring with prosperity. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. Has God eyes of flesh, or does he see as man sees? JFB asks whether God sees as feebly as man, with the same uncharitable eye as Job's friends. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Are God's days as the days of man, or his years as man's days? The question presses whether God's time is as short as man's, explaining the rapid succession of his strokes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. God searches after Job's iniquity and seeks out his sin, as if time pressed. JFB links this to the preceding questions: the rapid strokes look as though God had no time to spare in overwhelming him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. God knows Job is not wicked, yet there is none who can deliver out of his hand. JFB notes the omniscient God knows, yet searches after sin, though he has no need to deal with the rapid violence man would use. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. God's hands have made Job and fashioned him together round about, yet he destroys him. JFB notes made implies a work of difficulty and art, and together round about implies a complete unity whose parts on all sides bear the closest scrutiny. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Remember that God made Job as clay, and will bring him into dust again. JFB notes the reference is less to perishable material than to wonderful fashioning by the divine potter. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. God poured Job out as milk and curdled him like cheese. JFB explains the body's organization from rude liquid beginnings gradually assuming solid consistency, like milk curdling into cheese. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. God clothed Job with skin and flesh and fenced him with bones and sinews. JFB renders fenced as inlaid or curiously wrought, the skin first, then flesh, then harder parts. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. God granted Job life and favour, and his visitation preserved his spirit. JFB explains visitation as God's watchful providence, and spirit as breath. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. These things God hid in his heart, and Job knows this is with him. JFB explains that all God's dealings with Job in creation, preservation, and present affliction were part of his secret counsel. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. If Job sins, God marks him and will not acquit him from his iniquity. JFB notes Job's perplexity: God marks every sin with ceaseless rigour, whether the man is wicked or comparatively righteous. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. If Job is wicked, woe to him; if righteous, he still cannot lift up his head, being full of confusion and seeing his affliction. JFB explains lifting the head as conscious innocence, and seeing affliction as seeing only what seems to prove him guilty. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. If Job's head increases, God hunts him as a fierce lion and shows himself marvellous upon him. JFB renders it as if he lifts his head God would hunt him, like a lion that does not kill at once but returns to torture again. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. God renews his witnesses against Job and increases indignation upon him, with changes and war against him. JFB explains the witnesses as accumulated trials, like witnesses brought to wear out the accused, and changes and war as host after host, afflictions then reproaches. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. Why then did God bring Job forth out of the womb, that he might give up the ghost unseen? The question returns to chapter 3: birth itself seems the first wrong. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. Oh that Job had been as though he had not been, carried from the womb to the grave. Unborn or stillborn, he would never have entered the suffering he now endures. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Are not Job's days few? Let God cease and leave him alone, that he may take comfort a little. JFB notes that since he was destined from birth to these ills, he asks at least a little breathing time during his few remaining days. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. Before Job goes whence he shall not return, to the land of darkness and the shadow of death. The request for respite is bounded by the grave's finality. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. A land of darkness, as darkness itself, of the shadow of death, without any order, where light is as darkness. JFB notes three Hebrew words for darkness here, and that its only sunshine is thick darkness — a bold poetic figure, though Job in a better frame has brighter thoughts of the unseen world. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
