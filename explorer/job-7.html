The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 7 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Is there not an appointed time to man upon earth? are not his days also like the days of an hireling?

2 As a servant earnestly desireth the shadow, and as an hireling looketh for the reward of his work:

3 So am I made to possess months of vanity, and wearisome nights are appointed to me.

4 When I lie down, I say, When shall I arise, and the night be gone? and I am full of tossings to and fro unto the dawning of the day.

5 My flesh is clothed with worms and clods of dust; my skin is broken, and become loathsome.

6 My days are swifter than a weaver’s shuttle, and are spent without hope.

7 O remember that my life is wind: mine eye shall no more see good.

8 The eye of him that hath seen me shall see me no more: thine eyes are upon me, and I am not.

9 As the cloud is consumed and vanisheth away: so he that goeth down to the grave shall come up no more.

10 He shall return no more to his house, neither shall his place know him any more.

11 Therefore I will not refrain my mouth; I will speak in the anguish of my spirit; I will complain in the bitterness of my soul.

12 Am I a sea, or a whale, that thou settest a watch over me?

13 When I say, My bed shall comfort me, my couch shall ease my complaints;

14 Then thou scarest me with dreams, and terrifiest me through visions:

15 So that my soul chooseth strangling, and death rather than my life.

16 I loathe it; I would not live alway: let me alone; for my days are vanity.

17 What is man, that thou shouldest magnify him? and that thou shouldest set thine heart upon him?

18 And that thou shouldest visit him every morning, and try him every moment?

19 How long wilt thou not depart from me, nor let me alone till I swallow down my spittle?

20 I have sinned; what shall I do unto thee, O thou preserver of men? why hast thou set me as a mark against thee, so that I am a burden to myself?

21 And why dost thou not pardon my transgression, and take away my iniquity? for now shall I sleep in the dust; and thou shalt seek me in the morning, but I shall not be.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Job pictures human life as hard service, like a hireling longing for the shadow at evening, and his own portion as months of vanity and wearisome nights of tossing. Turning to God, he asks why he is watched as if he were the sea or a sea-monster, why God visits him every morning and tries him every moment, and why his transgression is not pardoned before he sleeps in the dust and is no more. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Is there not an appointed time to man on earth, and are not his days like the days of a hireling? JFB renders appointed time as warfare, a hard conflict with evil, and notes Job details the miseries his friends will see if they look on him as he asked. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. As a servant earnestly desires the shadow, and a hireling looks for his reward, so Job longs for the close of his hard service. JFB notes Easterners measure time by shadow length, and that longing for the end proves Job did not regard the grave as mere sleep. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Job is made to possess months of vanity, and wearisome nights are appointed to him. JFB calls it an ironic inheritance: to be heir is usually joy, but here it is the entail of a dismal, involuntary lot, numbered out for him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. When Job lies down he asks when he shall arise and the night be gone, and he is full of tossings till dawn. JFB renders it literally: when shall be the flight of the night? Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Job's flesh is clothed with worms and clods of dust, his skin broken and loathsome. JFB notes maggots breed in elephantiasis sores, the clods are a crust of dried filth, and the skin heals and breaks out again with running matter. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Job's days are swifter than a weaver's shuttle and spent without hope. JFB explains that each day like the shuttle leaves a thread behind, but Job's thought is that his web must swiftly be cut off, without hope of recovery. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Remember, Job tells God, that his life is wind, and his eye shall no more see good. JFB notes this address to God marks a softer mood: a beam from former prosperity draws regret at leaving a world of light. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. The eye of him that has seen Job shall see him no more, and God's eyes are upon him but he is not. JFB stresses that Job disappears even while God looks on him, unable to survive the gaze of Jehovah. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. As the cloud is consumed and vanishes, so he who goes down to the grave shall come up no more. JFB notes Sheol is the place of departed spirits, and the sense is only that he shall not come up in the present order of things. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. He shall return no more to his house, nor shall his place know him any more. JFB notes the Eastern love of dwelling, whose desertion is a frequent theme of sorrow in Arabian elegies. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Therefore Job will not refrain his mouth, but speak in the anguish of his spirit and complain in the bitterness of his soul. JFB reads therefore I, at all events, as claiming at least the melancholy satisfaction of venting his sorrow in words. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Am I a sea, or a whale, that God sets a watch over me? JFB explains the sea as a violent rebel against the Lord of nature who curbs it, and the whale as a sea monster like the crocodile the Egyptians watched most carefully. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. When Job says his bed shall comfort him and his couch ease his complaint, God instead scares him with dreams and terrifies him through visions. The comfort he hoped for in sleep turns to fright. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. God scares Job with dreams and terrifies him through visions. JFB attributes the frightful dreams to his elephantiasis, which he ascribes to God, since the common belief assigned all night visions to God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. So Job's soul chooses strangling and death rather than his life. JFB notes one rendering as wishing to strangle himself, and the simpler sense as choosing any violent death rather than his wasted, diseased frame. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. Job loathes his life and would not live always; let God alone, for his days are vanity. JFB renders let me alone as ceasing to afflict him for the few vain days still left. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. What is man that God should magnify him and set his heart upon him? JFB notes Job wonders why God expends such attention on man as to make him the subject of severe trials, where David used the same words to wonder God does so much for insignificant man. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. God visits man every morning and tries him every moment. JFB gives the shepherd image: counting the flock every morning to see all are there, though Job complains it is trials, not mercies, that are new every morning. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. How long will God not depart from Job, nor let him alone till he swallows down his spittle? JFB explains the jealous keeper's gaze and the Arabic proverb for a brief respite, like our till I draw my breath. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Job confesses he has sinned, yet asks what he can do against God, the preserver of men, and why he is set as a mark. JFB notes Job bore his trials patiently as sent by God, and only his reason cannot reconcile their ceaseless continuance with his ideas of the divine nature. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. Why does God not pardon Job's transgression and take away his iniquity, for soon he shall sleep in the dust and God shall seek him in the morning but he shall not be? JFB explains the morning as a sick man sought at dawn and found dead in the night: if help does not come at once, it will be too late. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
