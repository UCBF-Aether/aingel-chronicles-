Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Judith 1 (King James Version, Apocrypha; public-domain text as received)

1 In the twelfth year of the reign of Nabuchodonosor, who reigned in Nineve, the great city; in the days of Arphaxad, which reigned over the Medes in Ecbatane,

2 And built in Ecbatane walls round about of stones hewn three cubits broad and six cubits long, and made the height of the wall seventy cubits, and the breadth thereof fifty cubits:

3 And set the towers thereof upon the gates of it an hundred cubits high, and the breadth thereof in the foundation threescore cubits:

4 And he made the gates thereof, even gates that were raised to the height of seventy cubits, and the breadth of them was forty cubits, for the going forth of his mighty armies, and for the setting in array of his footmen:

5 Even in those days king Nabuchodonosor made war with king Arphaxad in the great plain, which is the plain in the borders of Ragau.

6 And there came unto him all they that dwelt in the hill country, and all that dwelt by Euphrates, and Tigris and Hydaspes, and the plain of Arioch the king of the Elymeans, and very many nations of the sons of Chelod, assembled themselves to the battle.

7 Then Nabuchodonosor king of the Assyrians sent unto all that dwelt in Persia, and to all that dwelt westward, and to those that dwelt in Cilicia, and Damascus, and Libanus, and Antilibanus, and to all that dwelt upon the sea coast,

8 And to those among the nations that were of Carmel, and Galaad, and the higher Galilee, and the great plain of Esdrelom,

9 And to all that were in Samaria and the cities thereof, and beyond Jordan unto Jerusalem, and Betane, and Chelus, and Kades, and the river of Egypt, and Taphnes, and Ramesse, and all the land of Gesem,

10 Until ye come beyond Tanis and Memphis, and to all the inhabitants of Egypt, until ye come to the borders of Ethiopia.

11 But all the inhabitants of the land made light of the commandment of Nabuchodonosor king of the Assyrians, neither went they with him to the battle; for they were not afraid of him: yea, he was before them as one man, and they sent away his ambassadors from them without effect, and with disgrace.

12 Therefore Nabuchodonosor was very angry with all this country, and sware by his throne and kingdom, that he would surely be avenged upon all those coasts of Cilicia, and Damascus, and Syria, and that he would slay with the sword all the inhabitants of the land of Moab, and the children of Ammon, and all Judea, and all that were in Egypt, till ye come to the borders of the two seas.

13 Then he marched in battle array with his power against king Arphaxad in the seventeenth year, and he prevailed in his battle: for he overthrew all the power of Arphaxad, and all his horsemen, and all his chariots,

14 And became lord of his cities, and came unto Ecbatane, and took the towers, and spoiled the streets thereof, and turned the beauty thereof into shame.

15 He took also Arphaxad in the mountains of Ragau, and smote him through with his darts, and destroyed him utterly that day.

16 So he returned afterward to Nineve, both he and all his company of sundry nations being a very great multitude of men of war, and there he took his ease, and banqueted, both he and his army, an hundred and twenty days.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

What the chapter says, in plain language

In the twelfth year of Nabuchodonosor, who reigns in Nineve, and of Arphaxad, who reigns over the Medes in Ecbatane, the two kings go to war, Nabuchodonosor summons all the nations to his aid, and they refuse, making light of his commandment. He defeats Arphaxad, takes Ecbatane, and returns to Nineve with a very great multitude of men of war, where he takes his ease and banquets with his army for a hundred and twenty days. Interpreted Chapter text and study notes for this chapter

Unfamiliar terms

  • Ecbatane. the Median capital of Arphaxad, whose walls and towers the chapter describes (verse 1). Interpreted Study note for this chapter
  • Nabuchodonosor. Nebuchadnezzar, here king of the Assyrians reigning in Nineve (verse 1). Interpreted Study note for this chapter

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. The opening "Now" points to the author's internal purpose, and many books begin with "And," showing their connection with a general history; the building of Ecbatana took place soon after the destruction of Nineve mentioned in the preceding book. Arphaxad is probably the Dejoces of Herodotus, to whom he attributes the building of Ecbatana, capital of Media, or rather resembles Phraortes, who fortified and embellished the city; Ecbatana, or Agbata, signifies "variegated" in Arabic, as its seven walls rising one above another were marked white, black, red, blue, dark red, silver, and gold. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 2. The Greek adds that the hewn stones were "three cubits broad and six long," and the ancients aimed at solidity in architecture, as their ruins show; the height and breadth figures vary between the versions — Greek 70 high and 50 broad, Old Vulgate 60 high and 50 broad — and the Greek adds towers above the gates 100 cubits, with foundations 60 cubits broad and gates 70 cubits high and 40 broad, to send out armies and draw up infantry. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 4. On Arphaxad's glorying, the note recalls, "Fool, this night wilt thou perish" (Luke 12:20) — pride in such power is answered by sudden death. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 5. This Nabuchodonosor is not the king of Babylon who took and destroyed Jerusalem, but another of the same name who reigned in Nineve, called Saosduchin by profane historians, who succeeded Asarhaddon among the Assyrians and was contemporary with Manasses, king of Juda; the Jews frequently give foreign princes names different from those in profane history. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 6. Ragau lies near Rages, and Jadason is Mount Jason above the Caspian gates, unless it be the city Jassu in Armenia; the Greek has "the Hydaspes" here, and various battles were fought during this war, which the Greek intimates lasted seven years, ending with Arphaxad attacked, Nineve sacked, the king slain, and Nabuchodonosor abandoning himself with his army to pleasure in the conquered city for 120 days. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 8. Esdrelon is in Syriac "Jezrael," the usual name in Scripture (Joshua 17:16). Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 9. Jesse, or Gessen, is where Joseph placed his brethren (Genesis 46:34). Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 11. On their refusal, the Greek adds that they "did not come to help him in the war, because they feared him not," supposing he would have enough to do with Arphaxad, as he was but like their equal, or as one man. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
  • Verse 12. Those subject to the king did wrong in refusing aid, but the Jews were under no such obligation, and God espoused their cause the more as the king set up for a deity (chapter 3); elated by victory like other conquerors, he had at first entertained thoughts of universal dominion. Interpreted Study note, summarizing George Leo Haydock
