The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Psalms 86 (King James Version (1611) for Psalms 1–150; Charles Thomson, The Septuagint (1808) for Psalm 151)

1 Bow down thine ear, O LORD, hear me: for I am poor and needy.

2 Preserve my soul; for I am holy: O thou my God, save thy servant that trusteth in thee.

3 Be merciful unto me, O Lord: for I cry unto thee daily.

4 Rejoice the soul of thy servant: for unto thee, O Lord, do I lift up my soul.

5 For thou, Lord, art good, and ready to forgive; and plenteous in mercy unto all them that call upon thee.

6 Give ear, O LORD, unto my prayer; and attend to the voice of my supplications.

7 In the day of my trouble I will call upon thee: for thou wilt answer me.

8 Among the gods there is none like unto thee, O Lord; neither are there any works like unto thy works.

9 All nations whom thou hast made shall come and worship before thee, O Lord; and shall glorify thy name.

10 For thou art great, and doest wondrous things: thou art God alone.

11 Teach me thy way, O LORD; I will walk in thy truth: unite my heart to fear thy name.

12 I will praise thee, O Lord my God, with all my heart: and I will glorify thy name for evermore.

13 For great is thy mercy toward me: and thou hast delivered my soul from the lowest hell.

14 O God, the proud are risen against me, and the assemblies of violent men have sought after my soul; and have not set thee before them.

15 But thou, O Lord, art a God full of compassion, and gracious, longsuffering, and plenteous in mercy and truth.

16 O turn unto me, and have mercy upon me; give thy strength unto thy servant, and save the son of thine handmaid.

17 Shew me a token for good; that they which hate me may see it, and be ashamed: because thou, LORD, hast holpen me, and comforted me.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

A poor and needy speaker prays daily for mercy, preservation and a rejoicing soul, appealing to a God who is good, ready to forgive and plenteous in mercy to all who call. Among the gods none is like him, and all nations he has made shall come and worship; the speaker asks to be taught God's way and praises him for deliverance from the lowest hell. Against proud and violent men who seek his life, the psalm sets God's compassion, grace, longsuffering, mercy and truth, and asks for strength and a token for good that his haters may see and be ashamed. Interpreted Psalm text above; study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Bow down thine ear, O Lord, hear me, for I am poor and needy, is a begged gracious audience enforced by distress: he begs that God would give a gracious audience to his prayers — Bow down thy ear, O Lord! hear me. When God hears our prayers it is fitly said that he bows down his ear to them, for it is admirable condescension in God that he is pleased to take notice of such mean creatures as we are and such defective prayers as ours are. He repeats this again: Give ear, O Lord! unto my prayer, a favourable ear, though it be whispered, though it be stammered — attend to the voice of my supplications. Not that God needs to have his affection stirred up by anything that we can say, but thus we must express our desire of his favour. The Son of David spoke it with assurance and pleasure: Father, I thank thee that thou hast heard me, and I know that thou hearest me always. And he pleads his distress: Hear me, for I am poor and needy — therefore I want thy help, therefore none else will hear me. God is the poor man's King, whose glory it is to save the souls of the needy; those who are poor in spirit, who see themselves empty and necessitous, are most welcome to the God of all grace. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 2. Preserve my soul, for I am holy, O thou my God, save thy servant that trusteth in thee, is a begged special protection enforced by God's own work: he begs that God would take him under his special protection, and so be the author of his salvation — Preserve my soul, save thy servant. It was David's soul that was God's servant, for those only serve God acceptably that serve him with their spirits. David's concern is about his soul: if we understand it of his natural life, it teaches us that the best self-preservation is to commit ourselves to God's keeping and by faith and prayer to make our Creator our preserver. But it may be understood of his spiritual life, the life of the soul as distinct from the body: Preserve my soul from that one evil and dangerous thing to souls, even from sin — preserve my soul, and so save me. All those whom God will save he preserves, and will preserve them to his heavenly kingdom. And he pleads God's good work in himself, by which he had qualified him for the tokens of his favour: a conformity to God — I am holy, therefore preserve my soul, for those whom the Spirit sanctifies he will preserve. He does not say this in pride and vain glory, but with humble thankfulness to God: I am one whom thou favourest, so the margin reads it, whom thou hast set apart for thyself. If God has begun a good work of grace in us, we must own that the time was a time of love — Then was I in his eyes as one that found favour — and whom God hath taken into his favour he will take under his protection. All his saints are in thy hand. Observe, I am needy, yet I am holy — holy and yet needy, poor in the world, but rich in faith. Those who preserve their purity in their greatest poverty may assure themselves that God will preserve their comforts, will preserve their souls. And a confidence in God: Save thy servant that trusteth in thee. Those that are holy must nevertheless not trust in themselves, nor in their own righteousness, but only in God and his grace. Those that trust in God may expect salvation from him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 3. Mercy here covers both pardon and help out of distress, and Henry frames the plea starkly: men show no mercy, and we deserve none, so the prayer rests on mercy for mercy's sake. The daily crying matters too — prayer kept going at other times means affliction finds the wheels already turning, not needing to be set going then. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 4. Only God can put gladness into the heart, and only then is joy full; servants are both bound to serve with gladness and privileged to be filled with joy and peace in believing, so that the joy of the Lord becomes their strength. The ground of expecting that comfort is communion kept up: lifting the soul to God in prayer, which Henry calls the nurse of spiritual joy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 5. Goodness appears in giving and forgiving: a sin-pardoning God more ready to forgive than we to repent, and a prayer-hearing God plenteous, rich and liberal, to all who call, is the plea Henry builds the whole prayer on. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 6. A favourable ear is asked even for whispered, stammered prayer — not because God needs his affection stirred by our words, but because we must express our desire of his favour in this way. Henry contrasts this with the Son of David's assurance, that the Father hears him always, as the pattern of confidence behind the petition. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 7. In trouble David resolves to call, whatever others do, because God will hear and answer, and he shall not seek in vain as Baal's criers did, with no voice and none regarding. Henry's condition is practical: prayers made in trouble are heard with hope when conscience has been made of prayer at all times, so affliction finds the wheels of prayer already going. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 8. Among false gods, angels, and kings of the earth there is none like God — none so wise, so mighty, so good — and his works are the undeniable proof, praising him themselves. Henry's rule for praise follows: the best way to praise him is simply to acknowledge that there is none like him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 9. All nations whom thou hast made shall come and worship before thee, O Lord, and shall glorify thy name, is God as the fountain of all being and the centre of all praise: Thou hast made all nations, made them all of one blood — they all derive their being from thee, and have a constant dependence on thee, and therefore they shall come and worship before thee and glorify thy name. This was in part fulfilled in the multitude of proselytes to the Jewish religion in the days of David and Solomon, but was to have its full accomplishment in the days of the Messiah, when some out of every kingdom and nation should be effectually brought in to praise God. It was by Christ that God made all nations, for without him was not anything made that was made, and therefore through Christ, and by the power of his gospel and grace, all nations shall be brought to worship before God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 10. God's greatness is spelled out as King of nations: sovereignty absolute and incontestable, majesty terrible, power universal and irresistible, riches vast, dominion boundless. His wondrous things prove the conclusion Henry draws — he is God alone, not only none like him but none besides him, and he alone should have our hearts. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 11. Two graces are prayed for together: an understanding heart taught God's way, so that David, well taught already, still needs the voice saying 'This is the way' and resolves to be governed by the teaching; and an upright, united heart, single for God rather than double like a hypocrite's, fixed, firm, faithful and fervent, since our powers wander after a thousand foreign things unless grace gathers them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 12. Praise is resolved as personal and wholehearted — not only as Lord but as my God, with all the heart, cordially and cheerfully, glorifying his name not for a time but for evermore, in life and into eternity. The reason is particular favour: it is a satisfaction that others shall praise God, but David's greatest care and pleasure is to do it himself. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 13. The mercy is magnified as a fountain inexhaustibly full with inestimably rich streams, proved by rescue from the lowest hell — from death, from so great a death, from eternal death deserved for the sin in the matter of Uriah, taken away by Nathan's word. Even the best saints owe that rescue to mercy, not merit, and so glorious a rescue justly requires everlasting praise in return. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 14. O God, the proud are risen against me, and the assemblies of violent men have sought after my soul, and have not set thee before them, is complaint of restless and implacable malice: Lord, be thou for me, for there are many against me. He takes notice of their character: they were proud men that looked with disdain upon poor David — many are made persecutors by their pride. They were violent men, that would carry all before them by force, right or wrong. They were terrible formidable men, so some, that did what they could to frighten all about them. He notices their number: there were assemblies of them — they were men in authority and met in councils and courts, or men for conversation, and met in clubs — but, being assembled, they were the more capable of doing mischief. He notices their enmity to him: they rise up against me in open rebellion — they not only plot, but they put their plots in execution as far as they can — and the design is not only to depose me, but to destroy me: they seek after my life, to slay me, after my soul, to damn me, if it lay in their power. And, lastly, he notices their distance and estrangement from God, which were at the bottom of their enmity to David: they have not set thee before them — and what good can be expected from those that have no fear of God before their eyes? Lord, appear against them, for they are thy enemies as well as mine. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 15. But thou, O Lord, art a God full of compassion, and gracious, longsuffering, and plenteous in mercy and truth, is God infinitely good set against man's badness: man is bad, very wicked and vile — no mercy is to be expected from him — but thou, O Lord! art a God full of compassion, and gracious. This is that attribute by which he proclaims his name, and by which we are therefore to proclaim it. It is his goodness that is over all his works, and therefore should fill all our praises — and this is our comfort, in reference to the wickedness of the world we live in, that, however it be, God is good. Men are barbarous, but God is gracious; men are false, but God is faithful. God is not only compassionate, but full of compassion, and in him mercy rejoiceth against judgment. He is long-suffering towards us, though we forfeit his favour and provoke him to anger, and he is plenteous in mercy and truth, as faithful in performing as he was free in promising. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 16. With enemies turning against him and friends turning from him, David asks God to turn to him as to one he loves with tender concern, and to give strength both into him and forth for him against ruin. He pleads relation: a servant by birth, the son of God's handmaid born in his house, so that his rightful owner may be expected to protect him — 'I am thine; save me.' Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
  • Verse 17. Shew me a token for good, that they which hate me may see it, and be ashamed, because thou, Lord, hast holpen me, and comforted me, is prayer for a reputation from God: that God would put a reputation on him — Show me a token for good — make it to appear to others as well as to myself that thou art doing me good, and designing further good for me. Let me have some unquestionable illustrious instances of thy favour to me, that those who hate me may see it, and be ashamed of their enmity to me, as they will have reason to be when they perceive that thou, Lord, hast helped me and comforted me, and that therefore they have been striving against God, opposing one whom he owns, and that they have been striving in vain to ruin and vex one whom God himself has undertaken to help and comfort. The joy of the saints shall be the shame of their persecutors. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry (Complete)
