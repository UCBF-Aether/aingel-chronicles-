The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Ecclesiastes 6 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 There is an evil which I have seen under the sun, and it is common among men:

2 A man to whom God hath given riches, wealth, and honour, so that he wanteth nothing for his soul of all that he desireth, yet God giveth him not power to eat thereof, but a stranger eateth it: this is vanity, and it is an evil disease.

3 If a man beget an hundred children, and live many years, so that the days of his years be many, and his soul be not filled with good, and also that he have no burial; I say, that an untimely birth is better than he.

4 For he cometh in with vanity, and departeth in darkness, and his name shall be covered with darkness.

5 Moreover he hath not seen the sun, nor known any thing: this hath more rest than the other.

6 Yea, though he live a thousand years twice told, yet hath he seen no good: do not all go to one place?

7 All the labour of man is for his mouth, and yet the appetite is not filled.

8 For what hath the wise more than the fool? what hath the poor, that knoweth to walk before the living?

9 Better is the sight of the eyes than the wandering of the desire: this is also vanity and vexation of spirit.

10 That which hath been is named already, and it is known that it is man: neither may he contend with him that is mightier than he.

11 Seeing there be many things that increase vanity, what is man the better?

12 For who knoweth what is good for man in this life, all the days of his vain life which he spendeth as a shadow? for who can tell a man what shall be after him under the sun?

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Riches, honour, many children and long life are no good if a man cannot enjoy them; appetite is never filled and the stillborn child, who never saw the sun, has more rest. The chapter ends asking who knows what is good for a person in this brief life, since no one knows what comes after. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. There is an evil seen under the sun, and it is common among men, namely the case of a man given everything yet given no power to enjoy it, which Henry makes the next proof of the vanity of riches. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. A man to whom God has given riches, wealth and honour, so that he wants nothing, yet God gives him not power to eat thereof, but a stranger eats it, which is vanity and an evil disease, because possession without enjoyment is possession in name only. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. If a man beget a hundred children and live many years, yet his soul be not filled with good and he have no burial, an untimely birth is better than he, because a life unfilled and unhonoured at the end is worse than a life never begun. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. The untimely birth comes in with vanity and departs in darkness, and its name is covered with darkness, yet it has known neither the toil nor the disappointment that embitter the long life. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Moreover it has not seen the sun nor known anything, and this has more rest than the other, because rest from trouble, even in oblivion, is better than a restless life of unsatisfied desire. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Though a man live a thousand years twice told, yet has he seen no good, and do not all go to one place, so that length of days without goodness adds nothing at the end, since all end in the same grave. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. All the labour of man is for his mouth, and yet the appetite is not filled, because the labour that feeds the body can never feed the soul, which craves beyond bread. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. What has the wise more than the fool, and what has the poor, that knows to walk before the living, but the same unsatisfied appetite, since wisdom and poverty alike end at the grave's door. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Better is the sight of the eyes than the wandering of the desire, because contentment with what is present is worth more than a roaming appetite that is never at home, which is also vanity and vexation of spirit. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. That which has been is named already, and it is known that it is man, and he cannot contend with him that is mightier than he, because man's nature and limits were fixed long ago, and he cannot dispute with God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Seeing there are many things that increase vanity, what is man the better, since multiplying words and schemes about earthly things only multiplies the emptiness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Who knows what is good for man in this life, all the days of his vain life which he spends as a shadow, and who can tell him what shall be after him under the sun, since man cannot see either his true good or his own future. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
