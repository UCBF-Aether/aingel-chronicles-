The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Sirach 22 (King James Version, Apocrypha; public-domain text as received)

1 A slothful man is compared to a filthy stone, and every one will hiss him out to his disgrace.

2 A slothful man is compared to the filth of a dunghill: every man that takes it up will shake his hand.

3 An evilnurtured man is the dishonour of his father that begat him: and a [foolish] daughter is born to his loss.

4 A wise daughter shall bring an inheritance to her husband: but she that liveth dishonestly is her father's heaviness.

5 She that is bold dishonoureth both her father and her husband, but they both shall despise her.

6 A tale out of season [is as] musick in mourning: but stripes and correction of wisdom are never out of time.

7 Whoso teacheth a fool is as one that glueth a potsherd together, and as he that waketh one from a sound sleep.

8 He that telleth a tale to a fool speaketh to one in a slumber: when he hath told his tale, he will say, What is the matter?

9 If children live honestly, and have wherewithal, they shall cover the baseness of their parents.

10 But children, being haughty, through disdain and want of nurture do stain the nobility of their kindred.

11 Weep for the dead, for he hath lost the light: and weep for the fool, for he wanteth understanding: make little weeping for the dead, for he is at rest: but the life of the fool is worse than death.

12 Seven days do men mourn for him that is dead; but for a fool and an ungodly man all the days of his life.

13 Talk not much with a fool, and go not to him that hath no understanding: beware of him, lest thou have trouble, and thou shalt never be defiled with his fooleries: depart from him, and thou shalt find rest, and never be disquieted with madness.

14 What is heavier than lead? and what is the name thereof, but a fool?

15 Sand, and salt, and a mass of iron, is easier to bear, than a man without understanding.

16 As timber girt and bound together in a building cannot be loosed with shaking: so the heart that is stablished by advised counsel shall fear at no time.

17 A heart settled upon a thought of understanding is as a fair plaistering on the wall of a gallery.

18 Pales set on an high place will never stand against the wind: so a fearful heart in the imagination of a fool cannot stand against any fear.

19 He that pricketh the eye will make tears to fall: and he that pricketh the heart maketh it to shew her knowledge.

20 Whoso casteth a stone at the birds frayeth them away: and he that upbraideth his friend breaketh friendship.

21 Though thou drewest a sword at thy friend, yet despair not: for there may be a returning [to favour.]

22 If thou hast opened thy mouth against thy friend, fear not; for there may be a reconciliation: except for upbraiding, or pride, or disclosing of secrets, or a treacherous wound: for for these things every friend will depart.

23 Be faithful to thy neighbour in his poverty, that thou mayest rejoice in his prosperity: abide stedfast unto him in the time of his trouble, that thou mayest be heir with him in his heritage: for a mean estate is not always to be contemned: nor the rich that is foolish to be had in admiration.

24 As the vapour and smoke of a furnace goeth before the fire; so reviling before blood.

25 I will not be ashamed to defend a friend; neither will I hide myself from him.

26 And if any evil happen unto me by him, every one that heareth it will beware of him.

27 Who shall set a watch before my mouth, and a seal of wisdom upon my lips, that I fall not suddenly by them, and that my tongue destroy me not?

How to read the labels

Every substantive historical claim in the editorial panels below carries one of three labels, plus a source reference:

  • Documented — directly supported by a cited source.
  • Interpreted — a reasoned explanation or proposed relationship.
  • Unresolved — evidence is insufficient to establish the claim.

A claim does NOT become Documented merely because a commentary states it. Commentary views are attributed to their authors and labeled Interpreted unless a primary textual source directly supports the claim. Where evidence runs out, the page says Unresolved and stops — it does not fill the gap.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

A slothful man is compared to a filthy stone, hissed out to his disgrace, and a fool is heavier than lead. The chapter closes as a prayer for a watch before the mouth and a seal of wisdom on the lips, lest the tongue suddenly destroy its owner. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. A slothful man is compared to a filthy stone, and everyone will hiss him out to his disgrace. Sloth is likened to a filthy stone, and all hiss the slothful man out in disgrace. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 2. A slothful man is compared to the filth of a dunghill; every man who takes it up will shake his hand. The slothful man is dunghill filth, and whoever handles it shakes his hand to be rid of it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 3. An evil-nurtured man is the dishonour of his father who begat him, and a foolish daughter is born to his loss. Bad upbringing dishonours the father, and a foolish daughter is born to her father's loss. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 4. A wise daughter will bring an inheritance to her husband, but she who lives dishonestly is her father's heaviness. A wise daughter enriches her husband by inheritance, while a dishonest life makes her a heaviness to her father. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 5. She who is bold dishonours both her father and her husband, but they both will despise her. A bold woman dishonours father and husband alike, and both will despise her. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 6. A tale out of season is as music in mourning, but stripes and correction of wisdom are never out of time. An ill-timed tale jars like music at a mourning, while wisdom's correction is always timely. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 7. Whoever teaches a fool is as one who glues a potsherd together, and as he who wakes one from a sound sleep. Teaching a fool is like gluing a broken pot or waking a deep sleeper: fruitless and resented. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 8. He who tells a tale to a fool speaks to one in a slumber; when he has told his tale, he will say, what is the matter? Telling a tale to a fool is speaking to a sleeper, who wakes only to ask what is the matter. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 9. If children live honestly and have wherewithal, they will cover the baseness of their parents. Honest, provided-for children cover their parents' baseness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 10. But children, being haughty, through disdain and want of nurture stain the nobility of their kindred. Haughty children, through disdain and lack of nurture, stain their kindred's nobility. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 11. Weep for the dead, for he has lost the light; and weep for the fool, for he wants understanding. Make little weeping for the dead, for he is at rest; but the life of the fool is worse than death. The dead have lost light and deserve weeping, yet little, since they rest; the fool, lacking understanding, lives a life worse than death and deserves more tears. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 12. Seven days do men mourn for him who is dead, but for a fool and an ungodly man all the days of his life. Mourning for the dead lasts seven days, but a fool and an ungodly man are mourned all their lives. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 13. Talk not much with a fool, and go not to him who has no understanding; beware of him, lest you have trouble, and you will never be defiled with his fooleries. Depart from him, and you will find rest, and never be disquieted with madness. Much talk with fools brings trouble and defilement by their fooleries, while departure brings rest from madness. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 14. What is heavier than lead, and what is its name, but a fool? A fool is heavier than lead; that is his name and his weight. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 15. Sand, salt, and a mass of iron are easier to bear than a man without understanding. Sand, salt, and iron are lighter burdens than an uncomprehending man. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 16. As timber girt and bound together in a building cannot be loosed with shaking, so the heart established by advised counsel will fear at no time. A heart bound by advised counsel is like bound timber in a building: shaking cannot loosen it, and it fears at no time. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 17. A heart settled upon a thought of understanding is as a fair plastering on the wall of a gallery. A heart settled on understanding is like fine plaster on a gallery wall, smooth and firm. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 18. Pales set on a high place will never stand against the wind, so a fearful heart in the imagination of a fool cannot stand against any fear. Pales on high ground fall before wind, and a fool's fearful, imagining heart falls before any fear. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 19. He who pricks the eye will make tears fall, and he who pricks the heart makes it show her knowledge. Pricking the eye brings tears, and pricking the heart brings out what it knows. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 20. Whoever casts a stone at the birds frightens them away, and he who upbraids his friend breaks friendship. Stones scatter birds, and upbraiding scatters friendship. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 21. Though you drew a sword at your friend, yet despair not, for there may be a returning to favour. Even drawing a sword against a friend need not end hope, since favour may return. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 22. If you have opened your mouth against your friend, fear not, for there may be a reconciliation, except for upbraiding, pride, disclosing of secrets, or a treacherous wound, for for these things every friend will depart. Open speech against a friend can be reconciled, but upbraiding, pride, disclosed secrets, and treacherous wounds make every friend depart. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 23. Be faithful to your neighbour in his poverty, that you may rejoice in his prosperity; abide steadfast to him in the time of his trouble, that you may be heir with him in his heritage; for a mean estate is not always to be contemned, nor the rich who is foolish to be had in admiration. Faithfulness in a neighbour's poverty and trouble leads to shared joy and heritage, since low estate is not always contemptible, nor foolish riches admirable. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 24. As the vapour and smoke of a furnace go before the fire, so reviling before blood. Reviling goes before bloodshed as furnace smoke goes before fire. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 25. I will not be ashamed to defend a friend, neither will I hide myself from him. Defending a friend openly, without hiding, is a point of honour. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 26. And if any evil happens to me by him, everyone who hears it will beware of him. If evil comes to me through a friend, hearers will learn to beware of him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
  • Verse 27. Who will set a watch before my mouth, and a seal of wisdom upon my lips, that I fall not suddenly by them, and that my tongue destroy me not? The prayer is for a guard on the mouth and wisdom's seal on the lips, lest a sudden fall come by the tongue. Interpreted Study note, summarizing W. O. E. Oesterley
