The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Jude 1 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 Jude, the servant of Jesus Christ, and brother of James, to them that are sanctified by God the Father, and preserved in Jesus Christ, and called:

2 Mercy unto you, and peace, and love, be multiplied.

3 Beloved, when I gave all diligence to write unto you of the common salvation, it was needful for me to write unto you, and exhort you that ye should earnestly contend for the faith which was once delivered unto the saints.

4 For there are certain men crept in unawares, who were before of old ordained to this condemnation, ungodly men, turning the grace of our God into lasciviousness, and denying the only Lord God, and our Lord Jesus Christ.

5 I will therefore put you in remembrance, though ye once knew this, how that the Lord, having saved the people out of the land of Egypt, afterward destroyed them that believed not.

6 And the angels which kept not their first estate, but left their own habitation, he hath reserved in everlasting chains under darkness unto the judgment of the great day.

7 Even as Sodom and Gomorrha, and the cities about them in like manner, giving themselves over to fornication, and going after strange flesh, are set forth for an example, suffering the vengeance of eternal fire.

8 Likewise also these filthy dreamers defile the flesh, despise dominion, and speak evil of dignities.

9 Yet Michael the archangel, when contending with the devil he disputed about the body of Moses, durst not bring against him a railing accusation, but said, The Lord rebuke thee.

10 But these speak evil of those things which they know not: but what they know naturally, as brute beasts, in those things they corrupt themselves.

11 Woe unto them! for they have gone in the way of Cain, and ran greedily after the error of Balaam for reward, and perished in the gainsaying of Core.

12 These are spots in your feasts of charity, when they feast with you, feeding themselves without fear: clouds they are without water, carried about of winds; trees whose fruit withereth, without fruit, twice dead, plucked up by the roots;

13 Raging waves of the sea, foaming out their own shame; wandering stars, to whom is reserved the blackness of darkness for ever.

14 And Enoch also, the seventh from Adam, prophesied of these, saying, Behold, the Lord cometh with ten thousands of his saints,

15 To execute judgment upon all, and to convince all that are ungodly among them of all their ungodly deeds which they have ungodly committed, and of all their hard speeches which ungodly sinners have spoken against him.

16 These are murmurers, complainers, walking after their own lusts; and their mouth speaketh great swelling words, having men’s persons in admiration because of advantage.

17 But, beloved, remember ye the words which were spoken before of the apostles of our Lord Jesus Christ;

18 How that they told you there should be mockers in the last time, who should walk after their own ungodly lusts.

19 These be they who separate themselves, sensual, having not the Spirit.

20 But ye, beloved, building up yourselves on your most holy faith, praying in the Holy Ghost,

21 Keep yourselves in the love of God, looking for the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ unto eternal life.

22 And of some have compassion, making a difference:

23 And others save with fear, pulling them out of the fire; hating even the garment spotted by the flesh.

24 Now unto him that is able to keep you from falling, and to present you faultless before the presence of his glory with exceeding joy,

25 To the only wise God our Saviour, be glory and majesty, dominion and power, both now and ever. Amen.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Jude writes to believers he calls beloved, urging them to contend earnestly for the faith once delivered, because intruders have slipped in who turn grace into license and deny the Lord. The letter's centre is a chain of warnings from the past — Israel in the wilderness, the fallen angels, Sodom, and Cain, Balaam and Korah — set against a closing call to build on faith, pray in the Holy Spirit, keep in God's love, and rescue others with discernment, distinguishing cases that need compassion from cases that need fear. It ends in doxology to the God able to keep believers from falling and present them faultless with exceeding joy. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Jude presents himself modestly as a servant of Christ and brother of James, whose standing in Jerusalem was widely known. His readers are described as loved within God the Father, kept continually by him for Jesus Christ against the day of his coming, and effectually called — their safety rests on God's keeping, not their own. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Mercy comes first because the readers live in a time of wretchedness, and it is the mercy of Christ. Peace is peace in the Holy Spirit and love is the love of God, so the threefold greeting answers to the Trinity, and Jude prays all three may be multiplied in them and toward them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Jude had meant to write generally about the salvation all believers share, but the danger at hand forced a sharper letter. The faith was delivered once for all, so no new revelation can supersede it, and believers must both build themselves up in it and contend for it, like Nehemiah's workmen with a tool in one hand and a weapon in the other. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. The intruders slipped in stealthily and unlawfully, and their doom was forewritten in Scripture's long pattern of judgment on apostasy, from fallen angels to Sodom, Balaam, and Korah. They turn the gospel of grace into a license for sin and deny the only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ, whose absolute ownership over his creatures they refuse. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. Jude only reminds his readers of what they already know once for all: the same Lord who brought Israel safely out of Egypt afterward destroyed those who did not believe. The second act is the deliberate contrast to the first — being rescued does not exempt anyone from judgment for unbelief. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. The angels who would not stay content with the dignity assigned them under the Son of God left their bright heavenly habitation and aspired higher. God has kept them, in his purpose, in darkness for the judgment of the great day — already shut out from heaven and roaming the earth with restricted power, like condemned prisoners awaiting their doom. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. Sodom and the cities around it gave themselves over to unnatural sin, going after strange flesh, and now stand as a public example. The lasting marks of fire around the Dead Sea picture the eternal fire to which their inhabitants are consigned, a warning set before everyone's eyes. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. These men are dreamers in the sense that, like sleepers, they do not know what they are doing: their lusts stir them as dreams stir a sleeper, yet they have no self-command. Dreaming, they defile the flesh, despise lordship, and speak evil of dignities both earthly and heavenly, reviling what they do not understand. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Michael, the one archangel of Scripture and champion of Israel, disputed with the devil in a judicial contest over the literal body of Moses, which Satan opposed raising on the ground of Moses' sins. Even so, Michael would not bring a railing accusation, but said, 'The Lord rebuke thee' — not from fear of Satan, but from reverence for the delegated dignity God had once given him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. The intruders speak evil of the whole spiritual world, things they do not understand at all. What they do grasp, they grasp only by blind natural instinct, like unreasoning animals, and in those very things they corrupt themselves. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. Woe falls on three patterns at once: the way of Cain, whose root sin was hatred and envy of the godly; the error of Balaam, poured out like a burst torrent after gain whatever it costs; and the gainsaying of Korah, who aspired beyond his appointed ministry. The same seed bears the same harvest, so their end is already written in Korah's. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. At the love-feasts that accompanied the Lord's Supper these men are hidden rocks on which the fellowship may shipwreck, feasting without the fear such sacred meals require and pasturing themselves instead of the flock. They are clouds promising rain but carrying none, windswept and fruitless, trees of late autumn without leaves or fruit, twice dead and plucked up by the roots. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Their shame foams out like raging waves of the sea, uncontrolled and public. They are wandering stars that burst on the world with a strange glare like erratic meteors instead of moving in a steady orbit as lights, and their end is to fall back into the blackness of darkness forever. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. Enoch, seventh from Adam, prophesied from the very beginning of the world, and the sacred number seven marks how ancient this witness is. His prophecy of the Lord coming with his holy angels looked beyond the flood to the final judgment, and its certainty is so sure that prophecy speaks of it as already past. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. The Lord comes to convict, that is, to prove guilty, every ungodly person of ungodly deeds and of the hard speeches they have aimed at God. Those who speak against God's children are counted as speaking against God himself. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. These are secret murmurers against God's ordinances and ministers, and complainers never satisfied with their lot — the restless insatiability of their own lusts is the secret of both. Their mouths speak great swelling words, yet they flatter the wealthy and powerful for whatever they can gain from them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. In contrast to the reprobates, the beloved are told to remember words already spoken by the apostles, Peter and Paul among them. Jude does not exclude himself from the apostles; he points out that his readers themselves heard those apostles speak, which dates their warnings before this letter. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. The apostles used to tell them plainly that mockers, or scoffers, would come in the last time, walking after their own ungodly lusts. Jude quotes Peter's very words, showing that the present intruders are the fulfilment of that prediction, not a surprise. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. These men separate themselves from the vital, spiritual reality of church communion, even while they still outwardly take part in its ordinances. They are sensual, literally animal-souled: their spirit, which should receive the Holy Spirit and rule the soul, has sunk into serving earthly appetites, so they do not have the Spirit. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. Against that separation Jude sets the opposite work: building yourselves up on your most holy faith, as on a foundation, which is the same as building on Christ, the object of faith. That building goes together with praying in the Holy Spirit, who teaches what to pray and how, since no one prays aright except within his influence. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. Keeping yourselves does not mean keeping by your own strength, but staying in the love of God, which is the only guarantee of being kept safe. Verses 20 and 21 join Father, Son, and Holy Spirit with faith, hope, and love: faith built up, prayer in the Spirit, and hope looking for the mercy of the Lord Jesus Christ at his coming. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Only those who keep themselves are likely to save others, and different cases need different treatment. Some manuscripts read 'convict' here rather than 'have compassion': those who are contending or wavering should be reproved until convinced, with the difference between cases carefully kept in view. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. Others must be snatched like brands out of the fire, a narrow escape from a hell-fire already licking at them, and a third class met with compassion accompanied by fear. That fear guards pity from becoming connivance: believers must hate even the garment spotted by the flesh, avoiding the remotest contact with sin. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. The letter closes in doxology to the one able to guard believers so they do not stumble, and to present them blameless before the presence of his glory. That presentation is made with exultation, like those who leap for joy, because the keeping from first to last is his work. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. Glory, majesty, dominion, and power belong to the only God our Saviour, through Jesus Christ our Lord — the oldest manuscripts add those words, since the Father saves through the mediation of the Son. Dominion is might and power is legitimate authority, his before all ages past, now, and to all ages to come. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Unfamiliar terms

  • Lasciviousness. Unbridled, shameless indulgence — in verse 4, turning God's grace into a license for sin, as JFB's note explains it. Interpreted Study note, verse 4, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Feasts of charity. The love-feasts shared alongside the Lord's Supper, where JFB's note pictures the intruders as hidden rocks on which fellowship may shipwreck. Interpreted Study note, verse 12, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • The gainsaying of Core. Korah's rebellion: aspiring beyond an appointed ministry, as JFB's note explains the third pattern of verse 11. Interpreted Study note, verse 11, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • First estate. The dignity and place first assigned to the angels, which verse 6 says they did not keep. Interpreted Study note, verse 6, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Investigation: Jude 14–15 and 1 Enoch 1:9 (editorial — wording set side by side, not Scripture)

What can be shown here is a textual parallel: the wording of Jude 14–15 and the wording of 1 Enoch 1:9, set side by side. Both speak of the Lord coming with ten thousands of his holy ones to execute judgment and convict the ungodly of ungodly deeds and hard speeches. Interpreted Jude 14–15 (Text panel above) and 1 Enoch 1:9 (site corpus, Charles 1917)

Jude 14–15: “Behold, the Lord cometh with ten thousands of his saints, To execute judgment upon all, and to convince all that are ungodly among them of all their ungodly deeds which they have ungodly committed, and of all their hard speeches which ungodly sinners have spoken against him.”

1 Enoch 1:9: “And behold! He cometh with ten thousands of His holy ones To execute judgement upon all, And to destroy all the ungodly: And to convict all flesh Of all the works of their ungodliness which they have ungodly committed, And of all the hard things which ungodly sinners have spoken against Him.”

The parallel is in wording and theme, and it stops there. Jude does NOT quote any particular Enoch translation's wording: the English words above from 1 Enoch are Charles's 1917 rendering in the site corpus, not words Jude himself wrote, and no claim is made here that Jude used Charles, or any other single translation, as his source. JFB's note on Jude 14 reads the prophecy as looking beyond the flood to the final judgment. Interpreted Study note, verse 14, Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
