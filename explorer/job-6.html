The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Job 6 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 But Job answered and said,

2 Oh that my grief were throughly weighed, and my calamity laid in the balances together!

3 For now it would be heavier than the sand of the sea: therefore my words are swallowed up.

4 For the arrows of the Almighty are within me, the poison whereof drinketh up my spirit: the terrors of God do set themselves in array against me.

5 Doth the wild ass bray when he hath grass? or loweth the ox over his fodder?

6 Can that which is unsavoury be eaten without salt? or is there any taste in the white of an egg?

7 The things that my soul refused to touch are as my sorrowful meat.

8 Oh that I might have my request; and that God would grant me the thing that I long for!

9 Even that it would please God to destroy me; that he would let loose his hand, and cut me off!

10 Then should I yet have comfort; yea, I would harden myself in sorrow: let him not spare; for I have not concealed the words of the Holy One.

11 What is my strength, that I should hope? and what is mine end, that I should prolong my life?

12 Is my strength the strength of stones? or is my flesh of brass?

13 Is not my help in me? and is wisdom driven quite from me?

14 To him that is afflicted pity should be shewed from his friend; but he forsaketh the fear of the Almighty.

15 My brethren have dealt deceitfully as a brook, and as the stream of brooks they pass away;

16 Which are blackish by reason of the ice, and wherein the snow is hid:

17 What time they wax warm, they vanish: when it is hot, they are consumed out of their place.

18 The paths of their way are turned aside; they go to nothing, and perish.

19 The troops of Tema looked, the companies of Sheba waited for them.

20 They were confounded because they had hoped; they came thither, and were ashamed.

21 For now ye are nothing; ye see my casting down, and are afraid.

22 Did I say, Bring unto me? or, Give a reward for me of your substance?

23 Or, Deliver me from the enemy’s hand? or, Redeem me from the hand of the mighty?

24 Teach me, and I will hold my tongue: and cause me to understand wherein I have erred.

25 How forcible are right words! but what doth your arguing reprove?

26 Do ye imagine to reprove words, and the speeches of one that is desperate, which are as wind?

27 Yea, ye overwhelm the fatherless, and ye dig a pit for your friend.

28 Now therefore be content, look upon me; for it is evident unto you if I lie.

29 Return, I pray you, let it not be iniquity; yea, return again, my righteousness is in it.

30 Is there iniquity in my tongue? cannot my taste discern perverse things?

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

Job answers Eliphaz by asking that his grief be weighed in the balances: it would outweigh the sand of the sea, which is why his words run wild. He compares his friends to desert brooks that promise water to caravans and vanish in the heat — he asked them for no gift or ransom, only for pity and for teaching. Teach me, he says, and I will hold my tongue; show me where I have erred. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. Job answers Eliphaz. The reply turns from Eliphaz's doctrine to Job's own weighed grief and the failure of his friends' comfort. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 2. Oh that Job's grief were thoroughly weighed and his calamity laid in the balances together! JFB explains that Job asks Eliphaz to compare sorrow and misfortune accurately instead of censuring complaints he ought to have sympathized with. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 3. Weighed together, his calamity would be heavier than the sand of the sea, and therefore his words are swallowed up. JFB reads swallowed up as spoken rashly: Job apologizes for speaking too much and too boldly. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 4. The arrows of the Almighty are within Job, and their poison drinks up his spirit. JFB gives the poetic image of God armed with bow and arrows, the arrows poisoned like the burning pains penetrating Job's inmost parts, with God's terrors set in array against him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 5. The wild ass does not bray when it has grass, nor the ox low over its fodder. JFB argues that just as beasts cry only in want, Job's complaint is not without cause, though a rational being should show a better spirit than the brute. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 6. Can tasteless food be eaten without salt, or is there taste in the white of an egg? JFB notes salt is a chief necessary of life in the East, and the white is literally spittle, which egg-white resembles. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 7. The things Job's soul refused to touch have become his sorrowful meat. JFB explains that his taste refused even to touch such insipid food, yet sickness itself is now his nourishment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 8. Oh that Job might have his request, and God would grant him the thing he longs for! JFB cautions that desiring death is no necessary proof of fitness for it: the godly desire to be with the Lord but patiently wait God's will. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 9. Even that God would destroy him, let loose his hand, and cut him off! JFB explains destroy as grind or crush, and cut off as a weaver cutting the finished web from the loom. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 10. Then Job would still have comfort, hardening himself in sorrow, because he has not concealed the words of the Holy One. JFB renders it as exulting in pain if it hastened death, and notes Job answers Eliphaz's insinuation of hypocrisy by claiming he never disowned God's commands. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 11. What is Job's strength that he should hope, and what is his end that he should prolong his life? JFB reads this as despair of restoration: with only a miserable end before him, why desire to prolong life? Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 12. Is Job's strength the strength of stones, or his flesh brass? JFB notes disease has attacked him so that only stone-hard strength and brass-like flesh could stand under it, and he has only common flesh that must give way. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 13. Is not Job's help in him? No: wisdom, or deliverance, is driven quite from him. JFB drops the interrogation: there is no help in me. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 14. To him who is ready to faint, pity should be shown from his friend, even if he forsakes the fear of the Almighty. JFB calls pity a proverb: charity judges indulgently, and withholding it renounces the fear of God. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 15. Job's brethren have dealt deceitfully as a brook, passing away like streams that vanish. JFB compares them to Arabian wadies, full in winter but dry in summer, disappointing caravans that expected water, as loud past professions now fail when needed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 16. The brooks are blackish by reason of ice, with snow hidden in them. JFB gives the vivid image of a stream turbid and black with melted ice and snow descending into the valley. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 17. In the time of heat the brooks vanish and are consumed out of their place. JFB traces the stages: the stream flows narrower, then silent, then every trace disappears by evaporation under the hot sun. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 18. The paths of their way are turned aside, and they go to nothing and perish. JFB explains that caravans turn aside by circuitous routes to waters they remember, only to find an empty wilderness and perish, unable to regain their road. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 19. The troops of Tema looked, and the companies of Sheba waited for them. JFB locates Tema north of Arabia-Deserta, named from Ishmael's son, and Sheba among southern merchants, giving living reality to the disappointed caravans. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 20. They were confounded because they had hoped, and came there and were ashamed. JFB notes each had hoped his companions would find water, and the greater the hope the bitterer the disappointment, their countenances burning with shame. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 21. For now you are nothing: you see my casting down and are afraid. JFB reads it as the dried brook to the caravan — Job's friends are a nothing to him — and puts a mild construction on their failure: they are struck aghast at his misery and lose presence of mind. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 22. Did Job ask you to bring him a gift or pay a ransom from your substance? All he asked was affectionate treatment, not money or rescue. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 23. Or to deliver Job from the enemy's hand and redeem him from the mighty? JFB explains the mighty as the oppressor or creditor in whose power the debtor stood. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 24. Teach Job, and he will hold his tongue; cause him to understand where he has erred. JFB reads irony here: if the friends can teach the right view, Job will be set right, but their words prove feeble. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 25. How forcible are right words, but what does your arguing reprove? JFB reverses the English force: how feeble are the words of what you call the right view, and the reproofs proceed from you, who are not in Job's situation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 26. Do you imagine reproving words and the speeches of one desperate, which are as wind? JFB notes the speeches of a desperate man are mere nothings, not to be narrowly taken to task. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 27. You would overwhelm the fatherless and dig a pit for your friend. JFB explains the hunting image: causing a net of sophistry to fall on the desolate, and digging a covered pit to catch Job in unguarded language. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 28. Now therefore be content to look upon Job, for it is evident to you if he lies. JFB renders it as pleased to look: his countenance will betray him if he is the hypocrite they suppose. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 29. Return, let it not be iniquity; return again, for Job's righteousness is in it. Job asks his friends to reconsider rather than persist in an unjust judgment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
  • Verse 30. Is there iniquity in Job's tongue, and cannot his taste discern perverse things? He appeals to his own moral palate: he can tell right speech from perverse speech. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown

Where the speakers disagree

Eliphaz holds that suffering traces to guilt and that Job should seek God and be restored; Job answers that his grief outweighs the sand of the sea, that his friends have failed him like desert brooks that vanish in the heat, and that if they can teach him his error he will hold his tongue. The disagreement is not over whether God is just, but over whether Job’s case fits Eliphaz’s rule. Interpreted Study notes for this chapter, summarizing Jamieson-Fausset-Brown
