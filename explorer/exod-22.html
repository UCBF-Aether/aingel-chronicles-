The Text

Scripture — not editorial explanation

The Text — Exodus 22 (King James Version (1611); public-domain text as received (Project Gutenberg no. 10))

1 If a man shall steal an ox, or a sheep, and kill it, or sell it; he shall restore five oxen for an ox, and four sheep for a sheep.

2 If a thief be found breaking up, and be smitten that he die, there shall no blood be shed for him.

3 If the sun be risen upon him, there shall be blood shed for him; for he should make full restitution; if he have nothing, then he shall be sold for his theft.

4 If the theft be certainly found in his hand alive, whether it be ox, or ass, or sheep; he shall restore double.

5 If a man shall cause a field or vineyard to be eaten, and shall put in his beast, and shall feed in another man’s field; of the best of his own field, and of the best of his own vineyard, shall he make restitution.

6 If fire break out, and catch in thorns, so that the stacks of corn, or the standing corn, or the field, be consumed therewith; he that kindled the fire shall surely make restitution.

7 If a man shall deliver unto his neighbour money or stuff to keep, and it be stolen out of the man’s house; if the thief be found, let him pay double.

8 If the thief be not found, then the master of the house shall be brought unto the judges, to see whether he have put his hand unto his neighbour’s goods.

9 For all manner of trespass, whether it be for ox, for ass, for sheep, for raiment, or for any manner of lost thing which another challengeth to be his, the cause of both parties shall come before the judges; and whom the judges shall condemn, he shall pay double unto his neighbour.

10 If a man deliver unto his neighbour an ass, or an ox, or a sheep, or any beast, to keep; and it die, or be hurt, or driven away, no man seeing it:

11 Then shall an oath of the LORD be between them both, that he hath not put his hand unto his neighbour’s goods; and the owner of it shall accept thereof, and he shall not make it good.

12 And if it be stolen from him, he shall make restitution unto the owner thereof.

13 If it be torn in pieces, then let him bring it for witness, and he shall not make good that which was torn.

14 And if a man borrow ought of his neighbour, and it be hurt, or die, the owner thereof being not with it, he shall surely make it good.

15 But if the owner thereof be with it, he shall not make it good: if it be an hired thing, it came for his hire.

16 And if a man entice a maid that is not betrothed, and lie with her, he shall surely endow her to be his wife.

17 If her father utterly refuse to give her unto him, he shall pay money according to the dowry of virgins.

18 Thou shalt not suffer a witch to live.

19 Whosoever lieth with a beast shall surely be put to death.

20 He that sacrificeth unto any god, save unto the LORD only, he shall be utterly destroyed.

21 Thou shalt neither vex a stranger, nor oppress him: for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt.

22 Ye shall not afflict any widow, or fatherless child.

23 If thou afflict them in any wise, and they cry at all unto me, I will surely hear their cry;

24 And my wrath shall wax hot, and I will kill you with the sword; and your wives shall be widows, and your children fatherless.

25 If thou lend money to any of my people that is poor by thee, thou shalt not be to him as an usurer, neither shalt thou lay upon him usury.

26 If thou at all take thy neighbour’s raiment to pledge, thou shalt deliver it unto him by that the sun goeth down:

27 For that is his covering only, it is his raiment for his skin: wherein shall he sleep? and it shall come to pass, when he crieth unto me, that I will hear; for I am gracious.

28 Thou shalt not revile the gods, nor curse the ruler of thy people.

29 Thou shalt not delay to offer the first of thy ripe fruits, and of thy liquors: the firstborn of thy sons shalt thou give unto me.

30 Likewise shalt thou do with thine oxen, and with thy sheep: seven days it shall be with his dam; on the eighth day thou shalt give it me.

31 And ye shall be holy men unto me: neither shall ye eat any flesh that is torn of beasts in the field; ye shall cast it to the dogs.

Understand (editorial explanation — not Scripture)

Overview

The judgments continue: restitution for theft and for damage to field or flock, care for deposits left in trust, and laws for seduction, sorcery, and sacrifice to other gods. Alongside these penalties stand protections for the vulnerable: no afflicting the stranger, the widow, or the fatherless, no usury against the poor, and no cursing the ruler of the people. What the chapter says, verse by verse

The notes below are the site’s study-note summaries of the public-domain commentators named. They are paraphrase summaries, not quotations from those commentators.

  • Verse 1. If a man stole an ox or a sheep, and killed it or sold it, he would restore five oxen for an ox and four sheep for a sheep. Henry notes the scale of restitution taught that theft must cost more than it gained, and the people of God should ever show mildness and mercy according to the spirit of these laws. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 2. If a thief was found breaking up, and was smitten that he died, there would be no blood shed for him. Henry observes the householder defending his home by night was not a murderer, for the darkness hid the thief's intent. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 3. If the sun was risen upon him, there would be blood shed for him, for he should make full restitution, and if he had nothing, he would be sold for his theft. Henry notes daylight changed the case: the thief could be seen and caught, so killing him was no longer defence but revenge, and restitution, not death, was the remedy. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 4. If the theft was certainly found in his hand alive, whether ox, ass, or sheep, he would restore double. Henry observes the double payment marked the difference between a thief caught with the goods and one who had destroyed or sold them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 5. If a man caused a field or vineyard to be eaten, and put in his beast to feed in another man's field, he would make restitution of the best of his own field and vineyard. Henry notes we must answer to God not only for what we do maliciously but for what we do heedlessly, and the best, not the worst, was the measure of repayment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 6. If fire broke out and caught in thorns, so that stacks of corn, or standing corn, or the field was consumed, he that kindled the fire would surely make restitution. Henry observes carelessness with fire was treated like carelessness with beasts: the kindler bore the loss his fire caused. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 7. If a man delivered unto his neighbour money or stuff to keep, and it was stolen out of the man's house, if the thief was found, he would pay double. Henry notes the deposit was sacred: the keeper held his neighbour's goods in trust, and theft from trust was doubly punished. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 8. If the thief was not found, then the master of the house would be brought unto the judges, to see whether he had put his hand unto his neighbour's goods. Henry observes suspicion was not proof: the judges, not the owner, decided whether the keeper himself had taken the deposit. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 9. For all manner of trespass, whether for ox, ass, sheep, raiment, or any lost thing, both parties would come before the judges, and whom the judges condemned would pay double unto his neighbour. Henry notes the courts, not private hands, settled disputed ownership, that restitution might be just and not vengeful. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 10. If a man delivered unto his neighbour an ass, ox, sheep, or any beast to keep, and it died, or was hurt, or driven away, no man seeing it, the case turned on oath. Henry observes unwitnessed loss could not be tried by evidence, so it was tried by oath. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 11. Then an oath of the LORD would be between them both, that he had not put his hand unto his neighbour's goods, and the owner would accept thereof, and the keeper would not make it good. Henry notes the oath invoked God as witness where men could not see, and the owner's acceptance of it ended the matter. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 12. And if it was stolen from him, he would make restitution unto the owner thereof. Henry observes the distinction: theft implied the keeper's failure to guard, and failure to guard was itself a debt. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 13. If it was torn in pieces, then let him bring it for witness, and he would not make good that which was torn. Henry notes the torn carcass was evidence: a beast taken by wild animals was no man's fault, and the remains proved it. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 14. If a man borrowed ought of his neighbour, and it was hurt or died, the owner not being with it, he would surely make it good. Henry observes borrowing carried full risk: the borrower who used another's beast alone bore its loss. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 15. But if the owner was with it, he would not make it good, and if it was a hired thing, it came for its hire. Henry notes the owner's presence shared the risk, and a hired beast's loss was covered by its hire, for the owner had already been paid for the risk. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 16. If a man enticed a maid that was not betrothed, and lay with her, he would surely endow her to be his wife. Henry observes the law protected the maid by binding the seducer to marriage, so that wrong could not be done without lifelong responsibility. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 17. If her father utterly refused to give her unto him, he would pay money according to the dowry of virgins. Henry notes the father's refusal did not free the man: the dowry was still owed, for the wrong to the maid remained. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 18. Thou shalt not suffer a witch to live. Henry observes witchcraft was capital because it sought power from spirits other than God, a direct assault on the first commandment. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 19. Whosoever lieth with a beast shall surely be put to death. Henry notes the abomination was capital, for it defiled both man and beast and broke the order of creation. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 20. He that sacrificeth unto any god, save unto the LORD only, he shall be utterly destroyed. Henry observes idolatry was treason, not mere error, for sacrifice belonged to the LORD alone. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 21. Thou shalt neither vex a stranger, nor oppress him, for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt. Henry notes the reason was memory: Israel had been strangers and knew the stranger's fear, and mildness to strangers was the fruit of grace. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 22. Ye shall not afflict any widow, or fatherless child. Henry observes the defenceless were put under God's special guard, for they had no protector but him. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 23. If thou afflict them in any wise, and they cry at all unto me, I will surely hear their cry. Henry notes the promise was absolute: the cry of the afflicted widow and orphan reaches God's ear without fail. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 24. And my wrath shall wax hot, and I will kill you with the sword, and your wives shall be widows, and your children fatherless. Henry observes the punishment mirrored the crime: those who made widows and orphans would leave widows and orphans behind them. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 25. If thou lend money to any of my people that is poor by thee, thou shalt not be to him as a usurer, neither shalt thou lay upon him usury. Henry notes lending to the poor was charity, not business, and interest on misery was forbidden. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 26. If thou at all take thy neighbour's raiment to pledge, thou shalt deliver it unto him by that the sun goeth down. Henry observes the pledge was limited by mercy: a man's cloak might be held by day but must be returned by night. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 27. For that is his covering only, it is his raiment for his skin, wherein shall he sleep? And it shall come to pass, when he crieth unto me, that I will hear, for I am gracious. Henry notes the reason was God's own graciousness: the poor man's cry would be heard because God hears the poor. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 28. Thou shalt not revile the gods, nor curse the ruler of thy people. Henry observes the judges and rulers were not to be reviled, for authority itself was God's ordinance, even when its holders failed. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 29. Thou shalt not delay to offer the first of thy ripe fruits, and of thy liquors, and the firstborn of thy sons shalt thou give unto me. Henry notes delay in firstfruits was itself disobedience: God must have the first, not the remainder. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 30. Likewise shalt thou do with thine oxen and thy sheep: seven days it shall be with his dam, and on the eighth day thou shalt give it me. Henry observes the eighth day marked the offering as deliberate: the firstborn was given after its first week with its mother, not snatched at birth. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
  • Verse 31. And ye shall be holy men unto me, neither shall ye eat any flesh that is torn of beasts in the field, but ye shall cast it to the dogs. Henry closes on holiness: a people set apart to God must not eat what was torn, for grace teaches us to live soberly, righteously, and godly in this present world. Interpreted Study note, summarizing Matthew Henry
