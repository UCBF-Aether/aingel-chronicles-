The Ancient Maps Room

How Christians pictured the world before modern maps — not charts for sailing, but pictures of creation, each with its own center, its own errors, and its own faith.

Ptolemy’s World, Drawn Again: The Geographia World Map

The Ulm edition of 1482 — a Renaissance reconstruction of a second-century idea

Read this first — an honest label. No ancient original of Ptolemy’s maps survives. Every Ptolemaic world map shown today, including this one, is a later reconstruction drawn from the coordinates in his text. This map is not a second-century artifact. It is a woodcut printed at Ulm in 1482, nearly thirteen centuries after Ptolemy wrote.

The world map from Lienhart Holle’s 1482 Ulm edition of Ptolemy’s Cosmographia, as revised by Nicolaus Germanus and engraved by Johannes Schnitzer (Johannes de Armssheim). Image: Wikimedia Commons, file “1482 Cosmographia Germanus.JPG”.

Who made it. The idea belongs to Claudius Ptolemy, a Greco-Roman scholar who lived and worked in Alexandria in the second century (about AD 90–168, by the National Library of Scotland’s dating; other sources give about 100–170 — his exact dates are uncertain). In his Geographia, also called the Guide to Geography, he did not simply describe places: he gave mapmakers a method. The drawing itself belongs to other hands. The Greek text was recovered in Byzantium in the late thirteenth century, translated into Latin by Jacobus Angelus (Jacopo d’Angelo) in 1406, revised by the German cartographer Nicolaus Germanus (Donnus Nicolaus Germanus), and printed at Ulm by Lienhart Holle in 1482, with the woodcut engraved by Johannes Schnitzer. Ptolemy is the author of the geography; Germanus, Schnitzer, and Holle are the makers of this printed map. The two must not be confused.

When it was made. Ptolemy’s Geographia was written in the second century, describing the inhabited world as it was known around AD 150. The map shown here is dated 1482 on its source page: the Ulm edition of Claudii Ptolemaei Cosmographia. It was among the first printed Ptolemaic atlases — Bologna had printed an edition in 1477 and Rome in 1478, and the Ulm edition of 1482 was the first printed north of the Alps. There is, the National Library of Scotland notes, no definite evidence that Ptolemy himself constructed maps or had maps made from his text; the earliest surviving Greek edition containing maps dates from the thirteenth century, and its maps are assumed to have been constructed at the time of publication from the text.

What it shows. Look first at the frame, not the coastlines. Curved parallels and converging meridians bend around the world — Ptolemy’s projection, his way of laying a round earth onto a flat page. Inside that grid sit roughly 8,000 named places, each given a latitude and a longitude in Books II–VII of the Geographia, with Book VIII explaining how to build maps from those tables. The known world — the oikoumene — runs from Britain and the far north in the west to India and China (Sera and Sinae) in the east, and from northern Europe down past the sources of the Nile. The Mediterranean, the best-known sea of the ancient world, is recognisable at the centre. To the south-east, the great island of Taprobane (Sri Lanka) rides below India, and the Indian Ocean sits like a lake, ringed by land: Africa reaches eastward and joins Asia, closing the sea.

What it gets wrong. Judged against a modern map, this world is stretched and pinched in telling ways. Asia runs far too far east: Ptolemy spread Eurasia across 180 degrees of longitude instead of about 130, because he used a circumference of the earth that was too small — 500 stadia to a degree where Eratosthenes had reckoned about 700. Africa’s shape is distorted, its eastern coast swinging round to fence in the Indian Ocean, which is therefore shown land-locked rather than open to the southern seas. India itself is foreshortened, its peninsula squashed, while Taprobane is drawn many times its true size, stretching across the equator. The Mediterranean, too, is about twenty degrees too long. Most of these errors came honestly: longitude in the ancient world was usually guessed from travellers’ reported distances, not measured from the stars, so every coordinate was a best estimate built on someone else’s road.

Why it matters. Before Ptolemy, a map was largely a picture. After him, a map could be a set of numbers: give every place a latitude and a longitude, and anyone with skill and the tables can draw the same world again, check it, and correct it. That is the first systematic mathematical geography, and it is why this old, wrong map is a treasure rather than a curiosity. When the text returned to Western Europe in Latin in the early fifteenth century, after centuries of preservation in Byzantium and the Islamic world, it gave Renaissance mapmakers a grid, a projection, and a method — and for generations their atlases were built on Ptolemy’s frame, even as explorers’ voyages began to correct his coastlines. The same small earth that stretched Asia eastward also shrank the Atlantic in European minds, and helped persuade Columbus that Asia lay within reach by sailing west. Every coordinate map since — every time a place is fixed by two numbers rather than by a story — walks in the path this book opened.

Old, ancient, unheard, beautiful: a second-century mind’s whole world, kept alive through Byzantium and Baghdad, translated in Florence, cut in wood at Ulm — and still legible, grid-line by grid-line, more than five hundred years after the printing.

The Tabula Peutingeriana (the Peutinger Table)

A road map of the Roman world — in a medieval copy, not a Roman original.

Dating, stated plainly: the surviving object is a medieval parchment copy, described by the Austrian UNESCO Commission as a late-12th-century copy and elsewhere as dating from around 1200 — in other words, the 12th to 13th century. The Roman original behind it is lost. Scholars place its last revision in the 4th or early 5th century, but the deeper origin of that original is uncertain, as explained below. Nothing here should be read as if the parchment in Vienna were itself drawn in Rome.

The full strip of the Tabula Peutingeriana in Konrad Miller’s 1887/1888 facsimile edition — not the medieval parchment in Vienna, which, because of its fragility, is not usually on public display. 1. Who made it — three people, and a tradition, kept apart

First, the Roman original had an unknown author. One long-standing hypothesis traces it back, through later copies and revisions, to a world map prepared under Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa, a general and confidant of the emperor Augustus. That is a hypothesis, not a signed fact, and another scholar, Emily Albu, has argued instead for a Carolingian-period original. The honest label is: original author unknown; Agrippa origin proposed, not proven.

Second, the surviving copy was made by medieval copyists whose names are not established. It has been proposed that the copy was made by a monk in Colmar in 1265, but that proposal is disputed. The copyist tradition, not a single named medieval artist, is what we can responsibly credit.

Third, Konrad Peutinger did not make the map at all. He was a 16th-century German humanist and antiquarian in Augsburg who received it, gave it his name, and kept it in his family. The German scholar Conrad Celtes found it in a library in Worms in 1494, could not publish it before his death, and bequeathed it to Peutinger in 1508. Peutinger is the map's namesake and later owner — not its maker.

2. When it was made — a copy, a lost original, and layered dates

The parchment that survives today is a medieval copy, preserved in eleven sections and held as Codex 324 at the Austrian National Library in Vienna. The Austrian UNESCO Commission calls it a late-12th-century copy of an ancient scroll whose original has been lost. Other summaries put the copy at around 1200, or in the 12th or 13th century. Those wordings differ slightly, so this card gives the range rather than pretending to a single year.

The lost original is dated by its contents, and its contents do not belong to one year. It shows Constantinople, founded in 328, and gives prominence to Ravenna, seat of the Western Roman Empire from 402 to 476, which points to a 5th-century revision; it also still shows Pompeii, destroyed in AD 79 and never rebuilt, and mentions Francia, a state that came into existence only in the 5th century. The picture that emerges is a document revised and added to over centuries, last revised in the 4th or early 5th century, rather than a snapshot drawn on one day. Any earlier Agrippa-era layer, if it exists, survives only through those later revisions, and that link remains uncertain.

3. What it shows — the roads, not a portrait of the earth

The Tabula is an itinerarium: an illustrated Roman road map of the cursus publicus, the state-run road network of the Roman Empire. Unrolled, it is a long, narrow scroll — about 6.75 metres long and about 34–35 centimetres high — assembled from eleven surviving sections. Rome sits at the centre of its scheme, and the three great cities of the empire at the time — Rome, Constantinople and Antioch — are picked out with special, elaborate decoration.

Along the roads it names settlements, stations and the distances between them, and it also marks rivers, mountains, forests and seas. In total it carries no fewer than 555 cities and some 3,500 other place names. Its coverage runs beyond the empire itself: the Near East, Persia, India and the Ganges, Sri Lanka (called Insula Taprobane), and even an indication of China. A “Temple to Augustus” is marked at Muziris, a major port for Roman trade on the southwest coast of India.

The Holy Land portion is drawn in this same road language. Jerusalem appears in the centre of its section as a double-house icon — the mark of a major city — and is labelled, in the map's own wording, “formerly called Jerusalem, now called Aelia Capitolina” (antea dicta Hierusalem, nunc Helya Capitolina). That Roman renaming is itself part of the story: this is the empire's road book, written in the empire's terms. Nearby, the road from Caesarea (marked “Cesaria”) to Neapolis — Shechem — is given as 31 Roman miles. For a reader of the Bible, the value is not a devotional picture of the Holy Land, but a working record of the roads, towns and distances a traveller in the Roman world would actually have had to reckon with.

4. What it gets wrong — by a modern map's standards, a great deal

Judged as a modern geographical map, the Tabula is deliberately, extremely distorted. Its land masses are stretched, above all east to west, so that a world that is broad becomes a long ribbon. That is not a mistake in the copyist's draughtsmanship alone; it is the shape the scroll format imposed, and the shape a road list needs. Like a modern transit diagram, it cares about order along a route and distance to the next stop, not about true shape, scale or direction.

It is also incomplete in the form that survives. The western end is missing: Morocco, the Iberian Peninsula and the British Isles are absent, which indicates that a twelfth original section has been lost. That missing section was reconstructed in 1898 by Konrad Miller — a modern reconstruction, not part of the medieval parchment, and it should never be presented as if it were.

Finally, because the map was copied and revised over centuries, it mixes eras. A city destroyed in AD 79 sits in the same document as a city founded in 328. Copying, too, leaves its own trace: scholars discuss copyist errors in the transmission of this map, and the disputed Colmar proposal is a reminder that even the story of the surviving copy is not settled. Read it as a layered document, and its “errors” become evidence of how long it was used and re-copied.

5. Why it matters — a traveller's tool, and a survivor

The Tabula matters precisely because it is not trying to be a picture of the world. It is a functional network: the roads of an empire, with the stops in order and the miles between them. A traveller in the Roman world did not need a satellite view; the traveller needed to know what lay ahead on the road and how far away it was. This map preserves that knowledge in drawn form — the stations, the junctions, the distances — in a single, continuous strip from the Atlantic approaches to India. The Austrian UNESCO Commission makes the modern comparison explicit: it is closer in spirit to a diagram of underground lines than to a conventional atlas.

That practical character is also why it should not be romanticised. A 6.75-metre parchment scroll is not something an ordinary pilgrim would have carried on foot; the map preserves the itinerary tradition that travellers, soldiers, couriers and officials relied on, in monumental form. Its value for pilgrimage history is as a witness to the road system pilgrims and travellers moved through — the Caesarea road, the coastal route, the junctions toward Jerusalem — not as a pocket guide they are known to have held.

Its survival is itself remarkable. After Peutinger, his family kept it for more than two hundred years until it was sold in 1714. It passed between royal and elite hands, was bought by Prince Eugene of Savoy for 100 ducats, and on his death in 1737 came to the Habsburg Imperial Court Library in Vienna, where it has been conserved at the Austrian National Library since 1738. Printed copies followed — a partial edition in 1591 and a full edition in December 1598 — but the parchment itself, because of its fragility, is not usually on public display; it was shown for a single day, 26 November 2007, when UNESCO added it to its Memory of the World International Register. What survives is therefore both things at once: the only known surviving map of the Roman cursus publicus, and a medieval copy of a lost original. Hold both halves of that sentence, and the map is seen truly.

The Madaba Map — the pilgrim's Holy Land

6th-century mosaic · Church of St. George, Madaba, Jordan

This room links to the Madaba Map rather than rebuilding it. It is the oldest surviving cartographic depiction of the Holy Land, a sixth-century floor mosaic in the Byzantine church of St. George at Madaba — and it is the hinge of this room: the one map drawn on the ground pilgrims walked, its Jerusalem panel showing the Holy City as pilgrims themselves saw it.

The Saint-Sever Beatus World Map

Saint-Sever Abbey, Gascony, France · about 1050 (date range uncertain — see below) · Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, MS lat. 8878, folios 45bisv–45ter

Image held — no free license documented.

Open this map and you are not looking at a chart for sailors. You are looking at a sermon drawn as a world. It was made to sit inside a book about the end of the world — and to show, in one oval picture, where the good news was meant to go.

Who made it. The tradition begins with Beatus of Liébana, a Spanish monk whose Commentary on the Apocalypse was written in the late eighth century (776–784 in the sources consulted). Beatus himself did not make this copy: his original manuscript is lost, and the map survives only in later copies. This particular copy was made at the Abbey of Saint-Sever in Gascony, in what is now France — the only surviving Beatus manuscript known to have been produced across the Pyrenees in France, rather than in Spain. Its scribe signs himself Stephanus Garsia, and he dedicates the work to his abbot, Gregory (Gregori) de Montaner, abbot from 1028 to 1072. Other hands also worked on the manuscript. The map itself is therefore a copy in a long chain of copies, not Beatus’s own hand — and scholars debate how faithfully each copy preserves any lost original.

When it was made. About 1050, with the date frankly uncertain. Sources consulted give c. 1050, the mid-eleventh century, 1050–1070, and 1060–1070. This card uses “about 1050” and does not pretend to a single exact year. What is secure is the setting: the abbacy of Gregory de Montaner (1028–1072) and the middle of the eleventh century.

What it shows. The world is an oval ringed by the outer ocean, turned so that east — oriens, the direction of sunrise and of Paradise — is at the top, not north. That is why such maps are called “oriented.” Asia fills the upper half; Europe and Africa fill the lower half, divided by the Mediterranean, the Nile, and other seas into the familiar T-O shape: a T of water inside an O of ocean. The three continents were understood as the portions of Noah’s three sons, Shem, Ham, and Japheth.

At the eastern, upper edge stands the Garden of Eden, where Eve is shown in the very act of plucking the fruit, Adam beside her, and the serpent wound about the tree. Jerusalem stands near the centre of the world, as the umbilicus mundi, the navel of the world — the city of the Passion and Resurrection. Around the coasts are sausage-shaped islands in the outer ocean, with fish and boats, and in the Mediterranean the larger islands of Sicily, Crete, and Cyprus. City symbols crowd western Europe, northern Africa, and Asia Minor. The Saint-Sever map is the largest and most detailed of the Beatus maps, carrying about 270 names, with rivers, mountain chains, and long quotations — mostly from Isidore of Seville — about the wonders to be found in distant lands.

Its purpose explains its shape. The map appears in the prologue to the second book of the Commentary on the Apocalypse, and its main goal was not cartographic exactness but to illustrate the first dispersion of the apostles: the world as a field for preaching, with every land awaiting the gospel. It preaches as much as it describes. Even a local touch preaches loyalty: the humble church of Saint-Sever itself is drawn as large as the city symbols of Rome and Constantinople.

At the far south, beyond a Red Sea coloured red, the map admits what it does not know. A small sliver of a circle represents a fourth part of the world across the interior ocean, “unknown to us on account of the heat of the sun,” where the antipodes are reported, in story, to dwell. The far edges, in this tradition, are where ancient writers placed wonders and strange peoples — reported lore, drawn alongside real cities, not separated from them.

What it gets wrong, by modern geography. Almost everything a modern atlas would require. The world is not an oval disc with east on top and Asia taking half the picture. Africa is cut off by the Red Sea and stretched into a shape no modern map recognises; Britain, Sardinia, and other islands are given in miles from older writers such as Pliny rather than from survey. There are no Americas, no Australia, no Pacific as we know it, and no true sense of the size of Asia or Africa. Paradise is placed as a real place at the eastern edge, with its four rivers flowing out; a reported fourth continent is drawn from a quotation, not from travel; and monstrous and marvellous peoples at the edges are presented as part of the world’s furniture. None of this is a failure of honesty on the mapmaker’s terms — he was copying revered authorities, not measuring coastlines — but measured against the modern globe, it is a theological picture, not a survey.

Why it matters. Before modern maps, this is how ordinary Christians pictured creation: a bounded, ordered world, centred on Jerusalem, crowned by Paradise, ringed by ocean, and already claimed for Christ by the apostles’ mission. The map does not ask, “How do I get there?” It asks, “What is this world for?” Bound into a commentary on Revelation, it joins the beginning of the story (Eden at the top) to its end (the Apocalypse the book expounds). To stand before it is to see the world through eleventh-century Christian eyes: small, storied, sacred — and waiting.

Old, ancient, unheard, beautiful: a thousand-year-old picture of the whole world, painted by monks for monks, in which geography itself becomes theology. That is why it belongs in this museum — not as a reliable atlas, but as a treasure of how believers once saw the earth God made.

The Hereford Mappa Mundi

A world drawn as a story of creation, history, and judgment — Hereford Cathedral, England, about 1300

Image held — no free license documented. No image is shown on this card, because no versioned free license for an image of this map was pinned on the image source page examined for this piece. The story below stands on its own.

It is, by one plain measure, the largest medieval map still known to exist: a single sheet of vellum, calf skin, about 158 by 133 centimetres, with the known world drawn inside a circle about 52 inches across. It is not a chart for finding your way. It is a picture of the world as its makers understood it — in spiritual as well as geographical terms — and that is exactly why it is a treasure.

Who made it — attributed, with uncertainty

The map bears the name of its author, “Richard of Haldingham or Lafford” — Holdingham and Sleaford in Lincolnshire. On the map he is credited as the one who “made and laid it out.” That is the name the map itself gives us, and it is where certainty ends.

Scholars note that the links around this name remain speculative. One Richard of Haldingham died in 1278, before the map’s usual date of about 1300, and the production may have involved several patrons rather than a single maker working alone. So this card says “attributed to Richard of Haldingham or Lafford,” not that one man certainly drew every line. What is not in doubt is where the original survives: Hereford Cathedral in Hereford, England, where it is still displayed.

When it was made

About 1300 — that date is given as approximate, not exact. Hereford Cathedral’s own account says recent research suggests a date of about 1300. The map is therefore described here as c. 1300, a date range around the turn of the fourteenth century, not a single proved year.

What it shows

Turn the map in your mind until east is at the top, as its makers set it. At the very top edge, in a walled circle ringed with fire, lies the Garden of Eden — Paradise, where the story begins. At the dead centre lies Jerusalem, the holy city, with the crucifix above it. Around it, the world spreads out in a circle, and above the whole circle sits the Last Judgment, where the story ends.

The shape is the famous T-and-O pattern. The “O” is the encircling ocean around the known world. The “T” is made by the Mediterranean Sea and the rivers Don and Nile, dividing the land into three continents: Asia at the top, Europe at the bottom left, and Africa at the bottom right. In the medieval reading, those three parts belonged to the three sons of Noah — Shem, Ham, and Japheth.

Inside that frame, the makers crowded in the history of humankind and the marvels of the natural world — about 500 drawings in all, by Hereford Cathedral’s count, including around 420 cities and towns, 15 Biblical events, 33 plants, animals, birds and strange creatures, and 32 images of the peoples of the world. You can follow the Exodus route, find Noah’s Ark, the Tower of Babel, Babylon and the Euphrates, the Red Sea painted red, Egypt and the Nile, Rome, Constantinople, and — small, at the north-western edge, the bottom left — Great Britain and Ireland, far from the centre where Jerusalem sits.

The creatures and legends are part of the picture, not decoration added at the edges. Elephants and camels appear, alongside beasts from medieval bestiaries and the monstrous races of legend — including the Blemmyes, a headless people whose faces were said to sit upon their chests. Scenes from classical mythology appear too, and the map is noted as the earliest known map to depict the mythical St Brendan’s Isle. Its sources, as scholars list them, include the Bible, the Alexander tradition, medieval bestiaries, legends of monstrous races, and earlier maps such as that of the Beatus of Liébana codex.

What it gets wrong — by modern geography

Measured against a modern atlas, this map is wrong in ways its makers would not have counted as the point — but the differences are honest to name:

  • Shapes and sizes: continents are squeezed into a theological circle, not drawn to scale. Britain is small and pressed to the edge; the Nile’s delta is oversized; the world stops at the encircling ocean.
  • Placements: the labels for Africa and Europe are reversed, with Europe inscribed in red and gold as “Africa” and vice versa. Crete is misplaced, with the Minotaur’s labyrinth drawn upon it.
  • Seas that do not connect: the Caspian Sea is shown joining the encircling ocean, although William of Rubruck had already reported it landlocked in 1255, decades before the map was made.
  • Myth presented as real: monstrous races, the Garden of Eden as a place at the world’s eastern edge, St Brendan’s Isle, and the enclosed peoples of Gog and Magog are drawn among real cities and rivers, as part of the same world.

One correction matters for fairness: the circular T-and-O shape does not by itself mean its makers thought the earth was flat. The spherical shape of the earth was known to the ancient Greeks and Romans and was never entirely forgotten in the Middle Ages; the circle here is a conventional picture of the inhabited known world, not a claim about the shape of the planet.

Why it matters

The Hereford Mappa Mundi matters because it shows us a medieval Christian world-picture whole. It was made as an intricate work of art and a teaching object, not as a navigational aid. A pilgrim or visitor standing before it — very likely with a guide explaining what could be seen, heard, and read — would have been shown not only where places were, but where they belonged in the story: creation at the top, Jerusalem at the centre, the journey of God’s people across the map, and judgment above it all.

That is why Jerusalem is central and Britain is marginal, why east is up, and why monsters and saints share the same parchment. For its makers, geography, Scripture, history, and legend were not separate subjects. They were one creation, seen from inside the faith. The map also carries a harder honesty for a modern museum: recent scholarship reads some of its images — a horned Moses, Jews worshipping the Golden Calf as a devil figure, and the prominence of the Exodus route — in connection with the expulsion of Jews from England in 1290 and with the cult of Thomas de Cantilupe at Hereford. Those readings are interpretations, not settled facts, and they are noted here so the treasure is not smoothed into something simpler than it was.

After the Reformation the map was neglected, hidden for a time beneath the floor of Bishop Audley’s Chantry, cleaned and repaired at the British Museum in 1855, kept away from Hereford during the Second World War and returned in 1946. In 1988 the cathedral proposed selling it; public fundraising kept it in Hereford, and a new library building to house it opened in 1996. In 2007 it was inscribed on UNESCO’s Memory of the World International Register. It survived because people kept deciding it was worth keeping — a world, drawn by hand on one calf skin, still teaching anyone willing to stand before it and look.

Palaestinae sive Totius Terrae Promissionis Nova Descriptio

“Palestine, or a New Description of the Whole Promised Land” — Abraham Ortelius, after Tilemann Stella · first plate, Antwerp, 1570

Image held — no free license documented. No image is shown with this card. A freely licensed image of this exact map, with its license pinned on its own source page, was not found during research for this piece, so the story stands without a picture rather than borrowing one without proof.

Before there were Bibles with maps tucked in the back as a matter of course, there was this: a whole land, drawn so a reader could walk it with the eye. From Beirut in the north to Egypt and the Red Sea in the south, and from the Mediterranean to Damascus, Ortelius lays out the Bible’s stage as one continuous country — not a scatter of holy places, but a place you could travel.

Who made it. The map was published by Abraham Ortelius of Antwerp (1527–1598), the maker of the Theatrum Orbis Terrarum, the first modern atlas. Its title credits its author as Tilemann Stella of Siegen: “Auctore Tilemanno Stella Sigenensi.” Ortelius did not hide that debt — he named Stella on the map itself. Stella’s own Holy Land map is variously dated by dealers and reference works to 1552 or 1557, so that earlier date is left uncertain here. The engraver of this particular plate was not verified from a source read for this piece, and is therefore not named.

When it was made. The first plate of this map appeared in the Latin editions of Ortelius’s Theatrum in 1570, as plate 51 in the 1570 Latin printings. It was reprinted again and again: the same design, in later states, appears in later Theatrum and Parergon editions, including a 1624 Parergon printing from Antwerp. Later printings are not the first publication, and this card dates the map by its 1570 first plate, not by any single later copy.

What it shows. The map shows the Holy Land as the Bible’s stage. Its upper-left cartouche tells the land’s old names in sequence: first Canaan, named for a son of Ham whose sons divided it; then Israel, after Jacob; then Palestine, after the Philistines named in the sacred books; and in Ortelius’s own day, the Holy Land. Across that ground it traces the Exodus journey out of Egypt: the Israelites assemble at Rameses, cross the Red Sea, wander in the Sinai wilderness — the stations of the wandering numbered, year by year, toward Mount Sinai — and at last cross the Jordan, just north of the Dead Sea, into the Promised Land. It also lays out the territories of the Twelve Tribes on both sides of the Jordan, with towns, rivers, mountains drawn in profile, sailing ships and sea creatures in the Mediterranean, and two great strapwork cartouches framing the whole.

What it gets wrong. This is a Bible drawn from books, not a land surveyed on foot. Its coastline and its Dead Sea come down to Stella from Jacob Ziegler’s 1532 map of Palestine: Ortelius rounds Stella’s angular Dead Sea and Gulf of Aqaba, but the shapes remain simplified and schematic beside a modern surveyed map — the Dead Sea drawn crescent-like rather than in its true, longer form. The Exodus stations are plotted as a neat, numbered itinerary, as if forty years of wandering left a single traceable line with one station per year; the biblical text gives station lists, but no such surveyed route survives, and many proposed sites remain debated. Likewise the tribal allotments are drawn as tidy, bounded territories, although the allotment lists in Joshua are largely town lists, not surveyed borders. Read it, then, as a sixteenth-century reading of Scripture made visible — beautiful, learned, and not a modern archaeological map.

Why it matters. Ortelius’s Parergon — the series of maps of ancient history, sacred and secular, that grew out of the Theatrum — is regarded as the first historical atlas. This Holy Land sheet belongs to the first generation of maps made for Bible readers rather than for sailors or university geographers alone. Bound into an atlas that ordinary wealthy readers could buy, keep, and open beside the text, it taught a new habit: to picture the story on the ground, to follow Moses out of Egypt with a finger, to see where tribe bordered tribe, and to feel the Promised Land as a real country with edges, rivers, and roads. That habit never left Bible reading. Every Bible atlas since — including the plain maps in the back of a modern Bible — is this map’s descendant.

Old, ancient, unheard, beautiful: a 1570 engraving that let a reader hold the whole Promised Land in two hands, and walk the Exodus without leaving Antwerp.
