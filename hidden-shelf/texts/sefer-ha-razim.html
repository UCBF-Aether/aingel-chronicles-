What This Book Is

Sefer Ha-Razim, the Book of Secrets, is a Jewish work of late antiquity that sets out a map of the heavens and the angels said to dwell in them. Its picture of the world is ordered and hierarchical: heaven is not one undifferentiated space but a series of layered firmaments, each with its own hosts, officers, and overseers.

It is also, frankly, a magical book. Woven through its cosmology are instructions for workings meant to gain knowledge, favour, healing, protection, and other practical ends. That second element is exactly what this page does not reproduce: the cosmology can be described historically without handing over a working manual, and that is the line this page keeps.

The book belongs to the same broad world as the Hekhalot (heavenly-palace) literature and the Greek magical papyri of the same centuries. It shows Jewish and Greek traditions side by side — angels with Hebrew-style names alongside Greek terms and, in places, Greek material — which is part of why scholars value it as evidence for what ordinary religious life actually looked like, beyond the official literature.

It must not be confused with a later, different book of a similar name, Sefer Raziel HaMalakh (The Book of Raziel the Angel), a medieval compilation. Large parts of Sefer Ha-Razim were later absorbed into that compilation, but the two are not the same work.

When, and Whose Tradition

The book is Jewish, composed in the world of late-antique Judaism, in the centuries when rabbinic literature was taking shape and popular religion ran alongside it. Its Hebrew is midrashic in flavour, and its concerns — angels, heavens, divine names, purity — are recognisably Jewish, even where its practices sit uneasily with rabbinic norms.

Most scholars date the original work to around the turn of the fourth century CE, late third to early fourth century. One widely cited clue is an internal reference to the Roman tax-indiction cycle, which points to a date after 297 CE. A minority view, notably that of Ithamar Gruenwald, would place it later, in the sixth or seventh century. The earlier dating is the more commonly accepted one, and the later dating is noted here rather than hidden.

According to the book's own preface, the angel Raziel gave the book to Noah before the Flood, and it passed down through biblical history until it reached Solomon, to whom the book attributes great wisdom. That chain of transmission is the book's own story about itself, in the manner of ancient pseudepigrapha, and it is reported here as such — it is not a historical record of the book's composition.

The book as we have it was lost and then recovered in modern times. Mordecai Margalioth, working in Oxford in 1963, recognised that scattered Cairo Geniza fragments belonged to a single work, and published his reconstructed Hebrew edition in 1966. No complete ancient copy survives; the text is an editor's reconstruction from about fourteen fragments and related witnesses, which is why its details remain partly uncertain.

Structure: A Preface and Seven Firmaments

The book is short — roughly eight hundred lines in Margalioth's edition — and its plan is simple and regular. A preface tells the story of the book's giving and transmission, and then seven sections follow, one for each firmament, ascending from the lowest heaven to the highest.

Each of the first six sections describes the angels of that firmament — their encampments, steps, or princes — and, in the full text, attaches workings to them. The seventh section is different: it contains no comparable angel lists or workings, but a description of the divine throne and a long doxology, a hymn of praise to the One seated on the throne. That closing praise is the book's climax, and it is the one part of the book that reads as pure liturgy rather than as a manual.

Scholars count roughly seven hundred angel names across the whole work. The names themselves are part of the book's historical interest: many end in familiar angelic elements, while others carry Greek roots, a visible trace of the mixed cultural world in which the book was written.

The Seven Firmaments, Described

What follows is a descriptive summary of each firmament in general terms — what kind of place the book pictures it as, and who is said to stand there. No working, invocation, or procedure is included. Angel names are given as a descriptive list only, without their uses.

The First Firmament — Shamayim

The lowest heaven is called Shamayim, "Heavens." The book pictures it as filled with encampments of angels, arranged around seven thrones on which seven overseers sit. The overseers stand over the encampments and dispatch them on their business.

The seven overseers named for this firmament are: Aurpnial, Tigrh, Dnhl, Klmial (also given as Hlmia), Asimur, Psbr, and Bual. The book describes these overseers as fiery in appearance, formed of fire.

Note on names: spellings of these names vary between editions because the Hebrew is unvocalised and the manuscripts disagree. The forms above follow the commonly cited scholarly lists.

The Second Firmament — The Heaven of Heavens

The second firmament is called the heaven of heavens. It is a fearful place in the book's picture: cold and storm are stored there — treasuries of snow and hail, frost and fog — alongside angels of fire, angels of moisture, and spirits of terror and dread. Its hosts are described as armies upon armies, with officers and overseers over them.

Within it are twelve steps, and on each step stand angels in splendour, one rank of officials above another. The book counts the angels of this firmament in large numbers, roughly eight to twenty on each step.

The Third Firmament

The third firmament is pictured in mist and storm, with thunderous encampments. Three angelic princes rule it, described as fiery in appearance. They are named Ibnial, Rhṭial, and Dlqial.

The Fourth Firmament — The Sun's Course

The fourth firmament belongs to the sun. The book pictures two companies of angelic princes: one company leads the sun along its daytime course, and another leads it through the night — about thirty princes in each company. Angels of fire are associated with the day, and angels of cold and water with the night.

The Fifth Firmament — The Twelve Months

The fifth firmament is governed by twelve princes of glory, one for each month of the year. The book sets them near the throne of glory and pictures them as thronelike visions of fire, governing the turning of the months.

The Sixth Firmament — East and West

The sixth firmament is divided between east and west. Encampments stand on both sides — twenty-eight angelic leaders with their encampments in the east, and thirty-one in the west, by the commonly cited count. The head of the western encampments is named Aparkm, and the head of the eastern encampments Tuqpirs.

The Seventh Firmament — The Throne

The seventh firmament is the highest heaven, and the book pictures it as pure light. All of it is sevenfold light, the book says, and from its light all the seven heavens shine. Within it stands the throne of glory, borne upon the four glorious Hayot, the living creatures. There too are the storehouses of lives and the storehouses of souls.

The light of this firmament is described as beyond measure or calculation, filling the whole earth. Angels stand fixed in pillars of light at the margins of the divine light, their eyes like flashes of lightning, glorifying in fear the One seated on the throne. Before him, the book pictures books of fire opened and rivers of fire flowing, and hosts upon hosts standing in reverence, veiling their faces and singing praise.

The closing doxology praises God as alone, prior to all creatures, hidden from every eye yet seeing all, holding the world whole, searching hearts before they are formed, and blessed in every place the book can name — in snow and flame, in thunder and lightning, in sea and desert, on the throne and among all living things. It is a hymn, not a procedure, and it is summarised here rather than quoted at length, because the available English renderings of it are copyrighted translations.

Why It Matters

Sefer Ha-Razim matters first as evidence. It is one of the few surviving Jewish magical manuals from late antiquity, and it shows a side of ancient Judaism that the rabbinic literature alone does not show: a popular religion of angels, names, and heavens that lived alongside official teaching, sometimes in tension with it.

It matters second as a bridge. Its mix of Jewish cosmology and Greek magical material places it exactly at the meeting point of two worlds, and later European magic — including the medieval Sefer Raziel and, through it, Renaissance grimoires — drew on it. To understand where later angel-magic came from, this book is one of the starting points.

It matters third as a caution. The book's own closing movement — from lists and workings upward to a throne, a light, and a hymn of praise to God alone — is a reminder of the distance between describing a heaven and claiming power over it. This page keeps to the first side of that line, and leaves the second side closed.

And it matters, finally, as a piece of honesty about the past. A book can be old, strange, beautiful in its cosmology, and historically precious, without being Scripture. Keeping it on a shelf marked as such — read for extra thoughts and for historical value, weighed against the Book itself — is the whole point of the Hidden Shelf.

What Has Deliberately Been Left Out

By the project's rule for magical texts — history and description only, never working ritual instructions — the following have been deliberately omitted from this page, and must not be added in a later revision without revisiting that rule:

  • All step-by-step ritual procedures ("to achieve X, do Y"), including purifications, timings, garments, offerings, and materials.
  • All usable incantation sequences, adjurations, and divine-name formulas, including the Greek prayer to Helios preserved in the fourth firmament.
  • The purposes attached to individual angels and encampments in the full text (healing, harm, favour, foresight, dreams, and the like) beyond the bare general statement that such workings exist.
  • The "characters" (symbolic figures forming a quasi-magical alphabet) assigned to some angels.
  • Any passage that would let a reader reconstruct a working from this page alone.

What is kept: the book's identity, date, tradition, structure, cosmological descriptions in general terms, and the names and stations of angels as a descriptive list. That is the whole of the intended scope, and the gaps flagged above concern translation sourcing, not missing ritual content.
