Places of the Book

The museum’s third room is the ground itself. Everything in this hall is old, real, and freely given — photographs of the ancient places and churches of the canon that still stand, each hung with its documented history and the chapters of the 81 books that walk its ground. A gift should carry its giver’s name, so every photograph stands with its photographer and its license.

Two frames here are honest about what they show, and their cards say so: at Axum the camera stands inside the new church of the same compound, and at Dura-Europos the place itself survives only in its painting.

Wall One — Ethiopia, the Canon’s Homeland

The churches of the church that kept all eighty-one books — carved down into living rock, set on a rope-cliff, gathered around a great lake.

Bete Giyorgis — Lalibela

12th–13th century · Lalibela, Ethiopia

Lalibela holds eleven monolithic rock-hewn churches, carved in the 12th–13th centuries under King Gebre Mesqel Lalibela of the Zagwe dynasty, who is said to have built the town as a “New Jerusalem.” Bete Giyorgis, dedicated to St. George, patron saint of Ethiopia, is the most finely built and best preserved of them — a church in the form of a cross, cut down into a single block of living rock. The site has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1978 and remains in daily Ethiopian Orthodox worship: these churches still serve the church that kept the full 81-book canon, the Psalms and Gospels sung in Ge’ez on ground Lalibela shaped after Jerusalem itself.

Photograph by Bernard Gagnon · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0 — shared here under the same license.

St. Mary of Zion — Axum

The new church, 1950s · Axum, Ethiopia

Axum was the capital of the Aksumite kingdom. King Ezana’s conversion in the 4th century made it one of the first Christian kingdoms on earth; a church of St. Mary of Zion was built under Ezana, destroyed in the 1500s, and rebuilt. Ethiopian tradition holds that the Ark of the Covenant rests in the Chapel of the Tablet beside the old church, guarded by a single monk for life. The new church in this photograph — its interior, looking toward the curtained sanctuary — was built under Emperor Haile Selassie in the 1950s, within the same compound. The Kebra Nagast, one of the 81 books, tells how Menelik I, son of Solomon and the Queen of Sheba (1 Kings 10), brought the Ark to Axum; the Ark texts themselves are Exodus 25 and 37.

Photograph by A. Davey · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY 2.0

Debre Damo Monastery

Founded 6th century · Tigray, Ethiopia

Debre Damo is a flat-topped mountain in Tigray — about a thousand metres by four hundred, at 2,216 metres up — hosting a monastery tradition claims was founded in the 6th century by Abuna Aregawi, one of the Nine Saints. It is reached only by a rope up a sheer cliff some fifteen metres high, admits men only, and keeps both a famed collection of manuscripts and the earliest existing church building in Ethiopia still in its original Aksumite style. Its monks guarded Ge’ez manuscripts of the broad Ethiopian canon through the medieval centuries, and its founder belongs to the Nine Saints tradition that stands behind the Ge’ez Bible itself. The photograph shows a monastery church on the plateau’s edge.

Photograph by Rod Waddington · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 2.0 — shared here under the same license.

Ura Kidane Mehret — Lake Tana

14th century · Zege Peninsula, Lake Tana, Ethiopia

The convent of Ura Kidane Mehret was founded in the 14th century by the saint Betre Mariyam — “Rod of Mary” — and the present circular church dates from the 16th century. Its interior is covered with murals painted between roughly one hundred and two hundred and fifty years ago, the most significant by Alaqa Engida in the reign of Menelik II, and its treasury holds embroidered robes and the crowns of emperors Yohannes IV, Tewodros II and Tekle Giyorgis. Lake Tana’s island and peninsula monasteries have kept church treasures and manuscripts in isolation for centuries — part of the monastic world through which books like Enoch and Jubilees, lost elsewhere, survived complete inside the 81-book canon.

Photograph by Bernard Gagnon · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0 — shared here under the same license.

The Rome Stele — Axum

4th century · Axum, Ethiopia

The 24-metre re-erected Aksumite stele in the stele field at Axum, its carved false doors and window-tracery running up the shaft. Carved and erected in the fourth century by the Kingdom of Aksum, looted to Rome in 1937, and returned and re-erected at Axum in 2008. Captioned honestly: the stelae themselves are pre-Christian royal tombs, and this hangs here NOT as a Christian object but as the monument of the kingdom at its conversion under King Ezana (r. ca. 320s–360s) — the soil the Kebra Nagast, one of the 81 books, grows in: Menelik I, son of Solomon and the Queen of Sheba (1 Kings 10), bringing the Ark to Axum (Exodus 25, 37). Lalibela’s churches deliberately echo these stelae’s carved architecture.

Photograph by Ondřej Žváček · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0 — shared here under the same license.

Bete Medhane Alem — Lalibela

Rock-hewn · Lalibela, Ethiopia

The largest monolithic rock-hewn church in the world (33.5 × 23.5 m, over 11.5 m high), its temple-like colonnade of 34 exterior pillars, in its excavated pit. Northern group, Lalibela; thought by some to copy the original St. Mary of Zion at Axum; home of the Lalibela Cross (stolen 1997, recovered and returned 2001). Three symbolic empty graves inside are said to be prepared for Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. The Saviour of the World, in the New Jerusalem Lalibela built after Jerusalem — Genesis patriarchs honoured inside, Gospels sung in Ge'ez outside.

Photograph by Giustino · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY 2.0

Shrine inside Bete Medhane Alem

Lalibela, Ethiopia

A living shrine, not a ruin: the stand holding an image of the Virgin Mary with the infant Jesus under a knitted orange mantle, tin birds circling above painted metal cones, Ethiopian yellow-green-red everywhere. This is how the church is dressed for worship now, in the colours of Ethiopian Orthodox devotion. Mary and the infant Jesus (Luke 2; Matthew 2) — the devotional heart of the Ethiopian church, where Mary is venerated above all saints.

Photograph by A. Davey · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY 2.0

Adam and Eve — Abreha and Atsbeha

Tigray, Ethiopia

Abreha and Atsbeha, ~15 km from Wukro, is among Tigray's earliest churches — tradition dates it to the 4th-century twin kings Abreha and Atsbeha (identified with Ezana and Sayzana), whose bodies are claimed to be entombed there; its murals, in Ge'ez-labelled programmes of saints' lives and biblical scenes, are among the finest in Tigray. Genesis 2–3, the Fall itself, painted where the congregation stands. Pairs with the Dura-Europos Adam-and-Eve fresco already on the museum's wall — the same scene, an Ethiopian millennium later.

Photograph by Bernard Gagnon · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0, 2.5, 2.0, 1.0 (GFDL 1.2 also offered) — shared here under the same license.

Wukro Chirkos — Wukro, Tigray

4th century · Wukro, Tigray

The semi-monolithic church at the edge of Wukro town — side walls, front wall and roof fully freed from the rock, attached only at floor and back wall. Believed carved in the 4th century; rectangular carved columns of quartz-rich sandstone, arches, curved ceilings with carved decoration; an Aksumite-type inscribed sandstone slab found nearby supports the early date. The most accessible of all Tigray rock churches — 500 m from the main road. A 4th-century church of the Ezana generation — the conversion era made stone.

Photograph by Bernard Gagnon · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0, 2.5, 2.0, 1.0 (GFDL 1.2 also offered) — shared here under the same license.

Debre Berhan Selassie — Wall and Tower

Late 17th century · Gondar, Ethiopia

Debre Berhan Selassie ("Trinity and Mountain of Light"), built in the late 17th century, is the church that survived Gondar's 1888 sacking — tradition says a swarm of bees, aided by the Archangel Michael with a flaming sword, turned the Dervishes from its doors. Inside, every wall and the ceiling are painted: the life of Christ, the Passion, and the famous ceiling of winged cherub faces. The Trinity (Matthew 28:19) and the angelic host (Revelation 4–5; Psalm 91:11) — the church whose ceiling is the choir of heaven. Honest scope note: this keeper is the exterior; the famous angel ceiling itself is not in this haul.

Photograph by Bernard Gagnon · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0, 2.5, 2.0, 1.0 (GFDL 1.2 also offered) — shared here under the same license.

Horsemen Mural — Narga Selassie, Lake Tana

Late 18th century · Dek Island, Lake Tana

Narga Selassie ("Trinity of the Rest") on Dek Island, Lake Tana, was built by Empress Mentewab in the late 18th century, reputedly using a gigantic sycamore fig for doors and roof; its maqdas paintings completely cover the walls, including the foundress prostrated at the feet of Mary and Jesus. A new Lake Tana face to set beside the held Ura Kidane Mehret exterior — this one is the painting inside. The equestrian saints (George above all) who ride through Ethiopian church painting — the saints' lives as the congregation's picture-Bible, in the monastery tradition that preserved the Ge'ez canon.

Photograph by A. Davey · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY 2.0

Wall Two — The Ground of Scripture

The ground the Scriptures themselves walk: the mountain of the Law, the town of the birth, the city of the empty tomb, and the cave that kept the scrolls — and the Galilee Jesus walked, the mountain Moses viewed from, and the map the whole canon walks on.

St. Catherine’s Monastery — Sinai

Established AD 565 · Sinai, Egypt

Founded under the Emperor Justinian I and established in AD 565 at the foot of Mount Sinai, on the ground tradition identifies with the Burning Bush, St. Catherine’s is among the oldest continuously functioning Christian monasteries in the world, and its walled compound guards one of the great manuscript libraries of the ancient church. Exodus walks this ground: the Burning Bush (Exodus 3) and the Giving of the Law on the mountain above (Exodus 19–20).

Photograph by Berthold Werner · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0 — shared here under the same license.

Church of the Nativity — Bethlehem

Dedicated 339, rebuilt under Justinian · Bethlehem

The basilica over the Grotto of the Nativity was commissioned by Constantine the Great after his mother Helena’s visit of 325–326, likely built 330–333 and dedicated on 31 May 339. Destroyed by fire during the Samaritan revolts of the 6th century, it was rebuilt by the Emperor Justinian and stands basically unchanged since — the oldest major church in the Holy Land, and the grotto the oldest site continuously used for Christian worship. Here, in David’s Bethlehem, the town Micah named (Micah 5:2), the Gospels place the birth of Jesus (Matthew 2; Luke 2).

Photograph by Berthold Werner · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0 — shared here under the same license.

Church of the Holy Sepulchre — Jerusalem

Crusader church, rebuilt 1810 · Jerusalem

This church stands on the site Christian tradition regards as the place of Jesus’ crucifixion, burial and resurrection, and is named for his empty tomb. Crusader rebuilding in the 12th century gave much of the church its Romanesque character, gathering Golgotha and the tomb under one roof; the present fabric dates largely from the rebuilding after the fire of 1808, completed 1810. Its chapels are shared under the long-standing Status Quo arrangement among the Greek Orthodox, Armenian and Roman Catholic churches — with Ethiopian Orthodox worship on its roof at Deir es-Sultan. Its chapters are the Passion and Resurrection chapters of all four Gospels: Matthew 27–28, Mark 15–16, Luke 23–24, John 19–20.

Photograph by Berthold Werner · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0 — shared here under the same license.

The Scroll Cave — Qumran

c. 150 BCE – 72/73 CE · Judaean Desert

The settlement at Qumran, in the Judaean Desert above the Dead Sea, is associated with a community — usually identified with the Essenes — who lived there from about 150 BCE until 72/73 CE. In 1947 Bedouin shepherds found the first scrolls in the cave in this photograph; eleven caves eventually yielded the Dead Sea Scrolls, the oldest known biblical manuscripts. The caves also preserved fragments of books the Ethiopian canon never lost — Enoch traditions, including the Book of Giants, and Jubilees material, alongside the Temple Scroll and the great Isaiah Scroll. Enoch and Jubilees stand complete only in the 81-book canon.

Photograph by Effi Schweizer, released by the photographer into the public domain · Wikimedia Commons · Public domain

The Synagogue at Capernaum

4th-century synagogue over a 1st-century one · Sea of Galilee

Capernaum was Jesus’ base in Galilee. The standing white limestone synagogue is fourth-century, built over the black-basalt synagogue of Jesus’ own day; the town’s houses, the octagonal church over Peter’s house, and the synagogue were excavated from the nineteenth century on. Matthew 4:12–13 (Jesus “dwelt in Capernaum”), Mark 1:21 (teaching in the synagogue on the Sabbath), Luke 7:2–5 (the centurion who “built us a synagogue”). An honest note stands on this card: the camera stands on a later building over the original site — the fourth-century synagogue over the first-century one, not Jesus’ own walls.

Photograph by David Shankbone · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0 — shared here under the same license.

The Madaba Map — Jerusalem Panel

6th-century mosaic · Church of St. George, Madaba, Jordan

The oldest surviving cartographic depiction of the Holy Land, a sixth-century floor mosaic in the Byzantine church of St. George at Madaba; about a quarter of the original survives, naming some 150 towns. This panel shows Jerusalem — Damascus Gate, St. Stephen’s Gate, Golden Gate, Tower of David, Church of the Holy Sepulchre, Cardo Maximus. The map IS the geography of the canon in stone: Jerusalem, Bethlehem, Jericho, and the Jordan, drawn by Christians who still walked those roads, picturing the world of Joshua through the Gospels. An honest note: a detail panel of the map, not the whole fifteen-metre floor, and the church above it is modern, the mosaic Byzantine.

Photograph by David Bjorgen · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0 — shared here under the same license.

The Basilica of the Annunciation — Nazareth

Consecrated 1969 · Nazareth

The present basilica (consecrated 1969, architect Giovanni Muzio) stands over the Grotto of the Annunciation, on the site of three earlier churches — Byzantine, Crusader, and later — at the south of the ancient Nazareth village, where excavations also found houses, wells, and granaries of the old village. Luke 1:26–38, the Annunciation to Mary in Nazareth; Matthew 2:23 and Luke 2:39–52, Jesus’ upbringing in Nazareth. An honest note: a modern basilica on an ancient holy site, not an ancient building itself.

Photograph by David Shankbone · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0 — shared here under the same license.

The Memorial Church of Moses — Mount Nebo

Byzantine basilica under a modern memorial church · Jordan

On the summit where Moses viewed the Promised Land, a small church visited by the pilgrim Egeria in the fourth century was rebuilt and enlarged in the fifth–sixth centuries with side chapels, baptistery, and rich mosaics; the modern Memorial Church of Moses (restored 2013) protects the Byzantine basilica. Deuteronomy 34:1–4 — Moses on Nebo viewing the land he would not enter — and Numbers 27, the succession to Joshua on the same ground. An honest note: a modern protective church over a Byzantine basilica over a traditional site, and Moses’ burial place itself is unknown (Deuteronomy 34:6) — the photo shows the memorial, not a grave.

Photograph by David Bjorgen · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 2.5 — shared here under the same license.

The Garden of Gethsemane — Ancient Olive Trees

Jerusalem · Mount of Olives

"Gethsemane" means oil press. The garden at the foot of the Mount of Olives holds eight ancient olives, scientifically dated to around 900 years old but with rootstock possibly far older; a 1,500-year-old Byzantine church and a Second Temple mikveh were found at the site in 2020. The present Church of All Nations (1924) stands over a Byzantine church and a Crusader church, enshrining the Rock of Agony. Matthew 26:36–46; Mark 14:32–42; Luke 22:39–46 — Jesus' agony and arrest, the night before the Crucifixion. Honest note: the trees are ancient but are not proven to be the very trees of that night; the garden, not a building, is the keeper here.

Photograph by Ian Scott · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 2.0 — shared here under the same license.

Dominus Flevit — The Lord Wept

1953–55 · Mount of Olives, Jerusalem

Teardrop-shaped church (1953–55, Antonio Barluzzi) on a 5th-century Byzantine church site, over tombs back to c. 1600 BC; its west window frames Jerusalem, with the altar cross aligned on the Holy Sepulchre. Luke 19:41–44 — Jesus weeping over Jerusalem on the approach from the Mount of Olives; the prophecy of the city's fall, fulfilled AD 70. Honest note: a 1950s church on a Byzantine site marking a Crusader-or-later tradition; the weeping spot is traditional, not excavated fact.

Photograph by Alon Alush · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 4.0 — shared here under the same license.

The Cave of the Patriarchs — Machpelah, from the South

Herodian enclosure · Hebron

Over the cave stands a great Herodian-era rectangular enclosure; a Byzantine basilica was built on it, converted to the Ibrahimi Mosque after the Muslim conquest, retaken by Crusaders, restored as a mosque by Saladin in 1188, divided since 1967. The cave itself is rarely accessible; the building, not the cave, is what the camera sees. Genesis 23 — Abraham buying the field and cave of Machpelah from Ephron for Sarah's burial, the patriarchs' one deeded plot in the promised land; Genesis 25:9; 49:29–32; 50:13. Honest note: the camera shows the enclosure and later mosque/church layers, not the cave; and the site is a living, contested holy place for Jews, Christians and Muslims — recorded here as Genesis ground, not as any one community's alone.

Photograph by Djampa · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 4.0, 3.0, 2.5, 2.0, 1.0 (GFDL 1.2 also offered) — shared here under the same license.

Wall Three — The Apostolic Churches

The churches of the apostolic age: a library in Paul’s Ephesus, the cave of John’s Revelation, the first house church ever found — and the cave church where disciples were first called Christians.

The Library of Celsus — Ephesus

c. 110–117 AD · Ephesus, Türkiye

The Library of Celsus was built about 110–117 AD by the consul Tiberius Julius Aquila Polemaeanus as a memorial to his father, the former consul Tiberius Julius Celsus Polemaeanus; it held some 12,000 scrolls and was the third-largest library of the Roman Empire, after Alexandria and Pergamum. Its interior burned in 262 AD; the façade fell in an earthquake around 1000 AD and stood as rubble until German archaeologists completed its reconstruction in 1978. Paul lived and taught in this city (Acts 19), the Letter to the Ephesians bears its name, and Ephesus is the first of the seven churches addressed in Revelation (2:1–7).

Photograph by Warren LeMay · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 2.0 — shared here under the same license.

Cave of the Apocalypse — Patmos

Patmos, Greece · the cave of Revelation 1:9

The cave on the road between Chora and Skala is reputed to be where John received and dictated the Book of Revelation; the mosaic over its door shows him dictating to his scribe Prochoros. Above it on the hill stands the Monastery of Saint John the Theologian, founded in 1088, around which the town of Chora grew; cave, monastery and town together were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1999. Its chapter is Revelation’s first: “I John… was in the isle that is called Patmos, for the word of God, and for the testimony of Jesus Christ” (Revelation 1:9).

Photograph by Vladimir Boskovic · Wikimedia Commons · Public domain

The Good Shepherd — Dura-Europos

c. 233–235 AD · baptistry fresco, now at Yale

The Dura-Europos church is the earliest identified Christian house church: an ordinary house converted for worship about 233–235 AD, with one room made into a baptistry. When the city was besieged in 256 AD, defenders filled the buildings along the western wall with earth — a rampart that buried and preserved both this church and the neighbouring synagogue for some 1,700 years. The house does not stand to be visited; what survives is its baptistry’s painting, removed after excavation and now kept at the Yale University Art Gallery — the Good Shepherd, with Adam and Eve below, among the earliest depictions of Jesus ever found. The fresco preaches the canon in paint: Genesis 3 below the Shepherd, Psalm 23 and John 10 in the Shepherd himself, over the very font where new believers were baptised.

The ancient painter is unknown; photograph by Marsyas · Wikimedia Commons · Public domain

The Church of St. Peter — Antioch

Cave church, Crusader façade, 1863 restoration · Antakya, Türkiye

A cave carved into Mount Starius (13 by 9.5 by 7 metres), where tradition says Peter preached and founded the Antioch church. The oldest surviving parts are fourth–fifth century (mosaic floor, fresco traces); Crusaders lengthened it and built the façade, and Capuchins restored it in 1863. Now a museum; worship by permit. Acts 11:26 — “the disciples were called Christians first in Antioch” — Acts 13:1–3, the Antioch church sending out Paul and Barnabas, and Galatians 2:11, Peter at Antioch. An honest note: cave plus Crusader façade plus nineteenth-century restoration — the “oldest church” claim is tradition, and the visible front is not first-century.

Photograph by Volkan Hatem · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0 — shared here under the same license.

Philippi — Floor Mosaic Naming Paul

Octagonal Basilica · Philippi, Greece

Philippi, Roman colony on the Via Egnatia, UNESCO 2016: forum, theatre, Basilicas A and B (c. 550), and the Octagonal Church built over the "Paul" basilica. The so-called Prison of Paul is a Roman cistern later turned into a shrine — tradition, not proven prison. Acts 16 — Lydia's baptism (the first in Europe), Paul and Silas imprisoned and freed, the Philippian jailer's conversion; the Letter to the Philippians. Honest note: the mosaic names Paul but is centuries later than Paul; the photo is a detail, not the whole basilica.

Photograph by Berthold Werner · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0 — shared here under the same license.

St. Paul's Catacombs — Rabat, Malta

4th–8th centuries · Rabat, Malta

Underground Late Roman cemeteries (4th–8th centuries) outside the walls of Melite (Mdina/Rabat), over 30 hypogea, 2,000+ sq m; used by Punic, Roman, Jewish and Christian families; investigated by A. A. Caruana from 1894. Malta's earliest and largest archaeological evidence of Christianity, named for nearby St. Paul's Grotto, where tradition says Paul preached after the shipwreck. Acts 27–28 — the shipwreck on Malta (Melita), the viper, and Paul's three months on the island; the catacombs are the island's first Christian community made visible in stone. Honest note: the catacombs post-date Paul by centuries; they are evidence of the church Paul's visit seeded, not of Paul himself.

Photograph by G. Mannaerts · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 4.0 — shared here under the same license.

Wall Four — The Imperial Churches

The great churches of the Christian empire: Justinian’s Holy Wisdom, Ravenna’s sanctuary of gold — and Rome’s layered basilica, where the Cross grows as a tree.

Hagia Sophia — Istanbul

Built 532–537 · Istanbul, Türkiye

The present Hagia Sophia was built 532–537 on the orders of the Emperor Justinian I, designed by the Greek scientists Isidore of Miletus and Anthemius of Tralles — the third Church of the Holy Wisdom on the site. It was dedicated to the Logos, the Holy Wisdom of God, and its massive dome made it the world’s largest cathedral for nearly a thousand years; it is considered the epitome of Byzantine architecture. It is named for Sophia — Holy Wisdom, the Wisdom of Proverbs 8 and of the Wisdom of Solomon, a book the Ethiopian canon keeps — and for a thousand years it was the great church of the Greek-reading Bible world.

Photograph by Rraj89 · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 4.0 — shared here under the same license.

The Apse Mosaic — San Vitale, Ravenna

Consecrated 547 · Ravenna, Italy

San Vitale was consecrated in 547 AD, and its sanctuary mosaics are among the supreme survivals of Byzantine art. In the apse, Christ sits enthroned between two archangels, with St. Vitalis and Bishop Ecclesius, who offers a model of the church; around the sanctuary stand the offering scenes of Abraham entertaining the angels and the Sacrifice of Isaac, the sacrifices of Abel and Melchizedek, and the famous court panels of the Emperor Justinian and the Empress Theodora. The walls read Genesis in gold: Abraham and Isaac (Genesis 18 and 22), Abel and Melchizedek (Genesis 4 and 14), framing the altar — the old sacrifices gathered around the Lamb.

Photograph by Petar Milošević · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 4.0 — shared here under the same license.

The Apse Mosaic — San Clemente, Rome

12th-century mosaic · Rome

Three tiers: the present basilica (just before 1100) over a fourth-century basilica converted from a Roman nobleman’s house, part of which served as a house church in the first century; below that a second-century mithraeum. The twelfth-century apse mosaic shows the Cross as the Tree of Life. Dedicated to Pope Clement I, third successor of Peter — the “fellowlabourer” of Philippians 4:3 by early tradition — in the city where, by tradition, Peter and Paul were martyred, the city of Romans itself. An honest note: the mosaic is twelfth-century, not the first-century house-church layer, and the photo shows the upper church, not the dig below.

Photograph by Sailko · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0 — shared here under the same license.

Wall Five — The First Christian Nations

The nations that took the Gospel first and kept it: Armenia, the first state to adopt Christianity, and Georgia, converted in the fourth century — their great cathedrals, still standing.

Etchmiadzin Cathedral

Core 483/4, dome and belfries 17th century · Vagharshapat, Armenia

Mother church of the Armenian Apostolic Church. Tradition dates the original to 301–303 under Gregory the Illuminator after King Tiridates III adopted Christianity — Armenia the first state to do so. The core of the present building is 483/4, with a seventeenth-century dome and belfries and an 1868 sacristy; a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2000. Its canon tie is the first Christian nation itself, built on the apostolic preaching tradition (Thaddeus and Bartholomew in Armenia), and Gregory’s vision of the Only-Begotten descending — the Incarnation of John 1 — striking the ground with a golden hammer to mark the site. An honest note: a small file (800 by 600), usable as it stands.

Photograph by Heretiq · Wikimedia Commons · Public domain

Svetitskhoveli Cathedral from Jvari

Present building 1010–1029 · Mtskheta, Georgia

Svetitskhoveli (“Life-Giving Pillar”) is Georgia’s great cathedral, on the site of a fourth-century church; the present building is eleventh century (1010–1029). Tradition says Christ’s robe, brought by a Mtskheta Jew from Jerusalem, is buried beneath it, and a cedar pillar miraculously rose over the spot. Jvari monastery above it is sixth–seventh century. The robe of Christ (John 19:23–24, the seamless robe cast lots for at the Cross) is Georgia’s founding relic, and the church that guards it stands for the conversion of Georgia under St. Nino in the fourth century. An honest note: a distant view of the cathedral in its town, not a close architectural frame.

Photograph by Kober · Wikimedia Commons · Public domain

The Hanging Church — Old Cairo

5th–7th century, restored 9th–10th · Old Cairo, Egypt

The Church of St. Mary at Babylon, built over the gatehouse of the Roman fortress of Babylon; the nave suspended over a passage, hence "hanging"; principal church 5th–7th century, restored under Patriarchs Yusab I (830–849) and Abraham (975–978); seat of the Coptic Pope from 1047; 110 icons, ebony-and-ivory iconostasis. Matthew 2:13–15, the Holy Family's flight into Egypt (the neighbouring Abu Serga crypt is the traditional resting place on that flight); and the Coptic church that kept the Scriptures in Egypt while the Ethiopian church kept them in Africa — sister streams of the same ancient Bible. Honest note: the flight-into-Egypt resting place is tradition, and the Hanging Church itself is a Coptic shrine of Mary, not a Gospel event site; the tie is Egypt-and-exile, stated as such.

Photograph by Blueshade · Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 2.5 — shared here under the same license.
